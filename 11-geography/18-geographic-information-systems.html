Geographic information systems. Putting "where" into data, so the map can answer questions.

An ordinary map is a picture. A geographic information system, or GIS, is a map that you can question. Which homes lie within 500 metres of a river that floods? Where should the next ambulance station go so that nobody waits more than ten minutes? Which forests were cleared last year? Behind the delivery app on your phone, the flood map that sets your insurance price and the satellite images on the evening news sits the same idea: almost every fact happens somewhere, and knowing where lets you connect facts that otherwise look unrelated.

Question 1 · What is it?

A database where every record knows its place

A GIS is a combination of software, data and methods for capturing, storing, analysing and displaying information that is tied to locations on Earth.

Chapter 01

A doctor, a pump and a map of deaths

In late August 1854, cholera exploded in the Soho district of London. Within days, hundreds of people living within a few streets of each other were dead. Most doctors at the time believed cholera spread through "miasma", bad air from rotting matter. A physician named John Snow suspected the water instead.

Snow gathered the addresses of the dead and marked each death as a small bar on a street map. The bars piled up around a public water pump on Broad Street. He also found telling exceptions. A nearby brewery had almost no deaths; its workers drank beer and water from the brewery's own well. A woman who died miles away turned out to have had water from the Broad Street pump delivered because she liked its taste. Snow persuaded local officials to remove the pump's handle, and his map became one of the most famous arguments in the history of public health.

Snow had no computer, but he did exactly what a GIS does. He took records that were only a list of names and addresses, gave each one a location, placed it on a layer above the streets and the pumps, and looked for a pattern that the list could not show. The map did not just display the answer. It was how the answer was found. Epidemiology still uses the method, as Public Health · Epidemiology explains.

Chapter 02

Layers on a shared grid

The easiest way to picture a GIS is as a stack of transparent sheets laid over the same piece of ground. One sheet holds roads. Another holds rivers. Another holds the boundaries of land parcels, another the population of each district, another the height of the land. Because every sheet uses the same coordinates, you can look through several at once, or ask the computer to combine them.

Each object on a layer has two parts. One is its geometry: where it is and what shape it has. The other is its attributes: a table of facts about it, such as a road's name, speed limit and surface, or a district's population and average income. A GIS is therefore a database and a map at the same time. Click on a point and you get its record; query the records and you get a map.

The first real system of this kind was built in the 1960s by the Canadian geographer Roger Tomlinson for the Canada Land Inventory, which needed to assess land across a vast country for farming, forestry and wildlife. Doing that by overlaying paper maps by hand would have taken an army of staff. Tomlinson's computer system did it automatically, and he is often called the father of GIS. In 1969 Jack and Laura Dangermond founded Esri, the company whose ArcGIS software later became the industry standard. Today a free, open-source program called QGIS does much of the same work.

Turning an address into coordinates is called geocoding, and it is one of the most common steps in any project. It is also where many errors begin. A misspelt street, a new housing estate or a rural address with no street name can place a record kilometres from where it belongs.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Two ways to describe the world, one flat sheet, and a set of questions

Under the friendly map on the screen, a GIS has to solve three problems: how to store shapes, how to flatten a round planet, and how to turn layers into answers.

Chapter 04

Vector and raster: drawings and mosaics

There are two basic ways to store geographic data. The vector model works like a drawing made of precise lines. A well is a point. A road is a line, stored as a series of points joined together. A lake or a country is a polygon, a closed shape. Vector data is compact and exact, and it suits things with clear edges, such as buildings, pipes and borders.

The raster model works like a mosaic or a digital photograph. The land is divided into a grid of equal square cells, and each cell holds one value: the height of the ground, the temperature, the type of land cover. Satellite images are rasters. Rasters suit things that change gradually across space, such as rainfall or elevation, and make some calculations very fast.

The size of a raster cell is its resolution. A grid with 30-metre cells, the standard for the long-running US Landsat satellites, can show fields and city blocks but not individual cars. A grid with 30-centimetre cells, available from commercial satellites, can. Choosing the model and the resolution is choosing what the analysis is able to see.

Chapter 05

Flattening a round Earth

Every position needs coordinates. The most familiar are latitude, the angle north or south of the equator, and longitude, the angle east or west of a line through Greenwich in London. These describe points on a curved surface. But screens and paper are flat, and you cannot flatten the skin of an orange without stretching or tearing it. Every flat map is therefore a projection, a mathematical rule for flattening, and every projection distorts something: area, shape, distance or direction.

The most famous is the projection Gerardus Mercator published in 1569. It keeps angles correct, which made it ideal for sailors: a straight line on the chart is a constant compass bearing. The cost is that areas swell enormously towards the poles. On a Mercator map Greenland looks about as large as Africa, when in fact Africa is roughly fourteen times bigger. Most web maps use a close relative of Mercator, because it lets map tiles fit together seamlessly when you zoom.

The classic beginner's error

Mixing layers stored in different coordinate systems. If one layer uses latitude and longitude and another uses a national grid in metres, and the software is not told, roads can appear in the sea and distances can be wrong by large amounts. Professional GIS work starts by checking that every layer shares the same coordinate reference system.

Chapter 06

The core operations

Most GIS analysis is built from a small set of operations, combined like words in a sentence.

Buffer

Draw a zone of a set distance around something. "Everything within 200 metres of a school." "All land within 1 kilometre of the river." It turns a vague question about nearness into a precise shape.

Overlay

Combine layers to find where conditions coincide. A site for a wind farm might need strong wind, gentle slopes, no protected habitat and a power line nearby. Each condition is a layer; the overlay shows where all four are true.

Spatial join

Attach facts from one layer to another by location. Every reported crime is matched to the district it fell in, so crimes can be counted per district and compared with population.

Network analysis

Work along roads, pipes or rail lines rather than in straight lines. Find the fastest route, the area reachable within ten minutes, or the best set of depots to serve a list of customers.

A worked example shows how they chain together. A city wants to know how many elderly people live more than a 15-minute walk from a pharmacy. The analyst takes the pharmacies, runs a network analysis along footpaths to draw 15-minute walking zones, overlays those zones on census blocks, and uses a spatial join to add up the people aged over 75 who fall outside them. Each step is simple. The power comes from linking them.

Chapter 07

Near things are more related

"Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things."

Waldo Tobler · 1970 · the "first law of geography"

Tobler's sentence sounds obvious, and it is the foundation of spatial statistics. Rainfall at one weather station tells you a lot about rainfall a kilometre away and little about rainfall a continent away. Because of this, a GIS can estimate values between measured points, a technique called interpolation. Weather maps, pollution maps and maps of underground water levels are mostly interpolated from a limited number of measuring stations.

The same principle lets analysts find hot spots: places where high values cluster more than chance would produce, such as a cluster of cancer cases or traffic accidents. That is harder than it looks. Random events also clump, as Volume 39 explains in Random does not look random. A good hot spot analysis asks whether a cluster is bigger than chance alone would create, not just whether it looks alarming.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

From earthquake response to the shop on the corner

Location data quietly runs much of modern life. A few examples show the range.

Chapter 08

Mapping a disaster in days

When an earthquake struck Haiti in January 2010, rescuers arriving in Port-au-Prince found that detailed street maps of much of the city barely existed. Within days, volunteers around the world, working on OpenStreetMap from newly released satellite images, traced streets, buildings and the camps where survivors gathered. Aid groups downloaded the result to plan their work. The episode showed that a map could be built by a crowd, at speed, for a place that had never been properly mapped.

Since then, this kind of volunteer mapping has become a routine part of disaster response, and also of everyday planning in places that official mapping agencies neglect. Responders now combine it with satellite images taken before and after an event to find damaged areas, flood extents and blocked roads. The same tools help forecast where floods and wildfires are likely to spread, which links to Coastal regions and to the disaster chapters of Volume 23.

Chapter 09

Planning, business and health

Governments use GIS to decide where to put schools, clinics, bus routes and fire stations, to register who owns which land, and to draw electoral districts. Utilities use it to manage millions of pipes and cables. Farmers use it for precision agriculture, varying seed, water and fertiliser across a field according to maps of soil and past yields.

Businesses use it constantly. A retailer choosing a new store site overlays population, incomes, competitors and traffic. A delivery company plans routes for thousands of vans every morning. An insurer prices home policies partly on flood and fire maps. Health agencies map disease, vaccination rates and travel times to hospitals, which is how gaps in access become visible. Much of the "where" in daily decisions now comes from a GIS somewhere, whether or not anyone calls it that.

Chapter 10

Watching the whole planet change

The US Landsat programme has photographed Earth's land surface continuously since 1972, building the longest unbroken record of the planet from orbit. In 2008 the US government made the whole archive free to download, and use of the images exploded. The European Union's Copernicus programme, with its Sentinel satellites, also releases its data free. Together they let anyone with a laptop measure change over decades.

That record has transformed environmental science. Researchers have used it to map forest loss across the whole tropics year by year, to track the growth of cities, to watch glaciers retreat and lakes shrink, and to monitor crops for early warnings of famine. Free satellite data turned "how much has changed?" from a guess into a measurement. It is a key source for files such as Deforestation and Urbanization.

Chapter 12

How maps mislead

The commonest data map colours each area, such as a county or region, by a value like income or voting share. It is called a choropleth. It has a built-in bias: big, empty areas dominate the eye, while small, crowded ones almost vanish. An election map coloured by county can look like a landslide for the party that wins the countryside, even when the other party won more votes in the cities.

A subtler problem has a clumsy name, the modifiable areal unit problem, studied and popularised by the British geographer Stan Openshaw. The same data, grouped into different boundaries, can produce different averages and even opposite conclusions. Draw the districts one way and an area looks mixed; draw them another way and it looks segregated. This is also the mechanism behind gerrymandering, drawing electoral boundaries to favour one party, which needs nothing more than a GIS and a target.

A related trap is the ecological fallacy: assuming that what is true of an area is true of the individuals in it. A district with high average income can contain many poor households. The geographer Mark Monmonier made the general point in a well-known book, How to Lie with Maps: every map is a selection, and the selection carries a point of view.

Finally, privacy. A 2013 study of mobile phone records found that just four points in space and time, such as where a phone was at four different hours, were enough to single out around 95 per cent of the people in the data set. Location data stripped of names is often far less anonymous than it seems.

Chapter 14

Who owns the map?

For most of history, detailed maps were state secrets or expensive products. Today a few large technology companies hold the most used maps in the world, governments release free satellite data, and volunteers maintain an open alternative. That mix raises questions that are political, not technical. Who decides what a map shows, such as a disputed border or the name of a place? Who can see where people go? Should a company's map of a city's traffic be treated as a public resource?

The likely direction is towards maps that are more detailed, more frequent and more automated, and therefore more powerful in the hands of whoever controls them. The skill worth having is not operating the software but asking how a map was made: which data, which boundaries, which projection and which colours. Those four questions catch most of the ways a map can mislead.

Snow's map worked because he knew what question he was asking and went looking for the cases that did not fit. That habit matters more now than ever, because the maps are far more convincing than his.

The core idea

A GIS stores facts together with their locations, in layers that share one coordinate system, as vector shapes or raster grids. Simple operations such as buffers, overlays and spatial joins, chained together, answer questions that no single dataset could. The same power can mislead if boundaries, projections or colours are chosen carelessly.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1854: John Snow's cholera map of Soho.
  • 1960s: Roger Tomlinson's Canada Geographic Information System.
  • Landsat has imaged Earth since 1972; its archive has been free since 2008.
  • Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland, despite Mercator.
The common mistake

Treating a map as a neutral photograph of reality. Every map is a set of choices about data, boundaries, projection and colour, and each choice can change the story.

The words, in plain words
GIS
A system for capturing, storing, analysing and displaying data tied to locations.
Layer
One theme of data, such as roads or rivers, laid over the same ground as other layers.
Attribute
A fact stored about a mapped object, such as a road's name or a district's population.
Vector data
Geography stored as points, lines and polygons with exact coordinates.
Raster data
Geography stored as a grid of cells, each holding one value.
Resolution
The size of each cell in a raster, which sets the smallest thing it can show.
Projection
A rule for flattening the curved Earth onto a flat map, always with some distortion.
Geocoding
Turning an address or place name into coordinates.
Buffer
A zone of fixed distance drawn around a point, line or area.
Overlay
Combining layers to find where several conditions are true at once.
Interpolation
Estimating values between measured points, using the fact that nearby places are similar.
Choropleth
A map that colours areas by a value, such as income or vote share.
Modifiable areal unit problem
The way results change when the same data is grouped into different boundaries.
