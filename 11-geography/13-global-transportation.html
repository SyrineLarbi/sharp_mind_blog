Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 13 of 20

Global transportation. The world shrank, but only along certain lines.

In the 1840s a letter from London to Bombay took over a month. Today a person can make the same journey in about nine hours, and a phone made in Shenzhen can reach a shop in Lagos for a few dollars of freight. That collapse in the cost of distance is one of the great changes of modern history. It did not happen evenly. Transport runs on networks, and networks favour some places over others: the port with deep water, the airport where routes meet, the city on the railway. This file explains how the world's transport systems work as a whole, why they look the way they do, what they made possible, what they cost, and how they are likely to change.

Question 1 · What is it?

Five modes, one connected system

Global transportation is the network of ships, aircraft, trains, trucks and pipelines, plus the ports, terminals and rules that join them, that moves people and goods between places.

Chapter 01

The death of distance that never quite happened

In 1997 the journalist Frances Cairncross wrote a book called The Death of Distance, arguing that communications technology would make location matter far less. For information, she was largely right. For physical things, distance is alive. Economists have known since the 1960s that trade between two countries follows a simple pattern, known as the gravity model, first applied to trade by the Dutch economist Jan Tinbergen. Trade rises with the size of the two economies and falls with the distance between them, much as gravity depends on mass and distance. The pattern still holds remarkably well today.

What changed is not that distance stopped mattering, but that its cost fell steeply and unevenly. Geographers call this time-space compression: places connected by fast, cheap transport behave as if they were closer together, while places off the network stay far away. Transport does not shrink the world evenly. It pulls some places together and leaves others behind.

These modes are linked. A pair of shoes might travel by truck from a factory in Vietnam to a port, by ship to Rotterdam, by train to a warehouse in Germany, and by van to a customer. Moving goods between modes without unpacking them is called intermodal transport, and the standard container made it possible.

Chapter 03

Four revolutions in two centuries

The modern system was built in a few great jumps. Steam, from the early nineteenth century, freed ships from the wind and created railways. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, cut the sea route from Britain to India by thousands of kilometres, and the Panama Canal followed in 1914. In the same year as Suez, the first transcontinental railway across the United States was completed. Then came the jet age: from the late 1950s, jet airliners such as the Boeing 707 made long-distance travel fast enough for business and, later, mass tourism. Finally, from 1956, the container slashed the cost of handling cargo, a story told in Volume 12.

Each revolution changed the map. Railways created inland cities such as Chicago, which grew from a small town in the 1830s to one of the largest cities in the world by 1900, as the hub where rail lines from the West met the Great Lakes. Containers moved ports out of old city centres to huge new terminals with deep water and empty land. The docks of London and New York closed, while Felixstowe, Newark and later Shanghai grew.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Hubs, corridors and the economics of size

Transport networks are not spread evenly. They concentrate traffic in a few hubs and corridors, because concentration makes each journey cheaper.

Chapter 04

Why bigger is cheaper

The cost of moving goods falls with scale. A ship twice as long can carry far more than twice the cargo, because volume grows faster than the surface area that causes drag, while the crew stays about the same size. This is why container ships grew from about a thousand boxes in the early days to over twenty thousand today, and why oil tankers grew so large that some cannot pass through the Suez Canal fully loaded. The same logic applies to trains and aircraft, with limits set by track, runways and airports.

Scale explains where things go as well as how. Big ships can only call at ports with deep water, long quays and huge cranes, so cargo is concentrated in a small number of very large ports. In the mid-2020s Shanghai handled around fifty million standard containers a year, the most of any port, followed by Singapore and other Chinese ports such as Ningbo-Zhoushan and Shenzhen. Transport rewards the big with lower costs, which makes them bigger still. It is the same snowball described in the topic on geographic inequality.

Airlines and shipping lines face the same choice. A hub-and-spoke network sends traffic through a central point, filling large aircraft or ships and offering many connections. Dubai, Doha and Istanbul built their airlines on this model, using their position between Europe, Asia and Africa: Dubai International has for years been the world's busiest airport for international passengers. Atlanta, a hub for one of America's largest airlines, has usually been the busiest airport in the world by total passengers. Low-cost carriers such as Ryanair and Southwest instead fly mostly point to point, between pairs of cities, with no connections to wait for.

Hubs are powerful but fragile. When one is disrupted, delays spread across the whole network. A single storm at a major hub, or one ship stuck in a canal, can affect traffic on several continents.

Chapter 06

Rails across continents

Japan opened the first high-speed railway, the Shinkansen between Tokyo and Osaka, in 1964, in time for the Tokyo Olympics. France followed with the TGV in 1981. China then built the largest network by far, from almost nothing in 2008 to more than 40,000 kilometres by the mid-2020s, more than the rest of the world combined. High-speed rail competes best with flying on journeys of a few hundred kilometres, where city-centre stations beat the time spent getting to and through airports.

Freight rail is a different business. In the United States, heavy freight trains carry a large share of long-distance cargo, while passenger rail is small. Since the 2010s, regular container trains have run between China and Europe across Kazakhstan and Russia, taking roughly two to three weeks, faster than the sea and much cheaper than the air. They became part of China's Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013 to fund ports, railways, roads and power plants across Asia, Africa and beyond. Supporters see needed infrastructure. Critics point to debt burdens and Chinese strategic influence. Both views have evidence behind them, and outcomes have differed a lot between countries.

Chapter 07

Roads, pipelines and the last mile

Roads carry most freight within continents, because trucks can go door to door. Their weakness is cost over long distances and their dependence on infrastructure that many poor regions lack. In much of sub-Saharan Africa, poor roads and slow border crossings make moving a container inland cost more than shipping it across an ocean. The last mile, the final step to a home or shop, is often the most expensive part of any journey per kilometre, which is why delivery companies spend so much on route planning, lockers and local depots.

Pipelines are the least visible mode and among the most political. They lock buyer and seller into a long relationship, because the pipe cannot be moved. Europe's reliance on Russian gas pipelines shaped its politics for decades, and in 2022 explosions badly damaged the Nord Stream pipelines under the Baltic Sea. Investigations into who was responsible have been long and disputed.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Trade, cities, disease and power

Cheap transport reorganised where things are made, where people live, how fast diseases spread and what navies are for.

Chapter 08

The great unbundling

The economist Richard Baldwin describes globalisation as a series of "unbundlings". The first came with steam: once goods could be moved cheaply, production no longer had to happen near consumption. Britain could import grain and cotton and export cloth. The second came with containers and cheap communication from the 1980s and 1990s: factories themselves could be split across countries, with parts made in one place and assembled in another. That is the origin of the global value chain, in which a single product such as a smartphone or a car passes through many countries before it is finished.

This explains much of the economic rise of East Asia. Cheap, reliable shipping meant that a factory in Guangdong could serve customers in Germany and the United States. Without low transport costs, China's export model would have been impossible. Transport costs set the size of the market a factory can reach, and the size of the market decides what kind of factory is worth building.

Chapter 09

Disease travels at the speed of the network

Diseases have always followed trade routes. The Black Death travelled along them in the 1340s, a story told in Volume 18 (The year a third of Europe died). What air travel changed is the speed. In 2003 the SARS virus spread from southern China to several countries within weeks, partly through one hotel in Hong Kong where infected guests then flew home. In early 2020 COVID-19 reached every continent within a few months, following the routes of the air network. Researchers modelling outbreaks now use "effective distance", measured by flight connections rather than kilometres, because it predicts arrival times much better. A city a few hours from a major hub is, for a virus, closer than a village nearby with no airport.

Chapter 10

Fragility and strategy

The pandemic also showed how tightly the system had been tuned. In 2021, as demand for goods surged and ports struggled with outbreaks and labour shortages, ships queued outside ports in California and China, and the cost of shipping a container from Asia to Europe or the US rose several times over. In March 2021 a single ship, the Ever Given, blocked the Suez Canal for six days. From late 2023, attacks on ships in the Red Sea pushed many shipping lines to sail around Africa instead. A system optimised for low cost has very little slack when something goes wrong.

Because so much depends on sea lanes, transport is also strategy. Navies exist largely to protect trade routes and to be able to threaten an opponent's. The narrow passages listed in Volume 12 are central to the military planning of the United States, China, India and others. Control of ports, from Piraeus in Greece to Gwadar in Pakistan, has become part of competition between great powers.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every mode is a trade-off between cost, speed and damage

No mode is best at everything. Choosing one is choosing which costs to accept.

The tonne-kilometre, one tonne moved one kilometre, is the standard unit for comparing freight. By that measure a large container ship is extremely efficient, and air freight is the opposite, using many times more fuel for the same work. That is why fresh flowers, medicines and phones fly, while iron ore, grain and furniture sail.

Chapter 12

What cheap transport gave, and what it took

The gains

Cheaper goods for consumers everywhere. Access to world markets for poor countries that could export. Food from many climates, all year. Mass tourism and family visits across continents. Faster emergency aid. Specialisation that raised productivity in almost every economy.

The costs

Transport is one of the largest sources of greenhouse gases; shipping and aviation together account for roughly 5% of global carbon dioxide emissions, and road transport for far more. Air pollution near ports, roads and airports. Faster spread of disease and invasive species. Fragile supply chains. Jobs lost in places that could not compete.

Some of the costs have been tackled. In 2020 the International Maritime Organization, the UN body for shipping, sharply cut the permitted sulphur content of ship fuel, reducing a major source of air pollution near coasts. Emissions of carbon dioxide are harder. Shipping and international aviation fall outside most national climate targets, because they happen between countries. Both industries have set goals of net zero emissions by around 2050, but the means to get there are uncertain, and a global carbon price for shipping, agreed in principle at the IMO in 2025, had its formal adoption postponed later that year.

Chapter 14

Shorter chains, or just safer ones?

After the pandemic and the shocks of the early 2020s, many companies talked about nearshoring, moving production closer to customers, for example from China to Mexico for the US market, or to Eastern Europe and Turkey for the EU. There has been real movement, and Mexico became the largest source of US imports in 2023. But the overall picture is a reshuffle more than a retreat. Global trade by volume has kept growing, and many "new" supply chains still use Chinese parts at some stage.

The deeper constant is the gravity model from Chapter 01. Size and distance will keep shaping trade, but the meaning of distance depends on cost, and cost depends on fuel, politics and technology. If decarbonising shipping and aviation makes them more expensive, distance may matter a little more again, favouring regional trade. If new fuels prove cheap, the shrinking of the world will continue. For the engineering side of the networks, see Infrastructure, and for vehicles that drive themselves, Autonomous vehicles.

The core idea

Global transportation is five linked modes: sea, air, rail, road and pipeline. Economies of scale concentrate traffic in hubs and corridors, which pulls connected places together and leaves others behind. Cheap transport made global value chains possible, and also faster disease spread, fragile supply chains and large emissions.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Air freight: about 1% of trade by weight, roughly a third by value.
  • Shanghai: around 50 million standard containers a year, the busiest port (mid-2020s).
  • Shipping and aviation: roughly 5% of global CO₂ together.
The common mistake

Believing distance no longer matters. The gravity model still holds: trade falls with distance. What fell is the cost per kilometre, and only along well-connected routes.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a phone flies but a sofa sails, and why a landlocked African city can pay more to receive a container than Rotterdam pays to receive it from Shanghai? If you can, you understand modes, scale and networks together.
