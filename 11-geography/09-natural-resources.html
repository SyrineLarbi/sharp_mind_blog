Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 09 of 20

Natural resources. Geology deals the cards. What a country does with its hand is another matter.

The Earth's useful materials are scattered with no regard for borders or fairness. Some countries sit on oceans of oil, some on mountains of copper, some on almost nothing at all. You might expect the lucky ones to be rich and the unlucky ones poor. Often it works the other way: resource-poor Japan, South Korea and Switzerland became wealthy, while several countries with enormous mineral wealth stayed poor, unstable or both. This file explains what a resource is, where resources come from, how they are found and sold, why they can be a curse as well as a gift, and how the move away from fossil fuels is changing which ones matter.

Question 1 · What is it?

Anything in nature that people have learned to use

A resource is not just a substance. It is a substance plus the knowledge, the technology and the demand that make it worth taking.

Chapter 01

Resources are made, not found

For most of history, the black sticky liquid that seeped out of the ground in parts of Persia and Pennsylvania was a nuisance or, at best, a medicine and a lamp fuel. It became the world's most valuable commodity only after people invented refining, the internal combustion engine and the car. Uranium was a colouring for glass before nuclear physics. Lithium was a curiosity until rechargeable batteries. The economist Erich Zimmermann summed this up in the 1930s with a famous line: resources are not, they become.

So a natural resource is any material or energy source in nature that people can use, given their current technology and needs. The definition moves. Things can also stop being resources. Whale oil lit Western cities in the early nineteenth century, then lost its market to kerosene, which comes from petroleum. Saltpetre from Chilean deserts was a vital fertiliser and explosive until the Haber-Bosch process made nitrogen from the air, a story told in Volume 12 (Fertiliser).

Two words cause endless confusion in news stories. A resource, in the geologist's sense, is the total amount of something believed to exist in the ground. A reserve is the part that is known, measured and profitable to extract at today's prices with today's technology. Reserves are always much smaller than resources. And reserves can grow even as you dig, because higher prices or better methods turn resources into reserves. When someone says "we have forty years of oil left", they usually mean forty years of today's reserves, a number that has been close to forty for decades.

Chapter 03

Why they are where they are

Resources are concentrated because the processes that made them were concentrated. Oil and gas formed where ancient seas buried huge amounts of plankton under sediment, cooked it at the right temperature and then trapped it under rock that did not leak. The Persian Gulf had all three conditions in unusual abundance. Metal ores form where hot fluids moved through cracks in rock, usually along the edges of tectonic plates, which is why the Andes are lined with copper mines. Coal is the remains of vast swamp forests, many from a single geological period roughly 300 million years ago.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From rock in the ground to a price on a screen

A resource passes through finding, extracting, processing and selling. Each step has its own geography, and the most powerful step is often not the mine.

Chapter 04

Finding it, and why "running out" keeps not happening

Exploration is a gamble. Geologists use seismic surveys, which send sound waves into the ground and listen to the echoes, plus magnetic and gravity readings, satellite images and test drilling. Most exploration wells and prospects find nothing worth developing. A large new mine can take ten to twenty years from discovery to first production, because of permits, financing, roads, power and local agreements.

In 1956 the geologist M. King Hubbert predicted that oil production in the lower 48 United States would peak around 1970. He was right: conventional US output peaked that year and fell for decades. "Peak oil" became a global fear. Then, in the 2000s, two old techniques were combined: drilling sideways through thin layers of rock (horizontal drilling) and cracking that rock open with water, sand and chemicals at high pressure (hydraulic fracturing, or fracking). Oil and gas trapped in shale became reachable. By the late 2010s the United States was the world's largest oil producer again. Geology sets the ceiling, but price and technology decide how close you get to it.

Chapter 05

Booms, busts and the slow supply response

Resource prices swing far more than the prices of most manufactured goods. The reason is time. When demand rises, you cannot open a new copper mine next month. Prices shoot up. Companies then invest heavily. Years later all the new mines come online at once, just as demand cools, and prices crash. Investment stops, and the cycle prepares to repeat. Economists sometimes call the long waves of this pattern commodity supercycles. The rise of Chinese demand in the 2000s drove one of the largest.

Producers sometimes try to control the swings. The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, OPEC, was founded in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. A group like this, of producers who coordinate output to influence price, is called a cartel. Its most famous moment was the 1973 embargo, when Arab members cut supplies to countries that backed Israel in the October War, and the oil price roughly quadrupled. Since 2016 a wider group including Russia, known as OPEC+, has coordinated output. Its power is real but limited, because members cheat on quotas and because high prices invite new competitors such as US shale.

Chapter 06

Renewable is not the same as unlimited

The most dramatic resource failures involve resources that should have lasted forever. In the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the two rivers that fed the Aral Sea, in Central Asia, to irrigate cotton. The sea, once one of the four largest lakes in the world, lost most of its area within a few decades. Fishing towns were left tens of kilometres from the water, and dust from the dry seabed spread salt and chemicals over the region.

Groundwater can follow the same path more quietly. Water stored in underground rock layers, called aquifers, may have collected over thousands of years. The Ogallala aquifer under the American Great Plains and the aquifers beneath north-west India both supply huge farming regions, and in parts of both the water table has dropped steeply because pumping far exceeds refilling. Fish stocks too: the cod of Newfoundland's Grand Banks, fished for five centuries, collapsed so badly that Canada closed the fishery in 1992. A renewable resource becomes non-renewable the moment you take it faster than it grows back.

Chapter 07

The real chokepoint is processing

Digging ore out of the ground is only the first step. Most of it then has to be concentrated, smelted or chemically refined, which takes large factories, cheap energy and know-how. This middle step is often more concentrated than the mining itself. Lithium mined in Australia and cobalt mined in Congo are, for the most part, shipped to China to be refined. As of the mid-2020s China refined most of the world's lithium and cobalt and around nine-tenths of its rare earths.

That concentration became a political tool. In 2023 China placed export controls on gallium and germanium, two metals used in chips. In 2025 it added restrictions on several rare earths and the magnets made from them, which briefly disrupted car factories elsewhere. Western governments responded with lists of critical minerals and subsidies for mines and refineries at home. Volume 12 covers why rare earth processing is so hard to copy.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The paradox of plenty

Resources can build a nation or wreck one. The difference has much more to do with institutions than with geology.

Chapter 08

The resource curse

In 1993 the economic geographer Richard Auty gave a name to a puzzle: the resource curse. Many countries rich in oil or minerals grew more slowly, and governed themselves worse, than countries with little. A widely cited 1995 study by Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner found the same pattern across dozens of countries. Several mechanisms seem to be at work.

Dutch disease

After the Netherlands found a giant gas field at Groningen in 1959, export earnings pushed up the value of its currency. That made Dutch factory goods more expensive abroad, and manufacturing suffered. The Economist named the effect in 1977. Any resource boom can crowd out the rest of an economy in this way.

Volatility

Government budgets that depend on one commodity rise and fall with its price. Spending booms in good years leave debts and half-finished projects in bad ones.

Rent-seeking

Money that comes from the ground, not from citizens' work, is called a rent. A government funded by rents does not need to tax its people, so it has less reason to listen to them. Political energy goes into capturing the rent rather than creating new wealth.

Conflict

Lootable resources such as alluvial diamonds, which can be dug from riverbeds with simple tools, have funded rebel groups. Sierra Leone's civil war in the 1990s is the best-known case.

The curse is real but not inevitable, and researchers argue about its strength. Later studies found that much depends on how the data are measured and on the quality of institutions before the discovery. Norway, Australia, Canada and Chile all export large amounts of resources and are prosperous democracies. The curse seems to strike hardest where institutions were already weak when the money arrived.

Chapter 09

Two diamonds, two oils

Botswana was one of the poorest countries in the world when it became independent from Britain in 1966. Diamonds were discovered the next year. Instead of the usual story, it became one of the world's fastest-growing economies for several decades. Its government created a joint venture with the mining company De Beers, kept public finances careful, and invested in roads, schools and health. Diamonds also helped fund civil war in Sierra Leone in the same era. The stones were similar. The institutions were not.

Norway found oil in the North Sea in 1969. It put the state's oil income into a fund invested abroad, now known as the Government Pension Fund Global, and limited how much of it the government may spend each year. The fund is now worth well over a trillion US dollars, among the largest pools of investment in the world. Venezuela, with the largest proven oil reserves of any country, went the other way. Oil made up the great majority of its export earnings, public spending followed the oil price, and the collapse of prices after 2014, combined with economic mismanagement and falling output, led to hyperinflation and the emigration of millions of people.

Chapter 10

Resources and power between nations

Resources shape foreign policy. Britain switched its navy from coal to oil before the First World War and so tied its security to the Middle East. Japan's need for oil was one reason it went to war in 1941. And energy dependence shaped Europe's response to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Before the war Russia supplied a large share of Europe's natural gas, roughly 40 percent of EU imports. Within about two years that fell sharply as Europe turned to Norwegian pipelines and to liquefied natural gas (gas chilled into a liquid so it can travel by ship) from the United States and Qatar, at a high price for households and industry.

The lesson European governments drew is that dependence on a single supplier is a strategic risk, even when that supplier is cheap. The same reasoning now drives efforts to diversify supplies of the minerals in Chapter 07. Seen from the producer side, a resource is leverage. Seen from the consumer side, it is exposure.

Chapter 12

What breaks the curse

The countries that managed resource wealth well share a few habits. They saved part of the income in a sovereign wealth fund, a state-owned investment fund, often invested abroad so the money does not inflate the home currency. They set fiscal rules limiting how much of the windfall the government may spend each year. They published what companies paid and what governments received; since 2003 the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative has pushed for exactly this. And they used the money to diversify. The United Arab Emirates, especially Dubai, built aviation, trade, finance and tourism. Malaysia moved from tin and rubber into electronics manufacturing.

None of this is technically difficult. It is politically difficult, because it means a government refusing to spend money it already has. The best resource policy is often to act as if the resource were smaller than it is.

Chapter 14

Recycling, substitution and stranded assets

Metals, unlike oil, are not destroyed when used. A copper wire can be melted and drawn again indefinitely. As the stock of metal already in use grows, recycling can supply a rising share of demand, which Volume 15 discusses for batteries. Substitution matters too. Battery makers have shifted many cells to chemistries that use little or no cobalt, and sodium-ion batteries avoid lithium altogether. High prices tend to cure themselves in this way.

For fossil fuel producers, the risk runs the other way. If the world cuts emissions as governments have pledged, a share of today's oil, gas and coal reserves will never be burned. Investors call these stranded assets. Countries whose budgets depend on hydrocarbons, such as Saudi Arabia, Nigeria and Iraq, face the hardest version of the old question: how to turn a resource that may be worth less tomorrow into something lasting today. The geography of resources is shifting from where the oil is to where the metals are, and from who digs to who refines.

The core idea

A natural resource is a material that technology and demand make useful. Resources are unevenly placed by geology, often concentrated in a few countries, and the processing step is frequently more concentrated still. Resource wealth helps countries with strong institutions and can harm those without them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The DRC mines about 70% of the world's cobalt (mid-2020s).
  • China processes around 90% of rare earths.
  • Norway's oil fund is worth well over $1 trillion.
The common mistake

Treating reserves as a fixed tank that is slowly emptied. Reserves are an economic number: they grow when prices rise or technology improves, which is why predictions of running out have so often been wrong on timing.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Botswana and Sierra Leone, both diamond producers, had such different outcomes? If you can, you understand rents, institutions and the resource curse together.

The words, in plain words
Natural resource
Anything in nature people can use with their current technology.
Non-renewable resource
One formed over geological time, effectively finite on human timescales.
Renewable resource
One that regrows or refills, but only at a limited rate.
Reserve
The part of a resource that is known and profitable to extract today.
Aquifer
A layer of underground rock or gravel that holds water.
Fracking
Cracking rock with high-pressure fluid to release oil or gas.
Cartel
A group of producers who coordinate output to influence prices.
Commodity supercycle
A long rise and fall of raw material prices, driven by slow supply response.
Resource curse
The tendency of resource-rich countries to grow slower or govern worse.
Dutch disease
Damage to other export industries when a resource boom strengthens the currency.
Rent
Income from owning something scarce, not from producing more.
Sovereign wealth fund
A state-owned fund that invests national income, often abroad.
Critical minerals
Minerals a government judges essential and at risk of supply disruption.
Stranded asset
A resource or investment that loses its value before it is used, for example because of climate policy.
