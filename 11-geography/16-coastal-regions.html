Coastal regions. The most valuable land on Earth sits on the edge that moves.

A map makes the coastline look like a fixed line. It is nothing of the kind. Waves carry sand along it every day, storms redraw it in a night, deltas sink, and the sea itself has been rising for more than a century. Yet this unstable edge is where a huge share of humanity chose to live, trade and build its greatest cities. Understanding coasts means holding both facts at once: why the shore is so attractive, and why it will not keep still.

Question 1 · What is it?

A strip of land shaped by the sea, and crowded by people

A coastal region is the zone where land and sea influence each other: the shore itself, the shallow water in front of it, and the low land behind it that waves, tides and storms can reach.

Chapter 01

Where does the coast begin and end?

There is no single definition, and that is useful to know. A geologist might draw the coastal zone from the edge of the continental shelf, the shallow underwater platform that surrounds most continents, to the highest point storm waves ever reach. A planner might use a fixed distance, such as everything within 100 kilometres of the shore. A flood engineer cares about height above the sea more than distance from it.

The most revealing measure is the low-elevation coastal zone: land that touches the coast and lies less than 10 metres above sea level. It is a thin ribbon, only a few per cent of the world's land. Yet the IPCC, the UN's climate science panel, estimated that about 680 million people lived in it in 2010, close to a tenth of humanity, and that the number is likely to pass a billion by 2050. A much larger share of the world, often quoted at around 40 per cent, lives within 100 kilometres of a coast.

The point to take away is the mismatch. A tiny share of the land holds a large share of the people, and much of that land is exactly the part that the sea can reach.

Chapter 02

Why people crowd to the edge

People did not move to the coast by accident. For most of history, water was the cheapest way to move anything heavy. A sailing ship could carry more cargo across an ocean than a caravan of camels could carry across a desert, at a fraction of the cost. So trading cities grew where ships could shelter: natural harbours, river mouths and sheltered bays. Many of today's largest cities, from Tokyo and Shanghai to Mumbai, Lagos and New York, began as ports.

Several other advantages piled on top. Coastal plains and river deltas are flat and fertile, built from soil the rivers carried down. The sea provides fish. It also moderates the climate, keeping winters milder and summers cooler than inland. Later, beaches added tourism and retirement. Once a port city exists, its own size becomes the main reason people keep coming.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Waves move sand, rivers build land, and the sea is rising

A coastline is the result of a running battle between forces that remove material and forces that bring it. Change the balance, by building a dam, a harbour wall or a warmer climate, and the coast responds.

Chapter 04

Waves, tides and the river of sand

Most waves are made by wind blowing across the sea, sometimes thousands of kilometres away. When a wave reaches shallow water, its base drags on the bottom, it slows, rises and breaks. That breaking is where the work happens: it throws sand up the beach, pulls it back, and batters cliffs.

Waves rarely hit a beach straight on. When they arrive at an angle, they push sand up the beach at that angle, and gravity pulls it straight back down. Each wave moves each grain a little sideways. Over a year this adds up to a vast flow of sand moving along the shore, called longshore drift. A beach is not a pile of sand. It is a river of sand passing through. That is why a harbour wall or a row of short barriers called groynes can starve the beaches "downstream" of them: they catch the sand that would have fed their neighbours.

Tides are the twice-daily rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the Moon's gravity, with help from the Sun. Their size depends on the shape of the coast. In the open ocean the range is modest, but in some funnel-shaped bays it is enormous. In the Bay of Fundy in Canada, the difference between high and low tide can exceed 15 metres. Tides matter for flooding because a storm that arrives at high tide is far more dangerous than the same storm at low tide.

Chapter 05

Coasts that wear away and coasts that grow

On the Holderness coast of eastern England, cliffs made of soft clay left behind by Ice Age glaciers retreat by around a metre or two a year on average, one of the fastest rates in Europe. Villages recorded in medieval documents now lie under the North Sea. The material is not destroyed. It is carried south by longshore drift and helps build a long, curving spit of sand and shingle at the mouth of the Humber estuary.

That is the general pattern. Erosion in one place is usually deposition somewhere else. Hard rock headlands resist while soft rock bays between them wear back. Sand removed from one beach builds a spit, a sandbar or a dune field further along. A coast is a system of linked parts, and protecting one part often moves the problem down the shore.

Chapter 06

Deltas and the sinking cities

A delta is built by a river dropping its load of mud and sand as it slows at the sea. Left alone, the land is in a balance: the soft sediment slowly compacts and sinks, and each flood lays down a new layer on top. Human activity breaks both halves of that balance.

First, dams upstream trap sediment, so less reaches the delta. Embankments along the river, built to stop floods, also stop the floods from spreading new mud across the land. Second, pumping groundwater, oil or gas from under a delta speeds up compaction. The ground drops, a process called subsidence. In many big delta cities, subsidence is currently a larger threat than the rising sea.

Jakarta

Indonesia's capital sits on soft coastal ground, and decades of groundwater pumping have made parts of the north of the city sink by several centimetres to more than ten centimetres a year. Sea walls now hold back the water in places. In 2019 the government announced it would move the capital to a new city, Nusantara, on the island of Borneo, citing Jakarta's sinking and congestion among the reasons.

The Mississippi delta

Louisiana has lost thousands of square kilometres of coastal wetland since the 1930s. Levees keep the river from spreading its mud, canals dug for oil and gas let salt water in, and the land subsides. The lost marshes once softened the hurricane surges that reach New Orleans, which is part of why Katrina was so destructive.

Chapter 07

Why the sea is rising, and how fast

The global average sea level rose by about 20 centimetres between 1901 and 2018, according to the IPCC's 2021 assessment, and the rate is speeding up. In the early twentieth century it rose by a little over a millimetre a year. In recent decades it has risen by roughly three to four millimetres a year, and more since. Two causes dominate. Water expands as it warms, so a hotter ocean takes up more room. And melting glaciers and ice sheets on land, in Greenland, Antarctica and mountain ranges, add new water.

Floating sea ice, by contrast, adds almost nothing when it melts, for the same reason a melting ice cube does not overflow a glass. It is ice on land that raises the sea.

For 2100, the IPCC's central projections range from roughly 30 to 60 centimetres above the 1995–2014 level if emissions fall steeply, to roughly 60 centimetres to a metre if they stay very high. Larger rises cannot be ruled out, because scientists are unsure how quickly parts of the Antarctic ice sheet could break up. And the rise will continue for centuries after 2100 in every scenario, because the deep ocean warms slowly and ice sheets respond slowly.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lives, trade and the ecosystems that protect both

Coasts concentrate people, money and biological richness in the same narrow strip that is most exposed to the sea's worst days.

Chapter 08

Storm surges, and the lesson of Bangladesh

The deadliest coastal hazard is usually not the wind of a hurricane or cyclone. It is the storm surge: a dome of seawater pushed ashore by the storm's winds and lifted by its low pressure. On a shallow, flat coast it can flood tens of kilometres inland within hours.

Bangladesh shows both the danger and the cure. Most of the country is a vast, low delta at the head of the Bay of Bengal, where cyclones funnel water northward. In 1970, the Bhola cyclone killed somewhere between 300,000 and half a million people, one of the deadliest natural disasters of the twentieth century. In 1991 another cyclone killed well over 100,000.

Then the country changed its approach. It built thousands of raised concrete cyclone shelters, trained a large network of volunteers to carry warnings door to door, and improved forecasting. When Cyclone Sidr, a storm of similar strength, struck in 2007, the death toll was in the thousands. Each death was a tragedy, but the number was a small fraction of earlier storms. The same hazard produced a far smaller disaster because the arrangements that met it had changed. Volume 23 makes the same point about the 2004 tsunami, where no warning system existed in the Indian Ocean.

Chapter 09

The coast as the world's loading dock

Most of the world's goods, measured by weight, travel by sea at some point, and every one of those cargoes passes through a port. Ports and the industry clustered around them, such as refineries, steelworks and container depots, are tied to the coast by their nature. They cannot move inland. The global system is explained in Global transportation and in Volume 12's physical supply chain.

Coastal economies also include fishing, tourism and, increasingly, offshore energy such as wind farms. Some of the richest fisheries lie where winds push surface water away from the coast and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below to replace it. This is called upwelling. The coast of Peru, where upwelling feeds one of the world's largest fisheries, is the classic example. When an El Niño event weakens the upwelling, the fish catch can collapse.

The concentration of value is the reason coastal risk is so expensive. A storm that hits a port city damages not just that city but supply chains around the world.

Chapter 10

Natural defences: mangroves, reefs and marshes

Some of the best coastal defences grow by themselves. A belt of mangroves, trees whose tangled roots stand in salt water, slows waves and traps sediment. Coral reefs break waves offshore, absorbing much of their energy before it reaches the beach. Salt marshes in estuaries soak up storm water and build land by catching mud. These ecosystems are also nurseries for young fish and store large amounts of carbon.

Many have been lost. Mangroves have been cleared for shrimp farms, ports and housing across Asia, Africa and Latin America. Reefs suffer from warming seas, which cause bleaching, the loss of the tiny algae that live inside coral and feed it. Marshes have been drained and built on. When these living buffers disappear, the coast behind them becomes more exposed, and the cost of artificial defences rises. Pollution, a further pressure, is covered in Ocean pollution.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Concrete, sand or space: the three ways to defend a coast

Engineers distinguish "hard" defences, built structures, from "soft" ones that work with natural processes. A third option is to give the sea room. Each has a different balance of cost, reliability and side effects.

Chapter 12

The Netherlands: a country that negotiates with the sea

About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, and much more would flood without defences. On the night of 31 January 1953, a North Sea storm surge broke through dikes in the southwest and killed more than 1,800 people. The country responded with the Delta Works, a system of dams, sluices and storm barriers built over the following decades, and with safety standards designed against floods expected only once in thousands of years.

In recent decades, Dutch thinking shifted. Under a programme called Room for the River, the country lowered floodplains, moved some dikes back and gave rivers space to flood safely. In 2011 engineers piled about 20 million cubic metres of sand off the coast near The Hague in a single hook-shaped deposit, the Sand Motor, and let waves and currents spread it along the shore. The idea is to do what nature does, but on purpose.

The Dutch example is often presented as proof that any coast can be defended. The honest lesson is narrower. Defence works when a country is rich, densely populated behind the line, and willing to pay for maintenance forever. For a long, thinly populated coastline, or for a poor country, the same approach can cost more than the land is worth, and the money might save more lives spent on warnings and shelters, as Bangladesh did.

Chapter 14

Protect, accommodate or retreat

Coastal planners often sum up the choices in three words. Protect means holding the line with walls, barriers and nourished beaches. Accommodate means living with more water: raising houses on stilts, building parks that can flood, moving electrical equipment above the ground floor, and writing building codes for the sea of 2070 rather than the sea of 1970. Retreat means moving people and buildings away from the most exposed places over time.

Most real strategies mix all three along a single coast: protect the city centre and the port, accommodate the suburbs, and retreat from the most exposed fringes. The hardest part is not engineering. It is fairness. Defences protect some neighbourhoods and push water towards others. Buyouts can help homeowners and leave renters behind. Who gets protected is a political decision dressed up as a technical one, and Katrina showed what happens when poor neighbourhoods are last in line.

The core idea

Coasts are moving systems: waves carry sand along them, rivers build deltas, and storms reshape them. People crowd onto them for trade, food and flat land. Rising seas and sinking ground now make the most valuable land also the most exposed, forcing a choice between protecting, accommodating and retreating.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Global sea level rose about 20 cm from 1901 to 2018, and the rise is speeding up.
  • About 680 million people lived less than 10 m above sea level on the coast in 2010, likely over a billion by 2050.
  • Roughly 30 cm to 1 m more by 2100, depending mainly on emissions.
  • About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level.
The common mistake

Blaming all coastal flooding on climate change. In many delta cities the land is sinking faster than the sea is rising, mostly because of groundwater pumping and trapped river sediment.
