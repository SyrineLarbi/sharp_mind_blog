Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 06 of 20

Population distribution. Eight billion people, and most of them in a few crowded places.

If you spread the world's people evenly over its land, each person would have a patch of roughly two to three football pitches to themselves. Nothing like that happens. Some places pack more than a thousand people into a square kilometre. Others have fewer than one. The pattern is not random. It follows water, warmth and flat fertile ground, it was fixed in place by thousands of years of history, and it is now being redrawn by falling birth rates and rising seas. Knowing where people are explains a great deal about where markets, risks and politics are too.

Question 1 · What is it?

The map of where people actually are

Population distribution means the pattern of where people live across the Earth. The first thing to notice is how lopsided it is.

Chapter 01

A world of crowds and emptiness

The world's population passed 8 billion in late 2022, according to the United Nations, and stood at about 8.2 billion in 2025. Here is how unevenly those people are spread.

  • Close to nine in ten people live in the Northern Hemisphere, because that is where most of the land is.
  • India and China together hold more than a third of humanity, about 1.4 billion each. India passed China as the most populous country in 2023.
  • A circle drawn around China, India and South-East Asia, covering only a small fraction of the globe, contains more than half of all people.
  • Roughly two in five people live within about 100 kilometres of a coast.
  • Vast areas, including most of Siberia, the Sahara, the Amazon interior, northern Canada, central Australia and all of Antarctica, are almost empty.

Geographers have an old word for the inhabited parts of the world, the ecumene, from the Greek for "the inhabited earth". The rest, too cold, dry, high or wet for permanent dense settlement, is sometimes called the non-ecumene. Most of Earth's land belongs to the second category, and most people live crowded together in the first.

Chapter 02

Measuring it: density and its traps

The basic measure is population density: the number of people divided by the land area, usually per square kilometre. It is easy to calculate and easy to misread.

The Egypt row shows the trap. Divide its population by its total area and you get a modest number. But about 95% of Egyptians live on roughly 5% of the land, along the Nile. To fix this, geographers use physiological density: people per square kilometre of farmable land. By that measure Egypt is among the most crowded countries on Earth. The general lesson: a national average describes no actual place. Density has to be looked at on a map, at the scale where people live.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Geography pulls, history locks in, demography redraws

Three sets of forces explain the map: the physical features that make places liveable, the history that froze early patterns in place, and the changes in births and deaths that shift the balance between regions.

Chapter 03

The physical pulls: water, warmth, flat fertile ground

Before modern technology, people could live densely only where farming produced a reliable surplus. That required four things together. Water: enough rain, or a river to irrigate from. Warmth: a growing season long enough for crops. Flat or gently sloping land: easy to plough and irrigate. And fertile soil: often volcanic, or renewed by river floods.

Where all four meet, crowds gathered and stayed. The great dense belts of the world today are mostly old farming heartlands: the plains of the Ganges, the North China Plain and the Yangtze valley, the Nile valley, the island of Java, and the lowlands of Western Europe. Java is a striking case. It is about the size of England, yet it holds more than 150 million people, over half of Indonesia's population, thanks partly to rich volcanic soils that support intensive rice farming.

Where one ingredient is missing, density stays low. Deserts lack water (Topic 05). The far north lacks warmth. High mountains lack flat land and warmth (Topic 03). Dense tropical rainforests, despite their lush look, often have poor soils, because heavy rain washes nutrients out quickly.

Chapter 04

Coasts and rivers: the lines that attract

People also cluster along lines: coasts and rivers. Both offered water, food from fishing, and above all transport. Before railways, moving goods by water was far cheaper than by land, as Topic 04 described. So trade, and with it wealth and jobs, concentrated where boats could reach.

Look at a night-time satellite image of the world and the lines leap out. Lights trace the coasts of China, the eastern seaboard of the United States, the edges of Australia and Brazil, and the Nile as a bright thread running up through dark desert. In Australia, the great majority of people live near the coast, mostly in the south-east, while the dry interior is nearly empty. In Canada, the cold north pushes people south, towards the US border. In Russia, roughly three-quarters of the population lives in the European part, west of the Ural Mountains, even though that is only about a quarter of the country's territory.

Chapter 05

History locks the pattern in

Modern technology has weakened the physical pulls. Air conditioning makes hot places bearable, desalination makes water, and trade means you do not need to grow your own food. So why is the map so stable? Because of what economists call path dependence: once a pattern is established, it tends to reinforce itself.

A city founded by a river crossing in Roman times keeps its roads, its rail lines, its universities and its markets long after the river stops mattering. People move there because other people are already there: jobs, customers, friends, services. Economists call these benefits of being close together agglomeration effects. The best predictor of where people will live next year is where people lived last year. This is why the densest regions of the modern world are still, mostly, the densest regions of the ancient one.

Political history matters too. Borders, wars and colonial decisions placed capitals and ports, built railways towards some places and not others, and moved populations by force. Many African and Latin American countries still have their largest cities on the coast because colonial economies were designed to ship raw materials out. Topic 07 on urbanization explains how these patterns concentrate into cities.

Chapter 06

The demographic transition redraws the balance

The distribution between regions changes mainly because populations grow at different speeds. Demographers explain those speeds with a pattern called the demographic transition.

In stage 1, births and deaths are both high and population grows slowly. In stage 2, better food, clean water and medicine cut deaths, especially of children, while births stay high for a generation or two. Population surges. In stage 3, as families see children survive, as women gain education and work, and as people move to cities, birth rates fall. In stage 4, both are low and growth stalls. Some countries now appear to be in a further stage where births fall below deaths and population shrinks.

Europe went through stage 2 in the nineteenth century and its share of world population rose. East Asia went through it in the twentieth century. Sub-Saharan Africa is in the middle of it now, with the world's highest birth rates, while much of Europe and East Asia have birth rates well below the "replacement" level of about 2.1 children per woman that keeps a population stable in the long run. That difference is quietly redrawing the world map, as Question 5 shows.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where people are is where everything else is

Population distribution decides where markets and infrastructure are needed, who is exposed to disasters, and how we should think about crowding.

Chapter 07

It decides what has to be built

Every school, hospital, power line and road is a bet on where people are and will be. Density makes services cheap per person: one subway line, one hospital, one water main can serve hundreds of thousands. Sparse populations make them expensive. That is why rural parts of almost every country have fewer doctors, slower internet and longer journeys to school, and why governments subsidise services there.

Businesses read the map the same way. A retailer, a bank or a mobile network wants to know where customers cluster. Modern population maps are made by combining censuses with satellite images of buildings and night lights, divided into grid squares down to about a hundred metres. Topic 18 on geographic information systems explains how these are built. They are now used for everything from planning vaccination campaigns to deciding where to place a new store.

Chapter 08

It decides who is in harm's way

The same features that attract people often carry danger. Deltas are fertile and flat, and they flood. Coasts are rich in trade, and they face storms and rising seas. Volcanic soils are fertile because volcanoes are nearby. Many of the world's largest cities sit on or near active faults, from Tokyo to Istanbul to Los Angeles.

So the toll of a disaster depends on exposure, how many people live in the hazard's path, as much as on the hazard itself. A cyclone that hits an empty coast is a weather event. The same cyclone over a crowded delta can be a catastrophe. The 1970 Bhola cyclone killed a vast number of people in what is now Bangladesh, partly because so many people lived on low islands and coasts with no warning system. Bangladesh has since built cyclone shelters and early warning systems, and death tolls from later storms of similar strength have been far lower. Distribution sets the stakes. Preparation decides the outcome. Volume 23 tells similar stories (Katrina).

Chapter 09

Is crowding the problem?

A long tradition of thought links dense populations to poverty and shortage. In 1798 Thomas Malthus argued in An Essay on the Principle of Population that population tends to grow faster than food supplies, so that famine and disease would keep pushing numbers back down. In 1968 the biologist Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb warned of mass famines in the 1970s and 1980s.

Those specific predictions did not come true on the scale forecast. Food production rose faster than population, helped by fertilisers (see Volume 12, Fertiliser) and the high-yield crops of the "Green Revolution". And the map itself argues against a simple link. The Netherlands, Singapore and South Korea are densely populated and rich. Many sparsely populated places are poor.

The optimistic reading

Density brings markets, ideas and specialisation. More people close together means more inventors, more trade and cheaper services. On this view, people are a resource, and crowding is a sign of opportunity.

The cautious reading

Density still strains water, soil, housing and the wider environment. A planet of 8 billion uses resources at a scale that worries many scientists, even if the main pressure comes from how much people consume rather than simply how many there are.

Most researchers would put it this way: how many people a place can support depends mainly on technology, institutions and consumption, not on a fixed number. That is why arguments about "overpopulation" remain contested, and why they so often turn into arguments about lifestyles and fairness.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Concentration versus dispersal

Neither crowding nor spreading out is good in itself. Each pattern brings its own benefits and costs, and governments that tried to rearrange people learned this the hard way.

Chapter 11

When governments try to move people

Many governments have tried to redistribute their populations, usually to develop empty regions or relieve crowded ones. The record is mixed.

  • New capitals. Brazil built Brasília in the interior and moved its capital there in 1960 to pull development away from the coast. Kazakhstan moved its capital to Astana in 1997. Both cities grew, though the coastal or older centres remained dominant. Indonesia began building a new capital, Nusantara, on Borneo, partly to relieve sinking, crowded Jakarta. Its progress and future scale are still uncertain.
  • Resettlement programmes. Indonesia's "transmigration" programme moved millions of people from crowded Java to other islands over several decades. It opened new farmland. It also caused deforestation and conflict with local communities.
  • Controls on movement. China's household registration system, called hukou, tied access to public services to a person's registered home, slowing migration to big cities. It has been gradually loosened, but it still shapes who can settle where.

The general lesson is that people follow jobs and other people more than they follow plans. A government can build a city, but it cannot easily make people want to live there.

Chapter 13

Empty villages and crowded coasts

Two trends seem likely to define the next few decades in many countries at once. The first is rural emptying. In Spain, the large thinly populated interior has a name in public debate, la España vaciada, "emptied Spain". Some Italian and Japanese villages sell houses for a token price to attract newcomers. These places are not shrinking because they are unpleasant. They are shrinking because young people move to cities for work and few children are born to replace them.

The second is continued crowding of coasts and big cities, even where those places are exposed to rising seas and storms, because that is where jobs and connections are. Researchers have argued that for thousands of years most people have lived within a fairly narrow range of average temperatures, and that warming will push parts of today's populated world outside that range. How people respond, by adapting in place, moving within their country or crossing borders, is one of the large open questions of this century. Topic 10 on migration looks at the movement itself. The map of where people live has always changed. What is new is that we can watch it change in real time, from space.

The core idea

People cluster where water, warmth, flat land and fertile soil once made farming reliable, and along coasts and rivers that made trade cheap. Path dependence keeps those old patterns alive. Different speeds of demographic transition shift the balance between regions, today towards Africa.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 8.2 billion people in 2025, close to nine in ten in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • About 95% of Egyptians live on about 5% of Egypt's land.
  • Replacement fertility is about 2.1 children per woman.
The common mistake

Judging how crowded a country is from its average density. Averages describe no real place. Egypt looks roomy on paper and is extremely crowded where people actually live.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the densest regions today are mostly the same as two thousand years ago, even though technology has changed everything? If you can, you understand physical pulls and path dependence together.

The words, in plain words
Population distribution
The pattern of where people live across an area.
Population density
The number of people per unit of land, usually per square kilometre.
Physiological density
People per unit of farmable land, a better guide to real crowding.
Ecumene
The parts of the Earth that are permanently inhabited.

The tendency of an established pattern to reinforce itself over time.
Agglomeration effects
The benefits that people and firms gain from being close together.
Demographic transition
The shift from high birth and death rates to low ones as societies develop.
Replacement fertility
About 2.1 children per woman, the level that keeps a population stable long-term.
