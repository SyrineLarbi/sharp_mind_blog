Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 02 of 20

Climate zones. Why the same latitude can hold a rainforest and a desert.

Cairo and Shanghai sit at almost the same distance from the equator. Cairo gets a few days of rain a year. Shanghai gets more than a metre. London is further north than every major city in the United States outside Alaska, yet its winters are milder than New York's. Latitude matters, but it is only the first of four forces that decide a place's climate. Once you know all four, you can look at an unfamiliar spot on a map and guess, quite accurately, what it feels like to live there.

Question 1 · What is it?

A climate is a long-run pattern, and there are only a few kinds

Weather changes by the hour. Climate is what stays the same across decades. Sorting climates into types turns out to be possible, because nature produces only a handful of basic patterns.

Chapter 01

Weather is a mood, climate is a personality

A useful way to hold the difference: weather is what is happening in the sky today, and climate is what usually happens there. One cold week in Madrid says nothing about Spain's climate, in the same way that one bad day says little about a person's character. Meteorologists normally describe a place's climate using averages over 30 years, the standard period set by the World Meteorological Organization. They look at average temperatures in each month, how much rain or snow falls, when it falls, and how much these vary.

The word "zone" suggests neat bands around the globe, like stripes on a ball. Some climate boundaries do roughly follow lines of latitude. Many do not. They bend around mountains, along coasts and with ocean currents. A climate zone is better thought of as a family: places, often far apart, that share the same pattern of heat and moisture through the year. Perth in Australia, Cape Town, central Chile, California and the shores of the Mediterranean belong to one family, even though they are on four different continents.

Chapter 02

The Köppen system: five letters for the whole planet

The most widely used classification was devised by Wladimir Köppen, a climate scientist trained as a botanist, who published his first version in 1884 and refined it for decades. His insight was to let plants draw the borders. Vegetation integrates climate over many years: a forest cannot survive in a place that is too dry every few summers, whatever the average says. So Köppen drew his boundaries where the vegetation changed, then found the temperature and rainfall values that matched. The German climatologist Rudolf Geiger later revised it, which is why it is often called the Köppen-Geiger system.

LetterGroupRule of thumbExamples
ATropicalEvery month averages above 18 °C. No real winter.Singapore (rainy all year), Mumbai (monsoon), Lagos (wet and dry seasons)
BDryMore water could evaporate than falls as rain. Deserts and steppes.Riyadh, Cairo, Phoenix (deserts); Mongolia's grasslands (steppe)

Second and third letters add detail. A second letter "s" means a dry summer, "w" a dry winter, "f" no dry season. So Csa means temperate with a dry, hot summer: the Mediterranean climate. Cfb means temperate with no dry season and a mild summer: the cool, damp oceanic climate of Ireland, Brittany and New Zealand. Some versions add an H for highlands, because high mountains create their own climates in any latitude. The codes look like jargon, but they repay learning. Once you know that Rome and Los Angeles share a code, you understand why both grow olives and both face summer wildfires.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four forces: sun angle, moving air, the sea, and height

Every climate on Earth comes from the same few causes acting in different combinations. Learn them in order, from the biggest to the most local.

Chapter 03

Force one: the angle of the Sun

Shine a torch straight down at a table and you get a small, bright circle. Tilt it and the same light spreads into a long, dim oval. Sunlight behaves the same way on a curved Earth. Near the equator it arrives nearly straight on and concentrates its energy. Near the poles it arrives at a low angle, spreads over more ground and passes through more atmosphere on the way. The tropics receive far more energy per square metre than the poles, and that imbalance is the root of all climate.

Earth's axis is also tilted, by about 23.4 degrees. As the planet orbits the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans towards it. That tilt, not changing distance from the Sun, is what causes seasons. It is why the seasons are reversed between Europe and Australia, and why they are weak near the equator and extreme near the poles, where summer brings round-the-clock daylight and winter brings weeks of darkness.

Chapter 04

Force two: three belts of circulating air

The atmosphere tries to move heat from the hot tropics to the cold poles. On a planet that did not spin, one giant loop in each hemisphere might do it. Earth's rotation breaks that loop into three in each hemisphere. Volume 30 describes the first loop. Here is the full set.

The three-cell model, Northern Hemisphere (simplified)

Two belts of rising air bring rain: near the equator, and around 50 to 60 degrees of latitude where cold polar air meets warmer air. Rising air cools, and cool air cannot hold as much water vapour, so clouds and rain form. Two belts of sinking air bring dryness: around 30 degrees, and at the poles. Sinking air warms as it is compressed, which dries it out. That is why the world's great hot deserts, from the Sahara to the Kalahari to central Australia, sit near 30 degrees north or south. It also explains a fact that surprises people: Antarctica is technically a desert, because so little snow falls there.

The band of rising tropical air has a name, the Intertropical Convergence Zone, or ITCZ. It is where the trade winds of the two hemispheres meet. Crucially, it moves. It follows the Sun north in the northern summer and south in the southern summer. Places the ITCZ passes over get a wet season when it arrives and a dry season when it leaves. That is the climate of much of West Africa, India and northern Australia.

Chapter 05

The monsoon: a continent that breathes

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of the winds. The best-known is South Asia's. Land heats up faster than water. By late spring the Indian subcontinent is scorching while the Indian Ocean stays cooler. Hot air rises over the land, and moist air is drawn in from the sea to replace it. When that air meets the land and the Himalaya behind it, it rises and drops enormous amounts of rain. In winter the pattern reverses: the land cools faster, and dry winds blow from land to sea.

The stakes are enormous. In India the summer monsoon, from June to September, delivers roughly three-quarters of the year's rainfall. Harvests, reservoirs, electricity from dams and the income of hundreds of millions of farmers depend on whether it arrives on time and in normal strength. A weak monsoon can shave measurable growth off the national economy. Monsoon systems also govern West Africa, East Asia and northern Australia, each with its own timing.

Chapter 06

Force three: the ocean next door

Water warms and cools slowly. Land does both fast. So places near the sea have mild winters, cool summers and a small range between them. This is a maritime climate. Places deep inside a continent swing wildly. This is a continental climate. The extreme case is eastern Siberia. In Yakutsk, a city of several hundred thousand people, winter temperatures regularly fall below minus 40 °C, while summer days can pass 30 °C. The annual range is among the largest of any city on Earth.

Which coast you are on matters too. In the middle latitudes the prevailing winds blow from the west. So western coasts receive air that has crossed the ocean, and eastern coasts receive air that has crossed land. That is why Vancouver, on North America's west coast, is mild and damp, while places at similar latitudes on the east side of the continent have bitter winters. And Western Europe gets a double benefit: westerly winds off the Atlantic, plus the warm water carried north by the Gulf Stream and its extension.

Ocean currents can also produce deserts. Along the west coasts of South America and southern Africa, cold currents flow towards the equator. They chill the air above them, which stops it rising and making rain. The result is two of the driest places on Earth: the Atacama in Chile and the Namib in Namibia, both foggy and almost rainless right beside the sea. Topic 05 on deserts looks at them closely.

Chapter 07

Force four: height and mountains

Air gets colder as you climb, on average by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 metres. This is why Quito, the capital of Ecuador, sits almost exactly on the equator at about 2,800 metres and has a mild, spring-like climate all year, while the coast a short flight away is hot and humid. On a tall tropical mountain you can climb through rainforest, cloud forest, grassland and bare rock to snow in a single day. Geographers sometimes describe such mountains as climate zones stacked vertically.

Mountains also split climates sideways. When moist wind hits a range, it is forced upward, cools and rains on the windward side. By the time it crosses the top it has lost much of its water, and it warms as it descends the other side. The dry land behind the range is in a rain shadow. The Andes, the Himalaya and the Sierra Nevada of California all create one. Topic 03 on mountains explains the mechanism in more detail.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Climate decides what grows, what spreads, and how people live

Climate is the background condition for almost everything else in geography: vegetation, farming, disease, housing, energy use and, some argue, wealth itself.

Chapter 08

Climate draws the map of life

Large regions with similar climate and vegetation are called biomes: tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, temperate forest, grassland, the northern conifer forest called the taiga or boreal forest, and the treeless tundra. Their boundaries follow climate so closely that Köppen used them to draw his map. The taiga, which runs right across Russia, Scandinavia and Canada, is the largest land biome on Earth.

Farming follows the same lines. Rice needs heat and abundant water, which is why it dominates monsoon Asia. Wheat thrives in cooler, drier places, from the Great Plains to Ukraine to the Punjab. Olives, grapes and citrus grow where summers are dry and winters mild. Every cuisine is, at its root, a description of a climate. The Mediterranean diet exists because the Mediterranean climate makes some crops easy and others nearly impossible. Topic 14 on agriculture and geography goes deeper into these patterns.

Chapter 09

Climate and health

Many diseases live inside climate limits. Malaria is carried by Anopheles mosquitoes, and the parasite develops only above certain temperatures. That is why malaria is concentrated in warm, wet regions and was historically held back by cold winters in places such as northern Europe, although it once occurred there too. Dengue, carried by a different mosquito, is spreading into regions that used to be too cool for it, including parts of southern Europe where local cases have been recorded in recent years.

Heat itself is a health risk. Human bodies cool by sweating, which works only if the surrounding air can absorb more moisture. In very hot, very humid conditions the body struggles to shed heat, and the danger rises sharply, especially for older people and outdoor workers. Humid heat is more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature, which is why heat-health warnings increasingly look at humidity, not temperature alone.

General education

This section describes broad patterns, not personal medical advice. For travel health or heat risk, follow your doctor and your local health authority.

Chapter 10

Does climate decide wealth?

Look at a map of income per person and a striking pattern appears. Most of the world's richest countries lie in temperate zones, and many of the poorest lie in the tropics. Does climate cause this? Here the debate is real, and it is worth knowing both sides.

Geography matters directly

Some economists, notably Jeffrey Sachs, have argued that tropical climates impose real burdens: a heavier load of disease, poorer soils in some regions, and farming technologies developed for temperate crops that did not transfer well. On this view, climate makes development harder and needs to be addressed directly, for example through health investment.

Institutions matter more

Other economists, such as Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, argue that the pattern mostly reflects history: how colonial powers set up institutions in different places, and whether those institutions protected property and spread power. They point to rich tropical places such as Singapore, and to sharp differences between neighbours with the same climate.

Most researchers now accept that both matter and argue about the balance. What everyone agrees on is that climate is not destiny. Singapore, in the hot, wet tropics, is among the richest countries in the world, helped in part by mass air conditioning. Its founding prime minister, Lee Kuan Yew, is often quoted as calling air conditioning one of the most important inventions in history. Topic 12 on geographic inequality returns to this debate.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant simplification with blind spots

The idea of climate zones, and the Köppen map in particular, is one of the most successful tools in geography. Its weaknesses come from the same simplicity that makes it useful.

Chapter 12

Averages hide the extremes that matter

A climate type tells you what is normal. People and crops are mostly harmed by what is not normal: the drought year, the record heatwave, the once-a-century flood. Two places can have the same average rainfall and very different lives. In one, rain falls steadily through the year. In the other, it comes in a few violent storms separated by long dry spells. The second place is far harder to farm, even though its average looks the same.

This is why modern climate science pays as much attention to variability and extremes as to averages. A good test of whether you understand a climate is not "what is the average temperature in July?" but "how bad does a bad year get, and how often?" For anyone deciding where to build a house, plant an orchard or site a factory, the tails of the distribution matter more than the middle.

Chapter 14

Living with moving zones

The practical consequences are already visible. Wine-growing has spread into southern England, where sparkling wines now compete internationally, while traditional regions further south experiment with grape varieties that cope better with heat. Ski resorts at lower altitudes in the Alps face shorter seasons. Farmers in many countries are changing planting dates and crop varieties. Cities in the Mediterranean are planting trees and painting roofs to cope with longer, hotter summers.

The deeper issue is speed. Climate zones have always moved, over thousands of years, and forests and animals moved with them. The difference now is the pace. Trees cannot walk hundreds of kilometres in a lifetime, and towns, farms and water systems were built for the climate of the past century. Adapting to shifting zones is the subject of Climate adaptation in the Environment category. The geography of the next century will be partly a story of who can move, who can adapt in place, and who can do neither.

The core idea

A climate is the long-run pattern of heat and moisture in a place. It is set by four forces: sun angle, the circulating belts of air, the nearness of the sea and altitude. Köppen's five groups (tropical, dry, temperate, continental, polar) capture the main patterns, and they are shifting as the planet warms.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Climate is normally measured over 30 years.
  • The great hot deserts cluster near 30° north and south, where air sinks.
  • Air cools about 6.5 °C per 1,000 metres of height.
The common mistake

Thinking climate depends only on latitude, or that seasons come from Earth's changing distance to the Sun. Seasons come from the tilt, and coasts, currents and mountains can matter as much as latitude.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the Atacama, beside the Pacific Ocean, is one of the driest places on Earth? If you can, you understand sinking air, cold currents and rain shadows together.

The words, in plain words
Weather
The state of the atmosphere at a given moment: today's rain, wind and temperature.
Climate
The usual pattern of weather in a place, normally averaged over 30 years.
Köppen classification
The most common system for sorting climates into types, using temperature and rainfall.
Hadley cell
The loop of air rising at the equator and sinking near 30 degrees latitude.
ITCZ
The Intertropical Convergence Zone: the rainy belt near the equator where the trade winds meet.
Trade winds
Steady winds blowing towards the equator from the east in the tropics.
Westerlies
Prevailing winds from the west in the middle latitudes, such as over Europe.
Monsoon
A seasonal reversal of winds that brings a pronounced wet season.
Maritime climate
A climate moderated by the sea: mild winters, cool summers.
Continental climate
A climate far from the sea, with hot summers and very cold winters.
Rain shadow
A dry area on the side of a mountain range facing away from the moist wind.
Biome
A large region defined by its climate and typical vegetation, such as tundra or savanna.
Lapse rate
The rate at which air cools with height, on average about 6.5 °C per kilometre.
