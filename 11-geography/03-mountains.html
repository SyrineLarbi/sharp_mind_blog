Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 03 of 20

Mountains. The places where the planet's slow violence shows.

The summit of Everest is made of limestone that contains the fossils of sea creatures. Rock that formed on an ancient sea floor now stands nearly nine kilometres above sea level, and it is still being pushed upward. Mountains look permanent, the very symbol of things that do not change. In fact they are the most active landscapes on Earth: built by colliding plates, torn down by ice and water, and busy all the while making weather, storing water and shaping the lives of the people around them.

Question 1 · What is it?

Steep, high ground, and a surprisingly fuzzy definition

Everyone recognises a mountain. Defining one precisely is harder than it looks, and the records depend on how you measure.

Chapter 01

When does a hill become a mountain?

There is no single scientific definition. In Britain and Ireland, a common tradition counts summits above 2,000 feet (about 610 metres) as mountains. In other places the word is used for much lower ground if it stands out sharply from its surroundings. Geographers who need a global map usually combine two things: height and steepness. A high, flat plateau is not a mountain range. A steep, rugged area that rises sharply above the land around it is, even if its peaks are modest.

Using definitions of this kind, studies put mountains at very roughly a quarter of the world's land surface. They are home to something like one person in seven, many of them in the Andes, the Himalaya, the Ethiopian highlands and the mountains of Central Asia. And far more people, as Question 3 shows, depend on mountains without ever seeing one, because mountains are where much of the world's fresh water begins.

Chapter 02

The tallest depends on how you measure

The usual answer is Mount Everest, on the border of Nepal and China. A joint survey by the two countries in 2020 measured it at 8,848.86 metres above sea level. All fourteen of the world's peaks above 8,000 metres lie in the Himalaya and the neighbouring Karakoram range. But "tallest" can mean three different things.

The point is not trivia. It is that a "fact" often depends on a definition you did not notice. Asking "measured how?" is one of the most useful habits a curious person can have.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Built from below, carved from above

Every mountain range is the result of a contest between two forces. Movements deep in the Earth push rock up. Water, ice and gravity tear it down. The landscape you see is the score at this moment.

Chapter 03

Four ways to build a mountain

TypeHow it formsExamples
Fold mountainsTwo plates collide and the crust crumples, like a rug pushed against a wall. The biggest ranges on Earth form this way.Himalaya, Alps, Zagros, Atlas
Volcanic mountainsMolten rock from below erupts and piles up, layer on layer. Often found where one plate sinks under another, or over a "hot spot".Mount Fuji, Kilimanjaro, Etna, the volcanoes of the Andes, Hawaii
Fault-block mountainsThe crust is stretched and cracks into blocks. Some blocks tilt up, others drop down.Sierra Nevada (USA), the ranges of Nevada's Basin and Range, the Rwenzori
Dome mountainsMolten rock pushes up from below without erupting, bulging the surface. Erosion then exposes the core.The Black Hills (USA)

Real ranges often mix these types. The Andes are mainly the product of the Pacific sea floor sinking under South America, which both squeezes the crust and feeds a chain of volcanoes along it. The general process of building mountains is called orogeny, from the Greek words for "mountain" and "birth".

Chapter 04

The Himalaya: a continent still crashing

The Himalaya are the cleanest example of a collision range. Around 50 million years ago the plate carrying India, which had been moving north across the ocean for tens of millions of years, ran into Asia. Neither continent was dense enough to sink. So the crust between them buckled, thickened and rose. The ocean floor that had separated them was squeezed up between the two, which is why marine fossils are found high in the range, including near the summit of Everest.

India has not stopped. It still presses north by a few centimetres a year. That pressure is why the Himalaya are still rising, by millimetres a year in many places, and why the region suffers large earthquakes. In 2015 an earthquake in Nepal killed about 9,000 people and destroyed many of Kathmandu's historic buildings. Behind the range, the collision also raised the Tibetan Plateau, the highest large plateau on Earth, averaging more than 4,000 metres. Its crust is among the thickest anywhere.

Chapter 05

Erosion: the sculptor that never stops

The moment rock is raised, it starts being destroyed. Rain dissolves and loosens it. Water freezes in cracks, expands and splits it. Rivers carry the fragments away. The higher and steeper a mountain, the faster this happens. Young mountains are high and jagged. Old mountains are low and rounded, because erosion has had longer to work.

Compare the Alps with the Appalachians of the eastern United States. The Alps are young, still being pushed up, with sharp peaks above 4,000 metres. The Appalachians formed hundreds of millions of years ago, in the collisions that built Pangaea. Geologists think they were once far higher. Today their highest point is a little over 2,000 metres, and their summits are smooth and forested.

Ice is the most powerful sculptor of all. A river cuts a narrow V-shaped valley. A glacier, a slow river of ice, grinds out a broad U-shaped one, scrapes bowls called cirques high on the slopes, and sharpens the ridges between them into knife-edges and pyramid peaks. The Matterhorn's famous shape was carved by glaciers working on several sides at once. Norway's fjords are glacial valleys that the sea flooded when the ice melted.

The floating mountain

Mountains have deep roots of light crust beneath them, like icebergs (Topic 01 calls this isostasy). As erosion removes weight from the top, the root slowly rises to replace it. So a mountain range can go on yielding rock for far longer than its height alone would suggest, and erosion can even push the peaks a little higher by lightening the valleys around them.

Chapter 06

Mountains make their own weather

Topic 02 introduced the idea that air cools as it rises, by about 6.5 °C per kilometre. That single fact makes mountains weather machines. When moist wind meets a range, it is forced upward. It cools, its water vapour condenses, and it rains or snows on the side facing the wind. This is called orographic rain, from the Greek for mountain.

The contrasts can be extreme. The hills of Meghalaya in north-east India, where monsoon winds are forced up the first high ground they meet, include some of the wettest inhabited places on Earth, with annual rainfall measured in many metres. A few hundred kilometres north, beyond the Himalaya, much of the Tibetan Plateau is dry. In the same way the Andes leave Patagonia in Argentina in a rain shadow, and the Sierra Nevada helps make Nevada a desert.

The air that comes down the dry side warms as it descends, because it is being compressed. In the Alps this warm, dry wind is called the föhn. On the eastern side of the Rocky Mountains a similar wind is called the chinook, and it can raise winter temperatures by many degrees within hours and melt snow almost overnight.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Water towers, refuges and hazards

Mountains matter to far more people than live on them. They store water, shelter cultures and species, mark borders, and occasionally unleash disasters.

Chapter 07

The water towers of the world

Mountains catch more rain and snow than lowlands, and their cold heights store it as snow and ice. Through the dry season that store melts slowly and feeds rivers. Hydrologists call mountains water towers. The image fits: water is lifted up, held in reserve, and released downhill when it is needed.

The greatest example is the system of the Himalaya, the Hindu Kush and the Tibetan Plateau, which holds more ice than anywhere outside the polar regions. It is sometimes called the "Third Pole". It feeds some of Asia's largest rivers, including the Indus, the Ganges, the Brahmaputra, the Yangtze, the Yellow River and the Mekong. Well over a billion people live in their basins. In Europe, the Alps feed the Rhine, the Rhône and the Po, and supply much of the flow of the Danube's southern tributaries. Topic 04 on rivers follows that water downstream.

Snow and ice matter because of timing. Rain runs off in days. Snowpack and glaciers release water weeks or months later, often in the hot season when farms need it most. In places such as the dry valleys west of the Andes or the oases of Central Asia, summer meltwater is the difference between farming and not.

Chapter 08

Living where the air is thin

Air pressure falls with height, so each breath carries less oxygen. At the summit of Everest it holds roughly a third of the oxygen of a breath at sea level. Most climbers there use bottled oxygen. Even at 2,500 metres, many visitors feel headaches and breathlessness. Above that, some develop dangerous fluid build-up in the lungs or brain.

General education

This is not medical advice. Anyone travelling to high altitude should ascend gradually and seek medical guidance, especially with heart or lung conditions.

Yet millions of people have lived high up for thousands of years, and their bodies have adapted in different ways. Many people in the high Andes have more haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in blood. Tibetans, by contrast, tend not to, and seem to cope through other changes, including differences in breathing and blood flow. Geneticists found that a version of a gene involved in the body's response to low oxygen, common in Tibetans, appears to have been inherited from the Denisovans, an extinct group of ancient humans. Two populations, two separate solutions to the same problem: a clear case of evolution working differently in different places.

Chapter 09

Barriers, borders and refuges

For most of history mountains were walls. In 218 BCE the Carthaginian general Hannibal crossed the Alps with an army and elephants to invade Italy, and the feat is remembered precisely because it was so hard. Many modern borders follow ranges or the line of their highest ridges: the Pyrenees between France and Spain, the Andes between Chile and Argentina, the Himalaya between China and its southern neighbours. Where the crest is unclear, borders can be fiercely disputed, as along parts of the India-China frontier. Topic 11 on borders explores this.

Walls also protect. Mountains have long been refuges for peoples and languages that lowland states conquered or absorbed elsewhere. The Caucasus holds an unusually dense mix of languages in a small area. The mountainous island of New Guinea holds hundreds of languages, more than any comparable region. The anthropologist James C. Scott, in The Art of Not Being Governed (2009), argued that the highlands of mainland Southeast Asia were for centuries a deliberate refuge for people escaping lowland states. That thesis is debated, but the broader pattern, that rugged terrain is hard to govern, is widely accepted.

Mountains are refuges for life too. Because climate changes rapidly with height, a single mountain holds many habitats close together. That makes ranges such as the tropical Andes and the mountains of south-west China among the richest places on Earth for species.

Chapter 10

When mountains move fast

Mountains are dangerous because they store energy: the height of rock, the weight of snow, the water held behind ice. Earthquakes are the deep source, as in Nepal in 2015. Closer to the surface, the main hazards are:

  • Landslides, often triggered by heavy rain or earthquakes on steep, broken slopes.
  • Avalanches, when layers of snow lose their grip and slide.
  • Glacial lake outburst floods, when a lake trapped behind loose glacial debris suddenly bursts through and floods the valleys below. As glaciers melt, many such lakes are growing.
  • Volcanic eruptions and mudflows. In 1985 a modest eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia melted ice on its summit and sent mudflows that buried the town of Armero, killing more than 20,000 people.

Armero is remembered for a painful reason. Hazard maps had shown the town was at risk, and the warning came too late and too weakly to move people. The science was mostly there. The system for acting on it was not. It is a pattern Volume 23 describes in other disasters, for example the 2004 tsunami.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Generous and harsh at the same time

For the people who live among and below them, mountains give a great deal and demand a great deal. The two lists are mirror images.

Chapter 12

The crowded summit

In May 2019 a photograph of a long queue of climbers on the ridge below Everest's summit went around the world. Several climbers died that season. The image captured a real problem. Commercial expeditions, supported by Sherpa guides who carry much of the risk, have made the summit reachable for people with money and moderate experience. When good weather windows are short, many teams aim for the same days, and waiting in the "death zone" above 8,000 metres becomes dangerous in itself.

The same tension appears in gentler forms across the world's mountains. Tourism brings income where there are few other jobs. It also brings rubbish, crowding and pressure on water. Nepal has introduced rules aimed at reducing waste on Everest and has discussed tightening permits. The general lesson is that remoteness is part of what visitors pay for, and too many visitors destroy it.

Chapter 14

Treaties written for a different climate

Many arrangements for sharing mountain water assumed that rivers would behave as they always had. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, divided the rivers of the Indus system between India and Pakistan and survived several wars. In 2025, after a deadly attack in Kashmir, India announced it was holding the treaty in abeyance, and Pakistan said any attempt to stop its water would be treated as hostile. Whatever happens next, the episode shows how political shared mountain water can become.

Climate change adds a deeper difficulty. If glaciers shrink and the timing of snowmelt shifts, the same treaty can mean less water, or water at the wrong time, even if every word is respected. The likely responses are familiar: more reservoirs to replace the storage that ice used to provide, better forecasting of melt, more efficient irrigation and, ideally, new agreements that share data and adapt to changing flows. Topic 15 on water scarcity takes up that problem. The mountains will still be there. What is changing is the reliable service they quietly provided for thousands of years.

The core idea

Mountains are built from below by colliding, sinking and stretching plates and by volcanoes, and carved from above by water and ice. Their height makes them weather machines and water towers, and their steepness makes them refuges, barriers and hazards.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Everest is 8,848.86 m (China-Nepal survey, 2020).
  • Air cools about 6.5 °C per kilometre of height.
  • The Himalaya began forming about 50 million years ago and are still rising.
The common mistake

Thinking of mountains as permanent and unchanging. They are the most active landscapes on Earth, rising, eroding and shedding rock, snow and water all the time.

Cirque
A bowl-shaped hollow carved by a glacier high on a mountain.
Orographic rain
Rain caused by moist air being forced up over high ground.
Rain shadow
The dry area on the side of a range away from the moist wind.

Warm, dry winds that descend the sheltered side of a mountain range.

A mountain region whose snow and ice store water and release it to rivers.
Permafrost
