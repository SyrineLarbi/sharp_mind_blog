Water scarcity. The planet is not running out of water. Places are running out of cheap water.

Seen from space, Earth is a blue planet. Seen from a farm in Punjab, it can be a dry one. Almost all the water on Earth is salty or frozen, and the small share we can use is badly spread out in space and time. Water scarcity is rarely about the total amount. It is about the gap between where and when water arrives and where and when people need it, and about who pays to close that gap.

Question 1 · What is it?

Too little usable water, in the wrong place, at the wrong time

Water scarcity means demand for fresh water in a place comes close to, or beyond, what nature supplies and what people can afford to deliver.

Chapter 01

The city that counted down to Day Zero

In early 2018, Cape Town, a city of about four million people in South Africa, announced a date. After three dry winters in a row, its reservoirs were falling so fast that officials calculated the day when they would have to turn off the taps in most homes. They called it Day Zero. On that day, residents would queue at around two hundred collection points for a daily ration of water, guarded by police.

The city told every resident to use no more than 50 litres a day. People showered over buckets and used the grey water to flush toilets. Farmers upstream agreed to give up part of their share. The city roughly halved its water use, the rains returned in winter 2018, and Day Zero never came.

The story matters because Cape Town was not a poor city with no planning. What it lacked was a margin. Its supply depended almost entirely on rain falling in one season into one set of reservoirs. That is the core of water scarcity almost everywhere: not zero water, but no buffer.

Chapter 02

A blue planet with very little to drink

Picture all Earth's water as a bathtub. About 97.5 per cent of it is salt water in the oceans. Only about 2.5 per cent is fresh. Of that small share, roughly two-thirds is locked in ice sheets and glaciers, mostly in Antarctica and Greenland. Most of the rest is groundwater, water stored underground in the pores and cracks of rock. Lakes and rivers hold well under one per cent of the fresh water: a few drops in the bathtub.

Those drops are enough only because they are constantly renewed. Evaporation from the sea falls on land as rain and snow. This water cycle makes fresh water a flow rather than a fixed stock. The useful question is never "how much water is there?" but "how much falls here each year, and how much of it can we catch?"

That rain is shared out very unevenly. A handful of countries, such as Brazil, Russia and Canada, hold a large share of the world's renewable fresh water, while the Middle East and North Africa receive only a tiny fraction. And in monsoon climates, most of a year's rain can fall in three or four months.

Chapter 03

How scarcity is measured, and the two kinds

The most famous yardstick comes from the Swedish hydrologist Malin Falkenmark. In the late 1980s she proposed measuring the renewable fresh water available per person per year in a country. Her thresholds are still widely quoted.

Renewable water per person per yearLabelWhat it feels like
Above 1,700 m³No stressOccasional local shortages only
1,000 to 1,700 m³Water stressRegular shortages, hard choices between users
500 to 1,000 m³Water scarcityLimits on food production, health and growth
Below 500 m³Absolute scarcitySurvival depends on imports, desalination or reuse

A cubic metre (m³) is 1,000 litres. The numbers look generous, but they include the water needed to grow each person's food, by far the largest part. Many Gulf and North African countries sit far below 500 m³.

The index has a weakness. It averages a whole country over a whole year. A 2016 study by Mesfin Mekonnen and Arjen Hoekstra looked month by month and estimated that about four billion people face severe water scarcity for at least one month of the year. National averages had been hiding most of them.

The second weakness is deeper. Water experts distinguish physical scarcity, where there simply is not enough water to meet demand, from economic scarcity, where water exists but people lack the pipes, pumps, dams and money to use it. Much of sub-Saharan Africa is in the second group: rivers flow, yet people walk hours to fetch water. The two kinds need opposite remedies: physical scarcity needs people to use less or find new sources, while economic scarcity needs investment.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Farms, aquifers, trade and a changing climate

Four forces work together: what water is used for, hidden reserves being drawn down, water moving inside traded goods, and a warming climate.

Chapter 04

Where the water actually goes

Most people picture water use as showers and washing machines. Worldwide, roughly 70 per cent of the fresh water people withdraw goes to agriculture, mainly irrigation. Industry, including cooling power stations, takes about a fifth. Homes and municipal uses take only around a tenth.

Two words help here. Withdrawal is water taken from a river or aquifer. Consumption is the part that does not come back, because it evaporated, went into a plant or a product, or was polluted beyond use. A power station returns most of its cooling water, slightly warmer. An irrigated field returns much less, because crops breathe water out into the air.

The practical consequence is simple. In most dry regions, the water problem is a farming problem. If the farms around a city grow thirsty crops such as rice, cotton or alfalfa with cheap water, shorter showers save a bucket while the farms use a river.

Chapter 05

Groundwater: the bank account nobody can see

Under much of the world's farmland lie aquifers, layers of rock, sand or gravel that hold water like a sponge. Some are refilled each year by rain; others hold ancient "fossil" water that is barely refilled. Groundwater supplies a large share of the world's drinking water and a big part of its irrigation, and it has one dangerous property: you cannot see it running out.

Think of an aquifer as a savings account. Rain is the salary paid in. Pumping is spending. When spending exceeds salary, the balance falls, but nothing looks different at the surface for years. The only sign is that wells must be drilled deeper and pumps work harder. When electric pumps became cheap, millions of farmers each sank a well, and nobody tracked the total.

Satellites finally made the loss visible. From 2002, a pair of satellites called GRACE measured tiny changes in Earth's gravity: when an aquifer empties, the pull above it weakens very slightly. GRACE data showed large, steady losses of groundwater under northwest India, the North China Plain, California's Central Valley, the Middle East and the High Plains of the United States, where the Ogallala Aquifer feeds much of the country's grain and cattle feed.

Pumping too much can also do permanent damage. When the water is removed, the ground above can sink, a process called subsidence. Parts of Mexico City sink by many centimetres a year for this reason. Near coasts, a falling water table lets seawater seep into the aquifer and spoil it, which links this topic to the next one on coastal regions.

Chapter 06

Virtual water: the rivers hidden inside trade

In the 1990s the British geographer Tony Allan noticed something puzzling. The Middle East had run out of enough water to grow its own food decades earlier, yet there had been no great famine and no great water war. His explanation was virtual water: the water used to produce a good, which travels with it when it is traded. When Egypt imports a tonne of wheat, it is in effect importing the water that grew that wheat somewhere rainier.

Later researchers built on the idea with the water footprint, an estimate of the total water used to make a product. The figures are rough, but they show the scale. A kilogram of wheat takes on the order of 1,500 to 2,000 litres. A kilogram of beef is often estimated at around 15,000 litres, mostly because of the feed the animal eats.

Read the label carefully

Footprint numbers mix three kinds of water. Green water is rain held in the soil, used by crops where it falls. Blue water is taken from rivers and aquifers. Grey water, in this sense, is the water needed to dilute the pollution a product creates. Beef from rain-fed pasture uses mostly green water; cotton irrigated in a desert uses scarce blue water. The headline number does not tell you which.

Virtual water explains a quiet strategy. Dry countries can save water by importing thirsty goods instead of growing them. Saudi Arabia grew desert wheat from fossil aquifers from the 1980s, then largely phased it out from the late 2000s. The flip side is that some dry regions, such as parts of California, export their scarce water as almonds or cotton.

Chapter 07

A warmer world moves the water around

Climate change redistributes water and makes it less reliable. Warmer air holds more moisture, so heavy downpours become heavier, while heat dries soils and reservoirs faster between them. Many already dry subtropical regions, such as the Mediterranean, southern Africa and the American Southwest, are expected to become drier on average. More of the rain may arrive as floods that are hard to store, and less as steady flow.

Mountains matter enormously. Snow and glaciers act as natural reservoirs: they store winter precipitation and release it slowly in spring and summer, exactly when farms need it. The Himalaya feeds the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra, which support well over a billion people, and the Andes supply cities such as Lima. As glaciers shrink, their meltwater first increases, then falls once the ice is too small. Scientists call that turning point peak water. For some smaller glaciers, it has already passed.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Dying seas, shared rivers and lost hours

Water shortage shapes what a region can grow, how healthy its people are, and how neighbours treat each other.

Chapter 08

The Aral Sea: a lake turned into a desert

In 1960 the Aral Sea, on the border of what are now Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was one of the largest lakes in the world. Then Soviet planners diverted its two feeder rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, into vast irrigation canals to grow cotton in the desert. The cotton grew. The lake shrank.

Within a few decades the Aral had lost most of its volume and split into separate, much saltier bodies of water. Fishing boats were left rusting in sand tens of kilometres from the new shore. Winds lifted salt and farm chemicals from the exposed seabed, and local people suffered from respiratory and other illnesses.

There is a partial happy ending. In 2005 Kazakhstan finished a dam that kept the Syr Darya's water in the small northern part of the sea. The North Aral has partly recovered and fishing has returned. The larger southern basin has not. The Aral is the clearest lesson in the subject: a river can be used up completely, and the cost lands far downstream on people who never shared the benefit.

Chapter 09

Rivers that cross borders

Several hundred river and lake basins are shared by two or more countries. Upstream countries can take water first. Three cases show the pattern.

The Nile

Egypt gets almost all its fresh water from the Nile, most of which rises in the Ethiopian highlands. Ethiopia built the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, Africa's largest hydropower project, completed in 2025. Egypt and Sudan fear for their supply in drought years, and no binding deal on running it has been agreed.

The Indus

The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, brokered by the World Bank, split the river system between India and Pakistan. It survived several wars. In April 2025, after a deadly attack in Kashmir, India said it was holding the treaty "in abeyance". Pakistan rejected the move, and the treaty's future is now openly contested.

The Colorado

A 1922 compact divided the river among US states using figures from an unusually wet period. It promised more water than the river reliably carries. Long drought pushed its reservoirs to record lows, and the shortage rules are being renegotiated as the current guidelines expire in 2026.

Headlines often predict "water wars". The record is more reassuring. Research led by the American geographer Aaron Wolf found that cooperation has been far more common than violent conflict, and that hundreds of water treaties have been signed. Water does, however, raise tension and feed local violence, for example between farmers and herders.

Chapter 10

Health, time and who carries the bucket

Scarcity reaches people most directly through drinking water. According to WHO and UNICEF monitoring, around two billion people in the early 2020s still lacked "safely managed" drinking water, meaning water at home, available when needed and free from contamination. Unsafe water spreads diarrhoeal diseases that kill many young children. The health side is covered in Public Health · Clean water.

Where water is not piped, someone has to fetch it, and in most households that someone is a woman or a girl. Women and girls do most of the water carrying in such homes, and the hours come out of school, paid work and rest. A tap near the home is therefore also an education policy and a gender policy.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every source of new water has a price

There is no free fix. Each option costs money and energy, moves the problem elsewhere, or asks people to change habits.

Chapter 12

Why water is so badly priced, and the efficiency trap

In 1776 Adam Smith pointed out a puzzle that still sits at the centre of this subject. Water is essential to life and diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost a fortune and water costs almost nothing. The answer to this paradox of value is that price reflects the value of one extra unit, and when water is plentiful one more litre is worth little.

The trouble is that many governments keep water nearly free even after it has become scarce. Farm water is often subsidised, and electricity for pumps is cheap or free in some places, including several Indian states. When something scarce is priced as if it were plentiful, people use it as if it were plentiful. Yet in 2010 the UN General Assembly recognised access to safe drinking water and sanitation as a human right, and a pure market price could put a basic supply out of reach of the poor. Many utilities compromise with "block tariffs": a small first amount is very cheap, and the price rises steeply above it.

Efficiency has its own trap. Drip irrigation, developed commercially in Israel in the 1960s, delivers water drop by drop to each plant's roots instead of flooding a field. It sounds like a perfect saving. But studies have found that farmers often use the "saved" water to irrigate more land, and the water that used to seep back into rivers and aquifers no longer does. Efficiency saves water for the basin only if the savings are capped and not simply spent. Economists know this as a form of the rebound effect.

Chapter 14

What water security looks like: the Singapore lesson

Singapore, a crowded island with no large rivers or aquifers, long depended on water piped from Malaysia. Treating this as a security risk, it built what it calls the "Four National Taps": rain caught in reservoirs across the island, imported water, highly treated recycled water branded NEWater, and desalination. It also prices water to reflect scarcity.

Not every place can copy a rich city-state. But the lesson is that water security comes from diversity of sources and honesty about price, not from one heroic project. Elsewhere that means measuring groundwater and capping pumping, fixing the pipes, and agreeing in advance how shortages will be shared.

The last point matters most. Droughts are predictable in general, even if not in timing. The places that cope best decide before the crisis who gets cut first.

The core idea

Fresh water is a renewable flow, not a fixed stock, but it is unevenly spread in space and time. Scarcity comes from demand outrunning a local supply, mostly because of irrigation, from hidden groundwater depletion, and from a climate that makes rain less reliable. It is as much about prices, rules and infrastructure as about rainfall.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and most of that is ice or underground.
  • Agriculture takes roughly 70% of the fresh water people withdraw.
  • About 4 billion people face severe scarcity at least one month a year (2016 estimate).
  • Falkenmark's lines: under 1,700 m³ per person a year is stress, under 1,000 scarcity, under 500 absolute scarcity.
The common mistake

Thinking the solution is shorter showers. Household use is a small share. In most dry regions, water scarcity is decided on farms, by what is grown and how it is watered.
