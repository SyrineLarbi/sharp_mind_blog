Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 12 of 20

Geographic inequality. The single biggest fact about your life is the place it started.

Two babies are born on the same day, one in Oslo and one in a village in Niger. Before either has done anything at all, the first can expect to live around twenty years longer and to earn, over a lifetime, dozens of times more. The gap between them is not about talent or effort. It is about place. Geographic inequality is the study of how much a location shapes a person's chances, at every scale: between countries, between regions of one country, and between neighbourhoods a few streets apart. This file explains what the gaps look like, why they arise and persist, why they have become one of the main forces in politics, and what has and has not worked against them.

Question 1 · What is it?

Gaps in income, health and opportunity, mapped

Geographic inequality means systematic differences in how people live that follow location. It appears at three scales, and each has its own causes.

Chapter 01

The birthplace premium

The economist Branko Milanovic, who has studied global income for decades, made a striking calculation. If you split the differences in income among all people on earth into the part explained by which country they live in and the part explained by everything else, the country part is by far the larger, accounting for more than half. He called the advantage of being born in a rich country a citizenship premium, or citizenship rent. An unskilled worker in a rich country typically earns many times what an equally skilled worker earns in a poor one, for the same hours of the same kind of work.

The gaps are not only in money. Life expectancy at birth in the mid-2020s was above 80 years in Japan, Switzerland, Spain and Australia, and close to or below 60 in several countries of West and Central Africa. Child mortality differs between the richest and poorest countries by a factor of twenty or more. For most people in history, and still today, place of birth predicts life chances better than almost anything they later choose.

Chapter 02

Between neighbourhoodsLife expectancy, schools, crime, children's later earningsDistricts of the same city a few kilometres apart

The neighbourhood scale can be the most shocking, because the distance is so small. In 2008 a World Health Organization commission led by the epidemiologist Michael Marmot reported that a boy born in Calton, a deprived district in the east end of Glasgow, could expect to live to about 54, while one born in Lenzie, a suburb a few kilometres away, could expect to live to about 82. In the United States, researchers have found life expectancy gaps of around twenty years between the healthiest and least healthy counties.

Chapter 03

How it is measured

The simplest measure is average income per person, usually GDP per capita (the value of everything produced in a year, divided by population), adjusted for purchasing power parity so that a dollar buys a similar basket of goods everywhere. Broader measures include the UN's Human Development Index, which combines income, education and life expectancy, and can be calculated for regions as well as countries.

Where official statistics are weak, researchers use clever substitutes. Satellite images of night-time lights track economic activity, because richer places burn more electricity after dark. Mobile phone records, building roofs seen from space and household surveys fill other gaps. These tools have made it possible to map poverty district by district in countries that have not had a reliable census in years. The topic on geographic information systems explains how such maps are made.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Nature, institutions and the snowball

There are three families of explanation for why places differ. They are not exclusive, and the arguments between them are among the liveliest in social science.

Chapter 04

First nature: what the land gives

Geographers call the physical advantages of a place its first nature: climate, soil, rivers, coasts, minerals and disease. Some of these have clear effects. Countries without a coastline, called landlocked countries, pay more to reach world markets and depend on their neighbours' roads, ports and politics. Of the world's poorest countries, a disproportionate number are landlocked, many of them in Africa and Central Asia. The economist Jeffrey Sachs has argued that tropical climates impose extra burdens through diseases such as malaria and through poorer conditions for some crops.

The longer-run version of this argument is Jared Diamond's: that the shapes of continents and the plants and animals available for farming gave Eurasia a head start thousands of years ago. These arguments are strongest about the deep past and about specific burdens such as disease and distance. They are weaker at explaining why neighbours with the same climate differ so much.

Chapter 05

Institutions: the rules people live under

The town of Nogales sits on the US-Mexico border, split in two. The people on both sides share ancestry, food, music and climate. The northern half, in Arizona, is considerably richer, healthier and safer. In their 2012 book Why Nations Fail, the economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson used Nogales to make their case: what differs is institutions, the rules of the game, such as secure property rights, courts that work, and governments that answer to citizens.

With Simon Johnson, they argued that where European colonisers settled in large numbers, they built institutions that protected property and broadly shared power, and where they did not, they built systems to extract resources. They also documented a reversal of fortune: places that were relatively rich and densely populated around 1500, such as the Aztec and Inca lands, often became relatively poor, because colonisers found it profitable to extract from them. The three received the Nobel prize in economics in 2024 for this body of work. Critics question parts of the historical data and argue that culture, human capital and geography deserve more weight. The debate is unsettled, but almost everyone now agrees that institutions are a large part of the story.

Chapter 06

Second nature: why rich places stay rich

The third explanation looks at what places build for themselves, their second nature. In 1991 the economist Paul Krugman showed in a simple model how small initial advantages can snowball. Firms want to be near customers and suppliers. Workers want to be near jobs. Once a region gets ahead, it attracts more firms and workers, which makes it more attractive still. This is the core-periphery pattern. Krugman received the Nobel prize in 2008 partly for this work, now called the new economic geography. The Swedish economist Gunnar Myrdal had described the same snowball in the 1950s as cumulative causation.

The snowball explains why regional gaps are so stubborn. Italy's south, the Mezzogiorno, has been poorer than the north since before unification in 1861 and remains so despite decades of investment. Germany spent very large sums rebuilding the former East after reunification in 1990, funded partly by a special "solidarity surcharge" on income tax. Living standards rose a great deal and the gap narrowed, but more than three decades later average incomes in the East are still below those in the West.

Chapter 07

The neighbourhood effect

At the smallest scale, the mechanism is often about children. A team led by the economist Raj Chetty used tax records for tens of millions of Americans to measure how adult earnings depend on where a child grew up. They found that children who moved to better neighbourhoods at a younger age earned more as adults, and that the gains grew with each year spent in the better place. Their maps, published as the Opportunity Atlas, show large differences in children's later incomes between areas only a few kilometres apart.

Why do neighbourhoods matter so much? The suspected channels include school quality, exposure to violence, pollution, the employment of adult neighbours, social networks that lead to jobs, and residential segregation, the separation of groups by income or ethnicity into different districts. Housing policy matters here, because zoning rules that block affordable housing in rich areas keep poorer families out of them. Inequality between places is often inequality between people, sorted by the price of housing.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lives, migration and the revenge of places

Geographic gaps decide who lives longer, who moves, and increasingly how people vote.

Chapter 08

The global picture is improving, unevenly

For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the gap between rich and poor countries widened, a pattern economic historians call the Great Divergence. Since around 2000 the picture has changed. Very fast growth in China, then India, Vietnam, Bangladesh and others narrowed the gap between the average rich-country citizen and the average person in Asia. Measured across all people on earth, global income inequality has fallen over the past few decades, mostly because of that convergence.

Africa is the main exception to the convergence story. Several African economies have grown well, but sub-Saharan Africa as a whole has not closed the gap with rich countries, and its population is growing fastest. As a result, the world's remaining extreme poverty, as measured by the World Bank, is increasingly concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and in places affected by conflict. Global inequality is shrinking as a whole, while a smaller share of humanity falls further behind.

Chapter 09

Within countries, the gaps have widened

Inside many rich countries the trend has gone the other way. From about 1980, large cities with universities and service industries pulled ahead, while former industrial towns lost factories to automation and to competition from abroad. In the United States, wages in metropolitan areas such as the San Francisco Bay Area and Boston rose far faster than in much of the industrial Midwest and rural South. In Britain, London and the south-east grew richer relative to much of the north and Wales. In France the divide between big cities and smaller towns became a central theme of political debate.

For decades economists expected poorer regions to catch up, as workers moved to where wages were higher and firms moved to where labour was cheaper. In the United States this regional convergence worked well for much of the twentieth century and then slowed sharply from around 1980. One explanation is housing: in the most productive cities, rules limiting new building pushed house prices so high that moving there no longer paid for lower-wage workers.

Chapter 10

The politics of places left behind

In 2018 the economic geographer Andrés Rodríguez-Pose published a paper with a memorable title, "The revenge of the places that don't matter". He argued that votes for populist and anti-establishment parties across Europe and the United States were strongly tied to places in long-term economic decline, not only to individual poverty. Many studies have linked the 2016 votes for Brexit and for Donald Trump, and France's "yellow vest" protests that began in 2018 against a fuel tax, to regions that felt overlooked.

Not everyone agrees on the weight of economics here. Other researchers stress culture, age, education and attitudes to immigration, which also differ by place. Most likely they work together: places that lose jobs also lose young people, and the people who stay are older and feel their way of life is undervalued. Geography has become one of the main fault lines of politics in rich democracies, and governments of left and right have responded with promises to "level up" or rebalance their economies.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Help the people, or help the place?

Geographic inequality is costly, but some concentration is also what makes economies productive. The policy debate is about where to strike the balance.

Chapter 12

People-based against place-based policy

People-based policy

Help individuals directly, wherever they live: education, cash support, training and help to move to where jobs are. Many economists favour this, because money spent propping up a declining place may only slow an inevitable shift and may help landowners more than residents.

Place-based policy

Invest in particular regions: transport links, universities, research centres, tax incentives for firms. Supporters argue that many people cannot or will not move, that communities have value, and that the snowball can sometimes be pushed the other way.

The record of place-based policy is mixed. Italy's Fund for the South from 1950 to 1984 built roads and factories but did not close the gap. The European Union spends roughly a third of its budget on cohesion policy, aimed at poorer regions, and studies find it has helped growth in some, especially where local government is effective. China's coastal provinces raced ahead after 1980, and the state's "Go West" campaign from around 2000 pushed infrastructure into the interior, with real but partial results. The lesson most researchers draw is that infrastructure alone is not enough. It works when it connects a region to people, skills and good local government.

Chapter 14

What would really move the map

History shows that the map of advantage can change, but rarely quickly. South Korea was poorer than many African countries in the early 1960s and is now among the richest in the world. China's coast went from poverty to prosperity in a generation. In both cases the change came from a combination of investment in education, export industries, infrastructure and a state able to carry out long-term plans. It did not come from geography changing.

The two biggest levers remain the ones in this file: institutions that let people keep the gains from their work, and connection to markets, through transport, trade, migration and information. The next topic, Global transportation, is about the second. Places that are cut off, by distance, by borders, by conflict or by policy, tend to fall behind. Places that are joined to the world tend, eventually, to catch up.

The core idea

Where you are born shapes income and health at three scales: country, region and neighbourhood. The causes are first nature (climate, coasts, disease), institutions (the rules people live under) and second nature (the snowball of firms and talent). Between countries the gaps have narrowed since 2000; within many rich countries they have widened.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Country of residence explains more than half of global income differences (Milanovic).
  • Calton and Lenzie, near Glasgow: male life expectancy about 54 vs 82 (WHO, 2008).
  • The EU spends about a third of its budget on poorer regions.
The common mistake

Choosing one explanation for all gaps. Geography, institutions and the snowball work together, and the right weight differs between Niger and Nogales.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the two halves of Nogales differ, and why northern and southern Italy still do after decades of investment? If you can, you understand institutions and cumulative causation together.

Purchasing power parity
An adjustment so that incomes compare what money actually buys in each place.
Landlocked country
A country with no sea coast, dependent on neighbours for access to ports.
First nature
A place's physical advantages: climate, soil, rivers, coasts, minerals.
Second nature

Institutions
The rules of a society, such as property rights, courts and accountable government.
