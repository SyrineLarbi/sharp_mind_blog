Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 11 of 20

Borders. Lines that nature never drew, and that people will die to defend.

From space there are almost no borders. A few show up where policy has changed the land itself: the irrigated fields on one side of a desert line, the dark forest on one side of a logging boundary, the lit cities of South Korea next to the darkness of the North. Everywhere else, a border is an idea. It exists because governments and people agree that it exists, or because one side can force the other to accept it. That idea decides which laws you live under, which passport you hold, what you pay for imported goods and, too often, which war you fight in. This file explains how borders are made, how they work, and why they matter so much.

Question 1 · What is it?

A line where one government's authority stops

A border marks the edge of a state's sovereignty, its right to make and enforce law. It has a surprisingly short history and comes in more varieties than most people realise.

Chapter 01

The land nobody wants

Between Egypt and Sudan lies a patch of desert of about two thousand square kilometres called Bir Tawil. Neither country claims it. The reason is two different lines drawn by the British in 1899 and 1902. Egypt accepts the first, which puts Bir Tawil in Sudan. Sudan accepts the second, which puts it in Egypt. Each prefers the line that gives it a much more valuable area to the east, the Halaib Triangle on the Red Sea coast. Claiming Bir Tawil would mean accepting the other line and giving up Halaib. So the empty patch belongs to no state.

Bir Tawil shows what a border really is. A border is a legal claim that others accept, not a physical feature. Where the claims and the acceptance do not match, you get disputes, oddities and sometimes wars. In the Belgian town of Baarle-Hertog, surrounded by the Dutch town of Baarle-Nassau, the line runs through houses, cafés and shops. The address of a house depends on where its front door is.

Chapter 02

Kinds of borders, and the words around them

Geographers distinguish a few related words. A boundary is the line itself. A border is the line plus the controls and institutions around it, such as customs and passport checks. A frontier is an older idea: a broad zone at the edge of control, rather than a sharp line. A borderland is the region on both sides, often with its own mixed culture, trade and dialect.

Physical borders seem natural but cause their own problems. Rivers move. The Rio Grande changed course in the nineteenth century and left a strip of land called El Chamizal on the "wrong" side. The United States and Mexico argued over it for about a century before settling it in 1963. Glaciers move too. As Alpine ice melts, the watershed that marks the Swiss-Italian border has shifted, and in 2023 the two countries agreed to redraw part of the line near the Matterhorn.

Chapter 03

A young invention

For most of history, rulers controlled people and cities more than precisely measured territory. Empires faded out at their edges into zones of tribute, raids and trade. The Great Wall of China and Hadrian's Wall in Britain were military frontiers, not lines that separated equal states.

The modern idea of a world divided completely into sovereign states with exact borders grew in Europe from the seventeenth century. It is often linked to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, which ended the Thirty Years' War, though historians argue about how much those treaties really invented. Accurate surveying, printed maps and, later, nationalism made the idea practical. European empires then spread it across the world, often drawing lines on maps of places they had never seen. Most of today's borders are younger than the oldest buildings in the cities they divide.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From a deal on paper to a fence on the ground

Borders are agreed, described, marked and then run every day by officials. Each step can go wrong, and each has its own body of rules.

Chapter 04

The four stages of making a border

In 1945 the American geographer Stephen Jones described border-making in four stages. The scheme is still used by diplomats and surveyors.

Stephen Jones's four stages of boundary-making
  1. ALLOCATION     the political decision: which territory goes to whom
                    (a treaty, a peace settlement, a referendum, a conquest)
         |
  2. DELIMITATION   the line is described in words and maps, in a treaty text
         |
  3. DEMARCATION    surveyors mark it on the ground: pillars, posts, buoys
         |
  4. ADMINISTRATION the day-to-day running: crossings, patrols, customs, repairs

  most disputes come from a mismatch between 2 and 3:
  the words said one thing, the ground said another

A classic case is the border between Ethiopia and Eritrea. Eritrea became independent in 1993. The two countries fought a war over the small town of Badme and the border around it from 1998 to 2000, in which tens of thousands died. An international boundary commission ruled in 2002 that Badme lay on the Eritrean side. Ethiopia refused for years to accept the ruling, and the two only made peace in 2018. The treaty words from the colonial era were clear enough for lawyers. They were not clear on the ground.

Chapter 05

Keep the lines you inherited

When empires broke up, the new states faced a choice. They could redraw borders to fit peoples and languages, or keep the colonial lines. Almost all kept the lines. The legal principle is called uti possidetis juris, Latin for "as you possess under law". New states inherit the administrative boundaries of the colony. The newly independent states of Spanish America applied it in the nineteenth century. In 1964 the Organisation of African Unity formally committed its members to respect the borders they had at independence. The breakup of Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union in the 1990s followed the same idea, using the internal borders of their republics.

The logic was practical rather than just. Redrawing everything would have opened endless claims. The cost, discussed in Chapter 12, is that many borders still divide peoples and enclose rivals.

Chapter 06

Borders at sea

Most of the world's surface is ocean, and since 1982 it has had its own border system: the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, known as UNCLOS. It measures zones outward from a coastal baseline, usually the low-water line.

ZoneDistance from the baselineWhat the coastal state controls
Territorial seaUp to 12 nautical miles (about 22 km)Almost full sovereignty, though foreign ships may pass peacefully
Contiguous zoneUp to 24 nautical milesLimited powers over customs, immigration and pollution
Exclusive economic zone (EEZ)Up to 200 nautical miles (about 370 km)Rights to fish, oil, gas, minerals and wind; others may still sail and fly
Continental shelfCan extend further where the seabed doesRights to the seabed and what lies beneath

Because a tiny island can generate a huge EEZ, rocks and reefs become worth fighting over. In the South China Sea, China claims most of the sea within a "nine-dash line" and has built artificial islands on reefs. In 2016 an arbitral tribunal set up under UNCLOS, in a case brought by the Philippines, ruled that the line had no legal basis. China rejected the ruling and continues to enforce its claim. At sea, as on land, a ruling is only as strong as the willingness to accept or enforce it.

Chapter 07

Running a border: open, managed, closed

Borders differ enormously in how hard they are to cross. At one end, the Schengen Area in Europe has removed routine passport checks between member states. It grew from an agreement signed in the Luxembourg village of Schengen in 1985 and, from January 2025, includes 29 countries. You can drive from Lisbon to Tallinn without showing a document, though states may bring back temporary checks, as several did during the 2015 refugee movements and the pandemic.

In the middle are managed borders with customs and passport checks, the normal case. At the far end are fortified borders. The Korean Demilitarised Zone is about four kilometres wide and lined with mines and guard posts. The US-Mexico border runs about 3,100 kilometres, with barriers along a substantial part of its length. India has fenced much of its long border with Bangladesh.

Every border also has an economic gate, the customs system that collects tariffs (taxes on imports) and checks goods against rules on safety and origin. A customs union such as the EU's removes these checks between members and sets a single tariff for outsiders. That is why the question of where checks should happen after Brexit, at the Irish land border or in the Irish Sea, became one of the hardest parts of the negotiations.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

War, wealth and who you think you are

Borders cause conflict, cut trade far more than distance alone would, and shape the identity of the people who live along them.

Chapter 08

Territory and war

Studies of wars between states consistently find that disputes over territory are among the most common causes, and among the most likely to turn violent. Land carries resources, strategic position, people who share an identity with one side, and symbolic value that makes compromise feel like betrayal.

After 1945 something changed. The UN Charter forbids states to use force against the territorial integrity of another. Political scientists have documented that the conquest and annexation of territory, which was common for centuries, became rare in the decades after the Second World War. When Iraq annexed Kuwait in 1990, a broad international coalition reversed it. That is why Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 alarmed so many governments. They challenged not only one border but the norm that borders should not be changed by force. Most UN member states have refused to recognise the annexations.

Chapter 09

The border effect on trade

Borders cut trade even between friendly neighbours. In 1995 the Canadian economist John McCallum compared trade between Canadian provinces with trade between provinces and US states of similar size and distance. Provinces traded with each other roughly twenty times more. Later research by James Anderson and Eric van Wincoop revised the effect downward, but it remained large. This border effect arises from currencies, regulations, languages, networks and simple habit, not just tariffs.

Borders also create economies of their own. Twin cities grow on each side: El Paso and Ciudad Juárez, San Diego and Tijuana, Strasbourg and Kehl. Firms locate where wages on one side meet markets on the other, as in the Mexican assembly plants called maquiladoras. Price differences create cross-border shopping and smuggling. A border is a barrier, and every barrier creates someone who profits from getting across it.

Chapter 10

Divided peoples and the Irish example

Many borders cut through peoples who see themselves as one. The Kurds, often described as one of the largest peoples without a state of their own, live across Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria. Somali communities live in Somalia, Ethiopia, Kenya and Djibouti. Borders like these can keep alive demands for unification or independence, and they give neighbouring states reasons to interfere.

The island of Ireland shows how a border's meaning can change without the line itself moving. The border between Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom, and the Republic of Ireland was drawn in the 1920s. During the decades of violence known as the Troubles, it was militarised, with watchtowers and checkpoints. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998, together with both countries' membership of the EU single market, made it almost invisible. Brexit threatened to bring checks back. The solution, revised in the 2023 Windsor Framework, placed most checks on goods moving between Great Britain and Northern Ireland instead. The same line on the map can be a wall, a seam or a formality, depending on the politics around it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Open and hard, inherited and redrawn

Borders protect and divide at the same time. The choices are how open to make them, and whether to live with lines that were badly drawn.

Chapter 12

The "arbitrary borders" debate

It is common to blame Africa's and the Middle East's problems on borders drawn by Europeans. There is evidence for this. Economic research has found that ethnic groups split by colonial borders in Africa have, on average, experienced more conflict and lower development than groups that were not split. Many states contain rival groups that were pushed into one political unit.

The case against the inherited lines

They divided peoples, enclosed rivals, ignored grazing routes and trade, and created landlocked states with no say over their access to the sea. Some conflicts, from Kashmir, a legacy of partition, to parts of the Sahel, are tied directly to them.

The case for keeping them

Wars between African states over borders have been relatively rare since independence, partly because almost all states accepted the colonial lines. The few changes, Eritrea in 1993 and South Sudan in 2011, came after decades of war. Redrawing by ethnicity could have caused far more violence than it prevented.

A fair summary: the borders were drawn badly, but keeping them may have been the least bad choice afterwards. Most of the damage now comes less from the lines themselves than from what states do inside them: whether they share power and resources among the groups they contain.

Chapter 14

Where a border means nothing yet

Some spaces have weak or no borders at all. Antarctica's territorial claims, made by seven countries, have been frozen since the Antarctic Treaty of 1959. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says no nation may claim the Moon or other celestial bodies, though companies and states now debate who may own resources mined there. The internet crosses borders by design, yet states increasingly impose data localisation rules, requiring certain data to be stored within the country, and some filter what their citizens can reach.

The direction of travel is double. Borders are becoming easier to cross for goods, data and favoured travellers, and harder for others. They are also moving off the line itself, into visa offices abroad, airline check-in desks and databases. The border of the future may be less a place than a permission. For the laws that govern all of this, see International law.

The core idea

A border is a legal claim that others accept, marking where a state's authority ends. It is made by allocation, delimitation, demarcation and administration, extends to the sea under UNCLOS, and can be open or fortified. Borders cause wars, cut trade, and shape identity, and the post-1945 norm is that they should not be changed by force.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Territorial sea 12 nautical miles; exclusive economic zone 200.
  • Canadian provinces traded about 20× more with each other than with comparable US states (McCallum, 1995; later revised down).
  • The Schengen Area had 29 member countries from 2025.
The common mistake

Thinking natural borders are permanent and stable. Rivers shift and glaciers melt, and countries have had to renegotiate lines that followed them.
