Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 08 of 20

Megacities. Ten million people who chose crowding, and the reasons it keeps paying.

A megacity looks like a mistake. Traffic that does not move, rents that swallow half a salary, air you can taste, water that runs out. And yet every year millions more people move into the world's largest urban regions, and the money moves with them. They are not confused. A very large city is a machine for putting people near other people, and nearness turns out to be worth more than almost anything it costs. This file explains what counts as a megacity, why they grow, what they do to a country, and where the next ones are rising.

Question 1 · What is it?

A size threshold, and an argument about where a city ends

The definition sounds simple: ten million people. The hard part is deciding which ten million, because no city has a clean edge.

Chapter 01

From two to dozens in one lifetime

The United Nations uses the word megacity for an urban area with at least ten million inhabitants. In 1950 the world had two: New York and Tokyo. By the 2010s it had more than thirty, and the UN's population division counted 33 in its 2018 report. Most of the new arrivals were in Asia, and a growing share are in Africa and Latin America. Places like Dhaka, Lagos, Karachi and Kinshasa were modest cities within living memory.

This is a new thing in human history. Ancient Rome at its height probably had around a million people, and for most of the next two thousand years almost no city came close to that. Ten million people in one continuous built-up area became possible only with modern food transport, piped water, sewers and mass transit. A megacity is not just a big town. It is a technology stack that lets ten million people eat, drink and get to work every day without anyone being in charge of the whole.

Chapter 02

Three ways to draw the line

Ask "how big is Tokyo?" and you can honestly get three very different answers. The reason is that "city" means at least three things.

DefinitionWhat it countsExample
City properThe area inside the official administrative boundaryThe Tokyo Metropolis government area: about 14 million
Urban agglomerationThe continuous built-up area, whatever the boundaries sayTokyo plus the cities it has grown into, such as Yokohama and Kawasaki
Metropolitan areaEverywhere tied to the core by daily commutingGreater Tokyo: commonly put at around 37 million, the figure most rankings used for years

The boundary problem can mislead in both directions. The Chinese municipality of Chongqing is often called the world's largest city because it has over 30 million registered residents. But that municipality covers an area about the size of Austria, and much of it is farmland and small towns. Its actual built-up core is far smaller. Meanwhile Paris proper has only about two million people, which makes the city look small, even though its region holds around twelve million.

Rankings also change when the method changes. In late 2025 the UN published a revised method that draws urban areas from satellite data on built-up land and population density rather than from each country's own definitions. Under that method Jakarta came out on top, with Dhaka and Tokyo close behind. Nothing physical had moved. Only the ruler had. Whenever you see "the world's biggest city", ask which definition was used.

Chapter 03

Counting people nobody has counted

Even with a definition, you need a count. Many of the fastest-growing megacities have weak statistics. Nigeria has not held a full national census since 2006, so estimates for Lagos range from about fifteen million to well over twenty million depending on who is counting and where they stop. Large parts of many cities are informal settlements: neighbourhoods built without official permission, often missing from maps and registers. Satellite imagery, mobile phone data and building counts now help fill the gaps, but the honest answer for several megacities is a range, not a number.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Why people pile in, and how the place keeps running

Megacities grow because concentration creates value. They survive because a few hidden systems, water, transport and informal work, absorb the pressure.

Chapter 04

The pull: agglomeration economies

Economists call the benefit of being close together agglomeration economies. The idea goes back to the British economist Alfred Marshall, writing about industrial districts in 1890. He described three advantages, and they still explain most of the pull of a giant city.

Matching

A big labour market means a firm can find the rare specialist it needs, and a worker can find the job that fits their skills. In a small town a tax lawyer who speaks Korean might never find the right employer. In Seoul or London they can.

Sharing

Suppliers, airports, hospitals and universities are expensive to build. In a large city their cost is spread over millions of users, so services exist that a smaller place could never support.

Learning

Ideas spread by contact. People change jobs, meet at events, copy what works next door. Knowledge passes from person to person faster where people are dense.

"The mysteries of the trade become no mysteries; but are as it were in the air."

Principles of Economics · Alfred Marshall, 1890

Physicists have measured this pull. In a well-known 2007 study, Luís Bettencourt, Geoffrey West and colleagues compared cities of different sizes in several countries. They found that when a city doubles in population, measures such as wages, patents and economic output per person rise by roughly 15 percent. Infrastructure behaves the other way. A city twice as large needs less than twice as many petrol stations or kilometres of cable. Bigger cities are richer per head and more efficient per head at the same time. The same scaling, unfortunately, applies to some crime and infectious disease.

Chapter 05

The push: why the countryside empties

The pull of the city is only half the story. Farming needs fewer hands every decade as machines and fertiliser replace labour, so rural jobs disappear. Land is divided between children until plots are too small to live on. Drought, conflict and poor rural services push people out. And in many countries a large share of new city growth now comes not from migrants at all, but from natural increase: children born to people already living in the city.

Governments shape the flow too. China's household registration system, the hukou, long tied public services such as schooling to a person's place of birth. Hundreds of millions of rural people moved to cities anyway, but many lived for years as second-class residents without full access to those services. The rules have been loosened in stages, especially for smaller cities, but the largest cities still control entry tightly.

Chapter 06

The hidden systems that keep it alive

A megacity runs on a few systems most residents never think about until they fail.

Water. Ten million people use billions of litres a day. Many megacities pump it from underground faster than rain refills it. When water is pulled out of soft ground, the ground compacts and sinks, a process called subsidence. Parts of Mexico City have sunk by many metres over the past century, and in some districts the rate is still tens of centimetres a year. Northern Jakarta is sinking so fast that large areas now sit below sea level behind sea walls.

Transport. The difference between a functioning megacity and a jammed one is mostly rail. Greater Tokyo moves tens of millions of trips a day on trains, and Shinjuku station is usually described as the busiest in the world, with about three and a half million passengers a day before the pandemic. Cities that grew around roads alone, such as Lagos or Jakarta until recently, spend hours a day in traffic. Jakarta opened its first metro line in 2019.

The informal economy. In many megacities of the Global South, most people work in jobs that are not registered or taxed: street vending, small workshops, motorbike taxis, home services. UN-Habitat estimates that over a billion people worldwide live in slums or informal settlements. These places are often described only as failures. They are also where newcomers find their first cheap room and their first job. Dharavi in Mumbai, often called Asia's largest slum, is also a dense industrial district of recycling, leather and pottery workshops.

Chapter 07

Who governs a place this big?

Most megacities have no single government that covers the whole urban area. Greater Mexico City spreads across Mexico City itself, dozens of municipalities in the State of Mexico, and part of Hidalgo state. Each has its own mayor, budget and rules. Buses stop at boundaries. Water from one jurisdiction serves another that does not pay for it. This is called metropolitan fragmentation, and it is one of the main reasons big cities struggle with problems that cross lines on a map, such as transport, flooding and air.

Some places have built a regional layer on top. London created the Greater London Authority with an elected mayor in 2000. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government runs services for the whole metropolis proper. China plans whole city clusters from above, as in the Greater Bay Area that links Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Hong Kong, Macau and several other cities into a region of over eighty million people. The bigger the city, the more its problems are coordination problems rather than engineering ones.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where a country's wealth, power and risk all gather

A megacity often holds a fifth of a nation's people and a larger share of its money. What happens there decides a great deal of what happens everywhere else.

Chapter 08

The primate city

In 1939 the American geographer Mark Jefferson noticed that many countries have one city that is far larger than any other. He called it the primate city. Bangkok is many times the size of Thailand's second city. Lima dominates Peru, Dhaka dominates Bangladesh, and the Paris region produces roughly three-tenths of France's economic output while holding under a fifth of its people.

Primacy usually has historical roots. A capital that was also the main port and the colonial administrative centre gathered roads, railways, universities and ministries in one place, and everything after that followed the existing network. The result can be efficient, because everything important is in one place. It can also be unfair, because the rest of the country feels like a supplier to the capital. Countries with several strong cities, such as Germany or Italy, usually have older histories of separate states.

Contrast this with a pattern called the rank-size rule, or Zipf's law for cities. In many large countries the second city is roughly half the size of the first, the third about a third, and so on. The United States roughly follows it. Countries that break it sharply, with one giant and no middle, tend to be ones where political power has long been centralised.

Chapter 09

Engines of national growth

Megacities generate a share of national output far larger than their share of population. That follows directly from the scaling result in Chapter 04. Shenzhen grew from a set of small towns near Hong Kong in 1980, when it was named one of China's first special economic zones, into a metropolis of well over ten million and a global centre for electronics. Much of the story of China's rise is a story of a handful of coastal megaregions.

The American economist Edward Glaeser made the general case in his 2011 book Triumph of the City: dense cities are where poor people come to become less poor. Rural migrants to big cities, in most countries, earn more than they would have at home, even after high rents. That is why they keep coming. The slum is often a sign that a city is attracting the poor, not that it is creating them. Critics answer that this is true on average and can hide decades of hardship for the people who arrive without land, documents or rights.

Chapter 10

Concentrated risk

Putting so much in one place also concentrates danger. Many megacities sit on coasts and river deltas, because ports and rivers made them rich: Shanghai, Dhaka, Mumbai, Lagos, Jakarta, New York. Those are exactly the places most exposed to sea-level rise, storm surges and flooding. Others sit on active fault lines, including Tokyo, Istanbul, Mexico City and Los Angeles. A single disaster in such a place can damage a national economy.

Cities also trap heat. Asphalt, concrete and dark roofs absorb sunlight and release it at night, while there are few trees to cool the air. This urban heat island effect can make a city centre several degrees warmer than the countryside around it, most of all after sunset. And dense crowds help infections spread, as early COVID-19 outbreaks in cities such as Wuhan, Milan and New York showed. Density is not destiny, though: many dense Asian cities controlled that pandemic better than spread-out ones elsewhere. Governance mattered more than crowding.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The same density, counted twice

Almost every strength of a megacity is the flip side of a weakness. The question is whether a city manages the costs well enough to keep the benefits.

Chapter 12

What well-run giants do differently

Size itself does not decide whether a megacity works. Tokyo, larger than almost any other city on earth, is known for its punctual rail, low crime and relatively affordable housing. One reason for the housing is that Japanese zoning is set nationally and is permissive, so builders can add homes where demand rises. Other cities of similar size are known for chaos. The difference lies mostly in a few choices.

What tends to work

Building mass transit before the city sprawls. Letting housing supply grow with demand. Charging for road space, as Singapore has for decades and London has since 2003. New York started congestion pricing in January 2025. A regional authority with real power over transport and water.

What tends to fail

Building only roads, which fill as fast as they are widened. Strict limits on new housing in the centre, which push the poor to distant edges. Ignoring informal settlements instead of giving them water, drains and legal title. Dozens of municipalities with no shared plan.

The deeper trade-off is national. A country can let its biggest city keep growing and capture the productivity gains, or spread investment more evenly and accept a slower overall economy in return for fairer regions. There is no neutral answer. That tension runs straight into the next topics in this category on geographic inequality.

Chapter 14

Moving the capital, and the climate question

Some governments try to escape their megacities by building a new capital. Brazil opened Brasília in 1960. Nigeria moved its capital from Lagos to Abuja in 1991. Egypt is building a new administrative capital in the desert east of Cairo. Indonesia passed a law in 2022 to move its capital from sinking, congested Jakarta to a new city called Nusantara on the island of Borneo, though progress has been slower than first planned. The record of such projects is mixed. A new capital can move ministries, but it rarely moves the economy. Lagos stayed Nigeria's commercial centre and kept growing after the government left.

Climate change will test megacities more than anything else. Coastal giants face choices between very expensive sea defences, managed retreat from the lowest districts, or both. Heat waves will make cooling and green space a public health question rather than a matter of comfort. Remote work after 2020 showed that some office work can leave the centre, but it did not empty the great cities. The pull of being near other people has survived the telephone, the car and the internet. It will probably survive the next technology too. For the digital side of running such places, see Smart cities.

The core idea

A megacity is an urban area of ten million or more. It grows because concentration pays: better matching, shared infrastructure and faster learning. It survives on hidden systems of water, transport and informal work, and its biggest problems are usually coordination problems across many governments.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 2 megacities in 1950 (New York, Tokyo); 33 in the UN's 2018 count.
  • Doubling a city's size raises output and wages per person by roughly 15%.
  • Over a billion people live in slums or informal settlements (UN-Habitat).
The common mistake

Comparing city sizes without checking the definition. City proper, built-up area and commuting region can differ by a factor of two or more, which is why Chongqing can look like the world's biggest city and Paris like a small one.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a migrant might rationally choose a slum in a megacity over a house in a village? If you can, you have understood agglomeration, the urban wage premium and the push from the countryside together.

The words, in plain words

An urban area with at least ten million people, in the UN's definition.
City proper
The area inside a city's official boundary, whatever the built-up area looks like.
Urban agglomeration

Metropolitan area
The core plus everywhere linked to it by daily commuting.
Agglomeration economies

Informal settlement
A neighbourhood built without official permission or legal title, often lacking services.
Informal economy
Work that is not registered, taxed or protected by labour law.
Subsidence
Sinking of the ground, often caused by pumping water out from beneath a city.
Primate city
A city far larger than any other in its country, dominating its economy and politics.
Rank-size rule
The pattern in which the second city is about half the size of the first, the third about a third, and so on.
Urban heat island
The extra warmth of a city compared with surrounding countryside.
Metropolitan fragmentation
One urban area split among many local governments with no shared authority.
