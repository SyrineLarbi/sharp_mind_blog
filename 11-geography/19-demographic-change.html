Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 19 of 20

Demographic change. Three numbers, births, deaths and moves, quietly decide a country's future.

Elections, wars and inventions grab the headlines. Underneath them, slower forces are at work: how many children families have, how long people live, and who arrives or leaves. These change gradually, so they are easy to ignore, but they compound. A drop in births today becomes a shortage of workers in twenty years and a crowd of pensioners in sixty. Demography is the part of social science that comes closest to predicting the future, because most of the people who will be adults in 2050 have already been born.

Question 1 · What is it?

The changing size and shape of a population

Demographic change means changes in how many people live in a place, how old they are, and how the balance of men and women, natives and newcomers, shifts over time. Demography is the study of those changes.

Chapter 01

Two countries, two directions

In 2023, women in South Korea had on average about 0.72 children each over their lifetimes, the lowest figure recorded for any country. At that rate, each generation is roughly a third the size of the one before it. Schools in rural areas are closing for lack of pupils, and the government has described the situation as a national emergency. In 1960, South Korean women had around six children each.

In Niger, in the Sahel of West Africa, women in the early 2020s still had around six children each, among the highest rates in the world. Half the population is under about fifteen years old. The country's population is growing so fast that it could more than double within a few decades.

The two countries are moving in opposite directions, but they are on the same road. South Korea sixty years ago looked much like Niger today. Demographic change is mostly the story of countries moving, at different speeds, along one common path: from many births and many deaths to few births and few deaths. Where a country stands on that path explains much of its politics, its economy and its worries.

Chapter 02

Only three things can change a population

A population is like a bathtub with two taps and two drains. Births and arrivals pour in. Deaths and departures drain out. Demographers write this as a simple equation:

The demographic balancing equation
 population next year  =  population this year
                        +  births      -  deaths        (natural change)
                        +  immigrants  -  emigrants     (net migration)

Nothing else can change the total. That simplicity is powerful. Any claim about a population's future must say something about each of the three: fertility (births), mortality (deaths) and migration (moves). For the world as a whole, migration cancels out, so global population depends only on births and deaths. For a single country or city, migration can matter as much as the other two, and sometimes more.

Chapter 03

The measures that matter

MeasureWhat it meansA useful reference point
Total fertility rate (TFR)The average number of children a woman would have over her life if current birth rates heldWorld about 2.2 to 2.3 in 2024, down from about 5 in 1950
Replacement fertilityThe TFR at which each generation exactly replaces itselfAbout 2.1 where few children die; higher where many do

Median ageThe age that splits the population into two equal halvesUnder 20 in much of sub-Saharan Africa; around 49 in Japan
Dependency ratioChildren and older people compared with people of working ageRising fast in ageing countries

Two points about these measures are often misunderstood. Replacement is about 2.1, not 2.0, because slightly more boys than girls are born and a few girls die before they have children. And life expectancy at birth is an average that is pulled down heavily by deaths in infancy. When historians say life expectancy was around 30 in the past, they do not mean most adults died at 30. Many children died very young, while people who survived childhood often reached their fifties, sixties or beyond.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The demographic transition

Almost every country has followed the same sequence: deaths fall first, births fall later, and the gap between them produces a period of rapid growth that eventually ends.

Chapter 04

Four stages, one pattern

In the first half of the twentieth century, demographers including the American Frank Notestein described a pattern that became known as the demographic transition. It remains the single most useful idea in the field.

StageBirthsDeathsPopulationExample
1. Pre-transitionHighHighRoughly stable, with ups and downs from famine and diseaseMost of the world before about 1800
2. Deaths fallHighFalling fastRapid growthEurope in the 1800s; much of Africa in the late 1900s
3. Births fallFallingLowGrowth slowingIndia and much of Latin America in recent decades
4. Post-transitionLowLowStable or shrinking, and ageingEurope, Japan, South Korea, China

Many demographers add a fifth stage for countries where fertility has fallen well below replacement and stayed there, so that population shrinks unless migrants arrive. That stage was not part of the original model, because few people in the mid-twentieth century expected it.

The key to the whole pattern is the lag between stage 2 and stage 3. Deaths can fall within a generation, because better food, clean water and vaccines spread quickly. Births fall only when people's choices and expectations change, which takes longer. The world's population explosion of the twentieth century was not caused by people having more children. It was caused by far fewer children dying.

Chapter 05

Why deaths fall first

The decline of mortality started in parts of Europe in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Better harvests and transport made famines rarer. Cities built sewers and clean water supplies. Later came vaccines, antibiotics and the simple knowledge that germs spread disease. Each of these mostly saved the young, because infectious diseases kill infants and children above all.

In the twentieth century, the same improvements spread to the rest of the world much faster than they had arrived in Europe. A country could import vaccines, antibiotics and insecticides within a few years. This is why many countries in Asia, Africa and Latin America went through the mortality decline in a few decades rather than a century and a half, and why their populations grew faster than Europe's ever did.

As infections are brought under control, the main causes of death shift towards heart disease, cancer and stroke, which strike mainly in later life. Public health researchers call this the epidemiological transition, and Volume 28 describes its consequences in the diseases of long life and plenty.

Chapter 06

Why births fall: the forces, and the arguments

Every country that has become richer, more urban and more educated has seen fertility fall. That much is settled. Which force matters most is still debated, and the honest answer is that several work together.

Children survive

When parents can be confident their children will live, they no longer need many births to be sure of raising some to adulthood. Falling child mortality usually comes before falling fertility.

Children cost more

On a farm, children work. In a city, they need years of schooling and housing space. As children change from an economic asset to an expensive investment, families tend to have fewer and invest more in each.

Girls' education and women's work

Women who stay in school longer marry and start families later, and have more paid work to weigh against childcare. Female education is one of the strongest predictors of lower fertility across countries.

Contraception and ideas

Reliable contraception lets people act on their preferences. Television, migration and changing religious and cultural norms also spread the idea of the small family, sometimes faster than economics alone would predict.

The speed can be astonishing. In Iran, fertility fell from over six children per woman in the 1980s to around two by about 2000, one of the fastest declines ever recorded, helped by a national family planning programme and rapid gains in women's education. Bangladesh, still a poor country, went from around six children per woman in the 1970s to about two in recent years. Fertility falls faster than models built on Europe's history predicted, and it does not wait for a country to become rich.

Chapter 07

Pyramids, and the momentum hidden inside them

Demographers draw a population's age structure as a population pyramid: horizontal bars for each age group, men on one side and women on the other, the youngest at the bottom. The shape tells you a country's past and much of its future at a glance.

Three typical shapes
  growing fast            slowing / stable           shrinking and ageing
     (Niger)                 (India)                  (Japan, Korea)

        #                       ##                        ####
       ###                     ####                      ######
      #####                   ######                    ########
     #######                 ########                   ########
    #########                ########                   ########
   ###########               ########                    ######
  #############              ########                     ####
  wide base: many           straight sides:            narrow base: fewer
  children                  similar-sized cohorts      children than adults

The bottom-heavy shape hides an important force called population momentum. Even if every woman in a young country started tomorrow to have exactly two children, the population would keep growing for decades. The reason is that the many girls already born will become a large generation of mothers. Two children each from a big generation is still a lot of children. The reverse is also true: a country that has had low fertility for a long time has a shrinking number of young women, so even a recovery in births per woman produces fewer babies.

Momentum is why demographers can speak with such confidence about the next few decades. The parents of the 2040s are already alive and already counted.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Booms, burdens and attempts at control

A population's age structure shapes how fast an economy can grow, how a welfare state is paid for, and sometimes how stable a society is.

Chapter 08

The demographic dividend

When fertility falls, there is a window of a few decades when a country has an unusually high share of people of working age. The large cohorts born before the decline are now adults; the smaller cohorts after it mean fewer children to support; and the elderly are not yet numerous. If those working-age adults find productive jobs, savings and investment rise, and the economy can grow fast. Economists call this the demographic dividend.

East Asia is the classic example. South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and later China all went through this window during their periods of rapid growth. In a well-known 1998 study, the economists David Bloom and Jeffrey Williamson estimated that changes in age structure could account for roughly a quarter to a third of East Asia's growth "miracle".

The dividend is a chance, not a guarantee. A large young workforce becomes a dividend only if there are jobs for it. Without them, the same bulge of young adults can mean mass unemployment, frustration and emigration. Some researchers link large "youth bulges" with greater risk of unrest, although the strength of that link is debated. Whether sub-Saharan Africa can turn its coming working-age boom into a dividend is one of the big economic questions of the century, as Future population trends discusses.

Chapter 09

When the window closes: ageing

The dividend eventually reverses. The large cohorts who powered growth grow old, and the smaller cohorts behind them must support them. Japan is furthest along this road. Its population peaked around 2008 to 2010 at about 128 million and has been falling since, and close to three in ten Japanese people are now aged 65 or over.

Ageing puts pressure on pension systems, because most are "pay as you go": today's workers fund today's retirees. It raises health spending, because older people need more care. It can slow economic growth, as the workforce shrinks. It also changes politics, since older voters turn out in large numbers and tend to prioritise pensions and health care. The social and health-system side is covered in depth in Society · Aging populations.

Ageing is not only a burden. It is the result of two victories: people living longer, and people choosing their family size. The challenge is not that people live too long, but that institutions were designed for a younger population.

Chapter 10

When governments try to steer

Because population matters so much, governments have often tried to change it, with mixed and sometimes cruel results.

China's one-child policy, introduced around 1980, limited most urban couples to a single child. It was enforced with fines, pressure and, in many documented cases, forced abortions and sterilisations. Fertility in China had already fallen sharply in the 1970s, before the policy, so how much the policy itself reduced births is debated. Its side effects are not: a strong preference for sons led to sex-selective abortion and a ratio of boys to girls at birth far above the natural level of about 105 to 100. China relaxed the policy to two children in 2016 and three in 2021. Its population began to fall in 2022, and in 2023 the UN estimated that India had overtaken it as the most populous country.

Policies to raise births have a weaker record. In 1966, communist Romania banned most abortions and contraception. Births nearly doubled the next year, then fell back as people found ways round the ban, at the cost of many women's lives from illegal abortions. Today many rich countries offer generous child payments, parental leave and childcare. These seem to help families have the children they already wanted, often a little earlier, but no country has used incentives to bring fertility back up to replacement level once it had fallen far below.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every stage of the transition has gains and costs

Neither a young population nor an old one is simply good or bad. Each brings its own opportunities and pressures.

Chapter 12

From fear of too many to fear of too few

In 1798 the English clergyman Thomas Malthus argued that population tends to grow faster than food supply, so that growth would always be checked by famine, disease and misery. For most of history he looked right. Then farming productivity, and later fertility decline, broke the link he described. In 1968 the biologist Paul Ehrlich's book The Population Bomb predicted mass famines in the 1970s. The Green Revolution in crop breeding and fertiliser helped prevent the worst of them, even as population roughly doubled, although hunger remains real in parts of the world.

The public mood has now swung the other way, towards fears of population collapse and a world without enough young people. Both alarms share a weakness: they extrapolate one trend and ignore how people and institutions adapt. Fewer workers can be offset in part by later retirement, higher productivity, more women and older people in work, automation and migration. None of these is painless, and the size of the challenge differs hugely between a country with a TFR of 1.7 and one with 0.7. But demography is a slow force, and slow forces give time to respond.

Population change also carries an environmental question. More people generally mean more demand for land, water and energy, but consumption per person matters at least as much as numbers. A small rich population can have a larger footprint than a large poor one, which is why population and environmental debates so often talk past each other.

Chapter 14

What to watch

The Swedish physician and statistician Hans Rosling liked to point out that the world had reached "peak child": the number of children in the world has stopped growing, even though the total population has not. The growth still to come mostly comes from children already born growing up and from adults living longer. That single fact explains why world population will keep rising for decades even as families shrink.

Three indicators tell you most of what you need to know about any country's demographic future. First, the fertility rate, and whether it is above or below about 2.1. Second, the shape of the pyramid, which shows how much momentum is built in. Third, net migration, which in many rich countries is now the only reason the population is still growing.

Demographic change is slow, but it is not destiny. The numbers set the problem; policies, economies and choices decide how well a society handles it. The next file looks at the projections for the rest of this century and what they mean.

The core idea

Populations change only through births, deaths and migration. Almost every country follows the demographic transition: deaths fall first, births later, and the gap produces rapid growth that ends in an older, slower-growing population. The age structure this leaves behind shapes economies for generations.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Replacement fertility is about 2.1 children per woman.
  • World fertility is about 2.2–2.3 (2024), down from about 5 in 1950.
  • World life expectancy is about 73 years (2024).
  • South Korea's fertility rate was about 0.72 in 2023, the lowest recorded.
The common mistake

Thinking the population boom happened because people had more children. It happened because far fewer children died, while birth rates took a generation or two to follow.
