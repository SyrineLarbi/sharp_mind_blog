Deserts. Defined by missing water, not by heat or sand.

The largest desert on Earth is Antarctica. Most of the Sahara is not sand but rock and gravel. More people in deserts drown in floods than you might expect, and some of the most fertile soil in the Amazon rainforest is fed by dust blown across an ocean from Chad. Deserts are full of facts like these because almost everything people picture about them is wrong. They are not empty wastelands. They cover a large part of the planet, hold some of its greatest resources, and are home to a sizeable share of humanity.

Question 1 · What is it?

A place where the sky takes back more water than it gives

Heat and sand are optional. What every desert shares is a deficit: far more water could evaporate than ever falls.

Chapter 01

The real definition

A common rule of thumb says a desert gets less than 250 millimetres of rain or snow a year, about the height of a coffee mug. That is a useful start, but it misses half the story. A cold place can receive little rain and stay damp, because little evaporates. A hot place can receive more and still be parched. So scientists prefer the aridity index: the water that falls divided by the water the air could pull back out through evaporation and plant use. A desert is a place where that ratio is very low.

By that measure the world's drylands, a broad term that runs from true deserts through semi-arid grasslands, cover roughly two-fifths of the land surface, according to UN estimates. They are home to around two billion people. Geographers grade them from hyper-arid (the true deserts, such as the core of the Sahara), through arid, to semi-arid (the steppes and savanna edges, where farming is possible but risky).

On this definition, the largest desert in the world is Antarctica, where the interior receives very little snow each year. The largest hot desert is the Sahara, at about 9 million square kilometres, roughly the size of the United States or China. And most of the Sahara is not the rolling dunes of films. Sand seas cover only a minority of it. The rest is bare rock plateau, stony plain, gravel and mountains, such as the volcanic Tibesti range in Chad.

Chapter 02

Five kinds of desert

Deserts can be grouped by the reason they are dry. Topic 02 introduced the forces. Here they are as a family tree.

Many deserts combine causes. The Gobi is far from the sea and in the rain shadow of the Himalaya and Tibetan Plateau. The Atacama sits in the subtropical sinking zone, beside a cold current, and behind the Andes, which block moist air from the Amazon. That triple misfortune is why parts of it are among the driest places on Earth outside Antarctica, where some weather stations have recorded almost no rain for years at a time.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Landscapes of wind, rare water and extremes

With few plants to hold soil and few clouds to soften the sun, deserts follow rules of their own. Shapes form differently, temperatures swing harder, and life has to bargain for every drop.

Chapter 03

Shaped mostly by water, finished by wind

It sounds backwards, but water is the main sculptor of most deserts. Rain is rare, but when it comes it is often intense. With no plant roots to hold the ground and no soil to absorb it, water runs off bare rock in sheets and gathers into flash floods. These floods carry huge loads of gravel and boulders, cut steep-sided canyons and dry valleys called wadis, and dump the debris in fans at the foot of mountains. The canyons of the American Southwest were carved this way.

This is why flash floods are a real danger in deserts. A storm in hills you cannot see can send a wall of water down a dry wadi under a clear sky. Travellers who camp in a dry riverbed for its flat ground and shelter are choosing exactly the place the flood will go.

Wind does the finishing. It lifts fine dust high into the air and carries it thousands of kilometres. It rolls and bounces heavier sand grains close to the ground, which sandblasts rocks into strange shapes. And it sweeps away the fine particles from stony plains, leaving a tight surface of pebbles called desert pavement, which then protects the ground beneath.

Chapter 04

How dunes form and move

Where there is plenty of loose sand, the wind piles it into dunes. A dune grows when sand grains bounce up its gentle windward side and tumble over the crest onto a steeper sheltered side called the slip face. Grain by grain, the whole dune creeps forward. The shape depends on how much sand there is and how steady the wind is.

Where sand is scarce and the wind steady, dunes form isolated crescents like this, which can move tens of metres a year and bury roads and villages. Where winds alternate between two directions, long straight linear dunes run for many kilometres. Where winds come from many directions, star dunes grow into tall, many-armed peaks that barely move, and are among the largest dunes on Earth. Vast fields of dunes are called ergs, or sand seas.

Chapter 05

Burning days, freezing nights

Dry air and clear skies make deserts extreme in both directions. By day nothing blocks the sun, and the bare ground heats fast. At night there are no clouds and little water vapour to trap the heat, so it radiates straight back into space. A daily swing of 20 °C or more is common in hot deserts. People who cross the Sahara are often surprised to need warm clothes after dark.

The official record for the highest air temperature, 56.7 °C, was recorded in Death Valley, California, in 1913, although some climate scientists doubt that reading. Ground surfaces get far hotter than the air above them. Satellite measurements have found surfaces above 70 °C in the Lut Desert of Iran. Cold deserts swing the other way: the Gobi can reach well below minus 30 °C in winter. "Desert" describes dryness, and dryness amplifies both heat and cold.

Chapter 06

How life gets by

Every desert organism follows one of three strategies: store water, dodge the dry times, or find water no one else can reach.

Store

Cacti and other succulents keep water in thick, swollen stems and open their breathing pores only at night, when it is cooler, to lose less water. Camels can lose a large share of their body water and survive, then drink an enormous amount in minutes.

Dodge

Many desert flowers live as seeds for years. After rare heavy rain they sprout, bloom and set new seed within weeks. In the Atacama such events produce a famous "flowering desert". Many animals hide underground by day and come out at night.

Find

Some shrubs send roots tens of metres down to groundwater. In the Namib, some beetles stand head-down into the morning fog so that droplets condense on their backs and run into their mouths.

Desert animals are also built to lose less heat and water. The fennec fox of the Sahara has huge ears full of blood vessels that shed heat. Kangaroo rats in North American deserts can survive largely on water produced inside their bodies as they digest dry seeds. Desert life is not rare. It is specialised. Many deserts hold more species than people expect, almost all of them invisible at midday.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Homes, highways, treasure chests and dust

Deserts have shaped trade routes and empires, hold much of the world's oil and future solar power, and send dust that fertilises distant continents.

Chapter 07

People who mastered the desert

People have lived in deserts for as long as there have been people. The traditional strategy is mobility. Pastoral nomads, such as the Bedouin of Arabia and the Tuareg of the Sahara, moved their herds of camels, goats and sheep between pastures and wells as rain shifted. Their knowledge of where water could be found was a kind of map carried in memory.

The other strategy is the oasis: a fixed place where groundwater reaches the surface or can be dug for. Oases grew date palms, gardens and towns, and became the stopping points on long trade routes. The camel, domesticated in Arabia, made those routes possible. Across the Sahara, camel caravans linked the Mediterranean with West Africa for centuries, carrying salt, gold, cloth, books and enslaved people. Volume 18 tells how that trade made Mali famous.

Persian engineers invented one of the cleverest desert technologies, the qanat. It is a gently sloping tunnel dug into the base of a hill to tap groundwater and carry it, by gravity alone, to fields and villages kilometres away. Because the water travels underground, little evaporates. Some qanats have run for many centuries, and the technique spread from Iran across the Middle East, North Africa and beyond.

Chapter 08

Treasure under dry ground

Deserts hold an outsized share of the world's resources. The countries around the Gulf sit on a large share of the world's proven oil reserves. Morocco holds most of the world's known phosphate reserves, a key fertiliser ingredient. The dry salt flats of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia, known as the lithium triangle, hold a large share of the world's lithium (Volume 12 explains how it is extracted). Desert air also preserves things: the Atacama's dry, clear, high skies make it home to some of the world's most important astronomical observatories.

The resource with the biggest future may be sunlight. Deserts get intense, reliable sun with few clouds, and land is often cheap. Some of the world's largest solar plants are in deserts: in Morocco near Ouarzazate, in the United Arab Emirates, in Rajasthan in India, and in north-western China. Volume 11 explains how solar panels work. Dust is the main enemy, because a dirty panel produces less power, so cleaning robots and water use are part of the cost.

Chapter 09

The dust that crosses oceans

Deserts do not stay put. Every year, winds lift enormous quantities of dust into the atmosphere. The single biggest source is the Bodélé Depression in Chad, the bed of an ancient lake, where fine dried sediment is scoured by strong, steady winds.

Satellite studies have tracked this dust across the Atlantic. A NASA study published in 2015 estimated that tens of millions of tonnes of Saharan dust settle on the Amazon basin each year. That dust contains phosphorus, a nutrient that the heavy rains of the Amazon constantly wash out of its soils. One of the world's greatest rainforests is partly fertilised by one of its greatest deserts. Saharan dust also feeds plankton in the Atlantic and reaches the Caribbean and the southern United States.

Dust has a darker side. Large dust storms close airports, damage crops and worsen asthma and other lung disease. Fine particles from the Gobi and the deserts of northern China reach Beijing, Seoul and Japan in spring. Dust storms in the Middle East have become a serious public health concern in recent decades, partly because drying lakes and marshes expose new dust sources.

Chapter 10

Cities in the sand

Some of the fastest-growing cities of the last half-century are in deserts: Dubai, Doha, Riyadh, Phoenix, Las Vegas. They rely on technologies that cancel the desert's two great problems. Air conditioning handles the heat. Water comes from far away or deep below: from desalination plants on the Gulf coast, from long canals and pipelines in the American Southwest, or from deep aquifers.

Some of those aquifers hold fossil water, rain that fell thousands of years ago when the climate was wetter and that is barely being refilled today. Libya's Great Man-Made River pipes water from such an aquifer under the Sahara to its coastal cities. Saudi Arabia used fossil groundwater to grow wheat in the desert from the 1980s, briefly becoming a major producer, and then phased the programme out as aquifers fell. Pumping fossil water is mining, not farming, because once it is gone it will not come back on any human timescale. Topic 15 on water scarcity returns to this.

Harsh limits, hidden gifts, and a misunderstood threat

Chapter 12

Desertification: not marching dunes

In the 1970s and 1980s, terrible droughts struck the Sahel, the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara. Harvests failed and many people died. Images of the time fed a popular idea: that the Sahara was advancing southward like an army, swallowing the land.

The reality is subtler. Desertification, as the UN defines it, means the degradation of land in drylands. It is caused by a mix of climate variation and human activity, such as overgrazing, clearing trees and poor farming practices. It does not mean sand dunes rolling forward. The soil loses its fertility and plant cover, the ground hardens, and the land produces less. It can happen in patches, far from any desert edge.

And since the 1980s, satellites have shown that large parts of the Sahel have become greener. Part of the reason is that rains partly recovered. Part is human: in Niger, farmers began protecting and pruning tree stumps that sprouted in their fields instead of clearing them, a practice called farmer-managed natural regeneration. Over millions of hectares it brought back trees, shade and soil. The African Union's Great Green Wall project, launched in 2007 to restore land across the Sahel, has made progress far slower than its targets, and its approach has shifted from planting a literal line of trees towards supporting this kind of local restoration. The lesson of the Sahel is that dryland degradation is real, but it is not destiny, and it can be reversed.

Chapter 14

When the Sahara was green

On rock faces in the Sahara, at sites such as Tassili n'Ajjer in Algeria, ancient artists painted and carved giraffes, cattle, elephants and hippopotamuses. Some images seem to show people swimming. Scientists have confirmed what the art suggests. Between roughly 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, during a period called the African Humid Period, much of the Sahara was grassland and scrubland with lakes. Lake Chad was far larger, and the Bodélé Depression, today's great dust source, was part of a huge lake bed.

The cause was a slow wobble in Earth's orbit that, over thousands of years, changes how much summer sunshine falls on the Northern Hemisphere. More summer sun strengthened the West African monsoon and pushed its rains far north. As the orbit shifted, the monsoon weakened and the Sahara dried, quite quickly in some places. The same orbital cycle will eventually bring wetter conditions again, but on a timescale of many thousands of years, far beyond any human plan. Deserts, like climate zones and coastlines, are not permanent features of the map. They are phases, and the people who painted those swimmers lived through the end of one.

The core idea

A desert is defined by a water deficit: far more could evaporate than falls. Deserts form under sinking air, behind mountains, beside cold currents, deep inside continents and at the poles. They are shaped mainly by rare floods and then by wind, and people have lived in them for millennia through mobility, oases and clever water engineering.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Drylands cover roughly two-fifths of the land and hold about two billion people.
  • The Sahara covers about 9 million km², and most of it is rock and gravel, not dunes.
  • A rule of thumb for a desert: under 250 mm of precipitation a year.
The common mistake

Picturing desertification as sand dunes swallowing farmland. It is mostly land losing its soil and plant cover, and in places such as the Sahel it has been partly reversed.
