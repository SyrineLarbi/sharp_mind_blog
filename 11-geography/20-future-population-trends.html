Future population trends. More people for now, fewer births everywhere, and a peak within this century.

For two hundred years, the big story of world population was growth. This century the story turns. The total will probably keep rising for several more decades, but more slowly each year, and then it is likely to stop. Meanwhile the world's centre of gravity is moving towards Africa and South Asia, several of today's giants are shrinking, and humanity as a whole is getting older than it has ever been. This file explains what the forecasts say, how they are made, and where they are most and least likely to be wrong.

Question 1 · What is it?

Educated estimates of how many people there will be, where, and how old

Population trends are the long-run directions in the size, location and age of humanity. Population projections are the tools used to describe them, and they are scenarios built on assumptions, not prophecies.

Chapter 01

Eight billion, and slowing down

The United Nations marked 15 November 2022 as the day the world's population reached about 8 billion. The exact date was symbolic, since nobody can count the whole planet to the day, but the order of magnitude is solid. It had taken roughly twelve years to add the last billion. In 1800 there were about one billion people, and it took all of human history to get there. The second billion arrived around 1927, the third in 1960. Then the billions came every twelve to fourteen years.

Behind the big total, the speed has already changed. The world's population growth rate peaked in the late 1960s at a little over 2 per cent a year. By the 2020s it was below 1 per cent and still falling. The world is still adding people, but each year it adds them a little more slowly. The question for this century is not whether growth will end, but when, at what level, and what the world will look like afterwards.

Chapter 02

A projection is a conditional sentence

A population projection says: if fertility, mortality and migration follow these paths, then the population will be this. It is a calculation, not a guess about the future in general. Demographers usually use the cohort-component method. They take today's population by single year of age and sex, then move each group forward one year at a time: apply death rates to each age, add newborns using fertility rates for women of each age, and add or subtract migrants.

The arithmetic is simple. All the uncertainty lies in the assumptions. That is why the main forecasters publish several scenarios, such as a "medium" path with lower and higher variants, or ranges of probability. When a headline says "the population will be X in 2100", read it as "if current trends continue roughly as expected".

Question 2 · How does it work?

What drives the forecasts, and why they disagree

Four forces shape the numbers: how fast fertility falls, how much growth is already built in, how long people live, and how many people move.

Chapter 04

Fertility: the biggest lever and the biggest unknown

Almost everything in a long-range projection depends on what happens to fertility in the countries where it is still high, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. A difference of half a child per woman, sustained over decades, changes the world total for 2100 by billions. The UN's high and low variants are built in exactly this way: the high variant adds half a child per woman to the medium assumption, and the low variant subtracts half.

Forecasters assume that high-fertility countries will follow the path of earlier countries through the demographic transition, but they must guess the speed. In parts of Asia and Latin America, fertility fell faster than expected. In several African countries, it has fallen more slowly than earlier projections assumed, and in some it paused for years. The pace of fertility decline in Africa is the single largest source of uncertainty in world population for this century.

At the other end, forecasters must also guess whether very low fertility, such as in East Asia and southern Europe, will stay low, fall further or recover a little. The UN's medium scenario assumes a modest recovery in the lowest-fertility countries. Critics point out that no country has yet recovered from very low fertility to near replacement, so this assumption may be optimistic.

Chapter 05

Growth that is already built in

Even if every country had replacement fertility from tomorrow, the world would keep growing for decades. This is population momentum, explained in Demographic change: the large young generations of Africa and South Asia are only now reaching the age of parenthood. The UN has estimated that most of the growth expected to mid-century would happen because of this momentum, even with no change in fertility.

The second built-in source is longer life. As older people live longer, fewer people leave the population each year. Rising life expectancy adds to the total even with no extra births. Most of the growth still to come is not from bigger families but from people already born growing up and growing old.

Chapter 06

Migration: small globally, decisive locally

For the world total, migration does not matter: a person who moves from Senegal to Spain is still counted once. For individual countries it can be the deciding factor. In many rich countries, deaths already outnumber births, and the population grows only because of immigration. The UN projects that in a number of high-income countries, migration will be the main or only source of population growth in coming decades.

Migration is also the hardest of the three components to forecast. It responds quickly to wars, economic booms and busts, and political decisions about borders, as Migration explains. Climate change may add to it. A World Bank study in 2021 estimated that, in a pessimistic scenario, over 200 million people could be pushed to move within their own countries by 2050 because of climate impacts. Such numbers are scenario estimates, not forecasts, and most of that movement would be within countries rather than across borders.

Chapter 07

Rival forecasts: why some expect an earlier peak

The UN is not the only forecaster. Two others are often quoted, and they differ mainly in how fast they expect fertility to fall.

The disagreement is not about whether the world will peak, but about when and how high. Models that give more weight to the spread of girls' education and contraception expect fertility to fall faster, so they project fewer people. All the major forecasters now agree that the age of world population growth is ending; they disagree about the decade.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A new map of humanity

Population shapes labour markets, political weight, demand for food and energy, and the balance between workers and retirees. This century changes all of them.

Chapter 08

The new centre of gravity: Africa and South Asia

Most of the world's population growth between now and 2050 is expected in a handful of countries, largely in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Sub-Saharan Africa's population, already well over a billion, is projected to grow to around two billion by mid-century. By 2100, roughly a third of humanity may be African, compared with fewer than one in five today. Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Ethiopia are each projected to be among the most populous countries in the world.

India became the world's most populous country in 2023, by UN estimates. Its population is expected to keep growing until around the 2060s, peaking at roughly 1.7 billion, even though its fertility rate is already around replacement. That is momentum at work.

This shift matters in several ways. Africa will hold a growing share of the world's young workers just as most other regions age. If those workers find good jobs, the region could enjoy the demographic dividend that powered East Asia, as described in Demographic change. It also means rapid urban growth, covered in Urbanization and Megacities, and rising demand for food, water and energy in places already under pressure, as discussed in Water scarcity.

Chapter 09

Shrinking giants

At the other end of the transition, some of the world's largest and richest populations are starting to shrink. China's population began to fall in 2022. Under the UN's medium scenario, it could fall by around half by 2100, to the mid-hundreds of millions, although such a long projection is very uncertain. Japan has been shrinking since around 2010. South Korea, with the world's lowest fertility rate, is projected to lose a large share of its population by the end of the century.

Much of Europe, including Italy, Spain, Poland and the countries of the Balkans, has fertility far below replacement. Several Eastern European countries have lost a large share of their people since 1990 through low births and emigration combined. In these places, the effects are already visible: villages emptying, schools merging, and regions competing for workers.

Not the same everywhere

The United States is projected to grow slowly for decades, mostly because of immigration. Some rich countries, such as Australia and Canada, also grow mainly through migration. Shrinkage is therefore not a single "rich world" trend. It depends on how each country handles both fertility and migration.

Chapter 10

The oldest world there has ever been

The most certain trend of all is ageing. The UN projects that people aged 65 and over will outnumber children under 18 worldwide by the late 2070s, and that people aged 80 and over will outnumber infants by the mid-2030s. This has never happened before in human history.

Ageing is fastest in countries that went through their fertility decline most quickly. France took over a century for the share of people aged 65 and over to double from 7 to 14 per cent. Countries such as South Korea, Thailand and China are going through the same change in about two decades. Many will grow old before they grow rich, with pension and care systems still being built when the demand arrives. The consequences for families, health systems and work are covered in Society · Aging populations.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What a peaking world gains and loses

An end to population growth is neither a catastrophe nor a cure. It shifts problems from one kind to another.

Chapter 12

How good are population forecasts?

Compared with most forecasts in economics or politics, population projections have a good record over a few decades. UN projections made in the 1950s to 1970s for the world population in 2000 came fairly close to the roughly 6.1 billion actually reached. The reason is momentum: most of the people who will be alive in twenty years are already alive.

The record is weaker for individual countries, for long horizons, and for turning points. Earlier forecasts underestimated how fast fertility would fall in places like Iran, Bangladesh and South Korea, and how low it would go in East Asia. They also struggle with sudden shocks: wars, epidemics and large migration flows. And the data itself is sometimes poor. Several countries have not held a reliable census for many years, so even today's population is an estimate.

Two common mistakes follow. One is to treat the 2100 figure as a fact; it is the least reliable number in any projection. The other is to dismiss the projections entirely; the next twenty years are among the most predictable things in social science. The near future of population is largely known; the far future is a range of scenarios.

Chapter 14

Living after the peak

For all of recorded history, nearly every generation was larger than the one before. Institutions quietly assumed it: pension systems relied on more workers tomorrow, cities planned for expansion, and economies counted on growing markets. A world that stops growing, and then slowly shrinks, will need to rethink those assumptions.

Several responses are already being debated. Raising retirement ages as people live longer. Bringing more women and older people into paid work. Automating jobs that cannot be filled. Making it easier for people to have the children they say they want, through cheaper housing, childcare and more equal sharing of family work. And managing migration from younger regions to older ones in ways that both sides can accept. Each is contested, and the mix will differ by country.

The deepest change may be in how we think. Growth in numbers has been the background assumption of modern history; this century it becomes a choice rather than a given. Whether a smaller, older humanity is richer, freer and kinder to the planet depends far less on the numbers than on what societies decide to do with them.

The core idea

World population is still growing but more slowly every year. Most forecasters expect a peak this century, followed by slow decline. Growth is shifting to Africa and South Asia, while China, Japan and much of Europe shrink, and the whole world is ageing. The near future is well known; the far future is a range that depends mostly on fertility.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 8 billion people in November 2022; about 8.2 billion in 2024.
  • UN medium projection: peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (2024 edition).
  • Growth rate peaked at a little over 2% a year in the late 1960s; now under 1%.
  • People 65+ are projected to outnumber under-18s by the late 2070s.
The common mistake

Quoting the 2100 figure as if it were a fact. The further ahead the projection, the more it is a scenario, and the 2100 numbers differ by billions between forecasters.

Test yourself

Can you explain why India's population will keep growing for decades even though its fertility rate is already around replacement? If you can, you understand population momentum and why forecasts for the next twenty years are so reliable.

The words, in plain words
Population projection
A calculation of future population, given stated assumptions about births, deaths and migration.
Scenario
One possible path, based on one set of assumptions, shown alongside others.
Medium variant
The UN's central projection, based on the most likely assumptions.
Cohort-component method
The standard technique: move each age group forward in time, removing deaths and adding births and migrants.
Population peak
The highest point a population reaches before it starts to fall.
Population momentum
Growth that continues because a large young generation is reaching parenthood.
Replacement fertility
About 2.1 children per woman, the level at which each generation replaces the last.
Natural decrease
When deaths outnumber births in a population.
Ageing
A rise in the share of older people in a population.
Demographic dividend
The economic boost possible when working-age adults make up an unusually large share of the population.
Census
An official count of a country's whole population, usually every ten years.
