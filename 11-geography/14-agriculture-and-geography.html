Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 14 of 20

Agriculture and geography. The largest thing humans have ever done to the surface of the Earth.

Fly over almost any inhabited region and most of what you see below is farmland: the green circles of irrigated fields in Kansas and Saudi Arabia, the rice terraces of Java, the patchwork of small plots in the Nile delta, the endless soy of central Brazil. Agriculture uses roughly half of the planet's habitable land. Where each crop grows is not an accident. It is set by climate, soil and water, then bent by markets, technology, history and politics. And the relationship runs both ways. Farming shaped the land, and it shaped the societies that live on it. This file explains the geography of how the world feeds itself, and how that geography is starting to move.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of where food is grown, and why there

Agricultural geography asks why particular crops, animals and farming methods are found in particular places, and what farming does to those places in turn.

Chapter 01

Half the habitable land

Take away the ice, the deserts, the bare mountains and the oceans, and what remains is the habitable land of the Earth. Roughly half of it is now used for agriculture. Forests cover most of the rest, and cities, despite their importance, take up only about one percent. Farming is by far the largest human use of land.

Most of that farmland is not used to grow crops for people. Around three-quarters of all agricultural land is used for livestock, as pasture for grazing or as fields growing animal feed. Yet meat, milk and eggs supply less than a fifth of the world's calories. Plants eaten directly by people supply the rest from a much smaller area. This single imbalance explains a great deal about debates over diets, forests and climate, which return in Chapter 10.

Chapter 02

What decides what grows where

Every crop has a range of conditions it can tolerate, and a narrower range where it thrives. Five physical factors set the basic map.

FactorWhy it mattersExample
Temperature and growing seasonThe number of frost-free days limits what can ripenMaize needs a long warm season; barley and rye tolerate cooler, shorter ones
Rainfall and its timingCrops need water when they grow, not only on average over the yearMonsoon rains decide rice planting across South Asia
SoilDepth, texture, nutrients and drainage decide fertilityThe deep black soils of Ukraine and southern Russia, called chernozem, are among the most fertile on earth
Slope and altitudeSteep land erodes; high land is colderCoffee grows on tropical mountains where heat is moderated by height
Day lengthSome crops flower only when days reach a certain lengthVarieties of soybean are bred for particular latitudes

These factors interact with the topic on climate zones. The great grain belts, the American Midwest, the Canadian prairies, the Pampas of Argentina, the steppes of Ukraine and Kazakhstan, sit in temperate grasslands where summers are warm, rainfall is moderate and the soils were built up over millennia under grass. Tropical rainforests, by contrast, often have thin, poor soils, because heavy rain washes nutrients away and most of the ecosystem's nutrients are held in the plants themselves.

Chapter 03

Where the crops came from

In the early twentieth century the Russian botanist Nikolai Vavilov travelled the world collecting seeds and noticed that each crop had a region where its wild relatives and its local varieties were most diverse. He proposed that these were its centres of origin. Later research, including genetics and archaeology, has refined his map, but the core idea holds: farming was invented independently in perhaps a dozen regions.

Wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats come from the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East; rice and millet from China; maize, beans and squash from Mexico; potatoes and quinoa from the Andes; sorghum from Africa's Sahel; bananas and taro from New Guinea and South-East Asia. Today, most countries depend mainly on crops that were first domesticated somewhere else. The world's farming map is built on a few ancient inventions that spread, first slowly by walking and then suddenly by ship.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Markets, water and science bend the natural map

Nature sets what is possible. Distance to markets, access to water and agricultural science decide what actually happens.

Chapter 04

Von Thünen's rings

In 1826 the German landowner and economist Johann Heinrich von Thünen published The Isolated State, one of the founding works of economic geography. He imagined a single city in the middle of a flat plain of identical soil and asked what farmers would grow at each distance from it. His answer was that land use would form rings, set by transport costs.

Real landscapes are not flat and uniform, and refrigeration and trucks have stretched the rings across continents. But the logic still works. Fresh vegetables and flowers are grown close to big markets or flown in. Grain is grown far away and shipped. Cattle ranches occupy the frontier, in places such as the Brazilian Amazon, where land is cheapest. The rings did not disappear; they became global. Kenya's flower farms and Peru's asparagus fields are, in effect, inner rings of European and American cities.

Most farms in the world are small. A widely cited estimate puts the number of farms worldwide at around 570 million, and more than four in five of them are smaller than two hectares, roughly the area of three football pitches. Yet the large farms, though few, cover most of the farmland. Hundreds of millions of smallholders feed their own families and local markets on very little land, often without irrigation or machinery.

Chapter 06

Water: the great multiplier

Irrigation, bringing water to fields artificially, changes the map more than any other technique. According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, irrigated land is about a fifth of the world's cropland but produces around two-fifths of its food. Agriculture accounts for roughly 70 percent of all the fresh water that humans withdraw from rivers, lakes and aquifers.

Some of the oldest irrigation systems still work. Underground channels called qanats, first dug in ancient Persia, carry water from the foot of mountains to villages kilometres away, without pumps. The great river systems, the Nile, the Indus, the Yellow River and the Ganges, still feed hundreds of millions through canals. Modern pumps changed the scale: a farmer with a diesel or electric pump can draw water from deep underground, which turned dry regions such as the Punjab and the American High Plains into breadbaskets. As the topic on natural resources explains, much of that groundwater is being used faster than it refills. The next topic, Water scarcity, follows the consequences.

Chapter 07

The Green Revolution

In the 1960s many experts feared mass famine in South Asia. India was importing large amounts of American grain. Then a package of new technologies transformed yields. The American plant scientist Norman Borlaug, working in Mexico, had bred short-stemmed wheat varieties that could carry heavy heads of grain without falling over when given more fertiliser. The International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines released a similar high-yielding rice, IR8, in 1966. With irrigation and synthetic fertiliser, these seeds roughly doubled yields in parts of India and Pakistan within a few years. Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.

Across the world, cereal production has roughly tripled since the early 1960s, while the area of land used for cereals has grown far less. Most of the extra food came from higher yields, not from more land. The Green Revolution also had costs: heavy use of water and chemicals, depleted aquifers in the Punjab, greater reliance on a few varieties, and gains that went mostly to farmers who already had irrigation and credit. Sub-Saharan Africa, where most farming depends on rain and many staple crops were not the focus of early research, benefited much less, and its cereal yields remain far below the world average.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Food, landscapes and the planet's limits

Agriculture decides who eats, shapes the land we live in, and puts more pressure on ecosystems than any other activity.

Chapter 08

A few breadbaskets feed many

World food trade depends on a small number of exporting regions. Before 2022, Russia and Ukraine together supplied close to a third of the wheat traded internationally, much of it to the Middle East and North Africa. When Russia's full-scale invasion halted Ukrainian shipments through the Black Sea, grain prices jumped. A UN- and Turkish-brokered deal, the Black Sea Grain Initiative, let Ukrainian grain ships sail from July 2022 until Russia withdrew a year later.

Other crops are just as concentrated. Brazil and the United States dominate soybean exports, most of which go to China to feed pigs and poultry. India became the world's largest rice exporter, and when it restricted exports of some types of rice in 2023 to keep domestic prices down, prices rose across Asia and Africa. Countries that import much of their food, such as Egypt, are exposed to weather, war and policy in a handful of places far away.

Chapter 09

Famine is usually about access, not just supply

The economist Amartya Sen, who received the Nobel prize in economics in 1998, studied famines including the Bengal famine of 1943, which he witnessed as a child. He found that famines often occur without a large fall in the total amount of food. What collapses is people's ability to obtain it, which he called their entitlement: wages fall, prices soar, crops fail for one group but not others, and authorities do not respond. In Bengal, wartime inflation and policy failures meant that millions of rural labourers could not afford rice.

Sen also made a famous observation: no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a relatively free press, because governments that face elections and criticism cannot ignore mass starvation. The Irish famine of the 1840s, mentioned in Volume 19, combined crop failure from potato blight with policies that let food exports continue. Geography can cause a harvest to fail. Politics decides whether that becomes a famine. Most recent famines and near-famines, as in parts of Somalia, Sudan, Yemen and Gaza, have been tied to conflict.

Chapter 10

The environmental footprint

Agriculture is the main driver of deforestation in the tropics. Large parts of the Brazilian Amazon and the Cerrado savanna have been cleared for cattle pasture and soy, and large areas of lowland forest in Indonesia and Malaysia for oil palm. Studies estimate that the food system as a whole, including land clearing, fertiliser, livestock, rice paddies, transport and waste, causes roughly a quarter to a third of global greenhouse gas emissions.

Other effects are local but serious. Fertiliser washing off fields into rivers feeds algae, which then die and decompose, using up oxygen in the water. The result is a dead zone, an area of sea where fish cannot survive, such as the one that forms each summer in the Gulf of Mexico off the mouth of the Mississippi. Converting wild land to farms is the leading cause of the loss of species worldwide. Soil erodes faster than it forms on much farmland. The environment category treats these in more depth, in Deforestation and Biodiversity.

Chapter 12

Land sparing or land sharing?

This trade-off has produced one of the main debates in conservation science.

Land sparing

Farm the best land as productively as possible, and leave the rest wild. Supporters point out that the Green Revolution almost certainly saved large areas of forest that would otherwise have been cleared to grow the same food at lower yields. Many species, especially in the tropics, survive only in untouched habitat.

Land sharing

Farm in ways that leave room for nature within the farmed landscape: hedgerows, mixed crops, trees on farms, fewer chemicals. Supporters argue that high yields do not automatically protect forests, because profitable farming can attract more clearing, and that pollinators and soil life need farmland to be habitable too.

Most researchers now see the answer as depending on place. Where wild habitat is rich and threatened, sparing usually protects more species. In landscapes farmed for centuries, as in much of Europe, sharing can bring back birds and insects. The question is not which farming is good, but which farming, where, and what happens to the land it frees or uses. For practices that aim at both goals, see Sustainable agriculture.

Chapter 14

Feeding around ten billion

The United Nations projects that the world population will rise from about 8.2 billion in the mid-2020s to a peak of around 10 billion later this century. Most of the growth will be in sub-Saharan Africa, which is also the region with the largest yield gap, the difference between what its farms produce and what similar land produces with better seeds, fertiliser and water. Closing that gap is probably the single most important lever for future food supply, and it depends as much on roads, markets, land rights and credit as on technology.

Two other levers are large. A substantial share of food is lost after harvest or wasted in shops and homes; UN estimates suggest well over a tenth is lost before it reaches shops and around a fifth wasted after. And diets matter, because of the imbalance in Chapter 01: producing meat, especially beef, uses far more land per calorie than plants. Rising incomes have pushed meat consumption up in Asia and Latin America. How those diets evolve will shape the world's farming map as much as the climate will. The geography of food will be decided by three things together: how warm it gets, how fast poor farms close the yield gap, and what people choose to eat.

The core idea

Agriculture is the largest human use of land. Climate, soil, water and day length set what can grow; markets, irrigation and science decide what does. Most extra food since the 1960s came from higher yields, at a heavy cost in water, emissions and lost habitat, and the farming map is now starting to move with the climate.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About half of habitable land is farmed; about three-quarters of that serves livestock.
  • Irrigated land: about a fifth of cropland, about two-fifths of food (FAO).
  • Agriculture uses about 70% of the fresh water humans withdraw.
The common mistake

Assuming famines are caused simply by not enough food. Most famines are failures of access: prices, wages, war and government decisions decide who can eat.

Test yourself

Can you explain why flowers for European shops are grown in Kenya, and why cattle ranches sit at the edge of the Amazon? If you can, you understand von Thünen's rings on a global scale.

The words, in plain words
Habitable land
Land that is not ice, desert or bare rock, and so can support farming or forest.
Growing season
The part of the year warm enough, and usually frost-free enough, for crops to grow.
Chernozem
Deep, black, very fertile grassland soil, found especially in Ukraine and Russia.
Centre of origin
The region where a crop was first domesticated and where its diversity is greatest.
Von Thünen model
A model showing how distance to market arranges farming in rings around a city.
Shifting cultivation
Farming a cleared patch for a few years, then moving on and letting it regrow.
Smallholder
A family farm working a small area, typically a few hectares or less.
Irrigation
Supplying water to crops artificially, from rivers, reservoirs or underground.
Qanat
An ancient underground channel that carries water by gravity from hills to fields.
Green Revolution
The rise in yields from the 1960s using new seeds, fertiliser and irrigation.
Entitlement
In Sen's sense, a person's practical ability to obtain food through work, money or support.
Dead zone
An area of water so low in oxygen that most marine life cannot survive.
Yield gap
The difference between what a farm produces and what it could produce with better inputs.
