Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 07 of 20

Urbanization. The largest migration in history, and it is still going on.

For almost all of human history, nearly everyone lived in the countryside. Cities were small islands of traders, priests, soldiers and rulers in a sea of farms. Within roughly two centuries that flipped. Around 2007, by the UN's count, the world's urban population overtook its rural population for the first time. Moving to the city changes how people work, how many children they have, how long they live, who they marry and what they believe. Urbanization is not just a change in where people sleep. It is a change in what kind of society they live in.

Question 1 · What is it?

The shift of people, and of life, from country to city

Urbanization has two meanings. One is a number: the share of people living in urban areas. The other is a process: the way societies change as that number rises.

Chapter 01

The great move

Cities are old. Uruk in Mesopotamia had tens of thousands of people more than five thousand years ago, and imperial Rome may have reached around a million. But such places were rare, and before modern times only a small percentage of humanity lived in towns of any size. Food was the limit. As long as most people had to farm to feed everyone, most people had to live on farms.

The Industrial Revolution broke that limit. Farms became far more productive, so fewer farmers could feed more people, and factories created jobs in towns. Britain became the first mostly urban country in the mid-nineteenth century. In 1950 roughly three in ten people in the world lived in urban areas. By the UN's usual measures, the figure passed half around 2007 and is now well over half. UN projections have pointed to about two-thirds by 2050.

The speed is what makes this era unusual. Britain took about a century to go from mostly rural to mostly urban. China's urban share rose from under a fifth in 1980 to around two-thirds by the early 2020s, which means several hundred million people moved to cities in a single working lifetime. It is probably the largest and fastest movement of people in history, and it happened mostly within one country's borders.

Chapter 02

What counts as "urban"? Each country decides

There is a catch in all these numbers. There is no single world definition of an urban area. Each country uses its own.

CountryRough rule for "urban"Effect
Denmark, SwedenA settlement of at least 200 peopleEven small villages count as urban.
IndiaAt least 5,000 people, a minimum density, and most men working outside farming (plus places with town status)Many large, dense villages count as rural, so India looks less urban than it may be.
JapanMainly municipalities ("cities") of at least about 50,000 peopleA high bar, though Japan is highly urban on any measure.
Many othersAdministrative status: places the government has declared to be townsThe figure can change overnight when boundaries are redrawn.

Because of this, comparing the urban share of two countries can mislead. To fix it, the EU, the World Bank, the OECD and UN agencies developed a harmonised method called the Degree of Urbanisation, which classifies every square kilometre by its population density and the size of the settlement it belongs to. It was endorsed by the UN Statistical Commission in 2020. It splits places into cities, towns and rural areas, and on this method much of the world turns out to be more urban than national figures suggest. When someone quotes an urban percentage, the first question is "by whose definition?"

Question 2 · How does it work?

Why cities grow, and how they spread

Cities grow in three ways: people move in from the countryside, people already in the city have children, and the city's boundary swallows nearby villages. The balance between the three, and the way a city spreads outward, explain most of what you see in any urban area.

Chapter 03

Push, pull, and the wage gap

Geographers describe migration in terms of push factors, which drive people out of a place, and pull factors, which draw them to another. In the countryside, pushes include small and shrinking plots of land, failed harvests, lack of jobs, and the mechanisation of farming, which means fewer hands are needed. Cities pull with jobs, higher wages, schools, hospitals, and simply the chance of a different life.

The economist W. Arthur Lewis described the core mechanism in 1954, work that later earned him a Nobel prize. Poor countries, he argued, have a large surplus of rural labour: people whose work adds little to farm output. When factories and services in cities offer even modestly higher wages, those workers move. Their output rises, the economy grows, and the move continues until the rural surplus runs out. Urbanization and economic development usually rise together, which is why the richest countries are almost all highly urban.

There are exceptions that interest economists. In parts of Africa and the Middle East, cities have grown fast without a matching boom in factory jobs. Some researchers argue that in places where mineral exports drive the economy, wealth spent in cities on services and government can pull people in without much industry. The pattern is debated, but it is a reminder that urbanization does not automatically bring industrial development with it.

Chapter 04

Cities that grow from within

The picture of urbanization as villagers arriving with bags at a bus station is only part of the story. In many fast-growing cities of Africa and South Asia, a large share of growth comes from natural increase: births to people who already live there, outnumbering deaths. Estimates vary by country, but in many places it accounts for roughly half or more of urban growth.

This matters for policy. If a government thinks cities grow mainly through migration, it may try to slow the flow, for example with registration controls like China's hukou (Topic 06). If growth comes mostly from births, those controls do little, and what matters more is planning for housing, schools and jobs for children already born in the city. Reclassification is the third, quieter source: when a fast-growing village is declared a town, or a city's boundary is expanded, people become "urban" without moving at all.

Suburbanization, the growth of low-density housing on the edges of cities, took off in the twentieth century with railways, then cars and highways, and with government-backed home loans in countries such as the United States. Many people wanted space, gardens and quieter streets. Counter-urbanization, people leaving cities for small towns and the countryside, was noticed in several rich countries from the 1970s, helped by better roads and later by remote work. And from the 1990s many city centres saw re-urbanization, as old industrial districts were converted to flats and offices.

Some cities simply shrink. Detroit, the centre of the American car industry, had about 1.85 million residents in 1950. By the 2020 census it had fewer than 640,000, after industry declined and many residents moved to the suburbs and other regions. Shrinking cities face a hard problem: the same streets, pipes and services must be maintained by far fewer taxpayers.

Chapter 06

The informal city

When people arrive faster than formal housing, roads and water pipes can be built, they build for themselves. The result is informal settlements, often called slums: neighbourhoods built without legal title or planning permission, usually lacking some combination of safe water, sanitation, durable housing, enough space or secure tenure. UN-Habitat estimates that over a billion people live in such conditions, most of them in Africa and Asia.

The word "slum" can hide how much goes on. Dharavi in Mumbai, one of the best-known informal neighbourhoods in Asia, is crowded and short of sanitation. It is also packed with small workshops in leather, pottery, textiles and recycling. Brazil's favelas began as self-built settlements on hillsides and have grown into dense, established districts with their own shops and culture, and also, in some, serious problems of crime and police violence.

Policy has changed over the decades. Many governments once demolished informal settlements and moved residents to distant estates, which often cut them off from their jobs. Today the more common approach is upgrading: bringing water, drains, paved paths, electricity and legal title to existing neighbourhoods, so that residents can improve their own homes. Upgrading has a mixed but generally better record. Informal settlements are a symptom of a housing system that cannot keep up, and they are also a first step into the city for millions. Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cities make economies, change people, and shape the planet

Cities are where most wealth is produced, where social life is reorganised, and where much of humanity's environmental footprint is decided.

Chapter 07

Why cities make countries richer

Cities are often estimated to produce around 80% of the world's economic output, on a small fraction of its land. The reason is agglomeration, the advantages of being close together, which Topic 06 introduced. Economists usually name three. Firms can share suppliers, transport and infrastructure. Workers and employers can find good matches in a big job market. And ideas spread faster when people bump into each other, which is why industries cluster: finance in London and New York, software around San Francisco, electronics in Shenzhen.

Physicists have measured this. In a 2007 study, Luís Bettencourt, Geoffrey West and colleagues compared cities of different sizes and found regular patterns. When a city doubles in size, measures such as wages, patents and economic output per person tend to rise by around 15%. Infrastructure, such as road surface and petrol stations, grows more slowly than population, so bigger cities need less of it per person. The same study found that crime and some diseases also scale up faster than population. Bigger cities are more productive and more efficient, and more intense in every direction, good and bad.

Chapter 08

How cities change people

Sociologists have argued about city life since the nineteenth century. In 1887 the German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies contrasted Gemeinschaft, the close community of the village based on family and tradition, with Gesellschaft, the looser society of the city based on contracts, roles and self-interest. In 1903 Georg Simmel argued in his essay "The Metropolis and Mental Life" that city dwellers protect themselves from constant stimulation by becoming reserved and calculating. In 1938 Louis Wirth of the Chicago School described urbanism as a way of life marked by size, density and diversity, producing weaker personal ties and more formal ones.

Later research softened the gloomy picture. City people do form strong ties, but by choice rather than by birth: friends, colleagues, interest groups and communities spread across the city rather than packed into one street. The writer Jane Jacobs, in The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), argued that busy, mixed streets create their own safety and community through constant casual contact, what she called "eyes on the street".

What the city tends to give

Anonymity, which can mean freedom from the watchful eyes of a village. More choice of work, partners, religion and identity. Access to education, which is a major reason women's opportunities often expand in cities. Exposure to different people and ideas.

What the city tends to take

The thick, automatic support of extended family and neighbours. Space, which pushes up housing costs and pushes down family size. Birth rates are lower in cities almost everywhere. Some people experience loneliness amid crowds.

Urbanization is therefore one of the strongest forces behind social change: smaller families, later marriage, more education, more secular and more diverse attitudes. The Society category's Communities and Cultural change topics follow these threads.

Chapter 09

From urban penalty to urban advantage

Volume 20 describes how the first industrial cities were deadlier than the countryside. Crowding, no sewers and dirty water let cholera and typhoid spread. Historians call this the urban penalty. In 1854 the London doctor John Snow traced a cholera outbreak to a single public water pump on Broad Street, an early landmark of epidemiology. After the "Great Stink" of 1858, when the Thames smelled so badly that Parliament could hardly sit, London built a vast sewer network under the engineer Joseph Bazalgette.

Clean water, sewers and later vaccines reversed the penalty. In most countries today, city dwellers live longer on average than rural people, because hospitals, doctors and emergency care are closer. New risks remain: air pollution from traffic and industry, heat that is amplified by concrete, traffic injuries, and sedentary lifestyles. Large studies have also found higher rates of some mental health conditions among people raised in cities. The causes of that association are still debated.

General education

These are population-level patterns, not personal medical advice. Individual health depends on far more than where you live.

Chapter 10

Cities and the planet

Cities cover only a few per cent of Earth's land, yet they are commonly estimated to account for around 70% of energy-related carbon emissions, because that is where most people live and most goods are made and consumed. That sounds damning, but per person the picture reverses. A resident of a dense city with good public transport usually drives less, lives in a smaller home that shares walls with neighbours, and uses less energy for heating and travel than a resident of a sprawling suburb. Density is one of the most effective climate policies there is, when it comes with transit and decent housing.

Cities also change their local climate. Concrete, asphalt and dark roofs absorb sunlight by day and release it at night, while there are fewer trees and less moisture to cool things. The result is the urban heat island: cities are often several degrees warmer than surrounding countryside, especially at night. During heatwaves this difference can be deadly for older and poorer residents. The Environment category's Sustainable cities covers the responses, from tree planting to cool roofs to better buses.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The best and worst places to live, often in the same city

Cities concentrate opportunity and problems at once. The balance depends less on size than on how well a city is planned, governed and housed.

Chapter 12

Two arguments that never end: sprawl and gentrification

Sprawl is low-density, car-dependent growth on the edges of cities. Critics say it wastes land, raises emissions, isolates people without cars and costs more in roads and pipes per household. Defenders reply that many families genuinely prefer houses with gardens, that suburbs often offer cheaper housing than city centres, and that blocking edge growth can push prices even higher. Both sides have evidence. The answer often depends on whether new housing is allowed anywhere else.

Gentrification, a term coined by the British sociologist Ruth Glass in 1964, describes wealthier newcomers moving into a poorer neighbourhood, renovating homes and raising prices. Supporters point to investment, safer streets and better shops. Critics point to rising rents that push out long-standing residents and change the character of a place. Research suggests displacement is real but often smaller and slower than feared, and that the deeper cause is a shortage of housing in cities that many people want to live in. Many urban arguments that look like culture wars turn out to be housing supply problems.

Chapter 14

The cities that have not been built yet

A striking fact about the coming decades is that much of the urban world of 2050 does not exist yet. In Africa especially, much of the growth is expected not only in famous capitals such as Lagos and Kinshasa, but in hundreds of smaller and intermediate cities that most people outside their countries have never heard of. Decisions made there in the next few years, about street grids, land titles, public transport and water pipes, will be very hard to change later. Planners often stress that it is far cheaper to reserve land for roads and drains before people build than to buy it back afterwards.

Whether these cities become engines of prosperity or crowded settlements without services depends on things that are not geography at all: whether local governments have money and authority, whether land ownership is clear, whether jobs grow as fast as populations. History offers both kinds of example, sometimes in the same country. Urbanization is the most reliable path out of poverty that humanity has found, but it works only when the city can keep up with its own growth. Megacities looks at what happens at the very top of the size scale.

The core idea

Urbanization is the shift of people from countryside to city, driven by push and pull factors and by the wage gap between farm and city work, and increasingly by natural increase inside cities. Cities raise productivity through agglomeration and reshape society, families and health, but only deliver their benefits when housing and services keep up.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 3 in 10 people were urban in 1950. The majority was passed around 2007.
  • UN projections have pointed to about two-thirds urban by 2050.
  • Over a billion people live in informal settlements.
The common mistake

Assuming all cities grow mainly through rural migrants. In many fast-growing cities, births to existing residents account for roughly half or more of growth, so policies that only target migration miss the point.

Can you explain why a dense city can have lower emissions per person than a suburb, even though cities produce most emissions overall? If you can, you understand agglomeration and density together.

The words, in plain words
Urbanization
The rise in the share of people living in urban areas, and the social change that comes with it.
Urban area
A built-up settlement defined as urban by a country's own rules, which vary widely.
Degree of Urbanisation
A harmonised international method that classifies land as city, town or rural by density and size.
Push and pull factors
Reasons that drive people out of one place and draw them to another.
Natural increase
Population growth from births exceeding deaths, without migration.
Reclassification
People becoming "urban" because a boundary or a place's status changes.
Agglomeration
The economic gains from people and firms being close together.
Suburbanization
The growth of lower-density housing on the edges of cities.
Counter-urbanization
Movement from big cities to small towns and the countryside.
Informal settlement
A neighbourhood built without legal title or planning, often lacking basic services.
Urban penalty
The historical pattern of cities being less healthy than the countryside.
Urban heat island

Gentrification
The arrival of wealthier residents in a poorer area, raising prices and changing its character.
Sprawl
Low-density, car-dependent growth spreading out from a city.
