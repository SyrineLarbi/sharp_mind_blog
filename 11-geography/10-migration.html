Migration. The oldest human strategy, and one of the most argued-about.

Every person alive descends from people who moved. Humans left Africa, crossed continents, settled islands across thousands of kilometres of ocean, and kept moving after they settled. Today migration is one of the most heated subjects in politics, and the argument is often conducted with very few facts. This file sets out the facts first: who moves, why, how many, how states decide who may enter, and what research says about the effects on the places people leave and the places they arrive. Then it looks at the parts that are genuinely disputed, and why.

Question 1 · What is it?

Moving home across a boundary, for a long time

Migration means changing where you usually live, across a line that matters: a border, or the boundary between a village and a city. It comes in many kinds, and the words for them carry legal weight.

Chapter 01

One person in thirty

The usual image of migration is of huge crowds on the move. The numbers tell a quieter story. The United Nations counted about 280 million international migrants, people living in a country other than the one they were born in, in 2020. Its 2024 estimate passed 300 million. That is roughly one person in thirty, a share that has risen only slowly over decades. In other words, about 96 or 97 people in every hundred live in the country where they were born.

Far more people move inside their own country. China alone has close to 300 million rural people working in its cities. Across the world, internal migrants outnumber international ones several times over. When people picture "migration" they usually picture crossing borders. Most migration in the world is a young person leaving a village for a city in the same country. The topic on urbanization covers that side in more depth.

Chapter 02

Irregular migrantSomeone who entered or stays without legal permissionOften called "illegal" or "undocumented"; the word chosen usually signals a political position

The single most important rule in refugee law is non-refoulement, from the French for "not pushing back". A state must not return a person to a country where they face serious threats to life or freedom. It does not give anyone the right to choose which country protects them, and it does not cover people fleeing poverty alone. Real cases are messier than the categories: a person may flee war and also hope for work. These mixed flows are where most legal and political fights happen.

UNHCR, the UN refugee agency, counted more than 110 million people forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024 and 2025, the highest figures it has recorded. Most of them were displaced inside their own country. Of those who crossed a border, most stayed in a neighbouring country. Türkiye, Iran, Colombia, Germany and Uganda have been among the largest hosts.

Chapter 03

A very old habit

Migration built the modern world map. Between the 1820s and the 1920s, by common estimates more than fifty million Europeans crossed the Atlantic, mainly to the United States, Canada, Argentina and Brazil, pushed by rural poverty and famine and pulled by land and factory jobs. Millions of Indian and Chinese labourers went to plantations and mines across the British Empire and the Americas. Between about 1910 and 1970, around six million African Americans left the rural South for northern and western cities in what is called the Great Migration. After 1945 Western Europe recruited workers from Turkey, North Africa, Southern Europe and former colonies to rebuild its industries.

Several of today's migration patterns run along these old channels. People from former colonies still go disproportionately to the former colonial power, because of shared language, legal ties and family already there: Algerians to France, South Asians to Britain, Surinamese to the Netherlands. Migration flows have memory.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Why people go, how they choose, and who lets them in

Individual decisions are shaped by wage gaps and danger, by family networks, by money and by the rules of the country at the other end.

Chapter 04

Push, pull and the obstacles in between

In the 1880s the geographer Ernst Georg Ravenstein studied British census records and proposed "laws of migration". Several still hold: most migrants travel short distances, many move in steps (village to town, town to city, city to abroad), and every flow creates a smaller counter-flow of people going back. In 1966 the American demographer Everett Lee turned this into a simple model that is still the starting point in textbooks.

The biggest single pull is the wage gap. The same worker, with the same skills, can earn several times more by doing the same job in a richer country. Economists have measured this for workers who move from countries such as Haiti, Nigeria or Bolivia to the United States, and the gain is often larger than any education or development programme could achieve at home. That gap, more than any policy, explains why people take huge risks to move.

Chapter 05

Networks, and the migration hump

Push and pull explain why people want to move. They do not explain why migrants from one small region of Mexico end up in one particular town in Illinois. The answer is networks. The first migrants are pioneers and take the biggest risks. They send back information, money for tickets and offers of a bed and a job. Each new migrant makes the next move cheaper. Over time a diaspora, a community living outside its homeland but still connected to it, forms its own shops, churches, mosques and associations.

One finding surprises almost everyone. The poorest people rarely emigrate far. They cannot afford the journey. As a poor country grows richer, emigration usually rises at first, because more people can pay for it, and only falls once incomes reach middle-income levels. This pattern is called the migration hump, associated with research by the economist Michael Clemens among others. It means that aid and development in poor countries do not quickly reduce emigration, and may increase it for a while. The exact shape of the hump is still debated.

Chapter 06

How states sort people

Every state decides who may enter, for how long, and with what rights. The main tools are similar everywhere, though the mix varies a great deal.

Points systems

Canada introduced a points system in 1967, and Australia and others followed. Applicants score points for age, education, language and job offers. The aim is to select for skills rather than nationality.

Family routes

The largest share of permanent immigration to the United States has long come through family ties: spouses, children, parents and siblings of citizens and residents.

Temporary labour schemes

The Gulf states rely heavily on temporary workers, often tied to an employer-sponsor under the kafala system. In the United Arab Emirates and Qatar, foreign nationals make up a large majority of residents. Reforms in recent years have loosened some rules.

Free movement zones

Within the European Union, citizens of member states may live and work in any other. Similar but more limited arrangements exist in parts of West Africa and South America.

Asylum works differently. A person cannot apply for refugee status from home. They must usually reach the territory of the country where they ask for protection. This creates the central dilemma of asylum policy. Wealthy states have signed a promise to protect refugees who reach them, and have also built systems to stop people reaching them. Visa requirements, airline checks and agreements with transit countries all serve that second aim.

Chapter 07

The money that flows back

Migrants send money home. These transfers are called remittances, and they are far larger than most people realise. According to the World Bank, remittances to low- and middle-income countries were around 650 to 700 billion US dollars a year in the mid-2020s. That is more than those countries received in foreign aid and, excluding China, more than in foreign direct investment. India is the largest recipient, at well over 100 billion dollars a year, followed by Mexico. In some smaller economies, such as Tajikistan, Nepal and Tonga, remittances equal a fifth or more of national income.

Remittances have a useful quality: they go straight to households, and they often rise when the home country suffers a disaster, because relatives abroad send more. They pay for food, school fees, medicine and new houses. Their cost has been a target of policy. Sending money abroad through traditional channels can still cost several percent of the amount sent, and mobile money services have pushed fees down in some corridors.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Effects at both ends, and the argument in the middle

Migration changes labour markets, public budgets, families and identities. Some effects are well measured. Others are disputed, and it helps to know which is which.

Chapter 08

What happens to wages: the Mariel debate

In 1980 about 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami within a few months during the Mariel boatlift, increasing the city's labour force by several percent almost overnight. It was a natural experiment. In 1990 the economist David Card compared Miami with similar cities and found little effect on the wages or employment of low-skilled workers already there. The study became one of the most cited in the field.

In 2017 the economist George Borjas reanalysed the same event, focused on a narrower group of low-skilled men, and found a large fall in their wages. Others then argued that his sample was too small and that changes in the survey data explained the result. The dispute is still alive. The broad reading of the research is that average effects of immigration on native wages are small, often close to zero, but that some groups, especially earlier immigrants and workers without qualifications who compete directly with newcomers, can lose out in the short run.

Fiscal effects, meaning what migrants pay in taxes compared with what they receive in services, depend mostly on age and skill. A working-age, employed migrant usually pays in more than they take out. Older or low-earning migrants, or refugees who cannot yet work, cost more at first. Studies across OECD countries generally find the net fiscal effect to be small in either direction.

Chapter 09

Brain drain, brain gain

For countries of origin, the worry is brain drain: the loss of doctors, nurses, engineers and teachers whom the country paid to train. Several countries in sub-Saharan Africa have a large share of their trained doctors working abroad, while their own hospitals are short-staffed.

The picture is more complicated than pure loss. The chance of working abroad encourages more people to train in the first place, and not all of them leave. The Philippines turned this into national policy. It trains far more nurses than it needs and manages the export of workers through a dedicated government department, and the remittances are a pillar of its economy. Emigrants who return bring skills and capital. Diasporas invest and open trade links, and India's software industry grew partly through ties with Indians in Silicon Valley. Whether the gains outweigh the losses depends on the country and the profession, and researchers disagree.

Chapter 10

The social side: integration, trust and politics

Migration changes who lives next to whom. Societies have handled that in different ways. Assimilation expects newcomers to adopt the host culture; France's republican model, which avoids official recognition of ethnic groups, leans this way. Multiculturalism accepts and sometimes supports distinct communities; Canada adopted it as official policy in 1971. Most countries mix the two, under the broad label of integration.

On the outcomes, some things are well established. Children of immigrants usually close much of the gap with natives in language and education by the second generation, though outcomes differ by group and country. Residential concentration in poorer districts can slow that progress. Whether diversity lowers social trust is contested. The political scientist Robert Putnam reported in 2007 that, in the short run, Americans in more diverse areas trusted their neighbours less. Later studies found the effect weaker, mixed, or tied more to poverty and segregation than to diversity itself.

Politically, migration became one of the defining issues of the 2010s and 2020s across Europe and North America. In 2015 more than a million people sought asylum in the EU, many fleeing the war in Syria, and German Chancellor Angela Merkel told Germans "Wir schaffen das", "we can manage this". Immigration was central to Britain's 2016 vote to leave the EU and to many election campaigns since. A fair summary is that most economists see modest net gains, while many voters weigh other things too: the speed of change, control over who comes, and pressure on housing and services locally. Both are real concerns, measured in different ways.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Gains that are spread out, costs that are concentrated

The overall effects of migration are usually positive. The distribution of those effects is what drives the argument.

Chapter 12

What research settles, and what it does not

Fairly well established

Migrants gain greatly. Average effects on native wages are small. Working-age migrants are net contributors in most rich countries. Remittances reduce poverty in origin countries. Second generations largely catch up. Development raises emigration before it lowers it.

Genuinely disputed

Effects on the lowest-paid native workers. Long-run effects on social trust. How much border enforcement deters arrivals compared with conditions at home. Whether brain drain harms poor countries overall. How large the gains from much freer movement would really be.

A useful habit in any migration debate is to ask three questions. Which kind of migration is being discussed: skilled, family, asylum or irregular? Over what time scale: the first year or the first generation? And for whom: the country on average, or one town or one group of workers? Many disagreements disappear, or become clearer, once those three are fixed.

Chapter 14

The likely shape of the next decades

The basic forces are not mysterious. Sub-Saharan Africa will have the world's largest growth in young adults this century, while Europe and East Asia age. Wage gaps between rich and poor countries remain large. Transport and information are cheaper than ever. All of that points to continued pressure to move. Against it stand political demands in destination countries for tighter control, which have produced stricter asylum rules, deals with transit countries and more enforcement in many places since the mid-2010s.

The likely compromise, already visible, is more selection and more temporary status: more workers admitted for specific jobs and fixed periods, fewer automatic routes to settlement, and asylum systems under constant strain. Technology may also allow some "virtual migration", in which a worker in Manila or Lagos does a job for a firm in London without moving. That can change who needs to move, but it cannot do a nurse's or a builder's work. For the long-run population forces behind all of this, see Future population trends.

The core idea

Migration is a long-term change of home across a border or within a country. It is driven by wage gaps, danger and networks, filtered by state rules, and returns money home as remittances. Its gains are large and widely spread; its costs are smaller but concentrated, which is why it is politically explosive.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About one person in thirty is an international migrant (over 300 million, 2024).
  • Over 110 million forcibly displaced (UNHCR, 2024–25), most inside their own country.
  • Remittances to poorer countries: roughly $650–700 billion a year, more than aid.
The common mistake

Assuming that the poorest people migrate most, and that development aid will quickly reduce emigration. Emigration usually rises as a poor country gets richer, before it falls.
