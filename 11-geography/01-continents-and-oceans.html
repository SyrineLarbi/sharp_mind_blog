Sharp Mind · Geography · Topic 01 of 20

Continents and oceans. Slow rafts of light rock on one connected sea.

School gives you a tidy list: seven continents, five oceans. The list is useful, and it hides almost everything interesting. The number of continents is a convention that changes from country to country. The oceans are really one body of water with five names. And the continents themselves are not fixed places at all. They are thick slabs of light rock that drift, collide and split, and their shapes have decided where ocean currents run, where ice sheets grow, and which peoples met which crops first.

Question 1 · What is it?

Seven pieces of land, one ocean, and a lot of convention

The basic facts are simple. The way we divide them is partly science and partly habit, and it helps to know which part is which.

Chapter 01

How many continents? It depends on your school

Ask a child in Britain, the United States or China how many continents there are and you will hear seven: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America and South America. Ask a child in much of Latin America, Spain or France and you may hear six, because America is taught as one continent. In some traditions Europe and Asia are joined as Eurasia. The five rings of the Olympic flag stand for five inhabited continents, with the Americas counted as one and Antarctica left out because nobody lives there permanently.

None of these answers is wrong. A continent is defined partly by geology and partly by history. Geologically, Europe and Asia sit on one continuous landmass. There is no ocean between them. The line usually drawn along the Ural Mountains, the Ural River and the Caucasus was fixed by European scholars over several centuries, mostly for cultural reasons. Meanwhile the Americas are joined only by the narrow isthmus of Panama, and Africa touches Asia only at the Sinai, which the Suez Canal now cuts.

So when someone says "Europe is a continent", they mean something closer to "Europe is a civilisation that we decided to treat as a continent". That is a perfectly reasonable thing to do. It is just worth knowing that it is a decision.

Chapter 03

One ocean with five names

Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface. Unlike the continents, the oceans are all connected. A drop of water off Lisbon can, in principle, reach Tokyo without touching land. The names divide one world ocean into regions.

  • Pacific. The largest by far. At around 165 million km² it covers more area than all the land on Earth put together. It also holds the deepest point, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, close to 11,000 metres down.
  • Atlantic. About two-thirds the Pacific's size. It is S-shaped and still widening, by a few centimetres a year.
  • Indian. Closed to the north by Asia, which is why its winds and currents flip with the seasons. Topic 02 explains the monsoon this produces.
  • Southern. The ring of water around Antarctica. Many geographers long treated it as the southern ends of the other three. National Geographic began mapping it as a fifth ocean in 2021. Its defining feature is a current that flows all the way around the planet without hitting land.
  • Arctic. The smallest and shallowest, mostly enclosed by North America, Europe and Asia, and covered by floating sea ice that shrinks every summer.

The average depth of the ocean is about 3.7 kilometres. That number is worth pausing on. If you could smooth Earth into a perfect ball, the sea would cover everything to a depth of more than two kilometres. Dry land exists only because some rock stands higher than other rock. The next part explains why.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Why there is land at all, and why it keeps moving

Continents and ocean floors are made of two different kinds of rock. That one difference explains why the land stands high, why it is old, and why the sea floor is young.

Chapter 04

Two kinds of crust

Earth's outer skin, the crust, comes in two types. Under the oceans it is oceanic crust: dark, heavy rock called basalt, only about 7 kilometres thick. Under the continents it is continental crust: lighter rock rich in granite, usually 30 to 50 kilometres thick, and up to about 70 kilometres under Tibet.

Think of blocks of wood and blocks of ice floating in a bath. The lighter, thicker blocks ride higher. In the same way, continental crust floats higher on the hot mantle below because it is less dense. Geologists call this balance isostasy. It is why continents stand above sea level and ocean basins sit low enough to collect water. Mountains have deep "roots" of crust beneath them for the same reason an iceberg is mostly underwater.

The density difference has a striking consequence for age. When two plates collide, the heavier oceanic plate is the one that bends down and sinks back into the mantle. This sinking is called subduction. So the sea floor is constantly destroyed and remade. The oldest ocean floor still in place is only around 180 to 200 million years old. Continental crust, too light to sink, survives. Some rocks in northern Canada are about four billion years old, and tiny mineral grains from Western Australia are older still. The land is ancient. The sea floor is young.

Chapter 05

Supercontinents come and go

Because plates move a few centimetres a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow, continents rearrange themselves over hundreds of millions of years. Volume 30 explains the engine. Here is the result. About 300 million years ago almost all land was joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by a single vast ocean. Starting around 200 million years ago it broke apart. The pieces drifted into the map we know, and the gap between the Americas and Europe-Africa became the Atlantic.

Pangaea was not the first. Geologists have reconstructed earlier supercontinents, most confidently one called Rodinia, about a billion years ago. The pattern seems to repeat: land gathers, the supercontinent traps heat beneath it, rifts open, and the pieces scatter before gathering again. This is sometimes called the supercontinent cycle. The broad idea is widely accepted. The details of the older supercontinents are much less certain, because the evidence has been folded, eroded and buried many times.

See it on any map

The Red Sea and the lakes of East Africa's Rift Valley are places where a continent is being pulled apart right now. If the process continues for millions of years, East Africa may separate from the rest of the continent and seawater may flood the gap, much as it once flooded the young Atlantic.

The continental shelf is the drowned edge of the continent, usually less than about 200 metres deep. It is where most fishing, most offshore oil and gas, and most sea life are. Then the continental slope drops steeply to the abyssal plain, a deep, cold, dark floor several kilometres down. Running through every ocean is the mid-ocean ridge, where plates pull apart and new crust forms. Joined end to end, it stretches about 65,000 kilometres, making it the longest mountain range on Earth, almost entirely underwater. Where plates sink, the floor plunges into narrow trenches, the deepest places on the planet.

Chapter 07

Where the land is decides where the water goes

Winds push the surface of the sea, and Earth's spin bends that motion. On an ocean with no land the currents would run in simple bands. Continents get in the way. They turn the currents into huge loops called gyres, which turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern. The Gulf Stream, the warm current that softens the winters of north-western Europe, is the western edge of the North Atlantic gyre, squeezed against the coast of North America.

Two events in the deep past show how much the arrangement of land matters.

Antarctica cut loose

Tens of millions of years ago, Australia and South America pulled away from Antarctica. That opened a clear path for water to circle the pole. The resulting current helped insulate Antarctica from warmer water, and the continent froze into the ice sheet we see today. Scientists still debate how much of the cooling came from the current and how much from falling carbon dioxide.

Panama closes the gap

A few million years ago the isthmus of Panama rose and joined North and South America. Atlantic and Pacific water could no longer mix there. Currents reorganised, and animals walked across in both directions, an event biologists call the Great American Interchange. Armadillos went north. Big cats and deer went south.

The lesson is that continents are not just where people live. They are the walls that steer the ocean, and the ocean steers the climate.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Shapes that shaped history, and a sea that runs the planet

The layout of land and sea is the stage on which every human story was performed. It decides how far crops and ideas could spread, how goods move, and how much heat the planet can absorb.

Chapter 08

Does the direction of a continent matter?

In his 1997 book Guns, Germs, and Steel, the geographer Jared Diamond made a famous argument. Eurasia stretches mostly east to west. The Americas and Africa stretch mostly north to south. Places at the same latitude tend to share day length, seasons and climate. So, Diamond argued, a crop first grown in one part of Eurasia, such as wheat, could spread thousands of kilometres along the same latitude without needing to adapt. In the Americas, a crop moving from Mexico to the Andes had to cross deserts and tropics, and spread far more slowly.

The argument is elegant and has influenced a generation of readers. It is also contested. Many historians and anthropologists say it gives geography too much weight and human choices, institutions and accidents too little. Some argue it can be read as making historical outcomes look inevitable. A fair summary is that geography loaded the dice without deciding the game. Most scholars accept that the shape and climate of continents affected how fast farming spread. They disagree sharply about how much that explains later inequality. Topic 12 on geographic inequality returns to this debate.

Chapter 09

The ocean is the world's highway

For most of history, moving goods by water was far cheaper than moving them over land. A single ship could carry what hundreds of animals could not. That is still true. By volume, roughly four-fifths of world trade travels by sea, much of it in steel containers on ships that carry thousands of them at once.

Because continents are shaped the way they are, this traffic funnels through a handful of narrow passages: the Strait of Malacca between Malaysia and Indonesia, the Strait of Hormuz at the mouth of the Gulf, the Suez and Panama canals, the Bab el-Mandeb at the southern end of the Red Sea. When one of them is blocked, as when the container ship Ever Given stuck in the Suez Canal for six days in 2021, the effects reach supermarket shelves on other continents. Volume 12 covers these pinch points in detail (Container shipping, and the six narrow places).

Who owns the sea? The main answer comes from the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, agreed in 1982. A coastal country controls its territorial sea out to 12 nautical miles (about 22 kilometres). It has rights to fish and drill in an exclusive economic zone out to 200 nautical miles. Beyond that are the high seas, which belong to no one. These lines turn tiny islands into large claims of ocean, which is one reason islands are argued over so fiercely. Topic 11 on borders looks at those disputes.

Chapter 10

The ocean is the climate's shock absorber

Water stores heat far better than air or rock. The top few metres of the ocean hold about as much heat as the whole atmosphere. This makes the ocean the planet's great buffer. Since the 1970s it has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases. It has also taken up roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide humans have emitted.

That buffering has slowed warming on land, and it has a price. Warmer water expands, which raises sea level. Carbon dioxide dissolved in seawater makes it slightly more acidic, which makes it harder for corals, shellfish and some plankton to build their shells. And marine heatwaves, periods of unusually hot sea water, bleach coral reefs and push fish towards the poles.

The ocean also breathes for the planet. Microscopic floating plants called phytoplankton carry out a large share of all photosynthesis on Earth. Estimates commonly put it at around half. Every second breath you take may owe its oxygen to the sea. That is one reason the health of the open ocean, far from anyone's coast, matters to everyone.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Useful categories, distorted pictures

The continent-and-ocean framework is a tool. Like any map, it helps you see some things by hiding others.

Chapter 12

The map in your head is the wrong size

Every flat map distorts a round planet. The most familiar one, the Mercator projection, was designed in 1569 for sailors. It has a valuable property: a straight line on it is a constant compass heading. To achieve that, it stretches everything near the poles. On a Mercator map Greenland looks about as big as Africa. In reality Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. Alaska looks far larger than Mexico. In fact Mexico is slightly bigger. Europe looks bigger than South America. It is only a little over half its size.

Other projections make different trade-offs. Equal-area maps, such as the Gall-Peters projection that became famous in the 1970s and 1980s, show true sizes but squash shapes. Compromise projections, such as the Robinson or Winkel tripel, accept a little error everywhere so that nothing is badly wrong. There is no perfect flat map. Topic 18 on geographic information systems covers how modern digital maps handle this.

A quick fix

When you compare the size of two places, use a globe, or an online tool that lets you drag one country's outline over another. The surprise you feel the first time you drag Greenland onto Africa is a direct measure of how much Mercator has shaped your picture of the world.

Chapter 14

A continent under water, and an unfinished map

In 2017 a group of geologists argued in a scientific paper that a large region of continental crust around New Zealand, mostly under the sea, qualifies as a continent in its own right. They called it Zealandia. Only about 6% of it is above water, mainly New Zealand and New Caledonia. Many geologists find the case convincing. But there is no official body that votes on continents, so whether school maps ever show an eighth one is a matter of convention, not discovery. It is a neat reminder of Chapter 01: continents are partly what we decide to call them.

The bigger surprise is how little of the sea floor we have actually measured. We have detailed maps of the surface of the Moon and Mars. Yet by the mid-2020s only around a quarter of Earth's ocean floor had been mapped in high resolution by ships using sound. The rest is known mainly from satellite measurements of the sea surface, which reveal large features but miss smaller ones. An international project called Seabed 2030 aims to map the whole floor by the end of this decade. Whether it gets there or not, most of the solid surface of our own planet is still less well mapped than the far side of the Moon.

The core idea

Continents are thick slabs of light, ancient crust that float high on the mantle. Ocean floors are thin, heavy, young crust that is constantly made at ridges and destroyed in trenches. The arrangement of land steers the ocean, the ocean steers the climate, and both shaped human history.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The ocean covers about 71% of the surface and averages about 3.7 km deep.
  • The Pacific alone is larger than all land combined.
  • The ocean has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat from global warming.
The common mistake

Believing the map you grew up with shows true sizes. On the usual Mercator map, Greenland looks like Africa. Africa is about fourteen times larger.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the oldest rocks on Earth are on land, not on the sea floor? If you can, you understand density, isostasy and subduction together.

The words, in plain words
Continent
A large area of thick, light crust, plus the islands usually grouped with it. Where one ends is partly convention.
Crust
The thin, rigid outer skin of Earth, made of oceanic and continental types.
Oceanic crust
Thin, dense crust of basalt under the sea. Young, because it is recycled.
Continental crust
Thick, lighter crust rich in granite. Old, because it is too light to sink.
Isostasy
The balance by which crust floats on the mantle, lighter and thicker parts riding higher.
Subduction
The sinking of one plate under another back into the mantle.
Supercontinent
A time when most land is joined in one mass, such as Pangaea.
Continental shelf
The shallow, drowned edge of a continent, usually under about 200 metres of water.
Mid-ocean ridge
The underwater mountain chain where plates pull apart and new sea floor forms.
Gyre
A huge circular system of ocean currents, shaped by winds, Earth's spin and the continents.
Exclusive economic zone
The sea out to 200 nautical miles where a coastal country controls fishing and resources.
Map projection
A method of flattening the round Earth onto a flat map, always with some distortion.
