A cultured mind · Volume 19 of 54 · history

The world opens. Printing, painting, and the ships that joined two halves of a planet.

In the space of about a century, Europe acquired a machine that could copy a book faster than a man could read it, rediscovered how to paint a face that looks back at you, and sailed west into an ocean nobody had crossed. Two of those things are usually told as triumphs. The third was a catastrophe for the people on the other side, and any honest account has to hold both.

Part I

The machine

Chapter 01

What a book cost before Gutenberg

To own a book in 1400 you needed a scribe to copy one, by hand, for months. A Bible might take a skilled man the better part of a year. The materials alone — prepared animal skin, enough for hundreds of pages — could mean a small herd.

So a book cost roughly what a house cost. Libraries were counted in dozens of volumes, not thousands, and chained to the desk. Almost nobody outside the church and the very rich had ever held one. And every copy was slightly different from every other copy, because human beings copying by hand make mistakes, and then the next scribe copies the mistake.

Around 1450 a goldsmith in Mainz put together several existing things — a screw press of the kind used for wine, oil-based ink, and above all metal type cast so precisely that individual letters could be assembled into a page, printed hundreds of times, then taken apart and used again for a different page.

That last part is the invention. Not printing — the Chinese had printed for centuries, and Korea had metal type before Europe did. The trick was the combination, in a place with an alphabet of two dozen letters rather than thousands of characters, at a moment when paper had arrived from the east and was cheap.

The number that tells the story

Within fifty years of Gutenberg's press, there were printing shops in more than two hundred European cities and something like twenty million books in circulation — in a continent that had possessed a few tens of thousands. The cost of a book fell by a factor of several hundred. Nothing comparable happens again until the internet.

Chapter 02

What cheap copying did to authority

The obvious consequence was more readers. The deeper consequence was that it became impossible to control what people read.

In 1517 a German monk nailed — or more likely posted — a list of ninety-five complaints about church practice to a door in Wittenberg. Men had written such complaints before and nothing had happened to them, or something had happened to them quietly. This time the list was set in type, and within weeks it was being read across Germany, and within months across Europe. By the time the authorities decided what to do about Martin Luther, tens of thousands of people had already read him.

The Reformation that followed split western Christianity permanently and set off more than a century of war. And it rested on a printing argument: Luther translated the Bible into German so that ordinary people could read it themselves rather than be told what it said. Once a text is cheap and in a language you speak, the person who used to interpret it for you has lost their monopoly.

Every information technology since has produced a version of this fight. The pattern is always the same — a drop in the cost of copying, a collapse in someone's authority, and a period of chaos while a society works out what it now believes and whom it now trusts.

Part II

The rebirth

Chapter 03

Why Italy, why then

The Renaissance is usually described as the rediscovery of classical learning, which is true and slightly misleading, since the classics had never been entirely lost — they had been in Arabic, in Byzantine libraries, in monastery copies.

What changed was the conditions, and they are the same four conditions as Baghdad. Money: Italian city-states had grown extremely rich on Mediterranean trade and banking. Patrons: families like the Medici in Florence had more money than any use for it and discovered that commissioning art and scholarship bought something money cannot otherwise buy, which is prestige. Competition: Florence, Venice, Milan and Rome were rivals, and a rival's cathedral is an argument you have to answer. And incoming ideas: Greek scholars arriving from a collapsing Constantinople, carrying manuscripts and the ability to read them.

What came out was not only beautiful but technically new. Painters worked out linear perspective — the geometry that makes a flat surface look like a room you could walk into — and it is genuinely a discovery, not a style. Anatomists cut up bodies to find out what was actually inside, which the Church disliked and did not entirely prevent. Leonardo filled notebooks with observations of water, birds, muscles and machines, most of it unpublished, much of it centuries early.

Chapter 04

The quiet revolution in how to know things

Alongside the art, something less visible was happening that mattered more in the long run.

The medieval way of settling a question about the natural world was to find what an authority had said about it — usually Aristotle — and reason from there. It is not a stupid method. Aristotle was extremely clever and had thought about almost everything.

The new method was to go and look, and then let anybody else look too. Copernicus suggested the Earth went round the sun, which contradicted both Aristotle and the obvious evidence of standing still. Galileo pointed a new instrument at the sky and saw moons going round Jupiter — objects plainly not orbiting the Earth — and published in Italian rather than Latin so that ordinary educated people could read it. He was tried, made to recant, and spent his last years under house arrest.

The Church's objection is often caricatured. The deeper conflict was about who is entitled to settle a question, and the new answer — that a measurement anybody can repeat outranks any authority, including a very great one — is the single most consequential idea in this entire volume. Everything in Volumes 25 to 30 depends on it.

Part III

The ships

Chapter 05

Why anyone would risk it

Sailing west into the Atlantic was not a romantic impulse. It was a business decision forced by the previous volume's last chapter.

Spices from Asia — pepper, cloves, nutmeg — were worth extraordinary sums in Europe, both as preservatives and as the conspicuous luxury of the age. The overland routes now ran through Ottoman territory, and every intermediary took a cut. If you could reach Asia by sea you would cut out all of them.

The Portuguese tried going round Africa, methodically, over decades, and eventually succeeded. Columbus proposed going west instead, on the basis of a calculation that was simply wrong — he had badly underestimated the size of the Earth, and the experts who told him so were right. He sailed anyway, and survived only because there happened to be an entire unsuspected landmass in the way.

He died still insisting he had reached Asia.

What made it possible at all

Not courage — instruments and hull design. The magnetic compass and the astrolabe, both arriving via the Islamic world, let a ship know roughly where it was with no land in sight. The caravel could sail closer to the wind than earlier ships, which meant you could get home again, and a voyage you cannot return from is not exploration but suicide.

Chapter 06

The other side of the ocean

Here the story turns, and it should be told plainly.

Within about thirty years of contact, the two largest states in the Americas had been destroyed by expeditions of a few hundred Spaniards. It is tempting to explain this by steel and horses and guns, and those mattered — but not nearly enough to account for it.

Two things account for it. The first is that both empires ruled over subject peoples who hated them, and those peoples supplied tens of thousands of allied troops. Cortés did not defeat the Aztecs with six hundred men; he defeated them with six hundred men and an enormous coalition of their enemies.

The second is disease, and it is the overwhelming factor. Smallpox, measles and influenza had circulated in Europe, Asia and Africa for thousands of years, and their populations carried inherited resistance. The Americas had been separated for far too long. The diseases arrived into populations with no immunity whatsoever, and they ran ahead of the Europeans themselves — reaching and emptying places before any European was seen.

The scale is almost impossible to take in. Estimates vary, but the mainstream range is that the indigenous population of the Americas fell by something like ninety per cent within a century. It is probably the largest demographic catastrophe in human history, and most of it was not intended by anyone, which does not make it an accident — the conquest and the enslavement and the forced labour that followed were entirely deliberate, and they fell on societies already devastated.

Chapter 07

The trade that followed

The Americas had land and minerals and, after the collapse, almost nobody to work them. The solution Europeans arrived at was to bring people by force from Africa.

Over roughly three and a half centuries, something in the order of twelve million people were shipped across the Atlantic in chains, and perhaps one and a half million died on the voyage. It is the largest forced migration in recorded history.

The system that grew around it was brutally efficient and is worth understanding as a system: manufactured goods from Europe to West Africa, enslaved people from Africa to the Americas, and sugar, tobacco and cotton from the Americas back to Europe. Every leg was profitable. The wealth it generated built ports, banks and industries in Britain, France, Portugal and the Netherlands, and a good deal of the capital that later financed industrialisation came from it.

It also required a justification, because Europeans of the period were perfectly capable of recognising cruelty. The justification constructed to meet that need — an elaborate account of inherited human difference, invented largely after the fact to excuse a trade that was already enormously profitable — is where a great deal of modern racism actually comes from. It is not an ancient prejudice that produced the slave trade. It is substantially the other way round.

Why this chapter is in the volume about opening

Because it is the same event. The voyages that produced the exchange of crops, the mapping of the globe and the beginnings of a single connected world economy are the voyages that produced the American demographic collapse and the Atlantic slave trade. They are not a triumph with a regrettable footnote. They are one process with two faces, and a cultured person should be able to hold both without flinching from either.

Chapter 08

What crossed in both directions

One consequence was so large and so quiet that people rarely notice it: the world's food changed completely, everywhere, within a few generations.

Potatoes and maize went east from the Americas. Both grow in conditions where wheat fails, and both yield far more calories per acre. European and Chinese populations began rising in a way they had not for centuries, and the Irish became so dependent on a single crop that its failure three hundred years later would kill a million people.

Tomatoes went to Italy, chillies to India and Sichuan, cacao to Switzerland, vanilla everywhere. Almost every national cuisine you can think of acquired its defining ingredient in this exchange. There was no tomato in Italian cooking before 1500, and no chilli in a curry.

Going the other way: wheat, sugar, coffee, horses, cattle — and the diseases of the previous chapter.

It is worth sitting with the strangeness of this. The food on your table is an artefact of the sixteenth century. Nothing else in this volume touched as many lives as directly, and almost nobody who caused it understood what they were doing.

Reference

What to carry forward

The machine

Movable type made copying cheap, and cheap copying destroys monopolies on interpretation. The Reformation is the first example; every information technology since has produced its own version of the same fight.

The method

The real Renaissance revolution was not the art but the answer to who settles a question. The new answer — a measurement anyone can repeat outranks any authority — is what the science volumes are built on.

The conquest, honestly

Not steel and horses. Local allies who hated their rulers, and above all disease — an indigenous population collapse of roughly ninety per cent, much of it running ahead of the Europeans themselves. Then twelve million people shipped from Africa in chains, and a theory of human difference invented afterwards to excuse it.

The quietest consequence

Potatoes, maize, tomatoes, chillies, cacao going one way; wheat, sugar, coffee, cattle the other. Every cuisine on Earth was rewritten within a few generations, and nobody involved understood that they were doing it.
