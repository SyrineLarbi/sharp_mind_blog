Part I · Teaching the body

Two drugs that work by sending a message

Neither one attacks anything. Both work by giving the body an instruction it already knows how to follow.

How it works 01

mRNA vaccines

In one sentence

An mRNA vaccine sends your own cells a recipe, and your cells make the practice target themselves.

Think of it like…

Older vaccines show your immune system a photograph of the criminal. A dead virus, a weakened one, or one piece of one — grown in a factory, purified, injected.

An mRNA vaccine does not bring the photograph. It brings the instructions for drawing it, and your own cells do the drawing.

That is the whole difference, and it is why these could be designed in days rather than years. You are not growing anything. You are writing a message.

How it works
① FIRST, WHAT mRNA IS
   Your DNA is the master copy of every recipe your body
   knows. It stays locked in the nucleus at the centre of
   the cell, and never leaves.
   When a cell needs to make something, it copies out ONE
   recipe onto a short, disposable working note and sends
   that to the factory floor.
   That note is mRNA. Messenger RNA.
   ⇒ YOUR CELLS DO THIS MILLIONS OF TIMES A SECOND, ALL
     DAY, YOUR WHOLE LIFE. It is not exotic. It is the
     most ordinary process in biology.

② SCIENTISTS WRITE ONE RECIPE
   For COVID: the recipe for the "spike", the knobbly
   protein on the outside of the virus. That one piece.
   Not the virus.

③ ⭐ THE HARD PART — GETTING IT INSIDE A CELL
   Naked mRNA in your blood is destroyed in minutes. It
   is MEANT to be disposable.
   So it is wrapped in a microscopic bubble of fat: a
   LIPID NANOPARTICLE.
   ⇒ THE FAT BUBBLE IS THE INVENTION. Decades of work
     went into it. The mRNA part was comparatively easy.
     Almost every story about this gets it backwards.

④ INJECTED INTO THE MUSCLE
   The fat bubble merges with a cell's outer membrane —
   because that membrane is also made of fat — and drops
   the recipe inside.

⑤ THE CELL READS IT AND MAKES THE SPIKE
   Which on its own is harmless. It is a shape. It cannot
   infect anything. It is one bolt from a car.

⑥ THE IMMUNE SYSTEM SEES THE SHAPE AND LEARNS IT
   Antibodies, memory cells, the usual response.

⑦ THE mRNA IS DESTROYED WITHIN DAYS
   ⚠ AND IT NEVER ENTERS THE NUCLEUS. It cannot reach
     your DNA, and human cells have no machinery to write
     RNA back into DNA. That is not a reassurance, it is
     a structural fact about how a cell is built.
The numberWhat it means
The sequence was designed in about two daysOnce the virus genome was published in January 2020. The rest of that year was trials and manufacturing — not invention. That is the real significance of a platform technology.
-20°C to -70°CStorage. mRNA falls apart at room temperature. A logistics problem, not a safety one — and the reason the rollout was much harder in hot countries with weak cold chains.
The 2023 Nobel PrizeTo Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, for the finding that made it work at all: unmodified mRNA triggers a violent inflammatory reaction, and swapping one of its four building blocks for a slightly altered version avoids it. Karikó spent years unable to get funding for this work.
What goes wrong
  • The cold chain. The biggest practical limit, and the source of a lot of the global inequity in who got these first.
  • Side effects are real and mostly short. Sore arm, fever, tiredness for a day or two — that is the immune response, which is the entire point of the product. Rare myocarditis (inflammation of the heart muscle) occurred mainly in younger males, usually mild and self-limiting. Better to say that plainly than to dodge it: the risk was real, small, and smaller than the equivalent risk from the infection itself.
  • It needs a good target. The approach only works where there is one protein worth teaching the immune system about. For many diseases there is not.
  • The big bet now is cancer. Because swapping the recipe is easy, you can sequence a patient's own tumour, find the mutations unique to it, and write a personalised vaccine teaching the immune system to hunt exactly those. In trials, genuinely promising, not proven.
Say this

"An mRNA vaccine doesn't put the virus in you. It sends a recipe, and your own cells make one harmless piece for your immune system to practise on."

"The hard part was never the mRNA. It was the fat bubble that gets it into the cell."

"It can't touch your DNA. It never goes near the nucleus, and it's gone in days."

How it works 02

GLP-1 drugs — Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro

In one sentence

These are copies of a gut hormone that tells your brain you have eaten enough — redesigned to last a week instead of two minutes.

Think of it like…

Your gut already sends this message. When food arrives, it releases a hormone meaning roughly: enough, slow down, you are full.

The problem is that the natural message is deleted almost immediately — an enzyme in your blood chops it up within about two minutes. It was built to be a brief signal after a meal, not a mood.

These drugs are the same message, altered so the enzyme cannot cut it. One injection, and the message keeps arriving for a week.

How it works
① GLP-1 IS A REAL HORMONE YOU ALREADY MAKE
   Glucagon-like peptide-1, released by your gut when
   food arrives.

② IT DOES THREE THINGS AT ONCE
   ⓐ tells the pancreas to release insulin — but ONLY
     when blood sugar is actually high
     ⇒ which is why these rarely cause the dangerous
       low-blood-sugar episodes older diabetes drugs
       could
   ⓑ slows the stomach emptying, so food sits longer and
     you feel full longer
   ⓒ acts on the appetite centres in the BRAIN, so you
     want less
   ⇒ ⓒ IS THE ONE PEOPLE DESCRIBE AS LIFE-CHANGING.
     Patients often say the constant background noise
     about food simply goes quiet. That is a brain
     effect, not a stomach effect, and it is why this
     worked where appetite advice did not.

③ THE NATURAL VERSION LASTS ABOUT TWO MINUTES
   An enzyme called DPP-4 cuts it up.

④ THE DRUG IS BUILT TO SURVIVE
   Two changes: the cutting site is altered so the enzyme
   cannot grip it, and a fatty chain is added so it
   sticks to albumin, a protein already circulating in
   your blood. It rides along instead of being filtered
   out.
   ⇒ result: one injection a week instead of a hormone
     lasting minutes.

⑤ THE NEWEST ONES COPY TWO HORMONES
   Tirzepatide adds a second gut hormone, GIP. It appears
   to work better still, and nobody is entirely sure why.
The numberWhat it means
~15%, and ~20%+ of body weightAverage loss in trials over roughly a year and a half, for semaglutide and tirzepatide. Weight-loss drugs before these managed about 5%. That gap is why this became a global story.
Roughly a quarter to a third can be lean massOf the weight lost. A genuine concern, and why protein and resistance training are now part of serious treatment rather than an afterthought.
~20% fewer major cardiac eventsIn the SELECT trial, in people with obesity and existing heart disease. This is the result that moved these drugs from a cosmetic story to a cardiovascular one — the single most important number here.
What goes wrong
  • The weight comes back when you stop. In trials most of it returned within about a year. This is what most reporting gets wrong: it is a treatment, not a cure — the same relationship you have with blood pressure medication. Nobody expects blood pressure to stay down after stopping the tablets.
  • Nausea, vomiting and constipation are common, especially while the dose is being increased. Most people manage by going up slowly. Some cannot tolerate it at all.
  • Cost and supply. Shortages have been persistent, and the price puts them out of reach of most people who would benefit — a compounding unfairness, since obesity is more common in poorer populations.
  • The long-term data is still arriving. Enormous numbers of people are taking these for years, and some effects only appear at that scale. Not a reason for alarm; a reason to be honest that "we know the ten-year picture" is not yet a true statement.
  • And the genuinely new part: the effect is large enough that food companies have revised forecasts and the economics of bariatric surgery has shifted. A drug changing a food industry's outlook is unusual.
Say this

"It's a copy of a gut hormone that tells your brain you're full. The trick was making it last a week instead of two minutes."

"The weight comes back when you stop. It's a treatment, not a cure — same as blood pressure pills."

"It stopped being a cosmetic story when the heart trial came out. Twenty percent fewer cardiac events."

Part II · Editing and targeting

Three tools that built modern biology

Cutting DNA, reading it, and making molecules that stick to exactly one thing.

How it works 03

CRISPR

In one sentence

CRISPR is a pair of molecular scissors you can aim at one exact spot in DNA.

Think of it like…

Find-and-replace in a document.

Editing DNA was possible before CRISPR. But the "find" part meant custom-building a new protein for every single sequence you wanted to locate — months of laboratory work and a lot of money, for one target.

CRISPR made the "find" part programmable. You type the address. That is the whole revolution, and it is why this went from a 2012 paper to being in every biology lab in the world within about three years.

How it works
① IT IS BORROWED FROM BACTERIA
   Bacteria get attacked by viruses too. So they keep a
   scrapbook: when a virus attacks and the bacterium
   survives, it files a snippet of that virus's code
   into its own DNA.
   If the virus returns, the bacterium uses the filed
   snippet to recognise and cut it.
   ⇒ IT IS A BACTERIAL IMMUNE SYSTEM, running for about
     a billion years before anyone noticed. Those filed
     regions are what CRISPR is named after.

② TWO PARTS
   THE GUIDE    a short piece of RNA matching the DNA you
                want to find. THIS IS THE PART YOU WRITE
   THE SCISSORS a protein, Cas9, that does the cutting

③ THE GUIDE FINDS THE MATCH, CAS9 CUTS
   Both strands, at that exact spot.

④ ⭐ THEN THE CELL REPAIRS IT — AND WHICH REPAIR YOU GET
   DECIDES WHAT YOU CAN DO
   SLOPPY REPAIR   the cell glues the ends back and
                   usually loses or gains a few letters
                   ⇒ this BREAKS the gene. Useful to
                     switch something OFF. Easy, reliable
   TEMPLATE REPAIR supply a replacement sequence and the
                   cell may copy it in
                   ⇒ this EDITS the gene. Much harder,
                     much less efficient

⑤ THE NEWER VERSIONS ARE GENTLER
   BASE EDITING   changes one single letter chemically,
                  without cutting both strands
   PRIME EDITING  writes a short new sequence in place
   ⇒ both avoid the double-strand break, which is where
     most of the mess comes from. This is the direction
     everything is moving.
The numberWhat it means
2012 paper → 2020 NobelTo Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier. Eight years is extraordinarily fast, and it reflects how quickly the technique spread.
First approved CRISPR therapy: late 2023Casgevy, for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia. The method: take the patient's own bone marrow stem cells out, edit them in a dish to switch fetal haemoglobin back on, wipe the marrow with chemotherapy, put them back.
Roughly $2-3 million per patientAnd this, not the biology, is now the central problem. Sickle cell is most common in populations with the least access to a two-million-dollar therapy. A cure nobody can buy is a strange kind of cure.
What goes wrong
  • Off-target edits — cutting somewhere that resembles your target but is not it. Much improved with better guides and better scissors. Not zero, and hard to prove zero.
  • Delivery. Again. Exactly as with mRNA: getting the machinery into the right cells inside a living body is the unsolved part. Which is precisely why blood disorders came first — you can take those cells out, edit them on a bench where everything is easy, and put them back.
  • The germline question. Editing an embryo changes every cell of the resulting person and is passed to their children. Banned almost everywhere. He Jiankui did it anyway in 2018, producing edited babies, and went to prison. The line between treating a patient and editing the species is the defining ethical question of the field, and it has been deferred rather than resolved.
Say this

"CRISPR is find-and-replace for DNA. The breakthrough wasn't the cutting — it was making the 'find' part programmable."

"It's a bacterial immune system. Bacteria have used it for a billion years; we borrowed it in 2012."

"The first approved therapy cures sickle cell and costs about two million dollars. That price is the real problem now, not the science."

How it works 04

Monoclonal antibodies

In one sentence

A monoclonal antibody is one single immune molecule, chosen because it sticks to one exact target, then copied billions of times.

Think of it like…

A key ring with millions of keys.

Your immune system makes an enormous variety of antibodies, hoping one of them happens to fit whatever has invaded. It is brute force, and it works.

A monoclonal antibody is: find the one key that fits, throw away the rest of the ring, and manufacture billions of copies of that single key. Mono = one. Clonal = copies.

How it works
① AN ANTIBODY IS A Y-SHAPED PROTEIN
   The two tips of the Y are the variable part — the bit
   that grips one specific shape. The stem is the handle
   the rest of the immune system grabs.

② FIND OR DESIGN ONE THAT GRIPS YOUR TARGET
   A protein on a cancer cell. An inflammatory signal.
   A virus.

③ ⭐ GROW IT IN LIVING CELLS
   Usually Chinese hamster ovary cells, in enormous
   sterile tanks, for weeks.
   ⇒ THIS IS WHY THEY COST WHAT THEY COST. You are not
     doing chemistry, you are FARMING. An ordinary pill
     is a small molecule you synthesise in a vat. An
     antibody is a large, delicate protein that only
     living cells know how to fold correctly.

④ IT HAS TO BE INJECTED
   Swallow it and you digest it — because it is a
   protein, and that is what your gut does to proteins.

⑤ ONCE ATTACHED, IT DOES ONE OF THREE THINGS
   BLOCK    sit on a signal so it cannot be received
   FLAG     mark the cell so the immune system kills it
   DELIVER  carry a chemotherapy drug attached to it, so
            the poison only lands where the antibody
            sticks ("antibody-drug conjugate")

⭐ THE NAME TELLS YOU WHAT IT IS
  Look at the ending:
    -ximab  part mouse ("chimeric")
    -zumab  humanised
    -umab   fully human
  adalimumab · pembrolizumab · trastuzumab
  ⇒ a small thing, and suddenly a wall of drug names
    becomes readable.
The one to know about · checkpoint inhibitors

Some cancers survive by sending your immune system a "stand down" signal — pressing a brake your own T-cells are built to obey.

A checkpoint inhibitor does not attack the tumour at all. It is an antibody that sits on that brake so the signal cannot be received. Your own immune system, released, does the work.

This won the 2018 Nobel Prize for James Allison and Tasuku Honjo, and it genuinely changed outcomes in melanoma and some lung cancers — from months to, for some patients, years. It is the clearest example of a whole category: medicine that removes an obstacle instead of adding a weapon.

What goes wrong
  • Cost, driven entirely by the manufacturing. Typically thousands to tens of thousands a year.
  • And they do not go generic — they go "biosimilar". You cannot make an identical copy of a protein grown in living cells, only one that is highly similar and proven to behave the same. So prices fall far less than when an ordinary pill loses its patent. This is one of the quiet reasons health budgets are under pressure everywhere.
  • Your immune system can attack the drug, since it is itself a foreign protein — which is why the mouse-to-human progression in the naming above happened.
  • Checkpoint inhibitors release the brakes generally, not selectively. So a meaningful share of patients develop autoimmune side effects — inflammation of bowel, thyroid, lungs or skin. Often manageable, occasionally severe. The price of releasing an immune system is that it is released.
Say this

"A monoclonal antibody is one immune key, copied billions of times, that sticks to one exact target."

"They're expensive because you grow them in living cells. It's farming, not chemistry."

"Checkpoint inhibitors don't attack the tumour. They take the brakes off your own immune system."

How it works 05

DNA sequencing

In one sentence

Sequencing means reading the order of the four letters DNA is written in.

Think of it like…

Reading a book of three billion letters with no spaces, no punctuation and no page numbers — by taking a thousand copies of it, shredding all of them, and reassembling the text from where the pieces overlap.

That sounds absurd and it is exactly what happens. It works because you have a thousand copies shredded differently, so every join is covered by some other fragment.

How it works · the industrial standard, and the interesting alternative
SHORT READS — the dominant method
  ① chop the DNA into millions of short fragments
  ② stick them to a glass slide, and copy each fragment
    many times ON THE SPOT, making a little cluster of
    identical copies
    ⇒ because one molecule is too dim to see. A thousand
      copies is bright enough to photograph
  ③ add ONE DNA letter at a time. Each of the four
    carries a different coloured fluorescent tag, and a
    chemical block stopping a second letter attaching
  ④ PHOTOGRAPH THE WHOLE SLIDE. Every cluster flashes
    the colour of the letter it just took
  ⑤ chemically remove the block and the tag
  ⑥ repeat, about 150 times
  ⑦ a computer lines the millions of short reads up
    against a reference and reconstructs the sequence
  ⇒ extremely accurate, extremely cheap per letter, and
    bad at long repetitive stretches — a short read from
    inside a repeat could have come from anywhere in it

LONG READS — nanopore, the one that sounds invented
  Pull a single strand of DNA through a hole in a
  protein, one letter at a time, and measure the tiny
  change in electrical current as each letter squeezes
  through. Each letter disturbs the current differently.
  ⇒ reads thousands of times longer, which solves the
    repeat problem
  ⇒ and the device can be the size of a USB stick —
    which means sequencing in a field hospital, a
    jungle, or on the space station. All three have
    happened.
The numberWhat it means
13 years and ~$3 billionThe first human genome, finished 2003. An international effort involving thousands of people.
About a day, and a few hundred dollarsThe same job today. That fall is steeper and longer-sustained than the famous one in computing — and almost nobody outside the field knows it happened.
3 billion letters, ~20,000 genesYour genome. Only about 1-2% of it directly codes for proteins; most of the rest is regulation, structure, and material we understand poorly.
What goes wrong
  • Reading is solved. Understanding is not. We can read everything and interpret a modest fraction. The most common result of a genetic test is a "variant of uncertain significance" — we found a difference, we do not know whether it matters. That helps nobody and frightens some people, and it is far more common than television suggests.
  • Privacy, and it is not like other privacy. Your DNA identifies you permanently and cannot be changed after a leak. It also identifies your siblings, parents and children, none of whom consented. Consumer genetics companies have been sold, have gone bankrupt with the database as an asset, and have handed data to law enforcement.
  • Reference bias. The reference genomes everything was compared against were built largely from people of European ancestry, which quietly made results less accurate for everyone else. Now being fixed with "pangenome" references covering many populations — a good example of a technical problem that was really a sampling problem.
Say this

"The first human genome took thirteen years and three billion dollars. Now it's a day and a few hundred."

"Reading DNA is solved. Understanding it isn't — most findings come back as 'we don't know what this means'."

"Your DNA is the one password you can never change. And it isn't only yours — it's your family's."

Part III · The hard problems

Three things that will not be solved soon

One is economic, one is statistical, one is a shortage. None of them is mainly a science problem, which is what makes them interesting.

How it works 06

Antibiotic resistance

In one sentence

Bacteria evolve to survive our antibiotics, and we are not making new ones fast enough.

Think of it like…

A lock where the locks change themselves.

Every time you use an antibiotic you kill the bacteria it works on. The handful that happened to survive are now the entire population — and they breed every twenty minutes.

This is not a flaw in the drug, and it is not misuse alone. It is evolution doing precisely what evolution does, on a clock fast enough to watch. Resistance was always coming. The only questions were how fast, and what we would have ready.

How it works · and the part that makes it dangerous
① IN ANY LARGE POPULATION, A FEW ARE ACCIDENTALLY
   DIFFERENT
   A pump that happens to eject the drug. An enzyme that
   happens to destroy it. A slightly changed shape the
   drug no longer fits.

② THE ANTIBIOTIC KILLS EVERYTHING ELSE
   Which is a wonderful outcome for those few survivors.
   They now have the whole territory and no competition.

③ ⭐ AND HERE IS WHAT MAKES IT DANGEROUS
   BACTERIA SWAP GENES SIDEWAYS.
   Resistance genes sit on small loose rings of DNA
   called PLASMIDS, and bacteria hand these to each
   other — INCLUDING ACROSS DIFFERENT SPECIES.
   ⇒ so resistance does not only pass down from parent
     to offspring. It spreads across the room. A
     harmless gut bacterium can hand a resistance gene
     to a dangerous one.
   ⇒ this is why resistance appears faster and in more
     places than simple inheritance predicts, and it is
     the most under-known fact in this entry.

④ TWO DRIVERS, AND ONE IS BIGGER THAN PEOPLE THINK
   HUMAN USE  unnecessary prescriptions, unfinished
              courses
   FARMING    enormous routine use in healthy farmed
              animals, for growth and to prevent disease
              in crowded conditions
   ⇒ in many countries, more antibiotics by weight go
     into animals than into people.
The numberWhat it means
~1.3 million deaths a yearDirectly attributable to resistant infections, with several million more associated, on the most-cited global estimate. Already one of the larger causes of death worldwide — and it does not feel like one, because the deaths get attributed to the underlying illness.
Genuinely new classes are rareMost recently approved antibiotics are variations on existing families, so resistance to them arrives faster. There have been gaps of decades between genuinely new classes for some bacteria.
The market is upside downA brilliant new antibiotic is used sparingly, for short courses, and held in reserve precisely because it is brilliant. So the better the drug, the less money it makes. Several companies have gone bankrupt shortly after getting one approved. This is the core of the problem, and it is not a science problem.
What goes wrong
  • The broken market above. Fixing it means paying differently — subscription models where a health system pays to have the drug available rather than per dose. A few countries are trying it. Unglamorous, and the actual answer.
  • Agricultural use is politically difficult to reduce and enormously effective to reduce.
  • And here is what people miss about the stakes. Antibiotics are not only a treatment for infections. Surgery, chemotherapy, transplants, intensive care and childbirth all depend on being able to prevent infection reliably. Losing antibiotics does not mean going back to 1930 — it means much of modern medicine becoming unsafe. That is the real argument, and it is far stronger than the one usually made.
  • Alternatives are promising and not ready. Bacteriophages (viruses that eat bacteria), antibodies, better vaccines. All real, none at scale.
Say this

"Resistance isn't a failure of the drug. It's evolution working exactly as expected, on a fast clock."

"Bacteria swap resistance genes sideways, between species. That's why it spreads faster than you'd expect."

"The problem isn't mainly science, it's economics. A great new antibiotic is one you're told not to use."

How it works 07

Medical imaging, and diagnostic AI

In one sentence

Four different ways of seeing inside a body, and software trained to spot patterns in what they show.

Think of it like…

Four different senses, not four versions of the same camera. Each sees something the others cannot.

X-ray and CT are shadows — dense things block the beam. MRI is a magnet nudging the water in you and listening to it settle. Ultrasound is echo, exactly like a bat. PET watches where sugar goes, because busy tissue is hungry tissue.

Knowing which is which explains most of what you hear about waiting lists, costs and radiation.

The four, and what each is for
X-RAY AND CT · a shadow
  X-rays pass through you; dense tissue absorbs more, so
  bone is white and air is black. A CT takes hundreds of
  X-rays from all around and computes cross-sections.
  ✓ fast, cheap, available, excellent for bone, bleeding
    and chest
  ⚠ uses ionising radiation — a real, small, cumulative
    risk. Which is why nobody scans "just to check"

MRI · a magnet, and water
  A very strong magnet lines up the hydrogen atoms in
  your body's water. A radio pulse knocks them out of
  line. As they settle back they emit a faint signal —
  and DIFFERENT TISSUES SETTLE AT DIFFERENT RATES, which
  is where the contrast comes from.
  ✓ no radiation, extraordinary soft-tissue detail —
    brain, spine, ligaments, tumours
  ⚠ slow, deafening, expensive, impossible with some
    metal implants

ULTRASOUND · echo
  Send sound pulses in, listen for the echoes, time them.
  ✓ cheap, portable, real-time, no radiation, safe in
    pregnancy
  ⚠ cannot see through bone or air, and quality depends
    enormously on the skill of the person holding it

PET · activity, not structure
  Inject sugar tagged with a weak radioactive tracer.
  Metabolically busy cells take up more — and tumours
  are busy.
  ✓ shows what is ACTIVE rather than what is SHAPED,
    which is why it is used for staging cancer
  ⚠ expensive, and the tracer must be made nearby
    because it decays within hours
And the AI part — where it works, and how it actually fails
WHERE IT WORKS TODAY, IN REAL CLINICS
  · screening eyes for diabetic damage — huge volume of
    routine images, one clear finding
  · triaging mammograms so suspicious ones are read
    first
  · spotting a large stroke on a CT within seconds, so
    the patient goes straight to treatment. MINUTES ARE
    BRAIN TISSUE HERE — this one saves real function
  · flagging a collapsed lung on a chest X-ray

⚠ HOW IT FAILS — specific documented failures, not
  general worries

  DISTRIBUTION SHIFT
    A model trained at one hospital, on one brand of
    scanner, with one population, often degrades badly
    somewhere else. The maths did not change. The
    pictures did.

  ⭐ SHORTCUT LEARNING — the famous one
    Models have been found keying on things that
    CORRELATE with the answer rather than the disease.
    The best-known example: a chest X-ray model that had
    partly learned to spot the marker left by a PORTABLE
    scanner — because sicker patients, who cannot walk
    to the radiology department, get portable scans.
    ⇒ it was not detecting illness. It was detecting
      "too ill to walk". It scored brilliantly until it
      met a different hospital.

  AUTOMATION BIAS
    Show a human a confident machine answer and they
    become less likely to disagree — including when the
    machine is wrong. So a model right 95% of the time
    can make the whole system WORSE, if the remaining
    5% now go unchallenged.

  THE REGULATORY GAP
    Approval mostly asks "does the model perform?"
    The question that matters is "does DOCTOR PLUS MODEL
    beat DOCTOR?"
    ⇒ different studies. The second is much rarer.
And one cost nobody counts

Better imaging finds more things. Many are harmless — a small nodule, a cyst, something that would never have caused trouble. But once seen it must be followed up: more scans, a biopsy, months of worry, occasionally a complication from a procedure that was never needed.

This is overdiagnosis, and it is a genuine harm of a genuinely good technology. Detecting more is not automatically helping more, and anyone who discusses screening without mentioning it is giving you half the picture.

Say this

"CT is a shadow. MRI is a magnet nudging the water in you. Ultrasound is echo. PET watches where the sugar goes."

"The question about medical AI isn't whether it beats a radiologist in a test. It's whether radiologist-plus-AI beats radiologist — and that's a different study, and it's rarer."

"One famous model turned out to be detecting portable scanners, not disease. Sicker patients get portable scans."

How it works 08

Transplants, and pig organs

In one sentence

Moving a working organ from one body into another — and, now, from a pig.

Think of it like…

An ID badge system with no exceptions.

Your immune system checks every cell it meets for a badge. Your own cells carry your badge and are waved through. A donated organ carries someone else's, and the immune system does exactly what it is built to do: attacks it.

Every single thing in transplant medicine is about managing that one fact. The surgery, remarkably, is the easier half.

How it works
① THE BADGE HAS A NAME — HLA
   A set of proteins on your cell surfaces. The closer
   the match, the less violent the attack. Siblings
   often match well. Strangers rarely match perfectly.

② MATCH, THEN CROSSMATCH
   Matching compares badges. Crossmatching mixes the
   recipient's blood with the donor's cells to check the
   recipient does not ALREADY have antibodies waiting —
   from a pregnancy, a transfusion, or a previous
   transplant.

③ THE CLOCK STARTS THE MOMENT IT COMES OUT
   Flushed with cold preservation fluid and chilled.
   Rough limits:  heart and lungs   4-6 hours
                  liver             8-12 hours
                  kidney            up to 24-36 hours
   ⇒ which is why transplant logistics involves police
     escorts and chartered aircraft, and why there is a
     geographic radius around every organ.

④ ⭐ MACHINE PERFUSION — the quiet recent change
   Instead of putting the organ on ice, keep it WARM and
   pumped with oxygenated blood or fluid in a machine.
   Three consequences, all large:
     · more time
     · you can ASSESS it — watch a liver make bile,
       watch a kidney make urine — before committing
     · you can sometimes IMPROVE a marginal organ
   ⇒ organs that would have been discarded get used. It
     is unglamorous and it is one of the biggest
     practical advances in the field.

⑤ THEN IMMUNOSUPPRESSION, FOR LIFE
   Drugs holding the immune system back permanently.
   Which means permanently raised risk of infection and
   of some cancers.
   ⇒ THAT IS THE TRADE. A working organ, and a
     deliberately weakened immune system, for life.
Pig organs · xenotransplantation

Pigs are roughly the right size, breed quickly, and are already farmed at scale. The idea is old. What changed is gene editing — because there were three specific obstacles, and CRISPR made it possible to remove them.

  • A sugar called alpha-gal sits on pig cells. Humans have strong pre-existing antibodies against it, so a pig organ was destroyed within minutes. Edited out.
  • Viruses written into the pig genome itself (porcine endogenous retroviruses), which cannot be bred out because they are part of the DNA. Edited out.
  • Slower rejection, and a mismatch in how pig and human blood clotting interact. Human genes added to compensate.

The first pig heart into a living person was in 2022; the patient survived about two months. Pig kidneys into living patients followed. This is early, working better than anyone expected a decade ago, and not yet a routine option. One of the fastest-moving areas in medicine right now, and worth watching.

What goes wrong
  • The shortage is the whole problem. Waiting lists exceed donors everywhere and people die waiting. Every other issue is downstream of this one.
  • Opt-out systems help less than people claim. Countries with presumed consent generally do better, but the effect is smaller than the headline suggests — Spain's famously high rates owe more to having a trained transplant coordinator in every hospital than to the legal default. The law gets the credit; the infrastructure does the work. That is a pattern worth recognising well beyond transplants.
  • Xenotransplantation carries an unknown infection risk — a virus crossing from pig to human, in a patient whose immune system has deliberately been switched off, deserves the caution it gets.
  • And allocation is a genuinely hard ethical question. Who gets the organ: the sickest, the one who will live longest with it, the one who waited longest, the youngest? Every answer is defensible and every answer is unfair to somebody. There is no clean solution, and it is worth knowing that before having a confident opinion about it.
Say this

"Your immune system checks every cell for an ID badge. A transplanted organ has someone else's badge — that's the entire problem, and the surgery is the easy half."

"They're putting pig organs into people now. They edited out the sugar we reject and the viruses in the pig's own DNA."

"Spain's transplant rate isn't about the opt-out law. It's about a trained coordinator in every hospital."

The words, in plain words

When they say…They mean
DNA / RNADNA is the master copy, locked in the nucleus. RNA is a disposable working copy sent out to be used.
Gene / genomeA gene is one recipe. The genome is the whole cookbook.
ProteinWhat recipes make. Proteins do essentially all the work in your body — structure, signals, enzymes, antibodies.
In vitro / in vivoIn glass (a dish) / in a living body. An enormous number of exciting results are in vitro, and most do not survive the journey.
Phase 1 / 2 / 3Trial stages. Phase 1 = is it safe, a few dozen people. Phase 2 = does it seem to work, a few hundred. Phase 3 = does it really work, thousands, against a comparison. Most drugs die in phase 2.
Randomised controlled trialPeople assigned by chance to treatment or comparison, so the groups differ only by luck. Without it you cannot tell a real effect from the fact that people who choose a treatment differ from those who do not.
Double blindNeither patient nor doctor knows who got which — because both unconsciously shade their judgement if they do.
PlaceboA dummy treatment. The point isn't that placebos are powerful — it's that getting better anyway is powerful, and you need a comparison to see past it.
Relative vs absolute risk⭐ The most important one here. "Halves your risk" is relative. If the risk was 2 in 10,000 and is now 1 in 10,000, that is a 50% reduction and also almost nothing. Headlines use relative; decisions need absolute.
Efficacy vs effectivenessHow well it works in a trial, versus how well it works in real life with real people who miss doses.
Biologic / biosimilarA drug grown in living cells / a near-copy of one. Not a generic, because an exact copy isn't possible.
Off-labelPrescribing an approved drug for something it wasn't approved for. Legal, common, and how a lot of medicine actually advances.

Your ten sentences

If you learn nothing else, learn these
1"An mRNA vaccine doesn't put the virus in you. It sends a recipe and your own cells make one harmless piece of it."
2"The hard part was never the mRNA. It was the fat bubble that gets it into the cell."
3"GLP-1 drugs copy a gut hormone that tells your brain you're full. The weight comes back when you stop — it's a treatment, not a cure."
4"CRISPR is find-and-replace for DNA. The breakthrough was making the 'find' part programmable."
5"Checkpoint inhibitors don't attack the tumour. They take the brakes off your own immune system."
6"Antibodies are expensive because you grow them in living cells. It's farming, not chemistry."
7"Reading DNA is solved. Understanding it isn't — most results come back as 'we don't know what this means'."
8"Bacteria swap resistance genes sideways, between species. And the problem isn't science — a great antibiotic is one you're told not to use."
9"The question about medical AI isn't whether it beats a radiologist. It's whether radiologist-plus-AI beats radiologist."
10"Halving a risk sounds enormous until you ask what the risk was. Relative for headlines, absolute for decisions."
