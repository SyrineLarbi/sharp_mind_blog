Part I

How scams actually work

Chapter 01

The shape they all share

Strip away the story and almost every scam has the same four-part structure. Learn the structure and you stop needing to recognise each individual variant, which matters because the variants change weekly and the structure has not changed in a century.

A trigger that produces emotion. Fear — your account has been compromised, there is a warrant, your child is in trouble. Or greed — a refund, a prize, an investment. Or affection. The emotion is not incidental; it is the mechanism, because feeling strongly is what stops the slow, deliberate thinking from Volume 35 engaging at all.

Authority or familiarity. Your bank, the tax office, the police, a delivery company, your boss, your child. Something that carries the right to ask you for things.

Urgency. Now, today, within the hour, or something bad happens. This is the load- bearing element and it is the one thing present in essentially every scam ever run — because the scam cannot survive you thinking about it, checking with someone, or sleeping on it.

An unusual channel or method. A transfer to a new account, a gift card, a cryptocurrency payment, a code read over the phone, an app installed so they can help you. The method is always one that is fast and irreversible, and that is not a coincidence.

One rule that defeats most of them

Hang up and contact them yourself, using a number or address you already had. Not the number they gave you, not a link in the message, not the number that appeared on your screen — caller ID is trivially faked. Use the number on your bank card, on the official website you navigated to yourself, or a contact you already stored.

A genuine organisation will never mind you doing this. Objecting to it is itself diagnostic. And no real institution loses anything if you take twenty minutes.

Chapter 02

The ones worth recognising by name

The bank or fraud-department call. Someone says your account is compromised and you must move your money to a "safe account" immediately. There is no such thing as a safe account, and no bank will ever ask you to move money to one. This variant takes very large sums from careful people, because it arrives dressed as protection.

The tax, police or immigration threat. A debt, a warrant, a visa problem, resolvable today by payment. Real authorities do not demand immediate payment by phone, do not accept gift cards or cryptocurrency, and do not threaten arrest to collect money.

The technical support call, or the alarming pop-up telling you your computer is infected and giving a number. The goal is remote access to your machine, which hands them your accounts. No company monitors your personal computer and calls you about it.

The invoice or payment-details change. A supplier or landlord emails to say their bank details have changed. This is enormously effective against businesses and individuals paying large sums, and the defence is a single phone call to a number you already had — never the one in the email.

The family emergency. A message from a new number: it's me, I've lost my phone, I need money urgently. Increasingly with a voice that sounds right, for reasons in Chapter 07. The defence is a question only the real person could answer, or calling their known number.

Romance and long-con investment fraud. Slow, patient, weeks or months of genuine- feeling attention before money is ever mentioned — and then an opportunity, often in cryptocurrency, on a platform that shows growing profits and permits small withdrawals early. The profits are pixels. These cause the largest individual losses and the deepest shame, which is why they go unreported.

Marketplace and delivery scams. An overpayment to refund, a missed-delivery fee, a buyer who wants to move off-platform. Small amounts, enormous volume.

Across all of them, one line is worth memorising. Nobody legitimate will ever ask you for a code that was sent to you. Not your bank, not the police, not a delivery company, not a colleague. That code is the last thing standing between someone and your account, and requesting it is proof of intent.

Chapter 03

Phishing, and what to actually look at

The old advice — look for spelling mistakes and bad grammar — is obsolete. Modern phishing is well-written, correctly branded, and often personalised with details taken from a data breach. Judging by appearance no longer works.

What still works is checking the things that cannot be faked convincingly.

The actual address behind a link. Hover, or long-press on a phone, and read where it goes — and read it from the right. The meaningful part of a web address is the bit immediately before the first single slash; everything to the left of that can be made to say anything at all. A link reading like your bank's name followed by a long string that ends somewhere else entirely is the most common shape.

The sender's real address, not the display name, which is free text anyone can set. Look for near-misses: a swapped letter, an extra word, a different ending.

Whether you expected it. An unexpected attachment, an unexpected invoice, an unexpected password reset. An unrequested password reset email is often the first sign that someone is trying to get into your account.

Whether it wants you to act fast. Same tell as everything else in this volume.

And a specific trap worth naming: search results and sponsored links. People searching for a company's support number or login page routinely land on a paid advertisement leading to a convincing fake. Navigate to important sites through a bookmark you made yourself, not through a search.

Part II

Securing things

Chapter 04

Passwords, and why the old advice was wrong

The familiar rules — a capital, a number, a symbol, changed every ninety days — were well intentioned and produced weak passwords, because they pushed people toward predictable substitutions and a single memorable base reused everywhere. The guidance has since changed.

Length matters far more than complexity. A long passphrase of unrelated words is both far harder to crack and far easier to remember than a short mangled word. Forced regular changes are no longer recommended, because they produce small predictable increments.

Reuse is the real vulnerability, and this is the point most people miss. When any service you use is breached — and they are, constantly — the stolen credentials are tried automatically against every other major service. One reused password turns one company's failure into the loss of your email, which is the key to everything else.

A password manager is the practical answer, and the common objection deserves a direct response. Putting everything in one place sounds worse and is much better: it means every account has a long unique password you never need to remember, which removes the failure mode that actually causes losses. The alternatives in practice are reuse or a notebook, and the manager beats both.

Protect the email account above all others. Almost every other account can be reset through it, which makes it the master key rather than just another account.

Chapter 05

Two-factor, and which kinds are worth having

A second factor means that knowing your password is not enough — something else is required. It is the single most effective thing you can switch on, and it is worth understanding that the kinds differ substantially in strength.

Codes by text message are the weakest form and still far better than nothing. The weakness is that a determined attacker can persuade a phone company to move your number to their device, after which the codes go to them. Worth using where nothing better is offered.

An authenticator app generating rotating codes on your device is meaningfully stronger, because nothing travels over the phone network. Keep the recovery codes somewhere safe and offline — losing the phone without them is a genuine problem.

A physical security key, or the equivalent built into a modern phone, is the strongest widely available option and has a property the others lack: it verifies the website you are on, so it simply will not authenticate to a convincing fake. That makes it the only form that defeats phishing outright rather than making it harder.

And the thing this all rests on, stated once more because it is where people lose: the code is never to be shared. A caller who has your password and needs only the code is one sentence away from your account, and that sentence is always some version of "read me the number we just sent".

Chapter 06

Privacy — what is actually collected

Most discussion of privacy is either alarmist or dismissive. The accurate picture is more specific and more useful.

The business model of most free services is attention sold to advertisers, and better targeting is worth more. So what is collected is whatever predicts behaviour: what you look at and for how long, what you buy, where you are and where you have been, who you are connected to, and what device you use. Location history is the most personally revealing of these by a wide margin — it shows where you sleep, work, worship and seek medical care, and it is difficult to anonymise meaningfully because your pattern of movement identifies you almost uniquely.

Tracking across sites happens through identifiers stored in your browser and, increasingly, through characteristics of your device and browser that together make you recognisable without storing anything at all. Which is why deleting cookies helps less than it used to.

The proportionate defences are few and genuinely effective. Review app permissions — particularly location, microphone, contacts and photos — and remove anything an app does not need for its actual function; a torch app does not need your contacts. Turn off location history where you do not need it. Use a browser or setting that blocks third-party tracking. Consider a separate email address for shopping and sign-ups. And be aware that uploading your contacts hands over other people's information, which they did not agree to.

A note on what a breach means for you: assume that the information you gave any company may eventually be public, and that its main use against you is making the scams in Part I more convincing. A caller who knows your address, your recent order and your account's last four digits is not thereby legitimate — those details are cheap.

And on public networks: the old advice that open wireless is dangerous has weakened considerably now that nearly all traffic is encrypted in transit. The remaining risks are worth knowing — a fake network named like the real one, and anything that warns you about a certificate — but a general fear of hotel wireless is out of date, and most consumer services sold to fix it oversell what they do.

Chapter 07

When you cannot trust your eyes or ears

Synthetic images, video and voice have crossed the threshold where casual inspection no longer settles the question. Voice in particular is now the practical problem: a short sample of someone speaking — from a voice message, a video, a meeting — is enough to generate convincing new speech, and this is already being used in the family-emergency scam.

Which means the old defence is gone. You cannot verify by listening, and the visual tells that circulate in advice articles — hands, teeth, blinking, lighting — are fixed as the systems improve and were never reliable on compressed video anyway.

What replaces it is not detection but verification through a second channel. If a voice asks for money or credentials, hang up and call the number you already have. Agree a family phrase in advance for exactly this situation. For a video call making an unusual request, verify through a different medium entirely.

The wider effect is worth naming too, and it runs in an unexpected direction. The real damage from convincing fakes is not only that false things are believed — it is that true things become deniable, because any genuine recording can now be dismissed as fabricated. The reasonable response is the one from Volume 40: ask where something came from, whether independent sources have it, and what would confirm it, rather than trusting or dismissing on appearance.

Chapter 08

If it has already happened

Speed matters, and shame is what prevents it. Worth saying plainly: victims of these scams are not stupid. They are targeted at scale by people who do this professionally, at a moment chosen for emotion, and the people who believe themselves immune are the easiest targets — which is the overconfidence finding from Volume 35, with a financial cost.

If money has gone: contact the bank immediately, because some transfers can be stopped or recalled within a short window, and report it to the relevant fraud authority. If credentials have gone: change that password, change it anywhere it was reused, and start with the email account. Check whether account recovery details have been altered — a changed recovery email or phone number is how access is kept after a password reset. If remote access was given, disconnect the device from the network and have it examined.

Then two follow-ups people skip. Expect a second approach — victim lists are resold, and a common follow-up is someone offering to recover your lost money for a fee. And tell someone, because the silence around this is precisely what keeps it working.

Reference

What to carry forward

The shape

Emotion, authority, urgency, and a fast irreversible payment method. Urgency is present in essentially every scam, because the scam cannot survive you thinking. Hang up and call back on a number you already had — objecting to that is itself the tell.

Two sentences to memorise

There is no such thing as a safe account, and no bank will ask you to move money to one. Nobody legitimate asks for a code that was sent to you — that request is proof of intent.

Securing it

Length beats complexity; reuse is the real vulnerability, because breaches are replayed everywhere. Use a manager, protect the email account above all, and turn on two-factor — a physical key or passkey is the only kind that defeats phishing outright.

Eyes, ears and aftermath

Voice can be cloned from a short sample, so verify through a second channel and agree a family phrase in advance. And if it happens: bank immediately, email account first, expect a follow-up scam offering to recover your money — and know that being targeted is not stupidity.
