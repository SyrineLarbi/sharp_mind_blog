Part I · The problem

Why reading the news does not work

And it genuinely does not. This is not a complaint about media bias. It is a complaint about shape.

Staying Updated 01

Why headlines don't work

In one sentence

News is shaped like events. The world is shaped like processes. That mismatch is why you can read the news every day for ten years and still not know how anything works.

Think of it like…

Watching one frame of a film every day — and a different film each time.

At the end of a year you have watched three hundred and sixty-five frames. You could not tell anyone what happened in a single one of those films, because you never saw two frames of the same one in a row.

That is the daily news, and it is not a criticism of journalists. It is what the format is for.

Five things the format does, structurally, regardless of who is writing
① IT SELECTS FOR WHAT CHANGED TODAY
   But most important things change SLOWLY. Aquifers
   emptying, a currency weakening, resistance spreading,
   a skill becoming obsolete.
   ⇒ so they are never today's news — until one day they
     are, and it is called a crisis, and it took thirty
     years.

② IT SELECTS FOR THE UNUSUAL
   That is what "news" means. Which systematically
   distorts your sense of how common things are — the
   availability effect from file 07. You will fear the
   rare vivid thing and ignore the common dull one.

③ THE HEADLINE IS OFTEN NOT WRITTEN BY THE REPORTER
   It is written to be clicked. It routinely overstates
   the article beneath it, and sometimes contradicts it.
   ⚠ so a headline you disagree with may sit above an
     article you would agree with entirely.

④ EVERYTHING IS PRESENTED AT THE SAME VOLUME
   A genuine breakthrough and a funding announcement get
   the same typeface and the same excitement. The format
   has no way to say "this one matters and this one does
   not".

⑤ ⭐ NOTHING IS EVER FOLLOWED UP
   The cure announced three years ago — what happened to
   it? The battery that would change everything?
   Nobody will tell you, because a follow-up is not
   news. So you accumulate hundreds of unfinished
   stories and never learn which ones were real.
   ⇒ THIS IS THE BIGGEST ONE. Your picture of the world
     is built almost entirely from beginnings, with the
     endings withheld.
Say this

"News is shaped like events. The world is shaped like processes. That's why reading a lot of it leaves you knowing very little."

"You only ever get the beginnings. Nobody publishes the follow-up, because a follow-up isn't news."

Staying Updated 02

Follow stories, not headlines

In one sentence

Pick four or five things you actually care about, and follow those over months.

Think of it like…

The difference between watching one football match and following a team for a season.

Same sport. Same ninety minutes. But if you have followed the team, you know why that substitution mattered, what the manager is under pressure about, and which of those players is playing for a transfer.

Everyone else is watching a match. You are watching a season. The information is the same — the context is what you built.

How to actually do it · the story file
① PICK 3 TO 5 — AND PICK STORIES, NOT TOPICS
   ✗ "water"              → a topic. Infinite. No end.
   ✓ "desalination in Tunisia" → a story. Actors, a
     timeline, and a question that will eventually be
     answered.
   ✗ "AI"
   ✓ "whether the EU AI Act actually gets enforced"
   ⇒ A STORY HAS AN ENDING IT IS HEADING TOWARDS. That
     is what makes it followable.

② ONE PAGE PER STORY, WITH FOUR SECTIONS
   ⓐ WHAT IS ACTUALLY HAPPENING — the mechanism, five
     lines. Not opinions. How the thing works.
   ⓑ WHO THE ACTORS ARE AND WHAT THEY WANT
     ⇒ "how is this person paid?" — the Munger question
       from file 07. It predicts behaviour better than
       anything about personality.
   ⓒ WHAT I EXPECT TO HAPPEN, AND HOW SURE I AM
     With a percentage. "I'm about 60% that the 2030
     target slips."
   ⓓ ⭐ WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND
     Written down BEFORE it happens.

③ ADD ONE DATED LINE WHEN SOMETHING HAPPENS
   Not an essay. A line and a date.

④ REREAD THE PAGE EVERY FEW MONTHS
   ⇒ AND SECTION ⓓ IS THE ONE THAT MAKES YOU HONEST.
     Without it you will quietly rewrite what you used
     to think. Everyone does. It is not dishonesty, it
     is memory — and a dated page is the only defence.

⭐ WHY THIS WORKS SO WELL
  After three months you know more about those four
  things than almost anyone you will meet. Not because
  you are cleverer. Because you were there for the whole
  season and they saw one match.
  ⇒ and four things is enough. You are not trying to
    know everything. Nobody knows everything. You are
    trying to know five things properly, which almost
    nobody does.
Say this

"I follow about four stories properly instead of skimming forty. After a few months you know more about those four than almost anyone in the room."

"I write down in advance what would change my mind. Otherwise I'd quietly rewrite what I used to think — everyone does."

Staying Updated 03

How to read a number

In one sentence

Most numbers in the news are technically true and arranged to mislead.

Think of it like…

A photograph. Everything in it is real. Somebody still chose where to stand, what to include, and what to leave just outside the frame.

Almost no statistic in the news is a lie. Almost every one is a choice.

Seven checks, in order of how often they catch something
① RELATIVE OR ABSOLUTE?
   "Doubles your risk" — of what, from what?
   2 in 10,000 → 4 in 10,000 is a doubling and is almost
   nothing.
   ⇒ headlines use relative because it is bigger.
     Decisions need absolute. (file 13)

② WHAT IS THE DENOMINATOR?
   "500 incidents last year" — out of how many? A large
   number with no base is not information, it is a
   feeling.

③ WHO COUNTED, AND WHY?
   A figure from an industry body about its own industry
   is not a lie and is not neutral. Neither is one from a
   campaign group. Both are selecting.

④ IS IT A RATE OR A LEVEL?
   ⚠ THE MOST COMMON CONFUSION IN ECONOMIC NEWS.
   "Inflation is falling" does NOT mean prices are
   falling. It means they are rising more slowly.
   Emissions growth slowing is not emissions falling.

⑤ COMPARED TO WHAT?
   "Record high" — against what baseline? Adjusted for
   inflation? For population? For the fact that we
   measure more things than we used to?
   ⇒ a "record number of cases" often means a record
     amount of TESTING.

⑥ IS IT A FORECAST WEARING A FACT'S CLOTHES?
   A number for 2040 is a guess with good formatting.
   Treat it as a scenario, never as a measurement.

⑦ DOES THE HEADLINE NUMBER MATCH THE ARTICLE'S NUMBER?
   Surprisingly often, it does not. Check.

⭐ AND THE ONE HABIT THAT DOES MOST OF THE WORK
  WHEN SOMEONE GIVES YOU A PERCENTAGE, ASK FOR THE TWO
  RAW NUMBERS IT CAME FROM.
  "Up 40%" — from what to what?
  ⇒ most misleading statistics simply do not survive
    that one question, and it is polite, and it works on
    your own reasoning too.
Say this

"When someone gives me a percentage I ask what the two numbers were. Most misleading statistics don't survive that."

"Inflation falling doesn't mean prices are falling. It means they're rising more slowly."

Staying Updated 04

Spotting hype

In one sentence

There is a short, reliable list of tells that separate a real advance from a press release.

Think of it like…

The difference between a recipe and a photograph of a meal.

The photograph proves someone made it once, beautifully, under perfect light, probably with professional help. The recipe tells you whether you could make it, in your kitchen, on a Tuesday, for a price you would pay.

Most technology announcements are photographs.

The hype checklist · eight questions
① IS THERE A NUMBER, WITH UNITS, AND A COMPARISON?
   Hype is adjectival. "Revolutionary", "game-changing",
   "unprecedented". Real work says "3.2 kWh per cubic
   metre, down from 10."

② LAB, OR PRODUCT?
   file 13's vocabulary generalises: IN VITRO means in a
   dish. Every field has its version — a demo, a pilot,
   a prototype, a single unit.
   ⇒ the gap between "works once in a lab" and "works
     every day at scale" is where almost everything dies.

③ WHAT SCALE? HOW MANY ORDERS OF MAGNITUDE FROM USEFUL?
   A breakthrough at milligram scale is not a
   breakthrough at tonne scale. Ask how many zeros are
   missing. Three is a hard decade. Six is a career.

④ WHO BENEFITS FROM YOU BELIEVING THIS?
   A company raising money. A university press office
   justifying a grant. A minister announcing.
   ⇒ none of them lying. All of them selecting. Again:
     how is this person paid?

⑤ HAS ANYONE INDEPENDENT REPRODUCED IT?
   One striking study is a hypothesis. (file 07)

⑥ IS THE WORD "COULD" DOING ALL THE WORK?
   "Could revolutionise" means "has not". Count the
   conditionals in the first paragraph.

⑦ ⭐ FOR ANYTHING INDUSTRIAL — IS THERE A FINAL
   INVESTMENT DECISION?
   ANNOUNCEMENTS ARE FREE. An FID means somebody
   actually committed the money.
   ⇒ a very large share of announced hydrogen, carbon
     capture and battery projects never reach FID and
     quietly disappear. This single filter will save you
     from most energy-transition hype, in both
     directions.

⑧ DOES IT QUIETLY VIOLATE SOMETHING THAT DOES NOT BEND?
   You cannot get more energy out than you put in. You
   cannot beat the thermodynamic minimum. You cannot
   separate something without spending energy.
   ⇒ perpetual motion arrives every few years in a new
     costume and better graphic design.

⭐ AND THE TIMESCALE RULE, WHICH PEOPLE GET WRONG
  CONSTANTLY
  SOFTWARE can go from prototype to millions of users in
  months.
  ANYTHING REQUIRING NEW PHYSICAL PLANT takes TEN YEARS
  OR MORE from working prototype to meaningful scale.
  Factories, permits, supply chains, trained people.
  ⇒ applying software timescales to steel, or steel
    timescales to software, is the single most common
    forecasting error there is — and it is made in both
    directions by serious people.
Say this

"Announcements are free. For anything industrial I wait for the final investment decision — that's when someone actually committed money."

"Software moves in months. Anything needing a new factory moves in decades. Most bad predictions mix those two up."

Staying Updated 05

Choosing sources

In one sentence

A small number of good sources beats a large number of fast ones.

Three, plus one
① ONE THAT REPORTS RATHER THAN REACTS
   Has its own correspondents, publishes corrections,
   and is willing to be slower than everyone else.
   ⇒ the correction policy is the tell. An outlet that
     never corrects anything is not more accurate.

② ONE SPECIALIST IN YOUR OWN FIELD
   ⭐ TRADE PRESS IS ENORMOUSLY BETTER THAN GENERAL
     PRESS ON ITS OWN SUBJECT, and almost nobody outside
     the industry reads it. It assumes you know the
     basics, so it skips the padding and goes to what
     changed. For supply chain, energy, shipping,
     pharma — the trade publication is where the actual
     information is.

③ ⭐ ONE THAT DISAGREES WITH YOU, READ CHARITABLY
   Not because balance is a virtue. For a practical
   reason: YOU CANNOT DEFEND A POSITION YOU HAVE ONLY
   EVER HEARD DEFENDED.
   ⇒ and read it looking for the strongest version of
     their argument, not the weakest. (The steelman,
     file 05 §06.)

④ AND PRIMARY SOURCES, WHEN IT MATTERS
   The actual report. The paper's own abstract. The
   company's own filing. The regulation's own text.
   ⇒ IT IS USUALLY ONE CLICK AWAY AND ALMOST NOBODY
     CLICKS IT. Doing so once on a story you care about
     is genuinely startling — the gap between what a
     report says and what is reported about it is often
     enormous, and not always in the direction you
     expect.

⚠ WHAT TO DISTRUST
  · aggregators with no reporters of their own
  · any piece where the headline and the body disagree
  · a single striking study, especially a small one
  · ⭐ YOUR OWN FEED. Algorithms optimise for
    engagement, and the most engaging emotion is anger.
    A feed is not a source. It is a slot machine that
    occasionally dispenses information.

⭐ AND BE HONEST ABOUT THIS ONE:
  EVERY SOURCE HAS A SLANT, INCLUDING THE GOOD ONES AND
  INCLUDING THE ONES YOU AGREE WITH.
  The aim is not to find a neutral source. There isn't
  one. The aim is to know which way each one leans and
  read accordingly — which is a skill, and it is
  learnable, and it is more useful than the search for
  neutrality.
Say this

"Three sources, and one of them disagrees with me. When it matters I read the actual report — it's usually one click away and almost nobody clicks it."

"Trade press is far better than general press on its own subject, and nobody outside the industry reads it."

What to actually do

Two habits and a schedule. Together they cost about an hour a week.

Staying Updated 06

The questions file

In one sentence

Write down every word you nodded at without being able to define.

How it works, and why it beats reading more
① SOMEONE SAYS A WORD YOU DO NOT ACTUALLY KNOW
   "basis points" · "feedstock" · "capacity factor" ·
   "Scope 3" · "FID" · "tokenisation" · "FOB" ·
   "working capital" · "the spread"

② YOU NOD. EVERYONE NODS.
   This is not a personal failing. Everyone in the room
   is nodding, and some of them do not know either.

③ WRITE IT DOWN. DO NOT LOOK IT UP IN THE MOMENT.
   Looking it up mid-conversation means you stop
   listening, and you will forget it by evening anyway.

④ LOOK UP TWO A WEEK.

⭐ WHY THIS BEATS SIMPLY READING MORE
  THE LIST IS GENERATED BY YOUR ACTUAL LIFE.
  Every single item on it is something you have already
  met and will meet again — otherwise it would not be on
  the list.
  ⇒ it is a personalised curriculum, built from the gaps
    you actually have, rather than from what somebody
    else thought you should know. No course can do that.

⇒ AND AFTER A YEAR IT IS THE BEST SOURCE OF
  CONVERSATION MATERIAL YOU OWN, because you learned
  each item at the precise moment it mattered — which is
  also why each one stuck.
Say this

"I keep a list of words I nodded at without knowing. Two a week. It's the best reading list I've ever had, because I didn't write it — my life did."

Staying Updated 07

Changing your mind cleanly

In one sentence

You will be wrong about some of this. The skill is updating without drama.

Think of it like…

A weather forecast.

Nobody is embarrassed that today's forecast differs from yesterday's. Yesterday's was the best estimate with yesterday's data. New data arrived. New forecast.

Nobody thinks the forecaster has lost credibility. They think the forecaster is doing their job. That is the entire attitude, and it is available to you.

Five things that make it easy
① HOLD OPINIONS WITH A NUMBER ATTACHED
   "I'm about 70% on this" is revisable without anyone
   losing anything.
   "This is obviously true" is not — and now changing
   your mind requires admitting you were foolish, which
   nobody wants to do. (file 07, Duke)

② ⭐ WRITE DOWN IN ADVANCE WHAT WOULD CHANGE YOUR MIND
   The hardest and most valuable habit here.
   Because when it happens, YOU HAVE ALREADY AGREED TO
   UPDATE. The argument with yourself took place months
   earlier, calmly, when nothing was at stake.

③ UPDATE OUT LOUD, ONCE, AND FLATLY
   "I thought X. The new data says otherwise. I now
   think Y."
   No apology, no performance, no extended explanation
   of how understandable the old view was.
   ⇒ the same shape as the public correction in file 04
     §06: flat, early, once, then move.

④ DO NOT LET THE BELIEF BECOME YOUR IDENTITY
   "I believe X" is cheap to revise.
   "I am an X person" is expensive, because now updating
   costs you something that has nothing to do with the
   evidence.
   ⇒ watch for the moment a position starts appearing in
     how you describe yourself. That is when it gets
     hard to drop.

⑤ KNOW THE ASYMMETRY, BECAUSE YOU CANNOT REMOVE IT
   You will examine evidence AGAINST your view far more
   carefully than evidence FOR it. Everyone does. It is
   not a character flaw and it does not go away with
   effort.
   ⇒ the only working defence is to know it, and to
     deliberately spend longer on the source that
     disagrees. Not to try to be unbiased. To
     COMPENSATE for a bias you know you have.

⭐ AND THE THING THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE
  SOMEONE WHO VISIBLY UPDATES IS TRUSTED MORE, NOT LESS.
  Because it means their agreement is contingent on
  evidence — which means when they DO agree with you, it
  is worth something.
  ⇒ if you never change your mind, your agreement is
    worthless. It was going to happen anyway.
Say this

"I try to put a number on an opinion. Seventy percent is something I can revise. 'Obviously' isn't."

"I thought X. The new numbers say otherwise. I think Y now." — said flatly, once, and then move on.

Staying Updated 08

The weekly system

In one sentence

About one hour a week, and it beats an hour a day of scrolling by a wide margin.

The whole system, on one card
ONE LONG READ            30-40 min
  One properly reported piece, start to finish, on
  anything at all. Not skimmed. Finished.

ONE MECHANISM            20 min
  One "how does this actually work". Files 11-15, a
  chapter of ../../culture/, or a good explainer.
  ⇒ ONE MECHANISM IS WORTH ABOUT A HUNDRED HEADLINES,
    because it explains all the headlines that come
    after it.

ONE CARD                 10 min
  Turn one of the two into a topic card — hook, middle,
  handoff — AND SAY IT OUT LOUD ONCE. (file 06)
  ⚠ THIS IS THE STEP EVERYONE SKIPS AND IT IS THE STEP
    THAT MAKES THE OTHER FIFTY MINUTES COUNT. Reading
    is input. Saying is stock.

PLUS, WHEN SOMETHING HAPPENS
  · one dated line in the relevant story file (§02)
  · two words from the questions file (§06)

⇒ TOTAL: ABOUT AN HOUR.

⭐ AND THE PERMISSION YOU PROBABLY NEED
  YOU ARE ALLOWED TO NOT FOLLOW MOST THINGS.
  Someone who understands eight systems properly is
  better company, and better informed, than someone who
  has skimmed four hundred stories.
  Depth in a few places plus honest ignorance elsewhere
  is a completely respectable position — and "I don't
  know anything about that, what's the shape of it?" is
  one of the better sentences available to you.
  (file 04 §05)

The words, in plain words

The vocabulary that appears in news about numbers, and that almost nobody explains.

When they say…They mean
Basis pointOne hundredth of a percent. "Fifty basis points" is half a percent. Used because "a quarter of a percent" is ambiguous about a quarter of what.
Year on yearCompared with the same month last year, not with last month. Avoids seasonal effects — December always beats November.
Seasonally adjustedThe predictable yearly pattern has been mathematically removed, so you can see the underlying trend. A real and necessary adjustment, and also somewhere assumptions hide.
Real vs nominal⭐ Adjusted for inflation, or not. "Record high wages" in nominal terms can be a fall in real terms. Always ask which one.
Median vs meanThe middle one vs the average. For anything with a long tail — income, house prices, waiting times — the median is far more honest, and the mean is often chosen for that reason.
Per capitaPer person. A big country doing a big thing is not news; a big country doing it per person might be.
CorrelationTwo things move together. That is all it means. It does not tell you which causes which, or whether a third thing causes both.
Statistically significantUnlikely to be pure chance. ⚠ It does not mean large, or important, or practically meaningful. A tiny effect can be highly significant in a big enough study.
Confidence intervalThe range the true answer probably sits in. When it is wide, the headline number is not really a number.
CAGRCompound annual growth rate — the smooth yearly rate that would get you from start to end. Hides everything that happened in between, including a crash.
FIDFinal investment decision. The moment a project stops being an announcement and becomes money. The most useful three letters in industrial news.
MoUMemorandum of understanding. A signed statement that two parties are interested. Almost entirely non-binding, and frequently reported as if it were a deal.
Order book / backlogWork that has been sold but not yet delivered. A good forward indicator, and one that can be inflated with soft orders.

Sharp Mind · everything, on one card
THINK (01-03)
  ⭐ SPEED IS RETRIEVAL, NOT PROCESSING
    The quick person pre-loaded. Name the CATEGORY of
    problem before answering. Pause three seconds, out
    loud.
  ⭐ THE BEST ANSWER BELONGS TO THE MOST SENIOR PERSON.
    THE BEST QUESTION IS AVAILABLE TO ANYONE.
    "What would have to be true?" · "What would make us
    regret this?" · and always climb from WHAT to SO
    WHAT.
  ⭐ ANSWER FIRST, THEN THE SUPPORT
    Two or three reasons, never seven. Say the structure
    before the content — it buys you nine seconds and
    sounds organised.

THE ROOM (04-06)
  ⭐ TWO AXES: WARMTH AND COMPETENCE
    Warmth is judged first. Capable people under-invest
    in it and land in the envied quadrant — respected,
    not trusted, not warned.
  ⭐ CUT THE PADDING, KEEP THE CALIBRATION
    Bounding what you know is precision. Apologising for
    saying it is cost.
    "I don't know" + what I'll do + WHEN.
  ⭐ EVERYONE IS ASKING THREE QUESTIONS
    Am I safe with you · do you see me · are you
    competent. Friction that doesn't add up is one of
    these.
  ⭐ BEING INTERESTING IS A SUPPLY PROBLEM
    Input is not stock. Nothing is stock until you have
    SAID IT OUT LOUD.

THE BOOKS (07-09)
  ⭐ Most non-fiction is one essay at three hundred
    pages. The outside view · resulting · kind vs wicked
    · "how is this person paid?" · impact not intentions
    · the third story.

THE WORLD (11-16)
  ⭐ MOST TECHNOLOGY STORIES ARE ENERGY STORIES
  ⭐ THE CHOKE POINT IS THE PROCESSING STEP, NOT THE
    RAW MATERIAL
  ⭐ THE CO2 OFTEN COMES FROM THE CHEMISTRY, NOT THE
    FUEL
  ⭐ EVERY DETECTOR CHOOSES WHICH ERROR TO MAKE
  ⭐ THE UNGLAMOROUS FIX IS USUALLY BEST AND NEVER
    GETS FUNDED
  ⭐ ONE MECHANISM IS WORTH A HUNDRED HEADLINES

AND THE ONE INSTRUCTION UNDERNEATH ALL SIXTEEN FILES
  ⭐⭐ SAY IT OUT LOUD ONCE.
    Everything in this library is input until you have
    said it. That is the difference between having read
    something and being able to use it, and it takes
    ten seconds.
