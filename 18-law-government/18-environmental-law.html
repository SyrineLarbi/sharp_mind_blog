Environmental law. Making someone pay for damage that used to be free.

For most of history, dumping waste into a river or smoke into the sky cost the polluter nothing. The fish, the neighbours and the next generation paid instead. Environmental law is the attempt to change that: to give air, water, wildlife and climate a legal voice, and to make the people who harm them stop, clean up or pay. It is young, mostly born after 1960, and it has some of the clearest successes and some of the biggest unfinished problems in all of law.

Question 1 · What is it?

A young field built from scandals

Environmental law is the body of national and international rules that controls how people and companies affect the natural world. Most of it was written in the last sixty years.

Chapter 01

The river that caught fire

In June 1969 oil-soaked debris on the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, caught fire. It was not even the river's first fire, nor its worst. But a national magazine ran the story, and the image of a river so polluted that it could burn became a symbol. It joined other alarms of the decade: Rachel Carson's 1962 book on pesticides, smog over Los Angeles, and a large oil spill off Santa Barbara earlier that same year.

The political response was fast. The first Earth Day in April 1970 brought millions of Americans onto the streets. That year the US created the Environmental Protection Agency and passed a much stronger Clean Air Act. The Clean Water Act followed in 1972 and the Endangered Species Act in 1973. Europe moved over the same years, and in 1972 the United Nations held its first conference on the human environment, in Stockholm.

Most environmental law was created in a short burst, by a public that had suddenly seen the damage. That pattern, scandal first and law second, recurs throughout the field.

National environmental law is mostly a form of regulation: agencies issue permits, set limits and inspect. International environmental law is a branch of international law, which depends on states agreeing to bind themselves. The two levels constantly interact.

Chapter 03

Five principles that run through it

Polluter pays

Whoever causes pollution should bear the cost of preventing or cleaning it. Economists explain why: pollution is an externality, a cost pushed onto others, and markets produce too much of anything whose cost someone else carries.

Prevention

It is better and cheaper to stop damage than to repair it. Many harms, such as an extinct species or a poisoned aquifer, cannot be repaired at all.

Precaution

Where there is a threat of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty should not be a reason to delay action. This wording comes from the 1992 Rio Declaration. How strongly to apply it is fiercely debated.

Sustainable development

Meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs, the definition popularised by the UN's Brundtland Report of 1987.

A fifth idea, environmental justice, grew from 1980s protests in the United States, where toxic waste sites were disproportionately placed near poor and Black communities. It asks who bears the burdens of pollution and who gets a say. The principles sound simple. Every hard case is about how far to push them.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Permits, prices, courts and treaties

Inside a country, the main tools are permits, assessments, markets and liability. Between countries, the main tool is the treaty, which works only as far as states cooperate.

Chapter 04

The permit and the assessment

The workhorse of environmental law is the permit. A factory, power station or waste site may operate only with a licence that sets how much it may emit, how it must monitor, and what it must report. Breaching the permit leads to fines, orders or closure. In the EU, large industrial plants must use the "best available techniques" for their sector, a standard that rises as technology improves.

For big new projects, the key tool is the environmental impact assessment (EIA). Before approval, the developer must study the likely effects on nature, water, air and communities, publish them, consider alternatives and take public comments. The US pioneered this with the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969. Today it is used in most countries. An EIA does not usually forbid a project. It forces the effects into the open before the decision.

Species protection can be stronger. In 1978 the US Supreme Court halted a nearly finished dam in Tennessee because it threatened a small fish, the snail darter, listed under the Endangered Species Act. Congress later passed a special exemption and the dam was completed. The episode showed both the power of a strict rule and the political pressure it invites.

Chapter 05

Putting a price on pollution

Permits tell each polluter what to do. Economists long argued that it is cheaper to set a total limit and let polluters trade. Under cap and trade, the government caps total emissions and issues allowances adding up to the cap. Firms that can cut emissions cheaply do so and sell their spare allowances. Firms for whom cuts are expensive buy them. The cap is met at lower total cost.

Cap and trade in one picture
  Cap: 100 tonnes allowed in total, 50 allowances each
                 Firm A (cheap to clean)     Firm B (costly to clean)
  emits before          60 t                        60 t
  cuts to               35 t   ---- sells 15 --->   65 t  (buys 15)
  total                 35 + 65 = 100 t   cap met, at the lowest cost

The US used this from 1995 to cut sulphur dioxide from power stations, the main cause of acid rain, and emissions fell faster and more cheaply than expected. The EU Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005, does the same for carbon dioxide and is the largest such market. Its early years were weak, because too many allowances were handed out and the price collapsed. Later reforms tightened it. The alternative is a carbon tax, which fixes the price and lets the quantity vary. Sweden has had one since 1991.

Chapter 06

Liability: making polluters clean up

In the late 1970s families at Love Canal, a neighbourhood in Niagara Falls, New York, discovered their homes stood on a buried chemical dump. Hundreds of households were evacuated. In 1980 Congress passed the law known as Superfund, which makes past and present owners of a contaminated site, and the companies that sent waste there, liable for clean-up, even if they acted legally at the time. This is called strict liability: you pay because you caused it, whether or not you were careless.

Ordinary courts matter too. Neighbours can sue for nuisance, and in many countries citizens and campaign groups may sue authorities that fail to enforce the law. Large disasters, from the Bhopal gas leak in 1984 (see Vol 23) to the Deepwater Horizon oil spill in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010, ended in huge settlements. Liability works after the damage. Its main value is that the fear of it changes behaviour before.

Chapter 07

Treaties: from the ozone hole to Paris

Pollution ignores borders, and no state can fix a global problem alone. So states negotiate treaties. The most successful is the Montreal Protocol of 1987, which phased out chemicals that destroy the ozone layer, the gas high in the atmosphere that blocks harmful ultraviolet light. Every member of the UN ratified it. It worked because the science was clear, the number of producing companies was small, substitutes existed, and poorer countries got time and money to switch.

Climate has proved much harder. The 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change set the goal. The 1997 Kyoto Protocol gave binding targets to rich countries only, and the US never ratified it. The 2015 Paris Agreement took a different approach. Every country sets its own target, called a nationally determined contribution, and must report progress and raise its ambition every five years. The shared goal is to keep warming well below 2°C and to pursue 1.5°C.

Paris traded strength for participation. Nearly every country joined because no one was forced to accept a target. Critics say the pledges add up to far more warming than the goal. Supporters reply that a weak treaty everyone signs can be tightened, while a strong one nobody signs cannot. Other important treaties cover trade in endangered species (CITES, 1973), hazardous waste exports (the Basel Convention, 1989) and biodiversity.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Clean air, a healing ozone layer, and climate in the courts

Environmental law has saved a very large number of lives. It is now being tested on the hardest problem it has faced, and courts have become a new battleground.

Chapter 08

The successes people forget

In December 1952 a thick smog settled over London for five days. Estimates of the deaths it caused have since risen to around twelve thousand. Britain passed a Clean Air Act in 1956 that pushed homes and factories away from burning raw coal in the city. Similar laws in Europe, North America and Japan cut the main air pollutants sharply over the following decades, even as economies grew.

The ozone layer is the clearest global success. A 2023 UN-backed scientific assessment reported that it is on track to recover to its 1980 state over most of the world around 2040, and over Antarctica around 2066. Lead was removed from petrol worldwide, the last country stopping sales in 2021, which lowered lead levels in children's blood. Rivers like the Cuyahoga now support fish. When environmental law has clear targets and serious enforcement, it works. See Air pollution for the health side.

Chapter 09

Climate change goes to court

Frustrated with slow politics, campaigners have turned to judges. Three cases changed the landscape.

Urgenda, Netherlands (2019)

The Dutch Supreme Court held that the government's duty to protect life and family life required it to cut emissions by at least 25% from 1990 levels by 2020. It was the first time a national top court ordered a government to do more on climate.

KlimaSeniorinnen v Switzerland (2024)

The European Court of Human Rights ruled that Switzerland had violated the rights of a group of older women by failing to act adequately on climate change. The ruling binds the Council of Europe's member states as a precedent.

The ICJ opinion (2025)

At the request of the UN General Assembly, pushed by the Pacific island state of Vanuatu, the International Court of Justice gave an advisory opinion that states have legal obligations under international law to protect the climate system. Advisory opinions are not binding, but they carry great weight.

In the United States, the Supreme Court ruled in 2007 (Massachusetts v EPA) that carbon dioxide counts as an air pollutant under the Clean Air Act, which opened the way to federal climate rules. Later rulings narrowed how far the agency could go. Courts cannot run climate policy, but they increasingly hold governments to their own promises. Critics argue this shifts political choices to unelected judges, a debate that mirrors the one in Separation of powers.

Chapter 10

Who pays, who gains: justice and new rights

The countries that emitted most of the carbon already in the atmosphere are mostly rich. The countries most exposed to its effects are often poor. Climate treaties recognise this in the phrase "common but differentiated responsibilities", and a fund for loss and damage was agreed at the 2022 UN climate summit. How much rich countries should pay, and to whom, remains one of the most contested questions in global politics.

Law is also experimenting with new kinds of rights. In 2022 the UN General Assembly recognised a human right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment, and many national constitutions contain one. Ecuador's 2008 constitution gave rights to nature itself, and New Zealand in 2017 gave legal personhood to the Whanganui River, reflecting Māori understandings of the river as an ancestor. Supporters see a needed shift. Sceptics question how such rights can be enforced in practice.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Clear wins on local pollution, a struggle on global commons

Environmental law is strongest when the polluter is identifiable and nearby. It is weakest when the harm is spread across the planet and across generations.

Chapter 12

The hard trade-offs

Carbon leakage. If one region makes polluting industry pay for carbon and others do not, production may simply move abroad, with no benefit to the climate. The EU's answer is a carbon border adjustment, which charges importers of goods such as steel and cement for the carbon emitted in making them. It began with reporting duties in 2023, with payments phasing in from 2026. Trading partners have called it disguised protectionism.

Green against green. Wind farms, transmission lines and mines for battery metals all need permits and assessments. Rules written to slow destructive projects also slow the clean infrastructure the climate needs. Many governments are now trying to speed up permitting without weakening protection, and the balance is disputed.

Cost and fairness. Environmental rules can raise prices for energy, cars and food, and those costs fall hardest on lower-income households. France's "yellow vest" protests, which began in 2018 over a planned rise in fuel taxes, showed how fast climate policy can lose public support when people feel it is unfair.

Chapter 14

The political swing

Environmental law depends on politics more than most fields, because its biggest costs come now and its biggest benefits later. In the United States, policy has swung with each administration. The country left the Paris Agreement under one president, rejoined under the next, and announced its withdrawal again in 2025. Federal agencies also moved to roll back several climate and pollution rules, which were challenged in court. In Europe, some parts of the Green Deal have been delayed or simplified under pressure about costs and competitiveness, while the core climate targets remain law.

The long-run forces push in different directions. Clean technology has become much cheaper, which makes rules easier to meet. Climate impacts are becoming more visible, which keeps the pressure on. Against that, cost-of-living worries and security concerns compete for attention. The test of environmental law in the coming decades is whether it can hold its course through political cycles that are shorter than the problems it is trying to solve. For the practical side of living with a warmer world, see Climate adaptation.

The core idea

Environmental law makes polluters account for harm they once pushed onto others. It rests on principles such as polluter pays, prevention and precaution, uses permits, impact assessments, markets and liability at home, and depends on treaties for global problems like ozone and climate.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Most core laws date from around 1970, the year of the first Earth Day and the US EPA.
  • The Montreal Protocol (1987) was ratified by every UN member; the ozone layer is expected to recover around 2040–2066, depending on region.
  • The Paris Agreement (2015) aims for well below 2°C, pursuing 1.5°C.
The common mistake

Assuming environmental law has failed because climate change continues. It has worked well on local pollution and ozone. Its weakness is global, long-term problems where many actors must cooperate.

The words, in plain words
Environmental law
National and international rules that control how human activity affects nature.
Externality
A cost, such as pollution, that falls on people outside the deal that caused it.
Polluter pays
The principle that whoever causes pollution should bear its cost.
Precautionary principle
Acting against serious threats even before science is fully certain.

A licence that sets how much a plant may emit and what it must monitor.
Environmental impact assessment

Cap and trade

Carbon tax
A fixed charge per tonne of carbon dioxide emitted.
Strict liability
