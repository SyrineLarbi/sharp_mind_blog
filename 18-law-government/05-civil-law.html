Civil law. Not who should be punished, but who should pay to put things right.

Criminal law gets the films and the headlines. Civil law gets almost everything else. When a surgeon makes a mistake, a builder leaves your roof leaking, a neighbour's tree falls on your car, a marriage ends or a parent dies, the rules that decide what happens next are civil law. It is the law of relations between private people and companies. Its aim is not to condemn but to repair, usually with money. Most people will never be accused of a crime. Almost everyone will be touched by civil law many times.

Question 1 · What is it?

The law between private parties

Civil law covers disputes in which one person or organisation claims something from another: compensation, property, the performance of a promise, or an order to stop doing something.

Chapter 01

Acquitted, then found liable

In 1995 the former American football star O. J. Simpson was tried for the murder of his former wife and her friend. A jury acquitted him. Two years later, the victims' families sued him in a civil court for causing the deaths. That jury found him liable, meaning legally responsible, and ordered him to pay damages of over 30 million dollars.

Many people found this baffling. How can a man be not guilty and responsible at the same time? The answer shows exactly how civil law differs from criminal law. The criminal trial asked whether the state had proved guilt beyond reasonable doubt. The civil trial asked whether the families had shown it was more likely than not that he caused the deaths. The second bar is much lower. The two verdicts did not contradict each other. They answered different questions to different standards.

The word tort comes from an old French word for "wrong". Civil law countries call the same area delict, from the Latin for an offence. Together with contract, it forms the law of obligations: the rules about when one person owes something to another. This file concentrates on tort, family and the process of civil disputes. Contract has its own topic.

Chapter 03

A word with two meanings

One warning before going further. "Civil law" has two meanings, and they are easily confused. In this file it means the area of law dealing with private disputes, as opposed to criminal law. But "civil law" is also the name of the legal tradition based on written codes, used in France, Germany and most of the world, as opposed to the common law of England and America. Courts explains that second meaning.

The two meanings are linked historically. The great codes of the civil law tradition, such as the French Civil Code of 1804, are mostly about private law: persons, property, contracts, liability, families and inheritance. So a "civil code" is a codified statement of civil law in the first sense. Common law countries have the same subjects, but much of their private law, including the core of tort and contract, was built by judges case by case.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Liability, damages, and the long road to a settlement

To win a civil claim for harm, you normally have to show that someone owed you a duty, failed in it, and caused you a loss. Then comes the question of how much they must pay, and the practical reality that most cases never reach a judge.

Chapter 04

A snail in a bottle

In August 1928, in a café in Paisley, near Glasgow, a woman named May Donoghue drank a bottle of ginger beer that a friend had bought for her. The bottle was made of dark glass, so nobody could see inside. When the rest was poured out, she said, the decomposed remains of a snail came out with it. She became ill. She had no contract with the manufacturer, because she had not bought the drink. Could she sue anyone?

In 1932 the House of Lords said yes. In Donoghue v Stevenson, Lord Atkin set out what he called the neighbour principle: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your "neighbour", meaning anyone so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought to have them in mind. A manufacturer owes that duty to the people who will consume its products. Whether there really was a snail was never proved; the case settled after the ruling. But the case created the modern law of negligence in the common law world.

Negligence now has four standard elements. The defendant owed the claimant a duty of care. They breached it by falling below the standard of a reasonable person, or a reasonable professional. The breach caused the harm. And the claimant suffered damage of a kind the law recognises, such as injury or financial loss.

Civil law codes reach a similar place by a different route. France's Civil Code, in what is now Article 1240, contains a single famous general rule: any act by which a person causes damage to another obliges the one at fault to repair it. Germany's Civil Code is more cautious. It lists protected interests, such as life, body, health, freedom and property, and liability follows when one of them is harmed unlawfully and through fault. Common law built from cases upward; the codes start from a principle and work down.

Chapter 05

Causation, and how much to pay

The usual first test of causation is simple: but for the defendant's act, would the harm have happened? If a doctor fails to examine a patient who would have died anyway, the failure did not cause the death. Then the law cuts off consequences that are too remote. A careless driver who dents your car is not responsible for the job interview you missed and the career that followed.

If the claimant wins, the usual remedy is damages: money meant to put them, as far as money can, in the position they would have been in without the wrong. That covers lost earnings, medical costs and repairs, and also non-financial losses such as pain and suffering, which courts value using guidelines and past awards. Sometimes money is not the point, and the court can grant an injunction, an order to do or stop doing something, such as stopping a factory's noise at night.

American law adds a feature unusual elsewhere: punitive damages, extra money awarded to punish especially bad conduct. The most famous case is often misremembered. In 1992 a 79-year-old woman in New Mexico spilled McDonald's coffee in her lap and suffered third-degree burns that needed skin grafts. Evidence at trial showed the company served coffee much hotter than most restaurants and had received hundreds of earlier burn complaints. The jury awarded about 2.7 million dollars in punitive damages, which the judge reduced to 480,000 dollars, and the parties later settled privately. The case became a joke about frivolous lawsuits. The real facts were far more serious than the story people tell.

Chapter 06

Liability without fault

Negligence requires fault. But in some areas the law imposes strict liability, where you are responsible for harm even if you were careful. The European Union's Product Liability Directive of 1985 made producers liable for damage caused by defective products without the need to prove negligence. A new directive adopted in 2024 extends this to software, including AI systems, and is due to apply in member states from the end of 2026.

Another important rule is vicarious liability: an employer is responsible for wrongs committed by employees in the course of their work. If a delivery driver crashes while on duty, the injured person sues the company, not just the driver. That makes sense practically, because the company is more likely to be able to pay and to carry insurance, and it gives employers a reason to train and supervise their staff.

Insurance is the hidden engine of the whole system. Most compensation for injuries is actually paid by insurers, especially for road accidents, where insurance is compulsory in most countries. In practice, much of tort law is a set of rules for deciding which insurer pays.

Chapter 07

How a civil case actually runs

A civil dispute typically starts with a letter setting out the claim. If that fails, the claimant files a claim with the court, and the defendant files a defence. Then comes the exchange of evidence. In England this is called disclosure; in the United States it is discovery, and it is famously wide, allowing each side to demand documents and to question witnesses under oath before trial. Civil law countries generally allow much narrower evidence-gathering, led by the judge.

Most cases never reach trial. They settle: the parties agree a payment and drop the case. Trials are expensive and unpredictable, and both sides usually prefer a certain result to a gamble. Who pays the legal costs shapes that calculation. England generally follows a "loser pays" rule, where the losing side pays much of the winner's costs. The United States mostly follows the "American rule", where each side pays its own lawyers. American lawyers often work on contingency fees, taking a share of the winnings and nothing if they lose, which makes it easier for people without money to sue.

When many people suffer the same harm, a class action lets one case represent them all. The United States has the most developed system. The EU adopted a directive in 2020 requiring member states to offer representative actions for consumers, though usually brought by approved organisations rather than entrepreneurial lawyers.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Repair, deterrence, and the shape of family life

Civil law compensates victims, but it also quietly changes how companies, doctors and drivers behave, and it decides what happens to families when they change.

Chapter 08

Lawsuits as a regulator

The threat of being sued changes behaviour before any harm happens. Manufacturers test products more carefully, hospitals adopt safety checklists, and building owners fix loose railings, partly because a claim would cost more. Economists who study law see tort as a way of making those who create risks bear the cost of them, so that they take the cost into account.

Sometimes litigation achieves what legislation could not. In the United States in the 1990s, dozens of states sued the major tobacco companies to recover the costs of treating smoking-related disease. In 1998 they reached the Master Settlement Agreement, under which the companies agreed to pay the states more than 200 billion dollars over 25 years and to accept limits on marketing, especially to young people. A private-law tool produced a public-health policy. Critics argued that such big policy choices should be made by elected legislatures, not negotiated in lawsuits.

Chapter 09

Families, divorce and inheritance

Family law is the part of civil law most people meet personally. For centuries divorce required proof that one spouse was at fault, through adultery or cruelty, which pushed couples into accusations or even staged evidence. Most Western countries now allow no-fault divorce. England and Wales finally removed the need to allege blame from April 2022. Family courts focus on dividing property fairly and, above all, on the welfare of children, which is the paramount consideration in most systems.

Inheritance shows a sharp difference between traditions. English law allows freedom of testation: in principle you can leave your property to anyone you like, although family members can ask a court for reasonable provision if they were left out. French law, like that of most civil law countries, protects children with a reserved share, the réserve héréditaire: a fixed part of the estate that a parent cannot leave to anyone else. Islamic inheritance law, applied in family matters in many Muslim-majority countries, also fixes shares for close relatives. Each system balances an individual's freedom against the claims of family, and they strike the balance in very different places.

Chapter 10

Ways around the courtroom

Because courts are slow and expensive, much civil justice happens elsewhere. In mediation, a neutral person helps the parties reach their own agreement but cannot impose one. In arbitration, the parties agree to have a private arbitrator decide, and the decision is binding. International business disputes are commonly arbitrated, partly because a treaty, the New York Convention of 1958, makes arbitral awards enforceable in most countries. Ombudsmen settle many consumer complaints against banks and insurers without cost to the consumer.

New Zealand went furthest. Since 1974 its Accident Compensation Corporation has paid for personal injuries from accidents regardless of fault, funded by levies. In return, people generally cannot sue for personal injury caused by an accident. The country chose a public insurance scheme over the tort system for accidents. It is cheaper to run and compensates more people, though critics say it removes the deterrent pressure of being sued.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Repair through litigation is fair in principle and costly in practice

The idea that whoever causes harm should repair it is simple and widely accepted. The machinery for doing it is not.

Chapter 12

Is there a "compensation culture"?

Newspapers in several countries regularly complain of a "compensation culture" in which people sue for every misfortune. The evidence is mixed. Studies of medical injuries in the United States and elsewhere have repeatedly found that most people harmed by negligence never make a claim. At the same time, some kinds of claims have grown sharply. In England, a surge in claims for minor whiplash injuries after road accidents led to fixed caps on payouts, which took effect in 2021.

Underneath the headlines are two genuine worries that pull in opposite directions. One is that the system is too hard to use, so wronged people get nothing. The other is that it is too easy to use for weak claims, raising insurance premiums for everyone. Both can be true at the same time, in different parts of the system. A sensible view asks about a specific area rather than the system as a whole.

Chapter 14

Making civil justice usable

The biggest practical question for civil law is not doctrine but access. For ordinary disputes about a few thousand euros or dollars, a full court case makes no economic sense. Several countries are experimenting with online procedures for small claims, where parties submit documents through a website, negotiate with automated help and see a judge only if needed. Early experience suggests these tools resolve many disputes quickly, though people with poor internet access or limited literacy can be left behind.

The deeper aim is old. The French Civil Code and Lord Atkin's neighbour principle both start from a simple moral idea: if you harm someone through your fault, you must put it right. The challenge for the future is building a system cheap and fast enough that the idea works for small harms as well as large ones. The next topic looks at the other great pillar of private law, the promises people and companies make to each other.

The core idea

Civil law governs disputes between private parties. The claimant must prove their case on the balance of probabilities. In tort, that usually means a duty, a breach, causation and damage. The aim is repair, usually through damages, and most cases settle before trial.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Donoghue v Stevenson, 1932: the neighbour principle.
  • The tobacco Master Settlement Agreement, 1998: over 200 billion dollars over 25 years.
  • New Zealand's no-fault accident scheme: since 1974.
The common mistake

Thinking a civil verdict that contradicts a criminal one means a court got it wrong. They answer different questions to different standards of proof.
