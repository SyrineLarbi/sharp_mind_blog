What constitutions do. The rules about who gets to make the rules.

Most laws tell citizens what they may and may not do. A constitution is different. It tells the government what it may and may not do. It says who holds power, how they get it, how long they keep it and where it stops. That sounds dry until you notice what happens without one: power goes to whoever can grab it, and stays there until someone stronger takes it away. A constitution is a society's attempt to replace that contest with a set of agreed procedures. Some succeed for centuries. Many fail within a generation. Both outcomes are worth understanding.

Question 1 · What is it?

The law that sits above the other laws

A constitution is the set of fundamental rules that creates a state's institutions, gives them their powers and sets their limits. Usually it is a single document. Not always.

Chapter 01

A rulebook for the rule-makers

Think of a football match. There are rules for the players: no handball, no tripping, stay onside. Those are like ordinary laws. But there is also a deeper layer. Who appoints the referee? Can the referee change the rules halfway through? What happens if a team refuses to accept a decision? Those questions are about the game itself, and the answers have to be settled before anyone kicks a ball. A constitution is that deeper layer for a country.

The word gives a hint. To "constitute" something is to make it up, to form it. A constitution constitutes the state. It says there shall be a parliament of so many members, elected in such a way, for so many years. It says there shall be a president or a prime minister, and how that person is chosen and removed. It says there shall be courts, and how judges are appointed. Before those rules exist, there is no parliament to pass laws, only a group of people in a room.

Lawyers express this with the idea of a hierarchy of norms. A norm is any binding rule. At the bottom are local regulations and administrative decisions. Above them are ordinary laws passed by the legislature, the body that makes laws. At the top sits the constitution. A rule lower down that contradicts a rule higher up is, in principle, invalid. The Austrian legal theorist Hans Kelsen described the legal order as a pyramid in this sense, and the image is still used in law schools across Europe.

Chapter 02

Written, unwritten, and everything in between

Almost every country today has a single document called its constitution. The United States constitution, drafted in 1787, is usually described as the oldest written national constitution still in force. France is on its fifth republic, with a constitution from 1958. Germany's is called the Basic Law and dates from 1949. India's took effect in 1950 and is one of the longest in the world.

A few countries do not have one document. The United Kingdom is the best-known example, along with New Zealand. Israel has a series of "Basic Laws" instead of a single text. People sometimes say these countries have "no constitution". That is wrong. The UK has a constitution; it is just not written down in one place. It is spread across important Acts of Parliament, court judgments, old documents such as Magna Carta and the Bill of Rights of 1689, and unwritten habits called conventions.

So the useful distinction is not written versus unwritten but codified versus uncodified. A codified constitution is gathered into one supreme text. An uncodified one is scattered, and its parts are not legally above ordinary law. Even in the United States, much of how government works, such as the size of the Supreme Court, is not in the document at all.

Chapter 03

What goes inside

Constitutions vary a lot in length and style, but most do the same five jobs.

JobWhat it doesExample
IdentityStates who the state belongs to and what it stands for, often in a preamble"We the People" (US); France as an "indivisible, secular, democratic and social Republic"
StructureCreates the institutions: legislature, executive, courtsParliament with two chambers; a president elected for a fixed term
PowersLists what each institution may do, and divides power between central and regional governmentOnly the federal government may print money
RightsSets out freedoms the state may not take away, or must protectFree speech, fair trial, equality before the law
ChangeExplains how the constitution itself can be amendedSpecial majorities, referendums, or both

Notice that two of the five jobs point in opposite directions. Structure and powers create government. Rights and limits restrain it. A constitution both builds the state and fences it in, and much of constitutional politics is the argument about where the fence should stand.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Making the rules hard to change, and finding someone to enforce them

Paper does not enforce itself. A constitution works through three mechanisms: it is harder to change than ordinary law, someone is given the job of checking that it is obeyed, and the people in power choose to respect habits that no court could force on them.

Chapter 04

Entrenchment: why changing it is hard on purpose

If a government could rewrite the constitution with the same simple majority it uses for a tax law, the constitution would offer no protection. The winners of each election could remove whatever limits annoyed them. So most constitutions are entrenched: changing them requires more than a normal majority.

The methods differ. In the United States an amendment needs a two-thirds vote in both houses of Congress and then approval by three-quarters of the states. That is so hard that the document has been amended only 27 times since 1787, and ten of those were the Bill of Rights added at the start. Ireland and Switzerland require a public vote, a referendum, for constitutional change. In France, an amendment can be approved either by referendum or by a three-fifths majority of both chambers meeting together. Germany's Basic Law needs two-thirds of both chambers.

Germany adds something stronger. Article 79 of the Basic Law, often called the eternity clause, says that certain principles cannot be amended at all: human dignity, democracy, federalism and the rule of law among them. The authors had just watched the Weimar Republic hand its own power away by legal votes. They wanted some things to be beyond the reach of any majority.

Rigid and flexible

Scholars call a constitution that is hard to amend rigid and one that is easy to amend flexible. The UK's is the classic flexible case: Parliament can change almost any constitutional rule with an ordinary Act. Neither is automatically better. The trade-off is in Question 4.

Chapter 05

Who checks: judicial review

A rule needs a referee. In most democracies that referee is a court with the power of judicial review: the power to examine a law and declare it invalid if it breaks the constitution.

The American version began with a case in 1803, Marbury v. Madison. The constitution did not clearly say that courts could strike down Acts of Congress. Chief Justice John Marshall argued that it followed logically: if the constitution is supreme law, and judges must apply law, then when a statute conflicts with the constitution, judges must apply the constitution. Since then any American court can, in principle, refuse to apply an unconstitutional law, with the Supreme Court having the final word.

Much of Europe chose a different design, associated with Kelsen. Instead of letting every court do this, they created one specialist constitutional court. Austria built the first in 1920. Germany's Federal Constitutional Court, set up in 1951, became one of the most influential in the world; ordinary citizens can bring complaints to it directly. France was slower. Its Constitutional Council, created in 1958, at first could only check laws before they came into force. Since 2010 a person involved in a lawsuit can ask for an existing law to be reviewed, a procedure known by its French initials as the QPC.

Then there is the UK, which follows the principle of parliamentary sovereignty: Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court can strike down an Act of Parliament. Under the Human Rights Act 1998, a court that finds a law incompatible with protected rights can say so in a formal declaration, but the law stays in force until Parliament acts. In practice Parliament usually does. The British system trusts elected politicians to fix the problem; the American and German systems trust judges to.

Chapter 06

Conventions: the rules nobody can enforce

Every constitution depends on habits that no court will enforce. In the UK, the monarch formally has to approve every bill before it becomes law, an act called royal assent. Legally, the monarch could refuse. The last time it happened was in 1708, under Queen Anne. Everyone understands that it will not happen again. That understanding is a convention: a rule that binds because people treat it as binding.

Conventions exist in codified systems too. For most of American history, presidents served at most two terms, following George Washington's example. Nothing required it. When Franklin Roosevelt won four elections, the country responded by writing the two-term limit into the constitution, the 22nd Amendment, ratified in 1951. A convention that broke was turned into hard law.

Conventions matter most when they are tested. In 2019 the British prime minister advised the Queen to suspend Parliament for five weeks during the Brexit crisis. The UK Supreme Court ruled, unanimously, that the advice was unlawful because it stopped Parliament doing its job without a reasonable justification. Some praised the court for defending Parliament; others said it had stepped into politics. The case showed that in an uncodified system the line between convention and law can move when a crisis pushes on it.

Chapter 07

Dividing power up and down: federal and unitary states

A constitution also decides how power is split between the centre and the regions. In a federal state, such as the United States, Germany, India, Brazil or Canada, the regions have powers guaranteed by the constitution itself. The central government cannot simply abolish a German Land or an American state, or take over its responsibilities for schools or policing.

In a unitary state, such as France or Japan, sovereign power belongs to the centre, and regions exercise only the powers the centre chooses to give them. The UK is unitary but has handed large powers to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, a process called devolution. Legally, Westminster could take those powers back. Politically, doing so would be explosive, which is another example of a convention doing the work of a rule.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The difference between rules on paper and rules in life

A constitution matters when it changes what powerful people actually do. Some documents achieve that. Others are decorations.

Chapter 08

Beautiful documents, empty promises

The Soviet constitution of 1936 promised freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly. It said no one could be arrested except by a court decision or with a prosecutor's approval. It was adopted at the end of 1936, on the eve of the worst of the purges, when hundreds of thousands of people would be shot after secret proceedings. The text was fine. Nothing in the system made anyone obey it.

Political scientists call this a sham constitution: one whose promises bear little relation to practice. The lesson is uncomfortable for lawyers. A list of rights does not protect anyone unless some institution with real independence can enforce it, and unless those in power believe that ignoring it will cost them. Courts, a free press, opposition parties, elections that can actually remove the government: these are what give the words weight.

The opposite failure is also possible. A constitution can be sincere and well designed and still be taken apart by its own procedures. Weimar Germany had emergency powers in Article 48, which let the president rule by decree in a crisis. They were meant for rare emergencies and became the normal way of governing in the early 1930s. Volume 21 tells how that ended. The German Basic Law of 1949 was written with that history in front of it, which is why it limits emergency powers carefully and places human dignity in Article 1.

Chapter 09

When the words start to bite

Constitutional promises are sometimes ignored for generations and then suddenly enforced. The American 14th Amendment, adopted in 1868 after the Civil War, promised every person the "equal protection of the laws". For decades it coexisted with legal racial segregation, which the Supreme Court accepted in 1896 under the slogan "separate but equal".

In 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education, a unanimous Supreme Court ruled that segregated public schools violated that same clause. The words had not changed. The court's reading of them had, pushed by years of careful litigation by civil rights lawyers. Enforcing the ruling took years more of struggle, and in some places federal troops. But it shows something important: a constitution can hold a promise in reserve until a society is ready, or is forced, to keep it.

Chapter 10

Constitutional moments: how countries remake themselves

New constitutions usually appear after a rupture: independence, revolution, war, or the end of a dictatorship. Scholars call these constitutional moments, times when ordinary politics pauses and a society argues about its foundations.

India's constitution was drafted after independence by an assembly that met for nearly three years. The chair of its drafting committee, B. R. Ambedkar, was a lawyer and economist born into a caste then treated as "untouchable". The text abolished untouchability and allowed reserved places for disadvantaged groups in education, jobs and parliament. South Africa's constitution of 1996, negotiated after the end of apartheid, created a Constitutional Court and one of the broadest bills of rights anywhere, including rights to housing, health care and water. Whether such economic rights can really be enforced by judges is a long-running debate, and South Africa's court has tended to require the state to act "reasonably" rather than to deliver specific results.

A constitution is partly a story a country tells about what it was and what it wants to become. That is why the fights over changing one are so bitter.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Stability against flexibility, judges against voters

Every design choice in a constitution buys something and costs something. The arguments are old, and none of them is settled.

Chapter 12

The dead hand problem

In 1789 Thomas Jefferson wrote to James Madison arguing that "the earth belongs to the living" and that no generation should be bound by the laws of the one before. He suggested that constitutions should expire roughly every generation. Madison disagreed. A constitution that had to be renegotiated constantly, he replied, would invite instability and faction.

The argument has never ended. Its modern form is the debate over interpretation. In the United States, originalists argue that judges should apply the meaning the words had when they were adopted, because otherwise judges are simply substituting their own views. Supporters of a living constitution argue that broad phrases such as "cruel and unusual punishment" were meant to be applied with each era's understanding, and that a rule written in the 1780s cannot be frozen. Both camps claim to be protecting democracy. European courts rarely use these labels, but they face the same tension when they read old principles into new situations.

There is also a practical weakness that no design fully solves. One widely cited study of national constitutions since 1789 found that the typical one lasts less than twenty years. Many are replaced after coups or by leaders who want to stay longer. The most common way a constitution dies is not by amendment but by being ignored and then replaced.

Chapter 14

Emergencies, and the slow erosion problem

Modern democracies rarely end with tanks in the street. Researchers who study democratic decline describe a slower pattern, often called backsliding: an elected government uses legal tools, one at a time, to weaken the institutions that could check it. It changes the courts, rewrites election rules, puts pressure on the media and extends emergency powers. Each step may be defensible. The total effect is a constitution that still exists on paper while the competition it was meant to protect becomes unfair.

The pandemic years from 2020 tested emergency powers everywhere. Governments closed borders, shops and schools by decree, often with little parliamentary debate. Most of those powers lapsed when the emergency passed, which is a sign that the systems worked. But the episode revived an old question: who decides when an emergency exists, and who decides when it is over? Constitutions that answer clearly, with time limits and a role for parliament and courts, are likely to be copied. Those that leave it to the executive's judgement will keep inviting abuse.

The core idea

A constitution creates the institutions of a state and limits them. It sits at the top of the legal hierarchy, is usually entrenched so it is harder to change than ordinary law, and is enforced by courts, by political pressure and by conventions that the powerful choose to respect.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US constitution: drafted 1787, amended only 27 times.
  • US amendments need two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of the states.
  • The typical national constitution has lasted under twenty years.
The common mistake

Saying the UK "has no constitution". It has one; it is uncodified, spread across statutes, judgments and conventions rather than gathered in one supreme document.

Can you explain why the Soviet constitution of 1936 promised so much and protected so little? If you can name what was missing, you understand what makes a constitution work.

The words, in plain words
Constitution
The fundamental rules that create a state's institutions, give them powers and set their limits.
Codified
Gathered into one supreme written document.
Uncodified
Spread across many laws, judgments and customs, as in the UK.
Hierarchy of norms
The idea that legal rules are ranked, with the constitution at the top.
Entrenchment
Making a rule harder to change than ordinary law, for example by requiring a two-thirds majority.
Eternity clause
A provision that forbids certain principles from ever being amended, as in Germany's Basic Law.
Judicial review
The power of courts to declare a law or government act invalid because it breaks the constitution.
Constitutional court
A specialist court whose main job is to rule on constitutional questions.
Parliamentary sovereignty
The principle that Parliament can make or repeal any law and no court can overrule it.
Convention
An unwritten rule of government that is followed because everyone treats it as binding.
Federalism
A system where regions have powers guaranteed by the constitution, not just lent by the centre.
Devolution
The transfer of powers from a central government to regional bodies, which the centre could legally take back.
Backsliding
The gradual weakening of democratic checks by an elected government using legal means.
