Contracts. The legal technology that lets strangers trust each other.

You make several contracts a day without noticing. Buying a coffee is one. So is getting on a bus, downloading an app, or ordering something online. Most are never written down and never thought about again. Yet the idea underneath them, that some promises can be enforced by the state, is one of the foundations of every economy. It lets you deal with people you will never meet and have no reason to trust. This topic explains which promises count, what happens when they are broken, and where the law refuses to let people bind themselves.

Question 1 · What is it?

A promise the law will enforce

A contract is an agreement that creates legal obligations. If one side fails to do what it promised, the other can go to court.

Chapter 01

Why promises need a referee

In a small village, you do not need courts to keep promises. If the baker cheats you, everyone will know by evening, and nobody will buy from him again. Reputation does the work. But reputation works only among people who know each other and expect to deal again. A merchant shipping wool to a buyer in another country, a bank lending to a company it has never met, a worker joining a firm in a new city: none of them can rely on gossip.

Contract law fills the gap. It tells both sides in advance that if a promise is broken, the state will help the injured party get what they were owed, or its value in money. That assurance allows people to trade with strangers, to plan for months or years ahead, and to invest money now in return for something later. The Latin phrase pacta sunt servanda, "agreements must be kept", expresses the principle, and it runs through every legal system.

Chapter 02

You do not need paper

A common belief is that a contract must be written and signed. In most legal systems, for most transactions, that is wrong. An oral agreement, or one made simply by conduct, is usually a valid contract. When you put an item on the counter and the shop assistant takes your money, a contract of sale has been made without a word of legal language.

Writing matters for proof, because a spoken agreement is hard to prove if the other side denies it. And some contracts do have to be in writing or follow a formal procedure. England's Statute of Frauds of 1677 required writing for certain contracts, and today a sale of land in England must be in writing. In France and many civil law countries, a sale of property goes through a notaire, a public legal officer who drafts and records the deed. Guarantees of another person's debt often require writing too, precisely because people agree to them too lightly.

Clicking "I agree" on a website is also a form of signature in most countries. Laws such as the US ESIGN Act of 2000 and the EU's eIDAS regulation of 2014 confirm that electronic agreements and signatures can be legally valid.

Chapter 03

Promises the law ignores

Not every promise is a contract. If you promise to meet a friend for dinner and then cancel, they cannot sue you for the cost of the train ticket. The law asks whether the parties intended to create legal relations. In social and family settings, the starting assumption is that they did not.

A well-known English case from 1919, Balfour v Balfour, makes the point. A husband working abroad promised his wife a monthly allowance while she stayed in England for health reasons. When the marriage broke down, he stopped paying, and she sued. The court held that the promise was a domestic arrangement, not a contract. As one judge put it, the courts could not police every promise between spouses. In business, the opposite assumption applies: commercial agreements are presumed to be legally binding unless the parties clearly say otherwise.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Making it, testing it, breaking it

A contract has a life cycle. It is formed, it can be challenged as invalid, it is interpreted when the parties disagree about what it means, and if it is broken, the law provides remedies.

Chapter 04

Offer and acceptance: the smoke ball case

In 1891 a London company sold a device called the Carbolic Smoke Ball, a rubber ball filled with a chemical powder that users inhaled through the nose. Its advertisements promised to pay 100 pounds, a large sum, to anyone who used the ball as directed and still caught influenza. To show it was serious, the company said it had deposited 1,000 pounds in a bank. A woman named Louisa Carlill bought one, used it faithfully, and caught the flu. The company refused to pay, arguing that an advertisement was just sales talk.

The Court of Appeal disagreed, in a judgment given at the end of 1892. The advertisement was an offer to the whole world, and anyone who did what it asked accepted it by doing so. Mrs Carlill won her 100 pounds. The case is still taught in every common law school because it shows the two basic building blocks clearly. An offer is a clear proposal that can be accepted; acceptance is agreement to its terms. When the two meet, a contract exists.

Most advertisements and shop displays, by contrast, are not offers. They are an invitation to treat, an invitation for customers to make an offer. That is why a shop that labels a television with the wrong price is usually not obliged to sell it at that price: the customer offers to buy at the till, and the shop can refuse. The rules differ in detail between countries, and consumer law adds protections, but the basic logic is widely shared.

Chapter 05

Something in return: consideration

Common law adds a requirement that surprises people from other traditions. A promise is enforceable only if something is given in exchange for it. This is called consideration. If I promise to sell you my bicycle for 100 euros, each of us gives something: the bicycle and the money. If I simply promise to give you my bicycle next week for nothing, English law will not force me to, because you gave nothing for my promise. The exchange does not have to be fair; a famous phrase says a peppercorn will do. It just has to exist.

Common law makes an exception for promises made in a deed, a formal signed and witnessed document, which binds without consideration. Civil law systems take a different route. They do not require an exchange, so a free promise can be binding, but they often require a formality for gifts. In France, a gift of significant property generally has to be made before a notaire. Both systems are guarding against the same danger: rash promises made without thinking. One uses the test of exchange, the other the test of formality.

Chapter 06

When an agreement does not hold

Even when offer and acceptance are present, a contract can be void (treated as never having existed) or voidable (valid until the wronged party chooses to cancel it). The main reasons are shared across most systems.

GroundWhat it meansExample
Lack of capacityOne party could not legally agreeA young child, or a person without mental capacity, signs a loan

The last ground is the most important in daily life. The EU's Unfair Contract Terms Directive of 1993 says that a standard term in a consumer contract that was not individually negotiated, and that creates a significant imbalance against the consumer contrary to good faith, does not bind the consumer. Similar rules exist in many countries. Freedom of contract assumes two parties who can actually negotiate, and modern law recognises that a consumer clicking "accept" cannot.

Chapter 07

What the contract means: terms and good faith

Most contract disputes are not about whether a contract exists, but about what it means. Courts start with the express terms, the words the parties actually used, read in their context. Then they add implied terms: things so obvious that the parties did not bother to say them, or protections that the law inserts automatically, such as the rule that goods sold must be of satisfactory quality.

Here the legal traditions diverge again. German law has long relied on the principle of Treu und Glauben, good faith, in Section 242 of its Civil Code, and German courts have used it to adjust contracts in hard cases. France's 2016 reform of its contract law states that contracts must be negotiated, formed and performed in good faith. English law has traditionally refused any general duty of good faith, preferring that commercial parties look after themselves and that contracts mean what they say. English judges have recognised good faith duties in some kinds of long-term relationship, but the general position remains more cautious than on the continent. That difference is one reason many international businesses choose English law: it is seen as predictable, even if sometimes harsh.

Chapter 08

When a contract is broken

A failure to perform what was promised is a breach. The usual remedy in common law is damages calculated to put the injured party in the position they would have been in if the contract had been performed. This is called the expectation measure: you get the value of the bargain you were promised.

But not every loss counts. In 1854, in Hadley v Baxendale, a mill in Gloucester stopped working because its crankshaft broke. The millers sent it to be copied by engineers, using a carrier who delivered it days late. The mill stayed idle, and the millers sued for their lost profits. The court said the carrier was liable only for losses that were reasonably foreseeable when the contract was made, or that had been specifically drawn to his attention. Nobody had told him the mill would stand idle without the shaft. The rule that you pay only for foreseeable consequences is still the foundation of contract damages in the common law world.

Civil law systems treat specific performance, a court order to do what was promised, as a normal remedy in principle. Common law treats it as exceptional, used mainly for unique things such as land. In practice the difference is smaller than it looks, because most businesses prefer money to a forced performance by an unwilling partner.

Finally, sometimes performance becomes impossible through no one's fault. Common law calls this frustration; the classic case, Taylor v Caldwell in 1863, involved a music hall hired for concerts that burned down before the first one. Civil law speaks of force majeure, an unforeseeable and irresistible event. Many contracts include a force majeure clause listing such events, and the pandemic from 2020 tested thousands of them at once.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Markets run on enforceable promises

Contract law matters because almost every economic relationship depends on it, and because the question of how far people can bind themselves is a political one.

Chapter 09

The invisible foundation of an economy

Economists who study institutions, such as the Nobel laureate Douglass North, argue that reliable enforcement of contracts and property rights is one of the main reasons some economies grew rich while others did not. If you cannot count on a court to enforce a deal, you trade only with family and people you know, you demand payment in advance, and you avoid long-term investment. Weak contract enforcement keeps markets small.

The speed of enforcement matters as much as the rules. Where a commercial dispute takes many years to resolve, a contract is worth much less, because the wronged party may give up or settle cheaply. Businesses respond by choosing where their disputes will be heard. Many international contracts specify English law and the courts of London, or arbitration in centres such as London, Paris, Singapore or New York, even when neither party is from there.

Chapter 10

Freedom of contract, and its limits

In the nineteenth century, many lawyers and economists treated freedom of contract as almost sacred: adults should be free to make whatever bargains they like, and courts should simply enforce them. In the United States, this view reached its peak in Lochner v. New York in 1905, when the Supreme Court struck down a New York law limiting bakers' working hours, on the ground that it interfered with the freedom of employers and workers to agree their own terms. Over the following decades that approach was abandoned, and from 1937 the Court accepted that governments may regulate employment contracts.

The critics' point was simple. A hungry worker and a large employer are not equals, so the "agreement" between them may reflect necessity, not choice. Today, the law in most countries protects weaker parties with mandatory rules that no contract can override: minimum wages, maximum working hours, protection against unfair dismissal, consumer cancellation rights, rules against unfair terms. The modern position is freedom of contract between equals, and protection where one side has far more power. The topics on labor law and consumer protection take those protections further.

Chapter 11

The contracts nobody reads

The classic picture of a contract is two parties negotiating terms across a table. The reality for most people is a standard-form contract: dozens of pages written by one side and offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. Phone plans, insurance policies, software licences, social media terms. Hardly anyone reads them, and it would be irrational to try, because reading every set of terms you accept would take an enormous amount of time and you could not change them anyway.

The law has responded in two ways. One is to regulate the content directly, through unfair terms rules and mandatory consumer rights. The other is to require transparency: key terms, such as price, renewal and cancellation, must be clearly presented, and surprising terms buried in fine print may not bind. Neither solves the basic problem, which is that consent by click is a thin kind of consent. How much weight it should bear is one of the central questions of modern contract law.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Freedom and predictability against fairness

The main design choices in contract law trade certainty for fairness, or the other way round.

Some economists defend the possibility of paying damages instead of performing as efficient breach: if a better use for the goods appears, a party should be able to break the contract, pay the other side's full loss, and still come out ahead, leaving everyone at least as well off. Critics answer that this treats a promise as a mere price, and that full compensation rarely happens in practice because legal costs and unforeseeable losses go unpaid. The debate is about whether a contract is fundamentally a moral promise or an economic option.

Chapter 14

From one-off deals to long relationships

Classical contract law was built around single exchanges, such as selling a horse or a cargo of wool. Much modern business is different: long supply agreements, joint ventures, outsourcing deals, franchises. The American legal scholar Ian Macneil called these relational contracts, arguing that they work more like ongoing relationships than like one-time bargains, and that the parties rely on cooperation and trust far beyond the written terms.

Courts in several countries now recognise this. They are more willing to imply duties of cooperation and honesty into long-term deals than into a one-off sale. At the same time, the pressure of global trade keeps pushing toward standard terms and predictable rules. The likely future is a two-track system: strict, predictable rules for transactions between sophisticated parties, and growing protection and flexibility for consumers, workers and long-term partners. The core idea

A contract is a promise the law will enforce. It is formed by offer and acceptance, plus consideration in common law or the right formality in civil law, with an intention to be bound. It can be undone for fraud, duress, mistake, incapacity or unfairness. If it is broken, the usual remedy is damages for foreseeable losses.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball, 1892: an advert can be an offer.
  • Hadley v Baxendale, 1854: only foreseeable losses are recoverable.
  • EU Unfair Contract Terms Directive: 1993.
The common mistake

Thinking a contract must be written and signed. Most oral agreements are valid contracts; writing mainly helps you prove them.
