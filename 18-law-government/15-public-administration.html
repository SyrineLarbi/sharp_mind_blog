Public administration. The law says what should happen. Administration decides whether it does.

Elections choose who governs, and parliaments pass the laws. But a law is only words until somebody prints the forms, hires the inspectors, pays the pensions and answers the phone. That somebody is the public administration: the civil servants, agencies and local offices that turn political decisions into things that happen to real people. It is the least glamorous part of government and probably the most important. Two countries with almost identical laws can give their citizens very different lives, because one of them can actually carry its laws out.

Question 1 · What is it?

The permanent part of government

Ministers come and go. The people who run the tax office, the hospitals' budgets and the passport service mostly stay. Public administration is that permanent layer, and the study of how it should be organised.

Chapter 01

The official at the counter is making policy

Imagine you apply for a disability benefit. The law says you qualify if you "cannot reasonably be expected to work". Who decides what "reasonably" means in your case? Not the parliament that wrote the sentence, and not the minister. It is an assessor you will meet once, working through forty files a week, with guidance notes, targets and a few minutes per decision.

In 1980 the American political scientist Michael Lipsky gave this person a name: the street-level bureaucrat. Teachers, police officers, social workers, benefit assessors and border officers all apply general rules to individual cases. They have to use judgement, because no rule can foresee every situation. Lipsky's point was uncomfortable. The policy citizens actually experience is the sum of thousands of small decisions made at the front line, often under pressure, and often quite differently from what the law's authors imagined.

That is the first thing to understand about public administration. It is not just "carrying out orders". It is where abstract law meets messy life, and where much of the real shape of government is decided.

Chapter 02

A definition, and an old argument

Public administration means two related things. It is the activity of running public services and carrying out government decisions. It is also the academic field that studies how to do that well: how to organise departments, hire and manage officials, spend public money and hold all of it to account.

The field has an origin story. In 1887 a young American academic called Woodrow Wilson, who later became president, published an essay called "The Study of Administration". He argued that politics and administration should be kept apart. Politicians decide what to do. Trained administrators decide how, efficiently and without party favour. This is the politics–administration dichotomy.

Almost nobody now believes the line is that clean. Deciding how to do something often decides what actually gets done, as the benefit assessor shows. But the ideal behind the line still matters. Most democracies want a civil service that serves whichever party wins, loyally and competently, without being the property of any of them. Much of this topic is about how countries try to hold that balance.

Chapter 03

Weber's machine

The German sociologist Max Weber, writing in the early twentieth century (his main work, Economy and Society, was published after he died in 1920), described what he called the bureaucracy. Today the word is an insult. For Weber it was a description of the most efficient form of organisation humans had invented. Its features are worth knowing by heart.

FeatureWhat it meansWhat it replaced
Written rulesDecisions follow published rules, not the official's moodArbitrary favours from a lord or king
HierarchyA clear chain of command, so every decision can be appealed upwardOverlapping personal loyalties
Expertise and meritOfficials are hired and promoted for qualificationsJobs bought, inherited or given to friends
ImpersonalityThe same case gets the same answer, whoever you are"It depends who you know"
FilesEverything is recorded, so decisions can be checked laterMemory and word of mouth
Office separate from personThe job and its tools belong to the state, not to the officialOffices treated as private property

Weber also saw the danger. He worried that this rational machine could become an "iron cage", a world run by procedure in which nobody takes personal responsibility. Both halves of his view, the efficiency and the cage, are still the two sides of every argument about bureaucracy.

Question 2 · How does it work?

People, structures, money and checks

Every administration has to answer four questions. Who gets the jobs? How is the work divided? Where does the money come from and go? And who checks that it was spent properly?

Chapter 04

From jobs for friends to jobs by exam

For most of history, government jobs were rewards. The idea of choosing officials by examination is old in China, where imperial examinations selected scholar-officials for well over a thousand years until they were abolished in 1905. In Europe and America it arrived late, and usually after a scandal.

In Britain, the Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854 recommended recruiting civil servants by competitive exam and promoting them on merit. It took decades to apply fully, but it became the model for a permanent, politically neutral civil service. In the United States, jobs were handed out by the winning party, a practice called the spoils system ("to the victor belong the spoils"). In 1881 President James Garfield was shot by a man who believed he was owed a government post. Two years later Congress passed the Pendleton Act, which began a merit-based federal civil service.

France took a third route. After the Second World War it founded the École nationale d'administration (ENA) in 1945 to train a small elite of senior officials. Its graduates filled ministries, top corporate posts and the presidency. Critics said it produced a closed caste, and in 2022 it was replaced by a new institute for public service. The common thread is merit recruitment, the single reform most associated with a capable and less corrupt state.

Chapter 05

How a ministry is put together

Countries organise the top of government differently, and the differences shape how power flows.

The Westminster model (UK and others)

Ministers are politicians. Below them, almost everyone is a permanent official, up to a senior civil servant (in the UK, a permanent secretary) who runs the department and stays when the government changes. A new government brings only a handful of political advisers.

The American model

A new president fills roughly four thousand posts with political appointees, including the top layers of every department. Career officials sit below them. This gives the president more direct control, and also means long gaps while posts wait for Senate approval.

The French model

A strong central state with powerful elite corps of officials. Ministers rely on a cabinet of close advisers, often senior civil servants themselves. Movement between top administration, politics and big business is common.

Below the ministries sit agencies: semi-independent bodies that run one service, such as a tax authority, a medicines regulator or a statistics office. Then there is local government, which in many countries delivers most of the services people actually touch: schools, roads, rubbish collection, social care. How much power sits at the centre and how much is pushed down to regions and towns is called the degree of decentralisation. Germany and the US are federal and quite decentralised. France and the UK have historically been more centralised, though both have devolved power since the 1980s and 1990s.

Chapter 06

Following the money: budgets and audit

A government's real priorities are written in its budget, not its speeches. Each year the finance ministry negotiates with every department, the cabinet decides, and the parliament votes. This annual cycle is one of the oldest democratic controls: the principle that the executive cannot spend public money without the legislature's permission goes back centuries in England.

The accountability loop, simplified
  Parliament votes the money  -->  Ministry spends it  -->  Agency delivers
         ^                                                       |
         |                                                       v
  Committee hearings  <--  Audit office reports  <--  Accounts and records

The last link is the supreme audit institution: the National Audit Office in the UK, the Government Accountability Office in the US, the Cour des comptes in France. These bodies check the accounts and, increasingly, whether money achieved anything, which is called a value-for-money audit. Their reports go to parliamentary committees that can call senior officials to explain themselves in public. Without this loop, nobody would know whether spending worked, and nobody would have to answer for it.

Chapter 07

The great reform wave: running government like a business

By the 1970s many voters saw big government as slow and wasteful. From the 1980s, governments in Britain, New Zealand, Australia and later the US tried a set of reforms that academics grouped under the name New Public Management. The idea was to borrow tools from private business.

Britain's "Next Steps" programme, launched in 1988, split delivery work out of ministries into executive agencies with their own chief executives and performance targets. Services such as cleaning, IT and later prisons and parts of health care were opened to private contractors. Citizens were rebranded as "customers". Performance league tables appeared for schools and hospitals.

Some of this worked. Agencies became clearer about what they were for, and some services got faster and cheaper. But targets brought their own trouble. When a hospital is judged on how many patients wait more than four hours, staff learn to manage the measure, not just the patient. This is a version of Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Contracting out also made it harder to see who was responsible when things went wrong.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

State capacity decides outcomes

Economists and political scientists increasingly agree that the ability of a state to do things, not just its laws or its spending, explains much of the difference between countries.

Chapter 08

The same law, two different countries

Suppose two countries pass the same tax law. In one, the tax office has accurate records, honest staff and computers that talk to the banks. In the other, records are on paper, inspectors are badly paid and some take bribes. The first collects the tax. The second collects part of it, mostly from people too poor to bribe their way out. Same law, opposite results.

This is what researchers call state capacity: the ability to collect revenue, enforce rules and deliver services across the whole territory. It is built slowly, over decades, from things that sound dull: land registries, population records, payroll systems, trained staff. A state that cannot count its citizens cannot tax them fairly, vaccinate them or pay them a pension. Studies of economic development regularly find that the quality of government matters as much as the amount of money it spends.

Chapter 09

When delivery fails, everyone notices

Administrative failures are rarely dramatic at first. They are a website that does not work, a letter sent to the wrong address, a computer rule nobody checked. Three cases show the pattern.

A website launch, 2013

The US healthcare.gov site, the front door to a major health insurance reform, crashed on launch in October 2013. Only a tiny number of people could sign up in the first days. A policy debated for years nearly failed on software procurement.

Robodebt, Australia

From 2016 an automated system matched welfare records against averaged tax data and sent hundreds of thousands of debt notices, many of them wrong. A royal commission in 2023 called it a crude and cruel scheme. The government settled a class action for well over a billion Australian dollars.

Childcare benefits, Netherlands

The Dutch tax authority wrongly treated thousands of families as fraudsters, demanding back large sums. Dual nationality was used as a risk factor. The scandal led the whole cabinet to resign in January 2021.

None of these was caused by a bad law. Each was caused by how the law was administered: weak technical oversight, rules applied without human judgement, and officials who did not or could not raise the alarm. Hurricane Katrina in 2005 (see Vol 23) adds a fourth lesson. When responsibility is split between agencies and nobody is clearly in charge, a crisis exposes it within hours.

Chapter 10

Trust, corruption and the digital state

Most people form their view of the state from a few encounters: registering a birth, renewing a passport, dealing with the police. If those encounters are fair and fast, trust in government tends to be higher. If they require a bribe or a contact, people conclude that the whole system is rigged, and they are less willing to pay taxes or obey rules. Good administration and public trust reinforce each other.

Two small countries are often cited as examples. Singapore built a reputation for a clean civil service after independence, partly through strict anti-corruption enforcement and partly by paying senior officials salaries close to the private sector. The approach is debated, but the results on corruption rankings are consistently strong. Estonia, after regaining independence in 1991, built government almost from scratch around digital identity. Its data exchange system, X-Road, has linked public databases since 2001, so a citizen gives information once and agencies share it with permission. Nearly all its public services can be used online.

Context matters

What works in a small, wealthy, high-trust state does not transfer automatically to a large, federal or low-trust one. Reformers who copy a model without its surrounding conditions often get the form without the results. Scholars sometimes call this isomorphic mimicry: looking like a capable state without becoming one.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every strength of bureaucracy has a shadow

Red tape is annoying. It is also what stops an official from treating you differently because of your name. The trade-offs are real on both sides.

One famous joke about bureaucracy is Parkinson's law, from an essay by the British historian C. Northcote Parkinson in The Economist in 1955: "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion". He used it to mock the steady growth of official staff regardless of workload. It is satire, not a measured finding, but it names a real tendency.

Chapter 12

Three ideas of a good administration

The debate over the last century has moved through three broad models. Most real governments mix all three.

ModelThe citizen is…Good atWeak at
Traditional bureaucracy (Weber)A subject of rules with rightsFairness, legality, honestySpeed, innovation, responsiveness
New Public Management (1980s on)A customerEfficiency, clarity of targetsJoined-up problems, public values beyond price
Networked or "public value" governance (1990s on)A partner and co-producerComplex problems needing many organisationsClear accountability: when everyone is responsible, nobody is

No model wins outright. A tax office should be rule-bound and impersonal. A programme to reduce homelessness may need flexibility and partnerships. The skill is matching the model to the task.

Chapter 14

The live fight: who controls the civil service?

The oldest question in this topic has returned. Should permanent officials be protected from political pressure, or should elected leaders be able to hire and fire them more freely? Supporters of more control argue that voters elect a government to change things, and that an entrenched bureaucracy can block that. Defenders of protection argue that a civil service which can be purged after every election will tell ministers what they want to hear, and will drift back towards the spoils system.

The debate is sharpest in the United States. In 2020 an executive order tried to create a new category of federal employees who could be dismissed more easily. It was cancelled in 2021, then revived in 2025, alongside large cuts to the federal workforce. Legal and political challenges were still running as of 2026. Similar arguments, in milder forms, appear in Britain over the role of special advisers and in several European countries over appointments to senior posts.

Whatever the outcome, the underlying lesson holds. A state is only as good as the people and systems that carry out its decisions. Law, the next topics show, increasingly depends on specialised regulators, which are themselves a branch of public administration.

The core idea

Public administration is the permanent machinery that turns political decisions into services. Its classic form is Weber's bureaucracy: written rules, hierarchy, merit and impersonality. Its quality, called state capacity, explains why the same law works in one country and fails in another.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Britain's merit-based civil service traces to the Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854; the US Pendleton Act followed in 1883.
  • A new US president fills roughly 4,000 political posts; a new British government brings only a small number of advisers.
  • China selected officials by examination for well over a thousand years, until 1905.
The common mistake

Thinking that passing a law solves a problem. Delivery is where most policies succeed or fail, and the people at the front line shape what a law really means.

The words, in plain words
Public administration
Running public services and carrying out government decisions, and the study of how to do it well.
Civil service
The permanent, professional staff of central government.
Bureaucracy
An organisation run by written rules, hierarchy and trained officials. Weber's term, not originally an insult.
Street-level bureaucrat
A front-line official, such as a teacher or benefit assessor, who applies rules to individual people.
Spoils system
Giving government jobs to supporters of the winning party.
Merit recruitment
Hiring officials through open competition and qualifications.
Politics–administration dichotomy
The idea that politicians decide what to do and administrators decide how.
Agency
A semi-independent public body that runs one service or task.
Decentralisation
Moving power and services from the central government to regions and towns.
Supreme audit institution
The national body that checks public accounts and whether spending achieved its aims.
New Public Management
Reforms from the 1980s that brought business tools, targets and contracts into government.
State capacity
A state's practical ability to tax, enforce rules and deliver services everywhere it governs.

When a measure becomes a target, people manage the measure and it stops telling the truth.
