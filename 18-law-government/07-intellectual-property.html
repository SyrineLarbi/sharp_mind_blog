Intellectual property. A temporary monopoly, granted to reward ideas that would otherwise be free to copy.

If someone steals your bicycle, you no longer have it. If someone copies your song, your novel or your invention, you still have it, and so do they. Ideas are strange things to own. Yet much of the world's wealth now lies in them: medicines, software, films, brands, chip designs. Intellectual property law is the set of rules that lets creators control copying for a while, so that creating is worth the effort. Where to draw those rules, and for how long, is one of the hardest balancing acts in law, and artificial intelligence has just made it harder.

Question 1 · What is it?

Property rights in things you cannot touch

Intellectual property, usually shortened to IP, is a group of legal rights that give creators and businesses control over certain uses of their ideas, works, names and secrets.

Chapter 01

Lighting a candle from another's flame

In 1813 Thomas Jefferson, who as a young official had helped run the first American patent system, wrote a letter about why ideas are different from land or cattle.

"He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."

Letter to Isaac McPherson · Thomas Jefferson, 1813

Economists have a word for this. An idea is non-rival: one person's use does not reduce anyone else's. It is also hard to exclude people from it: once a book is published or a machine is sold, anyone can copy it. Those two features cause a problem. Inventing a new medicine may cost hundreds of millions, while copying the finished pill costs very little. If anyone could copy it at once, no one would pay for the expensive first version, and the medicine might never be made.

Intellectual property is the legal answer. It creates an artificial scarcity: for a limited time, only the right-holder may make, copy or sell the thing, or license others to do so. The price of that reward is paid by everyone else, through higher prices and restricted use, until the protection ends and the idea becomes free for all.

There are smaller rights too, such as registered designs, which protect the look of a product, and plant variety rights for new breeds of crops. But the four above cover most of what matters. The key difference to remember: a patent protects the idea of an invention itself, while copyright protects only the particular expression of an idea, not the idea underneath.

Chapter 03

Where the idea came from

The Republic of Venice passed a patent law in 1474 that gave inventors of new devices ten years of protection against copying. England's Statute of Monopolies of 1624 abolished most royal monopolies, which kings had sold to favourites, but kept an exception for "the true and first inventor" of new manufactures. That compromise, ban monopolies in general but allow limited ones for genuine invention, is still the logic of patent law.

Copyright followed the printing press. Once books could be copied cheaply, printers wanted protection against rivals reprinting their editions. England's Statute of Anne of 1710 is usually called the first copyright law. Importantly, it gave rights to authors, for a limited term, rather than to the printers' guild. The American constitution of 1787 gave Congress power to grant such rights "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts", which states the purpose openly: the reward is a means to encourage creation, not an end in itself.

International agreements followed as trade grew: the Paris Convention of 1883 for patents and trademarks, and the Berne Convention of 1886 for copyright, which established that works are protected automatically without registration. In 1994 the TRIPS agreement, part of the deal that created the World Trade Organization, required all its members to adopt minimum standards of IP protection, a major extension of rich-country rules to the rest of the world.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four different bargains between creators and the public

Each type of IP strikes its own deal. Patents trade disclosure for exclusivity. Copyright gives long protection but only for expression. Trademarks protect consumers as much as owners. Trade secrets offer no protection once the secret is out.

Chapter 04

Patents: tell the world, and you get twenty years

To get a patent, an invention must normally be new (not already public anywhere in the world), involve an inventive step (not obvious to a skilled person in the field) and be capable of industrial application, meaning it can actually be made or used. In the United States the same tests are called novelty, non-obviousness and utility. Some things cannot be patented at all, such as scientific discoveries, mathematical methods and, in Europe, most software "as such".

The patent itself is a bargain. The inventor must disclose the invention in enough detail that a skilled person could reproduce it, and the document is published. In return the inventor gets the exclusive right, for about 20 years from filing, to stop others making, using or selling it. When the patent expires, the published description becomes free for everyone. Without patents, inventors might keep their methods secret instead.

The heart of a patent is its claims: precisely worded sentences that define what is protected. Patent disputes are mostly fights over what the claims cover. Patents are also national or regional. A US patent does not protect you in France. Europe added a single "unitary patent" and a Unified Patent Court, which began operating in June 2023 for most EU countries, to reduce the cost of protecting and enforcing inventions country by country.

Chapter 05

Copyright: automatic, long, and full of exceptions

Copyright arises automatically as soon as an original work is fixed: when you write the poem, record the song or save the code. No registration is needed in most countries. It gives the owner the exclusive right to copy, distribute, perform, adapt and publish the work. Many systems, especially in continental Europe, also grant moral rights, such as the right to be named as the author and to object to distortion of the work, which the author keeps even after selling the economic rights.

The crucial limit is the idea-expression distinction. Copyright protects your words, not your plot idea. Anyone may write a story about a boy wizard at a magic school. No one may copy the text of the Harry Potter books. The same principle explains why recipes, as lists of ingredients, and facts generally cannot be owned through copyright.

Copyright lasts much longer than patents: in the EU, the US and many other countries, the author's life plus 70 years. Terms have been extended repeatedly. The United States extended them by 20 years in 1998, an act critics nicknamed the "Mickey Mouse Protection Act" because the earliest Mickey cartoons were due to expire. The 1928 cartoon Steamboat Willie finally entered the US public domain on 1 January 2024. The public domain is the set of works no one owns, free for anyone to use, and it is where every protected work eventually ends up.

Every system also allows some uses without permission. The United States has a flexible fair use doctrine, which weighs factors such as the purpose of the use, how much is taken and the effect on the market. The UK has narrower fair dealing for listed purposes like criticism, news reporting and research. The EU has a fixed list of permitted exceptions, including quotation, parody and education.

Chapter 06

Trademarks: protecting the customer's memory

A trademark is any sign that tells customers where a product comes from: a name, a logo, a slogan, a shape, sometimes a colour. Its purpose is partly to protect the business, and partly to protect consumers from being fooled into buying a copy. A trademark can last forever, as long as it is used and renewed, because the danger of confusion does not expire.

Trademarks have a strange weakness: success can destroy them. If a brand name becomes the ordinary word for the product itself, it may become generic and lose protection. "Aspirin" was a Bayer trademark that became generic in the United States in the early twentieth century, though it remains protected in some other countries. "Escalator" and "thermos" followed a similar path in the US. That is why companies run campaigns asking people to say "tissue" rather than a brand name, or insist on phrases like "search with" rather than using their name as a verb. The Branding topic covers the business side.

Chapter 07

Trade secrets: protection by silence

Some companies choose not to patent at all. The formula for Coca-Cola has famously been kept as a trade secret for more than a century. A patent would have expired long ago and published the recipe; secrecy has no time limit. The law protects trade secrets against theft, breach of confidence and industrial spying. The EU harmonised its rules in a 2016 directive, and the United States passed the Defend Trade Secrets Act the same year.

The weakness is that a trade secret gives no protection against someone who discovers the same thing independently, or works it out by taking your product apart, which is called reverse engineering. So companies choose: patent what competitors could reverse-engineer, and keep secret what they cannot.

Chapter 08

Enforcement: licences, lawsuits and trolls

An IP right is valuable mainly because it can be enforced in court, or traded. Owners often do not use their rights themselves but license them: they let others use the invention or work in return for payments called royalties. Universities license patents to companies; music publishers license songs to streaming services; brands license their names for clothing in other countries.

Enforcement is expensive. A major patent case in the United States can cost millions in legal fees. This has produced a controversial business model: firms that buy patents not to make anything but to demand licence fees from companies that might be infringing. Critics call them patent trolls; the neutral term is non-practising entities. Defenders say they help small inventors who could not afford to sue a giant company themselves. Critics say they tax innovation, because paying is often cheaper than fighting even a weak claim.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicines, music and the price of access

IP decides who profits from innovation, how much new drugs cost, which songs musicians may borrow from, and whether poorer countries can afford technology.

Chapter 09

The medicine dilemma

The pharmaceutical industry is the clearest case for patents. Developing a new drug takes many years and very large sums, and most candidates fail in trials. Once a drug is approved, however, competitors can copy the molecule cheaply. Without patent protection, few companies would invest. When a patent expires, cheaper generic versions appear and prices usually fall sharply.

The same system creates painful conflicts. In the late 1990s, effective HIV drugs existed but cost thousands of dollars a year per patient, far beyond what most people in southern Africa could pay, while the epidemic was killing huge numbers there. When South Africa passed a law to make cheaper medicines easier to obtain, a group of drug companies sued, then dropped the case in 2001 amid a global outcry. The same year, WTO members adopted the Doha Declaration, confirming that countries may issue compulsory licences, allowing generic production of patented medicines in a public health emergency. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a proposal to waive vaccine patents led to a narrower WTO decision in 2022. Supporters of strong patents argued that the vaccines existed at all because of the incentives patents create; critics argued that knowledge built partly with public money was being rationed by price.

Chapter 10

Music, borrowing and the limits of originality

All creative work borrows. Musicians reuse chord progressions, rhythms and styles that have been shared for centuries. Copyright must decide when borrowing becomes copying. In 2015 an American jury found that the song "Blurred Lines" infringed Marvin Gaye's "Got to Give It Up", even though the melody and lyrics were different, in a verdict many musicians criticised for treating the "feel" of a song as property. In 2023 another jury found that Ed Sheeran's "Thinking Out Loud" did not infringe Gaye's "Let's Get It On", partly because the shared chord progression was a common building block. The line between influence and theft is drawn case by case, and juries do not always draw it in the same place.

The internet put enormous pressure on copyright. File-sharing services such as Napster, launched in 1999 and shut down after lawsuits, let millions copy music free. Recorded-music revenues fell for years. The industry recovered largely through licensed streaming, which made legal access cheaper and easier than piracy. That history matters: convenience and price often protect creative work better than lawsuits alone.

Chapter 11

IP as strategy and geopolitics

For technology companies, patents are weapons and shields. Smartphone makers fought each other across many countries in the 2010s; in 2012 a US jury awarded Apple about a billion dollars against Samsung over design and feature patents, an amount later reduced, and the companies settled in 2018. Firms build large patent portfolios partly so they can counter-sue anyone who sues them.

Between countries, IP is part of trade policy. Rich countries, which own most patents and copyrights, have pushed for stronger protection through trade agreements. Developing countries have often argued that today's rich economies industrialised by copying freely and now want to deny others the same path. Complaints about forced technology transfer and IP theft have been a central issue in trade tensions between the United States and China. IP rules decide where the profits from global innovation flow, which is why they are negotiated as hard as tariffs.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Incentive against access

Every IP right is a trade-off between encouraging new work and letting people use existing work. The question is never whether to have IP, but how much.

How strong the incentive effect really is remains debated. Surveys of companies have long found that patents matter a great deal in pharmaceuticals and chemicals, but much less in many other industries, where being first to market, secrecy and reputation matter more. Some economists argue that IP protection has grown far beyond what is needed to encourage innovation. Others argue that weakening it would reduce investment in exactly the high-risk research society most needs.

Alternatives exist alongside the system. Open-source software, such as Linux, uses copyright in reverse: licences that allow anyone to use and change the code, often on condition that they share their changes too. Much of the internet now runs on it. Prizes, public research funding and open-access science are other ways to reward discovery without monopoly. IP is one tool for encouraging innovation, not the only one.

Chapter 14

How much is enough?

The AI debate revives the oldest question in IP. Generative models learn from enormous amounts of human work, much of it protected, and can then produce new text, images and music in seconds. Creators argue that their work is being used without permission or payment to build products that compete with them. AI developers argue that learning from existing work is what every human artist does, and that requiring permission for every item would make the technology impossible. Both sides are partly right, which is why the question is so hard. The Generative AI and AI regulation topics follow it further.

Behind the technical arguments lies the bargain stated in the American constitution: rights for "limited Times" in order to promote progress. Every few decades a new technology, from the printing press to the photocopier to the internet, forces societies to renegotiate that bargain. The lasting principle is that IP is a means, not an end: protection should be as strong as needed to encourage creation, and no stronger. Where that point lies is a political choice each generation makes again.

The core idea

Ideas are non-rival and easy to copy, so the law creates temporary exclusive rights to reward creation. Patents protect inventions for about 20 years in exchange for disclosure. Copyright protects expression, automatically, for life plus 70 years. Trademarks protect brand signs for as long as they are used. Trade secrets last as long as they stay secret.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Venice patent law 1474; Statute of Anne 1710; Berne Convention 1886.
  • Patents: 20 years from filing. Copyright: life + 70 in the EU and US.
  • Steamboat Willie entered the US public domain on 1 January 2024.
The common mistake

Thinking copyright protects ideas. It protects only the expression of an idea. Plots, styles, facts and methods are free; the specific words, notes and images are not.

The words, in plain words
Intellectual property (IP)
Legal rights over creations of the mind: inventions, works, brands and secrets.
Non-rival
Usable by many people at once without being used up, as an idea is.
Patent
An exclusive right over an invention for a limited time, in return for publishing how it works.

The requirement that an invention not be obvious to a skilled person in the field.

Copyright

Moral rights

Generic

Trade secret
Valuable confidential business information protected as long as it stays secret.
Licence
Permission from a right-holder to use their IP, usually in return for royalties.
Compulsory licence
Permission granted by a government, without the owner's consent, to use a patent, for example for medicines.
