Technology law. Old principles, new machines, and a race the law usually starts late.

When someone posts a lie about you online, can you sue the website? When a hacker in one country attacks a hospital in another, whose law applies? When a software update crashes millions of computers, who pays? None of these questions existed fifty years ago. Technology law is the set of answers that courts and parliaments have built, often in a hurry, by stretching old ideas such as publishing, trespass and product safety to fit machines their authors never imagined.

Question 1 · What is it?

Not one law, but many laws meeting one technology

Technology law is less a single field than a crossroads, where contract, criminal, speech, product and competition law all meet the internet and software.

Chapter 01

Punished for trying to be careful

In the early 1990s two American online services were sued for things their users had posted. In 1991 a court found that CompuServe was not responsible, because it did not review the content; it was like a bookshop that cannot read every book. In 1995 a New York court found that Prodigy was responsible, because it advertised itself as a family-friendly service and removed offensive posts. By moderating, it had acted like a newspaper editor, and a publisher answers for what it publishes.

The result was perverse. A service that tried to remove harmful content took on more legal risk than one that did nothing. In 1996 Congress fixed this with a short provision, Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act. It says that an online service shall not be treated as the publisher of content provided by its users, and that it may moderate in good faith without losing that protection. A 2019 book by the lawyer Jeff Kosseff called its key sentence "the twenty-six words that created the internet".

That story shows the whole field in miniature. An old rule (publishers are liable) met a new technology (millions of users posting instantly), produced an absurd result, and lawmakers wrote a new rule. The new rule then shaped the industry for the next thirty years.

Chapter 02

"The law of the horse" and "code is law"

In 1996 the American judge Frank Easterbrook argued that there was no such thing as "cyberlaw", any more than a "law of the horse". Horses are involved in sales, injuries and licensing, he said, but studying them together teaches nothing about the general principles. Better to learn contract and property law properly, and apply them to computers.

The legal scholar Lawrence Lessig answered in his 1999 book Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace. He argued that behaviour online is regulated by four forces: law, social norms, markets, and architecture, meaning the design of the software itself. A website that simply does not let you copy a file controls you more effectively than any statute. His summary was that "code is law": whoever writes the software writes many of the rules.

Both views have survived. Most technology cases are decided by applying old principles. But Lessig was right that design choices, such as default settings, encryption and recommendation systems, now do much of the work law used to do, and that law increasingly targets design directly.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Safe harbours, duties of care, crimes and borders

Four mechanisms do most of the work: protecting intermediaries, imposing duties on the largest ones, criminalising attacks, and deciding which country's courts are in charge.

Chapter 04

Safe harbours: the deal that built the web

An intermediary is any service that carries, stores or organises other people's content: internet providers, hosting companies, social networks, search engines, marketplaces. Almost every legal system gives them some protection, called a safe harbour, because without it nobody could afford to host anyone else's words.

United States: broad immunity

Section 230 protects platforms from most lawsuits over users' content, whether or not they knew about it, with exceptions such as federal crimes and intellectual property. For copyright, a separate 1998 law, the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, gives protection only if the platform removes infringing material when notified.

European Union: conditional protection

The e-Commerce Directive of 2000 protects a host only if it does not know about illegal content, and only if it acts quickly to remove it once it is told. This is called notice and takedown. Platforms are not required to monitor everything in general.

The difference matters. In the US a platform is usually protected even if it is told about harmful user content and leaves it up. In Europe, knowledge brings responsibility. Both systems protect intermediaries, but Europe makes the protection depend on behaviour.

Chapter 05

From immunity to duties: the new platform laws

By the late 2010s many governments believed safe harbours alone were not enough. Platforms had become huge, and their recommendation systems were not neutral pipes; they chose what billions of people saw. The new approach keeps the safe harbour for individual posts but adds duties about systems.

The EU's Digital Services Act, adopted in 2022 and fully applicable from 2024, is the most ambitious. It sets layered obligations. Every intermediary must have clear terms and a way to report illegal content. Online marketplaces must check the identity of traders. The very largest platforms and search engines, those with more than 45 million users in the EU, must assess and reduce systemic risks such as the spread of illegal content or harm to minors, open their data to vetted researchers, and submit to independent audits. Fines can reach 6% of worldwide annual turnover.

Britain's Online Safety Act of 2023 takes a similar path, centred on a duty of care towards users, especially children, and enforced by the communications regulator Ofcom. Critics on one side say these laws give governments too much influence over lawful speech. Critics on the other say they are too slow and too weak. The shift is from asking "who posted it?" to asking "how was the system designed?"

Chapter 06

Cybercrime and cybersecurity

The first computer crime laws were written because ordinary laws did not fit. Theft requires taking something, but copying a file leaves the original in place. Trespass requires entering land. So countries created specific offences: the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986, Britain's Computer Misuse Act of 1990. Their core offence is unauthorised access, entering a computer system you have no right to enter.

Because attackers cross borders, states also cooperate by treaty. The Council of Europe's Budapest Convention of 2001 is the main one, joined by dozens of countries well beyond Europe. In 2024 the UN General Assembly adopted a separate global cybercrime convention, which human rights groups criticised for vague definitions that authoritarian governments could misuse.

The newer trend is cybersecurity law: duties to prevent attacks, not just punish them. The EU's NIS2 directive requires firms in critical sectors such as energy, health and transport to manage cyber risk and report serious incidents quickly. Its Cyber Resilience Act of 2024 will require connected products, from routers to smart toys, to meet basic security standards and receive security updates. Ransomware attacks on hospitals and pipelines (see Vol 14) drove much of this.

Chapter 07

Whose law applies?

In 2000 a French court ordered the American company Yahoo! to block French users from auctions of Nazi memorabilia, which are illegal in France but protected speech in the United States. Yahoo argued that a French court could not police an American website. The court disagreed, reasoning that the site was visible and harmful in France. Yahoo removed the auctions. A website is everywhere at once, so in principle every country's law can reach it.

Courts usually limit this by asking whether a site deliberately targets a country, for example by using its language or currency. But conflicts remain. In 2014 the EU's top court recognised what became known as the "right to be forgotten" against search engines (see Privacy law), and later ruled that it applies within the EU, not worldwide. Data held abroad raises similar fights. A US law of 2018, the CLOUD Act, lets American authorities demand data that American firms store overseas, under certain conditions. Many countries respond with data localisation rules requiring that some data be kept on their own territory.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Security, speech and responsibility

Technology law decides how private your messages are, what you are allowed to say online, and who answers when software fails.

Chapter 08

The encryption standoff

After a mass shooting in San Bernardino, California, in December 2015, the FBI recovered one of the attackers' iPhones, which was locked and encrypted. In 2016 a court ordered Apple to write special software to help unlock it. Apple refused publicly, arguing that any tool that weakened its security for one phone would endanger every user. The confrontation ended without a ruling when the FBI paid an outside party to get into the phone.

The underlying dispute has never been settled. Police and intelligence agencies say strong end-to-end encryption, where only sender and receiver can read a message, lets criminals "go dark". Security experts answer that there is no way to build a door only the good side can use; a weakness for police is a weakness for hackers and hostile states. In 2025 Apple withdrew one of its strongest encryption options for users in Britain after the government reportedly sought access under its surveillance law. Every country that tries to get around encryption runs into the same mathematics.

Chapter 09

Speech, platforms and the state

Platform rules now decide much of what can be said in public. That gives technology law a constitutional weight. Systems differ sharply. The US First Amendment strongly limits the government's power to control speech, including by pressuring platforms, and the Supreme Court has treated platforms' own content choices as a form of protected editorial judgement. European systems allow more limits, for example on hate speech and Holocaust denial, and Germany's 2017 network enforcement law required large platforms to remove clearly illegal content within twenty-four hours of a complaint.

Clashes between platforms and states have become frequent. In 2024 Brazil's Supreme Court ordered the social network X blocked nationwide for over a month after it refused to comply with orders, including naming a legal representative in the country. Supporters saw a state enforcing its law on a foreign company. Critics saw a single judge with too much power over speech. The live question everywhere is who should decide what stays up: the company, the government, the courts, or the users.

Chapter 10

When software breaks, who pays?

For decades software has been sold almost entirely "as is". Licence terms exclude most liability, and courts in many countries have been unsure whether software is a "product" at all under product-safety law. A car maker answers for a faulty brake. A software maker has usually not answered for a faulty update.

In July 2024 a faulty update from the security company CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers worldwide, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals and banks. The incident sharpened an existing debate. The EU's revised Product Liability Directive, adopted in 2024, explicitly treats software as a product, so people harmed by defective software can claim compensation without proving the maker was careless. The long era in which code carried less responsibility than hardware is ending, at least in Europe.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Light rules built the internet. Heavier ones are trying to tame it

The light-touch model gave us open platforms and fast innovation. It also left harms that law was slow to address.

Chapter 12

The pacing problem

Technology changes in months. Laws take years. Scholars call this the pacing problem. In 1980 the British researcher David Collingridge described a sharper version, now called the Collingridge dilemma. When a technology is new, it is easy to shape but we do not yet know its effects. By the time the effects are clear, the technology is embedded everywhere and very hard to change.

Lawmakers have three main responses. They can write technology-neutral laws, which regulate outcomes ("do not deceive consumers") rather than specific machines, so the law ages better. They can use principles plus regulators, leaving details to agencies that can update guidance quickly. Or they can experiment with sandboxes and sunset clauses. None of these solves the dilemma. The choice is between acting early on uncertain evidence and acting late on a settled industry.

Chapter 14

Digital sovereignty and the splinternet

The internet was designed as one network (see Vol 22). Its law is fragmenting. China has long run a separately controlled internet. Russia has built legal and technical tools to isolate its own. The EU speaks of digital sovereignty: the ability to set its own rules and to depend less on foreign firms for cloud services, chips and platforms. The US has used export controls and national-security reviews, including a 2024 law forcing the sale or ban of the TikTok app, whose application was repeatedly delayed.

The optimistic view is that different democracies can learn from each other's experiments, with Europe testing duties and the US testing freedom. The pessimistic view is a splinternet, in which a service legal in one region is blocked in the next, and users see different internets depending on where they stand. The most likely result is in between: shared technical standards, with increasingly national rules on top. Technology law is becoming part of foreign policy. Its newest and fastest-moving branch is the subject of the final topic.

The core idea

Technology law applies old principles, such as publisher liability, trespass and product safety, to new machines. Its central compromise is the safe harbour for intermediaries, now being supplemented by duties about system design. Its constant difficulty is the pacing problem.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Section 230 dates from 1996; the EU e-Commerce Directive from 2000.
  • The Digital Services Act's strictest duties apply to services with over 45 million EU users, with fines up to 6% of global turnover.
  • The 2024 CrowdStrike failure crashed about 8.5 million computers.
The common mistake

Thinking the internet is lawless. Almost every online act is covered by some law; the hard problems are enforcement across borders and deciding which law applies.

Section 230
The 1996 US provision that shields online services from most liability for users' posts.
Notice and takedown

The EU law setting layered duties for online services, strictest for the largest.
Duty of care
A legal obligation to take reasonable steps to protect others from harm.
Unauthorised access
Entering a computer system without permission, the core computer crime.
End-to-end encryption
Encryption where only the sender and recipient can read the message.
Jurisdiction
A court's or state's legal authority over a person, place or dispute.
Data localisation
Rules requiring certain data to be stored inside a country.
Pacing problem
The gap between how fast technology changes and how fast law can respond.
Collingridge dilemma
New technologies are easy to shape but poorly understood; by the time they are understood, they are hard to change.
Splinternet
An internet divided into regions with different rules and blocked services.
