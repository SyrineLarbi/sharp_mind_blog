Courts. The place where a society agrees to settle a fight with reasons instead of force.

Two neighbours argue about a fence. A company says a supplier broke its contract. The state accuses a man of theft. In each case, someone has to decide who is right, and the loser has to accept it. Courts are the institution that does this. They have no army of their own and rarely any money to spare, yet in a working legal system people obey them. Understanding how courts are built, and why they are trusted, explains a great deal about how a country really works.

Question 1 · What is it?

An impartial third party with the power to decide

A court is a body, set up by the state, that hears a dispute, finds the facts, applies the law and gives a decision that both sides are bound to obey.

Chapter 01

From feud to judgment

For most of human history, a serious wrong was answered by the victim's family. If someone killed your brother, your clan killed one of theirs, and the cycle could run for generations. Early legal codes, such as those of medieval Iceland or Anglo-Saxon England, tried to break the cycle by setting a price for each injury, paid as compensation instead of revenge. Someone still had to decide whether the wrong had happened and how much was owed. That someone was the beginning of a court.

The basic idea has not changed. Two parties disagree. Instead of fighting, they bring the dispute to a third party who has no stake in the outcome. That third party listens to both, decides, and the decision is backed by the power of the state. The impartiality is the whole point. A judge who is related to one side, or owns shares in the company being sued, is expected to step aside. Lawyers call this recusal.

There is an old principle, expressed in Latin as nemo iudex in causa sua: no one should be a judge in their own cause. A second principle travels with it, audi alteram partem: hear the other side. Together they are the core of what English lawyers call natural justice, and almost every legal system recognises them in some form.

The split between criminal and civil courts is the one to remember. In a criminal case, the state accuses a person and can punish them. In a civil case, one party claims something from another, usually money or an order to act. The next two topics take each side in turn.

Chapter 03

The ladder: trials and appeals

Courts are arranged in a hierarchy. A case starts in a court of first instance, where the evidence is heard, witnesses give testimony and the facts are decided. A party who thinks the result was wrong can usually appeal to a higher court. At the top sits a supreme court, which hears only a small number of cases, usually chosen because they raise legal questions of general importance.

A typical court ladder
                 SUPREME COURT          few cases, questions of law,
                       |                 rulings bind every court below
               COURT OF APPEAL          checks errors of law (and sometimes fact)
                /             \
     CRIMINAL TRIAL COURT   CIVIL TRIAL COURT    hears witnesses, finds facts,
                                                 gives the first judgment

Appeal courts normally do not rehear the whole case. They mostly look for errors of law: the judge misread a statute, admitted evidence that should have been excluded, or gave the jury wrong instructions. Facts found at trial are usually respected, because the trial court saw the witnesses in person. France's highest ordinary court, the Cour de cassation, shows the idea in its name: casser means to break. It does not decide the dispute itself. It "breaks" a judgment that applied the law wrongly and sends the case back to be decided again.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Two legal families, two styles of trial

Courts everywhere decide disputes, but they do it in recognisably different traditions. The two largest are common law and civil law, and they shape everything from who asks the questions to what a judge's ruling means for the future.

Chapter 04

Common law and civil law

The common law tradition grew up in England after the Norman conquest of 1066. Royal judges travelled the country deciding cases, and over centuries their decisions built up a body of law "common" to the whole kingdom. A key principle emerged: courts should follow earlier decisions on the same question. This is called precedent, or in Latin stare decisis, "to stand by what has been decided". Common law spread with the British Empire and is used today in England and Wales, the United States, Canada (except Quebec's private law), Australia, India, Nigeria and many other countries.

The civil law tradition descends from Roman law, rediscovered in medieval universities, and was transformed by great national codes. France's Civil Code of 1804, often called the Napoleonic Code, organised private law into one clear book of numbered articles. Germany's Civil Code, the BGB, took effect in 1900. These codes were copied or adapted across continental Europe, Latin America, much of Africa and parts of Asia. In this tradition, the main source of law is the written code, and the judge's job is to apply it. The words "civil law" here mean the legal family, not the opposite of criminal law, which is a frequent source of confusion.

Many countries are mixed. Scotland, South Africa, Quebec and Louisiana blend the two. Many Muslim-majority countries combine civil codes with Islamic law in family matters. And the two families have moved closer: common law countries now have huge amounts of written statute, and civil law judges pay close attention to what their highest courts have said before.

Chapter 05

Adversarial and inquisitorial trials

A second difference concerns who drives the trial. In the adversarial model, typical of common law countries, the two sides each present their case and test the other's. The lawyers choose the witnesses and do the questioning, including cross-examination, the hostile questioning of the other side's witnesses. The judge acts mainly as an umpire who keeps the rules and, where there is no jury, decides at the end.

In the inquisitorial model, more common in civil law countries, the judge takes the lead. In serious French criminal cases, an investigating judge, the juge d'instruction, has traditionally directed the inquiry and built a written file, though most cases are now investigated by prosecutors. At trial, the presiding judge questions the accused and the witnesses first, with lawyers adding questions afterwards. German judges also lead the questioning.

Each model rests on a theory of truth. The adversarial theory says truth is most likely to emerge from a fair contest between two motivated sides. The inquisitorial theory says it is most likely to emerge from a neutral official who investigates. In practice, both systems have borrowed from each other, and neither is pure.

Chapter 06

Juries, lay judges and the plea bargain

In England and the United States, serious criminal trials are traditionally decided by a jury: a group of ordinary citizens, usually twelve, chosen at random. The judge decides questions of law; the jury decides the facts and the verdict. The US constitution guarantees jury trial in criminal cases and many civil ones. The idea is that the community, not just the state's officials, should decide whether someone is guilty.

Many civil law countries also involve citizens, in different ways. France's courts for the most serious crimes, the cours d'assises, combine professional judges and citizen jurors who deliberate together. Germany uses lay judges, citizens who sit beside professional judges and vote with them.

Here is the surprise. In the United States, the jury trial is now the exception. The large majority of criminal convictions, more than nine in ten in federal courts, come from guilty pleas, usually through a plea bargain: the defendant admits guilt in exchange for a lighter charge or sentence. Supporters say the system could not function otherwise. Critics say that the gap between the sentence offered for pleading guilty and the sentence risked at trial can pressure even innocent people to plead. Other countries have adopted versions of plea deals too, including France since 2004.

Chapter 07

How a judge decides

Whatever the system, a decision has two parts. First, the court finds the facts: what actually happened. Second, it applies the law to those facts. Two rules govern the first step. The burden of proof says who has to prove things. In a criminal case it is the prosecution, because the accused is presumed innocent. The standard of proof says how sure the court must be. Criminal guilt in common law systems must be proved "beyond reasonable doubt". In civil cases, the claimant usually needs to show only that their version is more likely than not, called the "balance of probabilities" in England. Civil law systems express the criminal standard differently, often as the judge's "inner conviction", but the aim is similar.

Then the judge gives reasons. This matters more than it seems. Written reasons let the loser understand why they lost, let an appeal court check the logic, and let everyone else know what the law now means. In common law courts, lawyers then study the reasons carefully. The part of the reasoning necessary to the decision, the ratio decidendi, binds later courts. Remarks made along the way, obiter dicta, do not. A later court that wants to avoid a precedent will often distinguish it, arguing that its facts were different in some way that matters. This is how case law grows and bends without ever formally being rewritten.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Power without an army

Courts make the rule of law real. They also make law, settle political questions and decide whether ordinary people can afford justice at all.

Chapter 08

The least dangerous branch

Alexander Hamilton, writing in 1788 in defence of the new American constitution, made an observation that every judge since has had to live with.

"The judiciary … may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."

The Federalist No. 78 · Alexander Hamilton, 1788

A court cannot raise taxes or command soldiers. Its rulings are obeyed because governments, companies and citizens accept that they should be. That acceptance is called legitimacy, and courts guard it carefully. The American psychologist Tom Tyler spent decades studying why people obey the law. His central finding, known as procedural justice, is that people are much more willing to accept a decision, even one against them, when they believe the process was fair: they were heard, treated with respect, and judged by someone neutral. Courts earn obedience less through the outcomes they give than through the way they reach them.

Chapter 09

When judges make law

In theory judges apply law; in practice, especially in common law systems, they also make it. For centuries English common law held that a husband could not be guilty of raping his wife. In 1991, in a case known as R v R, the House of Lords, then the UK's highest court, declared that this rule no longer formed part of the law. Parliament had not changed anything. The judges decided that the old rule rested on assumptions about marriage that were no longer acceptable.

Most people now applaud that ruling. But the same power makes courts political. Highest courts decide questions about abortion, same-sex marriage, elections and the powers of government. In the United States, the Supreme Court's 1973 decision in Roe v. Wade recognised a constitutional right to abortion; its 2022 decision in Dobbs overruled it and returned the question to the states. Whatever one's view on abortion, the episode showed how much depends on who sits on the court. That is why appointments to highest courts have become fierce political battles in several countries.

Chapter 10

Justice you can reach

A right you cannot afford to enforce is not much of a right. Courts are expensive. Lawyers charge by the hour, cases last months or years, and the loser often pays part of the winner's costs, as in England. Many people with a valid claim give up. Governments fund legal aid, public payment for lawyers for those who cannot pay, but in many countries it has been cut, and people increasingly represent themselves.

Delay is the other enemy. An old saying holds that justice delayed is justice denied. Some systems carry enormous backlogs: India's courts have had tens of millions of pending cases in recent years, and some criminal trials in several European countries take years to come to court. For a person held in custody while awaiting trial, delay is a punishment imposed before any verdict. The quality of a justice system depends as much on speed and cost as on the brilliance of its highest judges.

Chapter 12

When courts get it wrong

In 1975 six Irish men were convicted of the IRA pub bombings in Birmingham that killed 21 people. They became known as the Birmingham Six. The evidence included confessions they said had been beaten out of them and forensic tests later shown to be unreliable. Their first appeal failed. They spent sixteen years in prison before the Court of Appeal quashed their convictions in 1991.

That case and others like it led England to create the Criminal Cases Review Commission in 1997, an independent body that investigates possible miscarriages of justice and can send cases back to the appeal courts. Other countries have built similar bodies. The lesson is humbling. Every court system makes mistakes, so a good one is judged partly by how well it finds and corrects them. Appeals, review commissions, the duty to disclose evidence to the defence and the right to remain silent all exist because confident officials have been confidently wrong.

Chapter 14

Trust as the scarce resource

Surveys in several democracies show trust in highest courts falling and becoming split along party lines. When half the population sees the court as a team playing for the other side, Hamilton's point about "merely judgment" becomes urgent. A court that loses legitimacy has nothing else to fall back on.

Reform ideas reflect this. In the United States, proposals include fixed eighteen-year terms for Supreme Court justices, so that each president appoints a predictable number. In Europe the debates focus on how judges are selected and whether governments should control judicial councils. Each proposal tries to answer the same question: how do you choose people to judge the powerful without letting the powerful choose them? No country has a perfect answer, and it will remain one of the central questions of constitutional politics.

The core idea

Courts let disputes be settled by an impartial third party who finds the facts, applies the law and gives reasons. They are arranged in a hierarchy with appeals. The two great traditions are common law (precedent, adversarial trials) and civil law (codes, judge-led trials). Courts have no force of their own; they rely on legitimacy.

The numbers worth knowing
  • France's Civil Code: 1804. Germany's BGB: 1900.
  • More than nine in ten US federal criminal convictions come from guilty pleas.
  • The Birmingham Six served 16 years before their convictions were quashed.
The common mistake

Confusing "civil law" the legal family (codes, as in France) with "civil law" the area (private disputes, as opposed to crimes). The same words mean two different things.
