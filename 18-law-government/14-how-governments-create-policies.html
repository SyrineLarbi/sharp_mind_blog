How governments create policies. Less a machine than a crowded room where timing decides a lot.

From the outside, government policy looks like a sequence of announcements: a minister declares a new tax, a parliament votes, a rule takes effect. The inside is messier. Ideas wait years for the right moment. Experts, lobbyists, journalists, judges and civil servants all push and pull. A policy that looks perfect on paper can fail in the hands of the officials who must carry it out. Knowing how this really works makes the news easier to read and politicians' promises easier to judge.

Question 1 · What is it?

A decision, plus the tools to make it real

A public policy is a government's chosen course of action on a problem, together with the means it uses to pursue it. Laws are one tool among several.

Chapter 01

A ban that travelled

In March 2004 Ireland became the first country to ban smoking in all enclosed workplaces nationwide, including pubs. This was a country where the pub was central to social life, and many predicted the law would be ignored and would ruin the trade. Neither happened on the scale feared. Compliance was high from the start, and support for the ban grew once people had lived with it.

The idea was not new. Researchers had shown for years that second-hand smoke harmed bar staff, and some American cities and states had already acted. What made it happen in Ireland at that moment was a combination: strong evidence, a health minister willing to take on the hospitality lobby, and a framing that presented it as protecting workers rather than punishing smokers. Within a few years Norway, Scotland, England, France and many others had passed similar laws, often citing the Irish experience.

The story contains most of what this file is about. Problems often exist for years before anyone acts. Action comes when evidence, an available solution and political will line up. And once one government shows a policy can work, others copy it, a process researchers call policy diffusion.

Chapter 02

The government's toolbox

The British political scientist Christopher Hood pointed out in 1983 that, for all their variety, governments have only four basic kinds of resource to work with. They are easy to remember by the letters NATO.

ResourceWhat it meansExample tools
Nodality (information)Government sits at the centre of information networksPublic campaigns, labels, published data, warnings
AuthorityThe legal power to command and forbidLaws, bans, licences, standards, fines
Treasure (money)The power to tax and spendSubsidies, taxes, grants, benefits
OrganisationPeople, buildings and services the state runs directlySchools, hospitals, police, public companies

Most policies mix several. A plan to cut smoking might combine a ban (authority), a tax (treasure), warning labels (nodality) and free help to quit through public health services (organisation). Choosing the mix is the real craft of policy, because each tool has different costs, speeds and side effects. A more recent addition is the nudge: changing how a choice is presented, such as automatic enrolment in a pension scheme, without forbidding anything. See Behavioral economics.

Chapter 03

The policy cycle, and why it is a simplification

Textbooks describe policy-making as a cycle of stages, an approach that goes back to the American political scientist Harold Lasswell in the 1950s.

The classic policy cycle
        1. AGENDA SETTING   a problem gets attention
               |
        2. FORMULATION      options are designed and compared
               |
        3. DECISION         someone with authority chooses
               |
        4. IMPLEMENTATION   officials put it into practice
               |
        5. EVALUATION       did it work? what did it cost?
               |
        back to 1: the results create new problems

The cycle is useful as a checklist, but almost every researcher warns that real policy does not move in neat steps. Solutions often exist before the problems they are later attached to. Decisions get made during implementation. Evaluation is frequently skipped. Think of the stages as activities that happen at once and loop back, not as a production line.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Attention, design, decision, delivery

Each stage has its own logic, its own players and its own ways of going wrong.

Chapter 04

Getting onto the agenda

Governments face far more problems than they have time for. Which ones get attention? The most influential answer came from the American political scientist John Kingdon in 1984. He described three streams that flow mostly independently.

Problems

Conditions that come to be seen as problems, often through a crisis, a shocking statistic or a disaster.

Policies

Ideas developed by experts, think tanks and officials, floating around waiting for a use.

Politics

Elections, public mood, changes of government, pressure campaigns.

When the three streams come together, a policy window opens: a short period in which action is possible. People Kingdon called policy entrepreneurs, who have spent years preparing a solution, push it through. Then the window closes, often quickly, as attention moves on. This explains why crises produce sudden bursts of law, and why reformers keep detailed plans ready for years.

Other researchers, notably Frank Baumgartner and Bryan Jones, showed a related pattern: long periods of stability, broken by sudden large changes. They called it punctuated equilibrium. Most of the time policy hardly moves; occasionally it lurches.

Chapter 05

Designing the options

Once a problem is on the agenda, officials design possible responses. In well-run systems this involves three things.

Evidence. What is known about the problem, and what has worked elsewhere? Civil servants, research bodies and outside experts gather it.

Impact assessment. Many governments require a formal estimate of the costs and benefits of a major new rule before it is adopted. In the United States this has been required for significant federal regulations since the early 1980s, checked by a small office in the White House. The European Commission publishes impact assessments for major proposals. The idea is to make trade-offs explicit. The difficulty is putting numbers on things such as a life saved, clean air or lost privacy.

Consultation. Those affected are asked for their views. In the UK the government may publish a green paper to discuss options and a white paper to set out firm plans. In the US, agencies must publish proposed regulations and invite public comment before finalising them, a procedure set by a 1946 law. Consultation improves policies by surfacing problems officials missed. It also gives organised interests, which can afford to respond in detail, more influence than individual citizens.

Chapter 06

Deciding: how systems differ

Who has the final word depends on the constitution. The differences are large.

SystemWho drives policyTypical result
United Kingdom (parliamentary, usually one-party government)The cabinet, which normally controls a majority in the House of CommonsFast decisions and clear responsibility; policies can be reversed quickly after an election
United States (presidential, separated powers)Congress makes law; the president proposes, signs or vetoes; courts reviewMany veto points; major laws are rare, so presidents rely more on executive orders, which the next president can undo
Germany (parliamentary, coalitions, federal)Coalition governments bound by detailed coalition agreements; states share power through the upper houseSlower, negotiated, stable policy
France (semi-presidential)A strong president and government; much policy made by decreeDecisive, but prone to street protest; the government can force some bills through without a final vote, as with the 2023 pension reform
European UnionThe Commission proposes; Parliament and the Council of ministers must both agreeLong negotiation; once passed, rules apply across 27 countries

Political scientists use the idea of veto players: the people or bodies whose agreement is needed for change. The more veto players a system has, the harder it is to change policy, for better (stability, protection against rash decisions) and worse (gridlock). Coalitions, federalism, two strong chambers and powerful courts all add veto players.

Chapter 07

Implementation: where good ideas go to struggle

In 1973 two American political scientists, Jeffrey Pressman and Aaron Wildavsky, published a book on a federal job-creation programme in Oakland, California. Money had been approved and support was broad. Yet years later few jobs had appeared. The subtitle of their book gives the conclusion: How Great Expectations in Washington Are Dashed in Oakland. They showed that each project needed dozens of separate approvals from different agencies, and even if each was likely, the chance of all of them happening was small.

Another insight came from Michael Lipsky in 1980. He pointed out that teachers, police officers, benefits officers and nurses, whom he called street-level bureaucrats, constantly interpret rules with limited time and resources. In practice, they make policy, because the policy citizens experience is whatever those officials do. A reform that ignores their workload or incentives will look different on the ground from how it looked on paper. This is the territory of the next topic, Public administration.

Chapter 08

The other people in the room

Governments do not make policy alone.

  • Interest groups and lobbyists represent businesses, unions, professions and causes. They supply information officials need and press for their side. Reported lobbying spending at the US federal level has exceeded 4 billion dollars a year in recent years. The EU keeps a public register of lobbyists.
  • Experts and think tanks design ideas and lend them credibility.
  • The media decides which problems feel urgent.
  • Courts can block policies or force action, as in climate and human rights cases (see Courts).
  • Other governments and international bodies spread ideas and set obligations.

The risk is regulatory capture, a term made famous by the economist George Stigler in 1971: an agency comes to serve the industry it oversees, because that industry is organised, well-informed and always present, while the public is scattered. Defences include transparency, rules on officials moving to industry jobs, and giving other voices a formal role.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Policy shapes behaviour, sometimes in the intended direction

Good policy can change a whole society's habits. Bad policy can make problems worse, and the difference often lies in details nobody voted on.

Chapter 09

A tax aimed at companies, not customers

In 2016 the UK announced a levy on sugary soft drinks, in force from April 2018. Its design was the clever part. The tax had tiers: drinks above a certain sugar level paid more, those below a lower threshold paid nothing. And it was announced two years in advance.

The aim was less to make consumers pay than to make manufacturers change their recipes. Many did, before the levy even started, cutting sugar to fall below the thresholds. Studies found the average sugar content of soft drinks fell substantially, while the overall volume of soft drinks sold changed little. The policy worked mainly by changing the product, not the shopper. Whether it reduced obesity is harder to prove, and researchers are still studying it.

The lesson is that design matters as much as direction. Two taxes with the same headline can have very different effects depending on who pays, at what thresholds and with how much warning. For more on tax design, see Taxes; for food policy, see Nutrition policy.

Chapter 10

When people respond in ways nobody planned

People do not passively obey policies. They adapt. In 1989 Mexico City, struggling with air pollution, banned each car from driving one weekday, based on its number plate. A study by the economist Lucas Davis in 2008 found no measurable improvement in air quality. Many households bought a second car, often an older and dirtier one, to drive on the banned day.

On a larger scale, the United States banned the making and sale of alcohol from 1920 to 1933. Drinking fell, according to most estimates, but a huge illegal market grew, controlled by organised crime, and many people lost respect for the law. The ban was repealed by a constitutional amendment.

These are cases of unintended consequences, and they are common. They do not mean policy is pointless. They mean the question "how will people change their behaviour in response?" must be asked before a policy starts, not after. Economists sometimes summarise the general point by noting that every rule creates incentives to get around it.

Chapter 11

Testing before scaling

One response to uncertainty is to test policies the way medicines are tested. In a randomised controlled trial, some people or places get the policy and similar ones do not, chosen by chance, so differences can be credited to the policy. Development economists such as Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer, who shared the 2019 Nobel prize in economics, used this method widely on anti-poverty programmes.

Governments use it too. In 2010 the UK set up a Behavioural Insights Team, often called the "nudge unit", which tested small changes such as rewording tax reminder letters, and found some raised payments. From 2017 to 2018 Finland ran a trial giving 2,000 unemployed people a monthly payment of 560 euros with no conditions. The effect on employment was small; recipients reported better well-being and less stress.

Trials have limits. Many policies, such as an interest-rate change or a national law, cannot be randomised. What works in one place may not work in another. And a small trial may not show what happens when everyone gets the policy. Evidence improves policy but cannot replace judgment about values: a trial can show what a policy does, not whether that is what a society should want.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every way of deciding has its own failure

There is no ideal policy process, only trade-offs between speed and care, expertise and consent, boldness and caution.

Chapter 12

Weighing the approaches

ApproachStrengthWeakness
Incremental change ("muddling through")Small steps limit damage; easy to correct.Too slow for big problems; drifts with existing interests.
Big-bang reformCan break deadlock and reshape a system.Large mistakes are hard to undo.
Evidence-led policyFewer expensive failures; learning built in.Slow; evidence is often missing or disputed; can hide value choices.
Broad consultationBetter information; more legitimacy.Slow; favours organised interests.
Centralised decisionsConsistency, speed, clear accountability.Blind to local conditions.
Devolved, local decisionsFits local needs; lets places experiment.Unequal services; harder to coordinate.

The phrase "muddling through" comes from a 1959 essay by the political scientist Charles Lindblom. He argued that real policy-makers cannot compare every option fully, so they sensibly move in small steps from what already exists. Critics replied that this works only when the current policy is roughly right.

Chapter 13

Experts or voters?

A deep tension runs through modern policy-making. Many problems, from pandemics to pensions to energy grids, are technically complex. That argues for giving experts a large role. But policies involve choices about values, such as who pays, who benefits and how much risk is acceptable, which in a democracy belong to voters and their representatives.

Lean too far toward experts and policy can feel imposed by an unaccountable elite, which feeds backlash. Lean too far toward short-term opinion and policy can chase headlines and ignore long-term costs. Many of the hardest problems, such as climate change, are what the planners Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber in 1973 called wicked problems: no clear definition, no single right answer, and every attempted solution changing the problem itself.

The best systems separate the questions: experts advise on what will happen under each option, and elected politicians decide which outcome to choose, and say so openly. Independent central banks and budget watchdogs are examples of handing one narrow, technical task to experts under a mandate set by elected representatives.

Chapter 15

Reading a policy announcement

Everything above can be turned into a few questions for the next time a government announces a policy. Which tools is it using: rules, money, information or services? Who has to carry it out, and do they have the resources? How will people change their behaviour in response? How will anyone know whether it worked? And why now: what opened this window?

Policy-making will keep changing as data and AI enter government, and as citizens demand more say. But the basic picture is likely to stay the same. A policy is a bet about how the world will respond, placed by people with limited time, limited information and competing goals. The governments that do it well are not the ones that never lose bets. They are the ones that check the results honestly and change course when the evidence says so.

The core idea

A policy is a decision plus tools: information, authority, money and organisation. It reaches the agenda when problem, solution and politics line up, is shaped by evidence, consultation and interests, is decided according to the country's veto players, and succeeds or fails in implementation.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hood's four tools: nodality, authority, treasure, organisation.
  • Kingdon's three streams: problems, policies, politics.
  • Five stages of the classic cycle: agenda, formulation, decision, implementation, evaluation.
The common mistake

Treating the announcement as the policy. Most of what decides success happens afterwards, in the hands of the officials who deliver it and the people who respond to it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the UK sugar levy changed recipes before it even started? If you can, you understand how design and timing shape behaviour.

The words, in plain words
Public policy
A government's chosen course of action on a problem, and the tools it uses.
Policy cycle
The simplified sequence of agenda, formulation, decision, implementation and evaluation.
Agenda setting
The process by which a problem comes to receive serious attention.
Policy window
A short period when conditions allow a policy change.
Policy entrepreneur
A person who prepares a solution and pushes it when a window opens.
Punctuated equilibrium
Long stability broken by sudden large policy changes.
Impact assessment
A formal estimate of a proposed rule's costs and benefits.
Veto player
A person or body whose agreement is needed to change policy.
Street-level bureaucrat
A front-line official whose daily choices shape how policy is experienced.
Regulatory capture
When an agency comes to serve the industry it is meant to oversee.
Unintended consequence
An effect of a policy that its makers did not plan or expect.
Randomised controlled trial
A test in which chance decides who gets a policy, so its effect can be measured.
Wicked problem
A problem with no clear definition or final solution, changed by each attempt to solve it.
