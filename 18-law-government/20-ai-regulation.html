AI regulation. Writing rules for a technology that changes faster than the rulebook.

Artificial intelligence now helps decide who gets a job interview, a loan or a second look from the police. It writes text, makes images and imitates voices. The companies building the most powerful systems say themselves that the technology carries serious risks. Governments face a hard task: protect people from real harms without freezing a technology that may bring large benefits, and do it while the technology keeps changing under their feet. AI regulation is the youngest branch of law in this category, and the one moving fastest.

Question 1 · What is it?

Rules for building and using AI

AI regulation means the laws, standards and official guidance that govern how AI systems are developed, sold and used. Part of it is new. Much of it is old law applied to a new tool.

Chapter 01

The recruiter that learned the wrong lesson

In 2018 the news agency Reuters reported that Amazon had abandoned an experimental tool for ranking job applicants. The system had been trained on ten years of CVs submitted to the company, most of them from men, as is common in technology. It learned that pattern. According to the report, it marked down CVs that included the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain". Engineers tried to correct it, but could not be sure it would not find other ways to discriminate, and the project was dropped.

Nobody had programmed the tool to discriminate. It learned from history, and history was unequal. That case captures why lawmakers became interested. An AI system can break rules that already exist, such as the ban on sex discrimination in hiring, without any person intending it, and in ways that are hard to see from outside. The legal questions follow at once. Who is responsible: the developer, the company using it, or nobody? How would a rejected applicant ever find out? And what should be checked before such a tool is used on real people?

Chapter 02

Two layers: old law and new law

A common mistake is to think AI is unregulated until a special AI law passes. In fact, existing law already applies. Discrimination law covers a biased hiring tool. Data protection law, such as Europe's GDPR, covers the personal data used to train and run models, and already gives people some rights over purely automated decisions (see Privacy law). Consumer law forbids misleading claims about what a product can do. Copyright law applies to training data and outputs. Product liability can apply when a system causes injury.

On top of this sits a newer layer of AI-specific rules: laws that classify AI systems by risk, require testing and documentation, ban certain uses outright, or impose duties on the makers of the most powerful models. Most real cases will be decided by combining both layers.

Chapter 03

Why AI is unusually hard to regulate

It is opaque

Modern AI systems learn from data rather than following written rules (see Machine learning). Even their builders often cannot explain why a particular output was produced. That makes it hard to prove a breach or to assign blame.

It is general-purpose

The same model can write poetry, summarise medical records and help write computer code. A rule written for one use fits badly on a tool that has thousands.

It changes fast

Capabilities have jumped within months. A law that takes three years to pass may regulate a technology that no longer exists in that form. This is the pacing problem from Technology law, in its sharpest form.

The risks are uncertain

Some harms, such as bias and fraud, are proven and present. Others, such as misuse for weapons or loss of control over advanced systems, are debated. Experts disagree about how likely and how close they are.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four approaches from four powers

The European Union wrote one broad law based on risk. The United States relies on existing law, executive action and its states. China issued targeted rules. Britain chose principles applied by existing regulators.

Chapter 04

The EU AI Act: a pyramid of risk

The European Union's AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024, is the world's first comprehensive AI law. Its central idea is that rules should depend on the risk of the use, not on the technology itself.

The AI Act's risk tiers
                 /\
                /  \     UNACCEPTABLE  - banned
               /    \    social scoring, manipulation, some biometric uses
              /------\
             /        \   HIGH RISK  - strict duties before and after sale
            /          \  hiring, credit, education, policing, medical devices
           /------------\
          /              \  LIMITED RISK  - transparency duties
         /                \ chatbots must say they are AI; deepfakes labelled
        /------------------\
       /                    \ MINIMAL RISK  - no new duties
      /______________________\ spam filters, game characters, most AI

At the top are banned practices, prohibited from February 2025. They include "social scoring" of citizens by public authorities, AI that manipulates people in harmful ways, scraping faces from the internet to build recognition databases, and emotion recognition in workplaces and schools. Real-time facial recognition in public spaces by police is banned except in narrow cases.

High-risk systems, used in areas such as hiring, credit scoring, education, critical infrastructure, law enforcement and border control, must meet strict requirements before they are sold: risk management, good-quality training data, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and security testing, and registration in an EU database. Most of these duties were scheduled to apply from August 2026, but in late 2025 the Commission proposed delaying some of them, so the exact dates are worth checking. Fines for the worst breaches can reach 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide turnover.

Chapter 05

The special rules for general-purpose models

The AI Act was first drafted before chatbots built on large language models (see Vol 14) became famous in late 2022. Negotiators then added rules for general-purpose AI models, the large models that many other products are built on. Since August 2025 their providers must keep technical documentation, give information to companies building on them, publish a summary of the content used for training, and have a policy to respect EU copyright law.

The largest models, those presumed to pose systemic risk, face extra duties: evaluating and testing for dangerous capabilities, reporting serious incidents, and ensuring strong cybersecurity. The law uses the amount of computing power used in training as a first test of which models count. In 2025 the EU published a voluntary code of practice to show companies how to comply, and most leading developers signed at least part of it. This is the first binding attempt anywhere to regulate the most capable AI models as a category.

Chapter 06

The United States: no single law, many moving parts

The US has no comprehensive federal AI law. Regulators such as the Federal Trade Commission have said that existing law on deception, discrimination and unfair practices applies to AI. Beyond that, policy has swung with the presidency. A broad executive order in 2023 asked developers of the most powerful models to share safety test results with the government. It was revoked in January 2025, and the new administration's AI Action Plan of July 2025 emphasised removing barriers to development and winning the competition with China.

Much of the action is in the states. Colorado passed a law in 2024 aimed at discrimination by high-risk AI systems, though its start date was pushed back. California's governor vetoed a broad frontier-safety bill in 2024, then signed a narrower one in 2025 requiring the largest developers to publish safety frameworks and report serious incidents. Many states have passed laws on deepfakes in elections and on AI-generated sexual images. A live dispute is whether federal law should pre-empt, or override, state AI laws so that companies face one set of rules. An attempt to impose a ten-year pause on state AI laws was dropped from a federal bill in 2025, but the push continued.

Chapter 07

China, Britain, and the international layer

China has issued a series of targeted rules rather than one law: on recommendation algorithms (2022), on "deep synthesis" such as deepfakes (2023), and interim measures on generative AI services (2023), which require providers to register certain services, label generated content and ensure outputs reflect what the rules call core socialist values. Rules requiring labels on AI-generated content took effect in 2025. The approach combines safety, social control and support for domestic industry.

Britain chose in 2023 not to pass a single AI law, instead asking existing regulators, for finance, health, competition and so on, to apply five principles such as safety, transparency and fairness in their own sectors. It also set up a government AI Safety Institute in 2023, renamed the AI Security Institute in 2025, to test advanced models.

Internationally, the OECD agreed AI principles in 2019. Britain hosted the first AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park in November 2023, where countries including the US and China signed a declaration on frontier risks. Later summits followed in Seoul and Paris. In 2024 the Council of Europe adopted a Framework Convention on AI and human rights, the first binding international treaty on AI, open to countries outside Europe. There is no global AI regulator, and none is close.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Present harms, contested ownership, uncertain futures

AI regulation matters because some harms are already happening to real people, and because the rules written now will shape a technology that may become as basic as electricity.

Chapter 08

Harms already here

In January 2020 police in Detroit arrested Robert Williams at his home, in front of his family, for a theft he did not commit. A facial recognition system had matched a blurry security image to his old driving licence photo. He was held for about thirty hours. His case, settled in 2024 with new limits on how Detroit police may use the technology, became one of the best-known examples of a false match leading to a wrongful arrest. Studies by US government scientists had found that many facial recognition systems made more errors on some demographic groups than on others.

Fake media is the other visible harm. Cheap tools can clone a voice from a short recording or put a real person's face into a fabricated video. Scammers use this for fraud (see Vol 47). Abusers use it to create sexual images of real people without consent, a harm that falls mostly on women and girls. In 2025 the US passed a federal law, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, making it a crime to publish such images and requiring platforms to remove them quickly. The EU AI Act and Chinese rules both require AI-generated content to be labelled. The laws that are moving fastest target specific, visible harms, not AI in general.

Chapter 09

Who owns what the machine learned from?

Large AI models are trained on enormous amounts of text, images, music and code, much of it copyrighted. Developers argue that learning from works is like a person reading a library: it extracts patterns and does not copy. In the US they rely on fair use, a flexible exception that allows some unlicensed use. Authors, artists, newspapers and record labels argue that their work is being used without permission or payment to build products that compete with them.

Dozens of lawsuits followed, including one filed by The New York Times against OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023. Early US rulings have been mixed. In 2025 one judge found that training on lawfully bought books could be fair use, while using pirated copies was not, and the developer in that case then agreed to pay about 1.5 billion dollars to settle with authors. Europe takes a different path: its law allows text and data mining but lets rights holders opt out, and the AI Act requires training summaries so they can check. See Intellectual property and Generative AI. The outcome will decide whether creators share in the value AI produces.

Chapter 10

The frontier debate

In May 2023 hundreds of AI researchers and executives signed a one-sentence statement saying that reducing the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war. Others in the field strongly disagree, arguing that such warnings are speculative, distract from present harms, and may help big companies argue for rules only they can afford. Both groups include serious, well-informed people. Volume 49 describes this honestly as genuine uncertainty.

The regulatory question is what to do under that uncertainty. One camp wants testing of advanced models before release, reporting of dangerous capabilities, and controls on the most powerful chips. The US has restricted exports of advanced AI chips to China since 2022, partly for this reason. Another camp worries most about concentration: if only a few firms may build advanced AI, regulation could entrench them. A third focuses on open-weight models, whose internal parameters are published so anyone can run and modify them. Supporters say openness spreads benefits and allows independent scrutiny. Critics say it makes misuse impossible to recall. See AI agents for the next set of questions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every approach buys something and gives something up

Strict rules protect people and may slow useful work. Loose rules encourage building and may leave victims without remedy. The models differ in which risk they choose to carry.

Chapter 12

Three tensions that will not go away

Innovation against precaution. Every rule adds cost, and AI may bring large gains in medicine, science and productivity. Waiting for proof of harm, however, risks repeating the social media experience, where rules came after the damage. Where to draw the line is a value judgement as much as a technical one.

Liability: who pays when AI harms someone? A victim may not know an AI was involved and cannot see inside it. The EU proposed a special AI liability directive to ease the burden of proof, then withdrew it in 2025, relying instead on its updated product liability law, which now covers software (see Technology law). Responsibility between the model maker, the app builder and the user is still unclear in most places.

Competition against cooperation. Governments want AI safety, but they also want to lead. The rivalry between the US and China makes each side reluctant to accept limits the other might ignore. A rule that only one side follows may protect nobody and cost that side the lead. That logic resembles the arms-control problems of the Cold War (see Vol 22).

Chapter 14

What to watch, and how to judge it

As of 2026 the direction is mixed. Europe is implementing the AI Act while debating whether to slow parts of it. The US federal government has favoured speed and competitiveness, while states keep legislating. China keeps adding detailed rules. Meanwhile AI systems are moving from answering questions to taking actions on people's behalf, which raises new questions about responsibility that existing law has barely touched. Effects on employment are another live area (see How work may change because of AI).

When you read about a new AI law, ask four questions. Which harm does it target? Who carries the duty: the model maker, the company using it, or the user? How will anyone check compliance? And what can an affected person actually do? A law that answers all four is serious. A law that answers none is a statement of hope. This category ends here because AI regulation draws on nearly every earlier topic: constitutions and rights, courts, contracts, privacy, intellectual property, competition and regulation itself.

The core idea

AI regulation combines existing law (discrimination, data, consumer, copyright, liability) with new AI-specific rules. The leading model, the EU AI Act, regulates by risk of use, with extra duties for the most powerful general-purpose models. Other powers chose looser, narrower or more state-controlled approaches.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024; bans applied from February 2025, model duties from August 2025.
  • Its maximum fine is 35 million euros or 7% of worldwide turnover.
  • The first AI Safety Summit was at Bletchley Park in November 2023.
The common mistake

Saying "AI is unregulated". Existing laws already apply, and the real gaps are in enforcement, transparency and responsibility along the supply chain.

Test yourself

Can you place a CV-screening tool, a customer-service chatbot and a spam filter in the right tier of the EU AI Act, and explain why? If so, you have the core logic of risk-based regulation.

The words, in plain words
AI regulation
Laws, standards and guidance governing how AI is built, sold and used.
AI Act
The EU's comprehensive AI law, which sets rules by level of risk.
Risk-based approach
Making duties stricter as the possible harm of a use grows.
High-risk system
Under the AI Act, an AI used in sensitive areas such as hiring, credit or policing.
Social scoring
Rating people's trustworthiness from their behaviour to decide how they are treated; banned in the EU.
General-purpose AI model
A large model that can be adapted to many tasks and built into other products.
Systemic risk
In the AI Act, risks from the most capable models that could have wide effects on society.
Frontier model
One of the most capable AI systems at a given time.
Open-weight model
A model whose internal parameters are published so anyone can run or modify it.
Pre-emption
When a federal law overrides state laws on the same subject.
Fair use
A US copyright exception allowing some unlicensed use, central to AI training lawsuits.
Deepfake
AI-generated audio, image or video that realistically imitates a real person.
False match
When facial recognition wrongly identifies one person as another.
