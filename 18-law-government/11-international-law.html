International law. Law with no police force, which works far more often than it fails.

Inside a country, law has a parliament to make it, courts to apply it and police to enforce it. Between countries there is none of that. No world government passes laws, no court can force a state to appear before it, and no army arrests a president who breaks the rules. People therefore often conclude that international law is not really law at all. That conclusion is understandable and mostly wrong. Every letter that crosses a border, every plane that lands abroad and every ship in foreign waters relies on it, quietly, every day.

Question 1 · What is it?

Rules that states make for themselves

International law is the body of rules that governs relations between states, and increasingly between states, international organisations and individuals. Its central feature is that the people bound by it are also the people who write it.

Chapter 01

A legal system with no sheriff

Imagine a village with no mayor, no judge and no constable. The families still trade, marry, borrow and settle quarrels. They do it through customs everyone recognises, promises they keep because they will need each other again, and the threat of being shunned if they cheat. Most of the time it works. Sometimes the strongest family simply takes what it wants.

That is roughly the world of international law. Its basic unit is the sovereign state: a country with its own territory, population and government, which recognises no legal authority above itself. The idea is often traced to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, which ended decades of religious war in Europe, although historians debate how much the treaties themselves really established. In 1625 the Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius had already argued, in On the Law of War and Peace, that even sovereign rulers were bound by rules in how they went to war and fought it.

Because no authority stands above states, international law rests on their consent. A state is generally bound only by rules it has agreed to, either by signing a treaty or by joining in a long-standing practice. That makes it different in kind from national law, where a citizen is bound whether they agree or not.

Chapter 02

Public, private, and who counts

Two quite different subjects share the name. Public international law governs states: borders, war, treaties, the sea, diplomats, human rights. Private international law, also called conflict of laws, decides which country's ordinary law applies when a private dispute crosses a border, such as a French buyer suing a German seller, or a marriage between nationals of two countries. This file is about the first.

For most of its history, public international law dealt only with states. That changed in the twentieth century. International organisations such as the United Nations gained legal personality, meaning they can sign treaties and be held responsible. And after the Second World War, individuals entered the system in two directions: they gained rights against their own governments (see Human rights), and they became personally liable for the worst crimes, whatever their rank.

Unlike most national systems, there is no strict ranking between treaty and custom. A later treaty can change custom between its parties, and a new custom can change how a treaty is read. Above both sit a few rules so fundamental that no treaty can override them, discussed in Chapter 05.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Promises, practice, courts and pressure

International law is made by agreement and custom, interpreted by courts that need consent, and enforced by a mix of reciprocity, reputation and occasionally force.

Chapter 04

Treaties: from signature to binding promise

A treaty usually goes through several steps. Diplomats negotiate a text. States sign it, which shows intent and creates a duty not to defeat its purpose, but does not yet fully bind them. Each state then ratifies it according to its own constitution, often with the approval of its parliament. In the United States, treaties need a two-thirds vote of the Senate, which is one reason the US has signed but never ratified a number of major agreements. The treaty enters into force once enough states have ratified it.

The rules about treaties are themselves in a treaty, the 1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties. Its core principle is pacta sunt servanda, Latin for "agreements must be kept". States may attach reservations excluding certain clauses, as long as these do not undermine the treaty's purpose. Many treaties allow withdrawal after notice.

How a treaty affects people inside a country depends on that country. In monist systems, such as France and the Netherlands, a ratified treaty becomes part of national law and can rank above ordinary statutes. In dualist systems, such as the UK, a treaty binds the state internationally but has no effect in national courts until Parliament passes a law to bring it in. Many countries mix the two.

Chapter 05

Custom, and the rules nobody may opt out of

Customary law needs two ingredients: widespread, consistent state practice, and the belief that the practice is legally required, known by its Latin name opinio juris. Diplomats being immune from arrest is custom, later written into a 1961 treaty. Customary rules bind all states, including those that never signed anything, unless a state objected clearly and persistently while the rule was forming.

A small group of rules goes further. These are called jus cogens, or peremptory norms: rules so fundamental that no treaty may set them aside. There is broad agreement that they include the bans on genocide, slavery, torture, crimes against humanity and aggressive war. Two states cannot make a valid treaty to commit genocide together. The exact list beyond the core is debated.

The common thread is consent. The ICJ has fifteen judges and decides important cases, but a state that has not accepted its jurisdiction cannot normally be brought before it. That is a sharp contrast with a national court, which can summon anyone.

Chapter 07

Enforcement without a sheriff

So why do states obey? Scholars point to several forces working together.

  • Reciprocity: you respect my diplomats so I respect yours. Break the rule and the same treatment comes back.
  • Reputation: a state that breaks treaties finds others less willing to sign new ones, lend to it or invest.
  • Self-interest: most rules, such as aviation standards, are useful to everyone.
  • Countermeasures and sanctions: injured states may lawfully take otherwise illegal steps in response, and the UN Security Council can impose sanctions binding on all members.
  • National courts and officials: in many countries, judges apply international law directly, and civil servants follow it as routine.

The weakness is obvious. These forces are strongest against small states and weakest against great powers, which can absorb reputational costs and veto Security Council action. The system enforces itself well on ordinary matters and poorly on the matters people care most about.

Chapter 08

The rules on war

International law has two separate bodies of rules on armed conflict, and confusing them is a common mistake.

When force is allowed (jus ad bellum)

Article 2(4) of the UN Charter of 1945 bans the threat or use of force against another state. There are two accepted exceptions: self-defence against an armed attack (Article 51), and action authorised by the Security Council. Everything else, including humanitarian intervention without Council approval, is legally contested at best.

How war must be fought (jus in bello)

Also called international humanitarian law. The four Geneva Conventions of 1949, accepted by every state, protect the wounded, prisoners and civilians. Core rules: distinguish soldiers from civilians, avoid attacks whose civilian harm would be excessive, and do not cause unnecessary suffering.

The two are independent. A state fighting a lawful war of self-defence must still obey the rules of conduct, and a state fighting an unlawful war still has its soldiers protected as prisoners. Whether a particular use of force was lawful, such as NATO's bombing of Yugoslavia over Kosovo in 1999 or the invasion of Iraq in 2003, can be fiercely disputed among lawyers. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 was condemned as a breach of the Charter by a large majority of the UN General Assembly.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The invisible law, and the visible failures

International law is judged by its most dramatic failures. Its daily successes are so routine that nobody notices them.

Chapter 09

The law that runs ordinary life

Think of an ordinary trip abroad. Your plane flies through other countries' airspace under the 1944 Chicago Convention on civil aviation. Your passport is accepted under shared standards. If you are arrested, your consulate has a treaty right to be told. A parcel you post follows rules of the Universal Postal Union, founded in 1874. Ships carrying your shopping pass through waters governed by the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, which gives each coastal state a 12-nautical-mile territorial sea and a 200-mile exclusive economic zone for fishing and resources.

"Almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law and almost all of their obligations almost all of the time."

Louis Henkin · How Nations Behave (1968)

Henkin's sentence is widely quoted because it captures something the headlines miss. One of the clearest successes is the 1987 Montreal Protocol, which phased out chemicals that were destroying the ozone layer. It became one of the first treaties ratified by every UN member state, and the ozone layer is now slowly recovering. When the interests line up and the rules are clear, international law can solve problems no single country could.

Chapter 10

From Nuremberg to the ICC

In 1945 and 1946 the victorious Allies tried leading Nazi figures before an international tribunal at Nuremberg. The defendants argued that they had followed orders and that the crimes were not crimes when committed. The tribunal rejected both arguments. Individuals, not only states, could be punished for crimes under international law, and obeying orders was no defence.

The idea lay mostly dormant during the Cold War. It revived in the 1990s, when the Security Council created tribunals for the former Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994). The Rwanda tribunal delivered the first conviction for genocide by an international court. In 1998 states agreed the Rome Statute, which created the permanent International Criminal Court in 2002.

The ICC's record is mixed. It has convicted a small number of people. Its early cases focused heavily on Africa, which led to accusations of bias. It has since issued arrest warrants for leaders of more powerful states, including Russia's president Vladimir Putin in 2023 and Israel's prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu in 2024, which have not been executed. In 2025 the former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte was arrested and sent to The Hague, a rare case of a former head of state being handed over. Supporters see slow progress against impunity. Critics see selective justice. Both views have evidence behind them.

Chapter 11

When great powers say no

The hardest test is a powerful state that refuses to comply. In 1986 the ICJ ruled that the United States had broken international law by supporting rebels and mining harbours in Nicaragua. The US had already withdrawn from the court's general jurisdiction and did not comply. In 2016 an arbitration tribunal ruled that China's sweeping claims in the South China Sea had no legal basis under the Law of the Sea. China called the ruling null and void. In 2022 the ICJ ordered Russia to suspend military operations in Ukraine. The fighting continued.

These cases show the limit clearly. International law can declare, but it often cannot compel. Yet the rulings still matter. They shape how other states vote, what allies support, and how disputes are later negotiated. Nicaragua, the Philippines and Ukraine all used the judgments to build diplomatic pressure. A law that is broken is not the same as a law that does not exist.

Chapter 13

Whose law is it?

A deeper criticism concerns origins. Modern international law grew up among European states, and for centuries it treated much of the rest of the world as territory to be claimed. Scholars from the Global South, sometimes grouped under the label "Third World Approaches to International Law", argue that the system still carries that history: in who sits permanently on the Security Council, in investment rules that favour capital-exporting countries, and in which leaders end up in the dock.

Defenders reply that the same law gave colonised peoples the legal right to self-determination, which helped dozens of countries become independent after 1945, and that newer states have used it effectively. In 2019, for example, the ICJ advised that Britain's continued administration of the Chagos Islands was unlawful, and in 2025 Britain agreed to hand sovereignty to Mauritius. The law reflects the power that made it, and it can also be used against that power. Both observations are true.

Chapter 15

Fragmenting, not disappearing

The international order built after 1945 is under visible strain: wars between states, a trade court that cannot hear appeals, sanctions on court officials, and great powers openly ignoring rulings. It is reasonable to ask whether the system is breaking down.

The more likely path is fragmentation. Shared rules on aviation, shipping, post, telecoms and diplomacy are too useful for anyone to abandon. Contested rules on force, human rights and trade will be applied more selectively, and regional or like-minded groups will build their own arrangements. History suggests that international law has always moved in this rhythm: setbacks in times of rivalry, growth in times of cooperation. The question for the next decade is not whether there will be international law, but how much of it will be shared by everyone. For the institutions that carry it, see International organizations.

The core idea

International law governs relations between sovereign states, which have no authority above them, so it rests on consent. It comes from treaties and custom, is interpreted by courts that need consent, and is enforced by reciprocity, reputation and sanctions. It works well on routine matters and poorly against great powers.

The numbers worth knowing
  • UN Charter Article 2(4) bans force; exceptions are self-defence and Security Council authorisation.
  • The Geneva Conventions of 1949 are accepted by every state.
  • The ICC has about 125 member states; the US, China, Russia and India are not among them.
The common mistake

Concluding that international law does not exist because it is sometimes broken. Domestic law is broken every day too. The real question is how often it shapes behaviour, and the answer is: most of the time.
