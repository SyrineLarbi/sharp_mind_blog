Regulation. The rules you never see, until the day one is missing.

The medicine you took this morning was tested before it reached you. The plane you last flew on was certified, inspected and flown by licensed pilots. The money in your bank account is partly protected if the bank fails. None of this happens because companies are naturally careful. It happens because the state sets rules for how they must behave and checks that they follow them. That is regulation. It is one of the main ways modern governments act, and one of the most argued about.

Question 1 · What is it?

State rules for private behaviour

Regulation is the state telling businesses, and sometimes individuals, what they must and must not do, usually through specialised bodies rather than through parliament directly.

Chapter 01

The drug that was never approved

In the late 1950s a sedative called thalidomide went on sale in West Germany and then in dozens of other countries. It was marketed as safe, including for morning sickness in pregnancy. It was not. Thousands of babies, commonly estimated at around ten thousand worldwide, were born with severe limb defects before the drug was withdrawn in 1961–62.

The United States was largely spared. The reason was one reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration, Frances Kelsey, who kept refusing the manufacturer's application because she found the safety evidence inadequate. In 1962 Congress passed the Kefauver–Harris Amendments, requiring drug makers to prove not only that a medicine is safe but that it actually works, with controlled trials, before it can be sold. Similar laws followed across Europe.

That story holds the whole topic in miniature. A market left alone did not protect people. A rule, applied by a careful official, did. And the scandal produced a stronger system. Much of modern regulation was written in the aftermath of a disaster that showed what was missing.

Chapter 02

What counts as regulation

In the broad sense, regulation is any rule the state uses to steer private behaviour. In the narrower sense used by lawyers and economists, it means the detailed rules set and enforced by specialised public bodies, called regulators or regulatory agencies. A parliament passes a framework law ("medicines must be safe and effective"), and delegates to an agency the job of writing the detail and checking compliance. This is called delegated authority.

Chapter 03

Why markets need rules: four classic reasons

Most economists accept that markets are usually good at deciding what gets made and at what price. The case for regulation rests on situations where they predictably fail, called market failures.

Externalities

A factory that pollutes a river does not pay the cost borne by the fishermen downstream. When costs fall on people outside the deal, the market produces too much of the harmful thing. Pollution rules exist mainly for this reason.

Information gaps

You cannot test a medicine, inspect an aircraft engine or audit a bank yourself. The seller knows far more than the buyer. Economists call this information asymmetry. Licensing and disclosure rules try to close the gap.

Natural monopoly

It makes no sense to build three sets of water pipes to one street. Where one network is cheapest, there is no competition to keep prices down, so a regulator sets prices instead.

Systemic risk

One bank's failure can bring down others and the whole economy. No single bank takes that wider damage into account, so regulators require extra safety buffers.

There is a fifth reason that is not about efficiency at all. Societies also regulate to protect rights and fairness: banning discrimination in hiring, or requiring access for disabled people. These rules reflect values, not market mathematics. Whether a market failure exists is an economic question. Whether regulation will fix it better than it makes things worse is a separate one.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Tools, rule-making, enforcement and independence

A regulator chooses a tool, writes the rule through a set procedure, enforces it, and usually does all this at arm's length from day-to-day politics.

The trend since the 1980s has been away from detailed prescriptions and towards performance standards and market tools, on the argument that firms know best how to meet a goal cheaply. The counter-argument is that outcome rules are harder to check, and give clever firms room to game them.

Chapter 05

How a rule gets made

Regulators cannot simply decree rules. In most democracies they must follow a procedure that gives the public a voice. In the United States, the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 created notice and comment: an agency publishes a proposed rule, anyone can send comments, and the agency must respond to the serious ones before issuing the final rule. Courts can strike down rules made without following this process, or rules they judge arbitrary.

Since 1981, major US rules have also had to pass a cost-benefit analysis reviewed by a small White House office. Britain and the EU use impact assessments for the same purpose: estimating what a rule will cost businesses and what it will gain in health, safety or savings, and comparing alternatives. Putting a money value on lives saved or a cleaner river is uncomfortable and contested. Its defenders say it forces honesty about trade-offs that would otherwise be hidden.

In the European Union, the big frameworks are set by EU law, in two forms. A regulation applies directly in every member state. A directive sets goals that each country must write into its own law. National and EU agencies then add the detail. In 2024 the US Supreme Court, in a case called Loper Bright, ended a decades-old practice of courts deferring to agencies' reasonable readings of unclear laws. That shifted power from regulators towards judges, and its full effects are still unfolding.

Chapter 06

Enforcement: the pyramid

A rule nobody enforces is a suggestion. But regulators cannot inspect everyone, and punishing every small breach harshly would turn cooperative firms into hostile ones. In 1992 the scholars Ian Ayres and John Braithwaite proposed an influential answer called responsive regulation, often drawn as a pyramid.

The enforcement pyramid
                      /\
                     /  \      licence revoked, firm closed
                    /----\
                   /      \    criminal prosecution
                  /--------\
                 /          \  large fines, suspension
                /------------\
               /              \  warning letters, orders to fix
              /----------------\
             /                  \  advice, persuasion, education
            ----------------------
   Start at the bottom. Move up only if the firm does not respond.

Most firms are dealt with at the base, by advice and persuasion. The heavy sanctions at the top matter mainly because everyone knows they exist. A regulator speaks softly most of the time, and is believed because it can strike hard. Modern agencies also use risk-based inspection, concentrating checks where harm is most likely, rather than visiting everyone equally.

Chapter 07

Why regulators are kept at arm's length

Many regulators are deliberately independent: their leaders are appointed for fixed terms and cannot be fired for unpopular decisions. Examples include the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Federal Communications Commission in the US, Ofcom and Ofgem in Britain, and the national energy and telecom regulators the EU requires every member state to have.

The logic is the same as for independent central banks (see Central banks). Investors will build a power station only if they trust that prices will not be changed for political convenience before the next election. Independence makes promises credible and rewards technical expertise. The cost is democratic: powerful decisions are taken by officials nobody elected. That tension is why regulators must publish reasons, consult, and answer to parliamentary committees and courts. In the US it is also the subject of recurring legal battles over how far a president may remove agency heads.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Safety, stability, and the shape of global markets

Regulation is invisible when it works. It becomes front-page news when a regulator is too weak, too close to the industry, or too slow for a new risk.

Chapter 08

When the referee lets the player hold the whistle

Commercial aviation is one of regulation's great successes. Flying on a major airline has become far safer per kilometre than driving, largely because every accident is investigated and its lessons written into binding rules. That makes the exception instructive.

In October 2018 and March 2019, two new Boeing 737 MAX aircraft crashed, in Indonesia and Ethiopia, killing 346 people. Investigations found that a flight-control system could push the nose down repeatedly on the basis of a single faulty sensor, and that pilots had not been properly told about it. They also found that the US Federal Aviation Administration had delegated much of the certification work to Boeing's own employees, a long-standing arrangement intended to save regulators' time. The aircraft was grounded worldwide for about twenty months.

Delegating checks to the people being checked saves money until it fails. After the crashes, the US Congress tightened the rules on how the FAA oversees manufacturers.

Chapter 09

2008: the cost of light touch

Before the 2008 financial crisis, many governments believed that banks, if lightly supervised, would manage their own risks because their own money was at stake. Large parts of the financial system, including complex mortgage-linked securities, fell outside or between regulators. When American house prices fell, losses spread through banks that had borrowed heavily and held thin safety cushions. Governments spent enormous sums rescuing them, and the recession that followed cost millions of jobs (see Financial crises).

The response was a wave of new rules. The international Basel III standards, agreed from 2010, required banks to hold much more capital, meaning their own funds that absorb losses before depositors or taxpayers do. Banks also faced regular stress tests, in which regulators model what would happen in a severe recession. The argument since then has been about how much of this to keep, and how much it slows lending and growth.

Chapter 10

The Brussels effect

Regulation also shapes the global economy in a way few people notice. The legal scholar Anu Bradford coined the term the Brussels effect for how European Union rules become world standards. The EU is a market of about 450 million relatively wealthy consumers. A global company that wants to sell there must meet EU rules, and it is often cheaper to make one version of a product for everyone than two. So EU rules on chemicals, data protection or phone chargers end up applying far beyond Europe.

The United States has historically regulated more through lawsuits and sector-by-sector agencies, and China increasingly sets its own standards for its huge market. Who writes the rules for a market decides, in part, how the whole world's products are made. That is one reason the contest over regulating technology and AI is also a contest between powers (see Technology law).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Protection has a price

Good regulation saves lives and money. Bad regulation wastes both, protects big incumbents, and can be quietly taken over by the industry it oversees.

Chapter 12

Capture, and the two ways to get it wrong

In 1971 the economist George Stigler argued that regulation is often "acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit". This is regulatory capture. It rarely means bribery. More often it means that regulators depend on the industry for information, meet its lawyers every week and rarely meet the public, and hope for well-paid jobs in it later (the so-called revolving door). Over time they come to see the world as the industry does. The 737 MAX certification shows a mild form.

The opposite danger is regulation that is too heavy, too detailed or too cautious, choking useful activity. Governments have tried several fixes: rules that a new regulation must be matched by removing older ones (Britain and later the US used versions of "one in, two out"), sunset clauses that make rules expire unless renewed, and regulatory sandboxes, pioneered by Britain's financial regulator in 2016, where new firms can test products under supervision with some rules relaxed. The honest position is that both over-regulation and under-regulation happen, and the evidence has to be examined sector by sector.

Chapter 14

The pendulum keeps swinging

Regulation moves in cycles. Disasters and crises bring new rules. Years of calm bring complaints about cost and red tape, and a push to simplify. Then something goes wrong again. The 1970s saw a burst of environmental and safety rules in the US and Europe. The 1980s and 1990s brought deregulation of airlines, telecoms and finance. 2008 brought re-regulation of banks.

As of 2026 the pendulum is moving towards deregulation in several places at once. The US administration since 2025 has made cutting federal rules a central goal, and the European Union has launched packages to simplify reporting duties it had only recently imposed, under pressure about Europe's competitiveness. Supporters see overdue relief. Critics fear that protections are being removed just as new risks appear. The useful question is never "more or less regulation?" but "which rule, for which risk, enforced how?"

The core idea

Regulation is the state setting and enforcing rules for private behaviour, mostly through specialised agencies with delegated authority. Its classic justification is market failure: externalities, information gaps, natural monopoly and systemic risk. Its classic danger is capture by the industry it oversees.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US drug law required proof of effectiveness from 1962, after thalidomide.
  • The two 737 MAX crashes of 2018–19 killed 346 people.
  • The EU market that drives the Brussels effect has about 450 million people.
The common mistake

Arguing about "regulation" in general. Rules differ enormously in quality, and the same person can rightly want tougher aviation rules and simpler business paperwork.

Test yourself

Pick one product you used today. Can you name which market failure its regulation addresses, which tool is used, and who enforces it? If you can, you understand the system.

The words, in plain words
Regulation
State rules that steer private behaviour, usually set and enforced by a specialised agency.
Regulator
The public body that writes the detailed rules for a sector and checks compliance.
Delegated authority
Power that a parliament hands to an agency to make detailed rules within a framework law.
Market failure
A situation where an unregulated market predictably produces a bad result.
Externality
A cost or benefit that falls on people outside a transaction.
Information asymmetry
When one side of a deal knows much more than the other.
Natural monopoly
A market where one network or supplier is cheaper than several, such as water pipes.
Notice and comment
The US procedure in which agencies publish proposed rules and must consider public responses.
Cost-benefit analysis
Estimating and comparing what a rule will cost and what it will gain.
Responsive regulation
Starting enforcement with persuasion and escalating only when firms do not cooperate.
Regulatory capture
When a regulator comes to serve the industry it is meant to oversee.
Regulatory sandbox
A supervised space where new firms can test products with some rules relaxed.
Brussels effect
The way EU rules become global standards because firms adopt one version worldwide.
