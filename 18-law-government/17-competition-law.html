Competition law. Markets do not stay competitive by themselves. Someone has to referee.

Every business wants to win, and the easiest way to win is to stop competing. Rivals can quietly agree on prices. A giant can crush a newcomer before it grows. Two leaders can merge so that customers have nowhere else to go. Competition law, called antitrust law in the United States, is the body of rules that forbids these moves. It rarely makes headlines in ordinary years, but it shapes the price of your groceries, the apps on your phone, and how much power a handful of companies hold over the economy.

Question 1 · What is it?

Rules that protect competition itself

Competition law does not protect any particular company. It protects the process of rivalry, on the theory that rivalry is what pushes prices down and quality up.

Chapter 01

The company that owned an industry

In the 1870s and 1880s John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil came to control the large majority of oil refining in the United States. It did so partly by being efficient, and partly by tactics that made rivals' lives impossible: secret discounts from railways that its competitors could not get, local price cuts to drive a rival out followed by price rises once it had gone, and buying up whoever was left. Its shares were held in a "trust", a legal structure that let one board control dozens of companies.

Public anger at Standard Oil and the other great trusts of the age led Congress to pass the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, still the core of American law. It bans agreements "in restraint of trade" and bans monopolising a market. In 1911 the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken into more than thirty separate companies. Several of their descendants are still among the world's biggest oil firms.

That case explains the American name. "Antitrust" means against the trusts, the giant combinations of the late nineteenth century. Most other countries say "competition law". The subject is the same.

Chapter 02

The three pillars

Almost every modern competition law, in more than a hundred countries, rests on the same three pillars.

PillarWhat it forbidsUS lawEU law
Agreements between firmsCartels: price-fixing, sharing out markets, rigging bidsSherman Act, section 1Article 101 of the EU Treaty
Single-firm conductA dominant firm abusing its position to exclude rivals or exploit customersSherman Act, section 2 ("monopolisation")Article 102 ("abuse of a dominant position")
MergersTakeovers that would substantially reduce competitionClayton Act of 1914EU Merger Regulation

The EU adds a fourth pillar that the US lacks: state aid control. Member states may not give their own companies subsidies or tax breaks that distort competition in the single market without the European Commission's approval. In 2016 the Commission ordered Ireland to recover 13 billion euros of tax benefits from Apple. The case went back and forth in the courts until the EU's top court upheld the Commission in 2024.

Chapter 03

Why bother protecting competition?

The suspicion of business collusion is older than any of these laws.

"People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices."

Adam Smith · The Wealth of Nations, 1776

Competition matters for three reasons. When firms compete, prices fall towards cost, because anyone charging too much loses customers. Quality and choice improve, because firms must keep giving people reasons to pick them. And innovation speeds up, because a firm that rests is overtaken. A monopolist, by contrast, can charge more, offer less and take its time, because customers have nowhere else to go.

There is also a political argument, popular in the United States around 1900 and again today. Very large concentrations of private economic power can turn into political power, through lobbying, funding and control of information. Some see competition law as protecting democracy as well as consumers. Others think that mixes up two different problems.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Catching cartels, judging giants, vetting mergers

Each pillar uses a different method. Cartels are hunted like crimes. Dominant firms are judged by economics. Mergers are reviewed before they happen.

Chapter 04

Cartels: secret, profitable, and hard to prove

A cartel is an agreement between competitors to stop competing: fixing prices, dividing customers or regions, or taking turns to win public contracts. Almost every country treats this as the most serious offence in the field. In the United States, and in a few other countries including the UK, individual executives can go to prison for it.

The difficulty is proof. Cartel members do not sign contracts. In the 1990s the FBI caught a global cartel in lysine, an animal-feed additive, only because an executive at the American firm Archer Daniels Midland secretly recorded meetings at which rivals agreed prices. The recordings became a famous piece of evidence, and the story was later made into a film.

The most powerful tool authorities found is the leniency programme. The first member of a cartel to confess and cooperate gets immunity from fines. Everyone else faces the full penalty. This turns the cartel's secrecy against itself, because every member knows that any other member might rush to confess first. Leniency turned cartel detection from luck into a race to the regulator's door. In 2016 the European Commission fined a group of truck makers almost 3 billion euros, a record for a cartel, after one of them confessed.

Chapter 05

Dominance: being big is allowed, abusing it is not

Competition law does not punish a firm for winning. If customers prefer your product and you end up with most of the market, that is the system working. What the law forbids is using that position to stop others from competing on the merits. Courts first decide whether a firm is dominant, meaning it can act largely without worrying about rivals or customers. Then they ask whether its conduct is abusive.

Common forms of abuse

Predatory pricing: selling below cost to drive out a rival, planning to raise prices later. Tying: forcing customers who want product A to take product B. Exclusive dealing: paying or pressuring partners not to deal with rivals. Self-preferencing: a platform favouring its own services over others that depend on it.

Landmark cases

In 2000 a US court ordered Microsoft split in two for monopolising PC operating systems. An appeal court overturned the break-up and the case was settled. The EU fined Microsoft in 2004 for tying its media player to Windows. In 2017 it fined Google 2.4 billion euros for favouring its own shopping service in search results, a decision the EU's top court upheld in 2024.

Chapter 06

Mergers: deciding before the eggs are scrambled

Once two companies have merged, untangling them is very hard. So most countries require large mergers to be notified in advance and cleared by the competition authority. Most are approved quickly. A small share get a deeper investigation, and a few are blocked or approved only with remedies, for example selling off part of the business to a rival.

The analysis starts with market definition: what customers would switch to if the price went up. Are Coca-Cola and Pepsi in the same market? Almost certainly. Coca-Cola and tap water? Probably not. Authorities then measure how concentrated the market would become, often using an index that adds up the squares of each firm's market share. A market with two firms of equal size scores far higher than one with ten.

Mergers between rivals (horizontal mergers) cause most concern. Mergers between a supplier and a customer (vertical) are usually more benign, though they can let a firm cut off rivals' access to an essential input. The deals most debated today are a third kind: large firms buying small, fast-growing start-ups before they can become a threat. Critics call these killer acquisitions. Facebook's purchase of Instagram in 2012, cleared at the time, is the case most often cited.

Chapter 07

Two traditions: consumer welfare and the competitive process

From the late 1970s American antitrust was reshaped by the Chicago School, associated with economists and lawyers at the University of Chicago. Its best-known book, Robert Bork's The Antitrust Paradox (1978), argued that antitrust should have one goal: consumer welfare, measured mostly by prices and output. On this view, a big firm that keeps prices low is no problem. Courts largely adopted the approach, and US enforcement became more cautious.

European competition law grew partly from a German school of thought called ordoliberalism, which saw free competition as a structure that the state must protect, partly so that private power could not dominate society. EU law therefore puts more weight on keeping markets open and on the rights of rivals to compete, even where short-term prices are low.

Since the late 2010s a group sometimes called the neo-Brandeisians, after the early twentieth-century judge Louis Brandeis, has challenged the Chicago view in the US. A 2017 student article by Lina Khan, "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox", argued that a price-only test misses how platforms gain power. Khan chaired the Federal Trade Commission from 2021 to early 2025. How far antitrust should look beyond prices is the central argument in the field today.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Big Tech, everyday prices, and global power

Competition law is how democracies decide how much power any single company may hold. The biggest tests of that power are happening now.

Chapter 08

The trials of the platforms

Digital platforms pose a problem the older law did not expect. They benefit from network effects: a social network is more useful the more people use it, and a search engine improves the more searches it handles. That makes markets tip towards one winner. Many services are free to users, so the usual test of "did prices go up?" says little. And the platforms are often both the marketplace and a seller in it.

In the United States, a federal judge ruled in 2024 that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in online search, partly by paying billions of dollars a year to be the default search engine on phones and browsers. In 2025 the court ordered remedies, including limits on exclusive deals, while declining to force a sale of the Chrome browser. A separate 2025 ruling found that Google had also illegally monopolised parts of the online advertising technology market. Cases against Meta, Amazon and Apple were also brought by US authorities. As of 2026 several of these cases, and their appeals, were still running.

Europe chose a different route as well as lawsuits. Its Digital Markets Act, adopted in 2022, names the largest platforms as "gatekeepers" and gives them a list of obligations in advance, such as allowing rival app stores or not favouring their own services. Instead of proving harm case by case over many years, the EU wrote the rules down beforehand. The first fines under it were imposed on Apple and Meta in 2025.

Chapter 09

Far from Silicon Valley: groceries, medicines and wages

Most competition cases are not about technology. They concern cement, construction contracts, shipping, banking fees, food supply and medicines. In pharmaceuticals, authorities on both sides of the Atlantic have challenged pay-for-delay deals, in which a brand-name drug maker pays a generic maker to postpone launching a cheaper copy. Patients and health systems pay more while the deal lasts.

Competition law also applies to the market for workers. When employers agree not to hire each other's staff, they hold down wages just as a cartel holds up prices. In the 2010s several large Silicon Valley companies settled lawsuits over agreements not to recruit each other's engineers. Authorities now treat no-poach agreements as a serious violation. Economists have also grown more interested in employer market power in small towns where one company is the main employer.

Chapter 10

A worldwide system, with different priorities

Competition law has spread around the world since the 1990s, often as countries opened their economies. China's Anti-Monopoly Law took effect in 2008. In 2021 Chinese authorities fined the e-commerce group Alibaba about 18 billion yuan, then a record, as part of a broad tightening on large technology companies. Brazil, India, South Africa, Japan and South Korea all have active authorities.

Big global mergers must therefore be cleared in many places at once, and the authorities do not always agree. In 2001 the European Commission blocked General Electric's planned takeover of Honeywell, even though US authorities had approved it. A deal now needs the approval of every major market it touches, and any one of them can stop it. Countries also differ on how much they let industrial policy, national security or employment influence decisions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Strong where conduct is clear, shaky where it is new

Competition law is very good at punishing cartels. It is much less certain when it judges whether a big, popular company has crossed a line.

Chapter 12

The champions debate

In 2019 the European Commission blocked a merger between the German group Siemens and the French group Alstom, which would have created a European rail-equipment giant. The two governments argued that Europe needed big firms to compete with state-backed Chinese rivals. The Commission said the merger would raise prices for European rail operators. The case started a debate that has only grown.

A 2024 report on European competitiveness by Mario Draghi, the former head of the European Central Bank, argued that EU rules should give more weight to scale, innovation and resilience. Supporters say a fragmented Europe cannot produce firms of global size. Critics answer that protecting home champions from competition makes them lazy, and that customers end up paying. The tension between competition and industrial strength is unresolved, and it is now explicitly political.

Chapter 14

What is competition law for?

Behind every case sits an argument about purpose. One side says competition law should stay narrow and technical: protect consumers from higher prices and lower output, and leave other goals such as jobs, privacy or political power to other laws. Its advantage is predictability. The other side says that a law built for oil and railways must adapt to an economy of data, platforms and AI, where power does not always show up in prices.

The direction differs by place and changes with governments. US enforcement priorities shifted again after 2025, while several of the big technology cases continued through the courts. The EU is balancing its digital rules against pressure to simplify and to build European scale. For an informed citizen, the useful question is not "is this company too big?" but "is it big because it is better, or because it stopped others from competing?" That question links this topic to the next areas of law, where the state governs what markets do to the environment and to technology.

The core idea

Competition law protects the process of rivalry, not individual firms. It rests on three pillars: banning cartels, stopping abuse of dominance, and reviewing mergers. Being big is legal; using size to exclude rivals is not.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The US Sherman Act dates from 1890; Standard Oil was broken up in 1911.
  • The EU's record cartel fine, against truck makers in 2016, was almost 3 billion euros.
  • More than a hundred countries now have competition laws.
The common mistake

Thinking a company is breaking the law just because it is huge. The offence is the conduct, not the size. A firm that wins by being better is what competition is supposed to produce.

Working out which products and places compete with each other, as a first step in analysis.

A big firm buying a young rival to stop it from becoming a threat.

When a service becomes more valuable as more people use it.
State aid
Government subsidies to firms, which EU law restricts when they distort competition.

The view that antitrust should focus mainly on prices, output and quality for consumers.
