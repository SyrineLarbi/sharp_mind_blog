Labor law. The weekend, the minimum wage and the right to strike were all written by someone.

A job looks like an ordinary contract: you sell your time, someone pays for it. But almost every country refuses to treat it as an ordinary contract. It limits the hours, sets a wage floor, forbids some reasons for firing, and lets workers bargain as a group. Labor law (spelled "labour" in British English) is the body of rules that does this. It exists because one side of the deal usually needs the job far more than the other side needs that one worker.

Question 1 · What is it?

The law that corrects an unequal bargain

Labor law covers the relationship between people who work and the people they work for. Its starting assumption is that, left alone, that relationship is unbalanced.

Chapter 01

A fire on the ninth floor

On 25 March 1911 a fire broke out at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in New York. The workers, most of them young immigrant women, were on the top floors of the building. Some exit doors were locked, a common practice at the time to stop theft and unauthorised breaks. The fire escape collapsed. 146 people died, many by jumping from the windows in front of the crowd below.

The factory's owners were tried for manslaughter and acquitted. But the public reaction pushed New York to pass dozens of new laws on fire safety, factory inspection and working hours over the next few years.

The story shows the basic pattern of labor law. The rules were mostly written after something went badly wrong, and they were fought over at every step. Britain's Factory Acts of the nineteenth century, which first limited children's hours and created inspectors, followed the same path. So did the eight-hour day, the weekend and paid holidays. None of them arrived because the market produced them on its own.

Chapter 02

Why work is not an ordinary contract

Contract law (see Contracts) assumes two parties who can each walk away. Labor law starts from the observation that this is often untrue. A large employer can usually replace one worker easily. The worker often cannot replace the job easily, because rent is due next week. Lawyers call this inequality of bargaining power.

There is a second difference. An employment contract is not a single exchange, like buying a car. It is a continuing relationship in which one side gives orders and the other follows them. The employee agrees to be directed, for many hours a day, often for years. That makes the terms of the relationship matter far more than in most contracts.

Labor law answers these problems in two ways. Individual labor law gives each worker minimum rights that cannot be signed away: a wage floor, maximum hours, safety, protection from discrimination. Collective labor law lets workers act as a group, through unions, bargaining and strikes, so that their combined power can balance the employer's. Different countries lean on the two methods to very different degrees.

Chapter 03

The gateway question: are you an employee?

Almost every protection depends on one prior question. Is this person an employee, or an independent contractor (self-employed, running their own business)? Employees get the minimum wage, paid leave, protection against unfair dismissal and, in many countries, employer-paid social insurance. Contractors usually get none of that. They are treated as businesses dealing with other businesses.

Courts in most countries decide the question by looking at reality rather than labels. Typical factors include:

  • Control: who decides how, when and where the work is done?
  • Integration: is the person part of the organisation, or offering services to many clients?
  • Risk and profit: can they make or lose money by how they run things?
  • Substitution: can they send someone else to do the work?

A contract that says "you are self-employed" does not settle it. If someone works like an employee, courts will often treat them as one. This is the battleground of the gig economy, covered in Chapter 10. The UK adds a middle category, the "worker", who gets some rights (minimum wage, paid holiday) but not all.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Floors, groups, and the rules for ending a job

Labor law is built in layers, from international standards down to a single worker's contract. The mix of layers is what makes one country's system feel so different from another's.

Chapter 04

At the top sits the International Labour Organization (ILO), founded in 1919 as part of the peace settlement after the First World War. It is unusual among international bodies because governments, employers and unions all vote in it. It has adopted a large number of conventions (treaties). It treats a small set as fundamental: freedom of association and collective bargaining, and the elimination of forced labour, child labour and discrimination. In 2022 it added a safe and healthy working environment to that list.

The key feature of the layers is the floor principle. A collective agreement can give more than the statute. A contract can give more than the agreement. But in general, nobody can agree to less. A worker cannot validly sign away the minimum wage, however freely they seem to agree.

Chapter 06

Collective labor law: unions, bargaining, strikes

A trade union is an organisation of workers that negotiates with employers on their behalf. The result is a collective agreement covering pay, hours and conditions. Behind the negotiation sits the possibility of a strike, a collective refusal to work. Most democracies protect the right to strike, within rules: notice periods, ballots, limits for essential services such as hospitals.

Countries organise this very differently, and the numbers can mislead.

Membership

The share of workers who belong to a union. Around one in ten in the United States in the mid-2020s, around one in five in the UK, and well above half in some Nordic countries, where unions traditionally help run unemployment insurance.

Coverage

The share of workers whose pay is set by a collective agreement. France has low membership, around one in ten, yet roughly nine in ten workers are covered, because the state extends sector agreements to every firm in the sector.

Germany adds a third tool, co-determination. Elected works councils have legal rights to be consulted, and in some matters to agree, on things like working hours and layoffs. In large companies, workers elect a share of the supervisory board, up to half in firms with more than 2,000 employees. German workers have a voice inside the company, not only across the bargaining table.

In the United States, collective rights rest on the National Labor Relations Act of 1935. A union usually has to win a vote at each workplace before it can bargain there, and agreements generally cover only that unit. That makes organising slow and fragmented, which is one reason American union coverage is low.

Chapter 07

Ending a job: at-will versus just cause

The biggest single difference between American and European labor law is dismissal. Most US states follow employment at will: an employer may fire an employee for any reason or no reason, without notice, as long as the reason is not illegal (such as race, religion, or punishment for reporting wrongdoing). The employee may also quit at any time. Montana is the main exception, with a law requiring good cause after a probation period.

Most other rich countries require a valid reason for dismissal: the worker's conduct, their capability, or the employer's economic needs. They also require a fair procedure, notice, and often severance pay. In the UK, employees generally need two years of service before they can claim unfair dismissal, a threshold that has been debated in recent reforms. France and Germany have stronger protections, and large layoffs require consultation with worker representatives.

Neither side of the Atlantic has the whole truth here. At-will employment makes hiring easy and firing easy; just-cause rules make both harder. Chapter 12 weighs what each costs.

Chapter 08

Discrimination and safety

Two areas of labor law apply almost everywhere, including in the United States. The first is anti-discrimination law. The US Civil Rights Act of 1964 forbade employment discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex or national origin. EU law covers similar grounds and adds age, disability and sexual orientation. Equal pay for equal work, or work of equal value, is a legal principle in the EU and the UK.

The second is health and safety. Employers must assess risks, provide protective equipment and training, and report serious accidents. In the US this is the job of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, created in 1970. In Britain the 1974 Health and Safety at Work Act set a general duty on employers to protect workers "so far as is reasonably practicable". Work deaths in Britain have fallen sharply since then.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Most adults spend most waking hours at work

Labor law shapes how much people earn, how much free time they have, how safe they are, and how the gains from growth are shared. It is also where the new economy is testing old rules hardest.

Chapter 09

Who gets the gains

Economists argue about how much labor law changes outcomes, rather than recording what growth would have delivered anyway. Some effects are hard to deny. Working hours in rich countries fell substantially over the twentieth century. Child labour, once universal in factories and mines, became illegal and rare there. Workplace deaths fell.

Unions and bargaining also affect how income is divided. Countries with wide bargaining coverage tend to have narrower gaps between high and low wages. The decline of union membership in the US and the UK since around 1980 is one of several factors economists link to rising wage inequality, alongside technology, trade and tax. How much weight each factor deserves is contested. See Inequality for the wider argument.

Chapter 10

The gig economy test

Ride-hailing and delivery apps built their model on treating drivers and couriers as independent contractors. The apps argued that these people choose their own hours, can work for rivals, and are customers of a technology platform rather than its staff. Drivers and unions argued that the app sets the price, assigns the jobs, rates the workers and can switch them off, which is control.

Courts and lawmakers have split.

  • United Kingdom, 2021: in Uber v Aslam, the Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers were "workers", entitled to the minimum wage and paid holiday, because Uber controlled the key terms.
  • California: a 2019 law made it much harder to classify workers as contractors. In 2020 voters approved a ballot measure, Proposition 22, that exempted app-based drivers, in return for some limited benefits.
  • European Union, 2024: the Platform Work Directive created a legal presumption of employment when certain signs of control are present, and set rules on algorithmic management. Member states have until late 2026 to apply it.

The fight is really about who carries risk. As contractors, workers carry the risk of quiet days, illness and old age. As employees, the company carries more of it, and its costs rise. Neither answer is free, which is why the question keeps returning.

Chapter 11

Labor law in a global supply chain

Labor law is national, but production is global. A shirt sold in Paris may be sewn in Bangladesh from cotton grown elsewhere. In 2013 the Rana Plaza building near Dhaka, which housed garment factories, collapsed and killed more than 1,100 people. Cracks had been seen the day before, and workers were told to return anyway.

The disaster led to a legally binding safety agreement between global brands and unions, covering inspections of Bangladeshi factories. It also strengthened a newer idea: that companies should be responsible for conditions in their supply chains, not only in their own workplaces. France passed a "duty of vigilance" law in 2017, Germany a supply-chain law in force from 2023, and the EU adopted a corporate sustainability due diligence directive in 2024, parts of which it moved to scale back in 2025. The ILO and UNICEF estimated that about 160 million children were in child labour in 2020, most in agriculture. The floors that rich countries built over a century are still missing for many of the people who make what they buy.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Protection versus flexibility

Every labor rule protects someone and costs someone. The honest debate is about the size of each and who bears them.

Denmark is often cited as a middle path, called flexicurity. Firing is relatively easy, but unemployment benefits are generous and the state invests heavily in retraining. The idea is to protect the worker rather than the specific job. It depends on high taxes and strong institutions, and it may not transfer easily to other countries.

Chapter 13

The minimum wage argument

The simplest economic model says that if you force the price of something above what the market would pay, people buy less of it. Applied to labour, a minimum wage should cost jobs. For decades that was the standard textbook view.

In 1994 the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which raised its minimum wage, with neighbouring Pennsylvania, which did not. They found no loss of jobs in New Jersey. The study was fiercely disputed and widely copied. Card later shared the 2021 Nobel prize in economics, partly for this method of using real-world "natural experiments".

The current mainstream view is more cautious than either extreme. Moderate minimum wages appear to raise pay with small or hard-to-detect job losses; very high ones relative to local wages carry more risk. Where the line falls is still argued over, and it differs between a rich city and a poor rural region. That is one reason many countries now set minimum wages through expert commissions rather than by political bargaining alone.

Chapter 15

The old question in new clothes

Labor law was built for the factory: a fixed place, fixed hours, a foreman giving orders. Much work no longer looks like that. People work from home, through apps, or under software that measures every minute. Yet the underlying question has not changed since 1911. When one side controls the work and the other depends on it for a living, what does the stronger side owe?

Each generation answers it again for its own technology. The likely direction is that protection will attach less to a particular job title and more to the person: benefits that follow a worker between gigs, rights against unfair algorithmic decisions, training throughout a career. How far and how fast that happens will depend, as it always has, on politics.

The core idea

Labor law corrects an unequal bargain. It sets individual floors (wages, hours, leave, safety, non-discrimination) that cannot be signed away, and gives workers collective tools (unions, bargaining, strikes). Almost everything depends on whether a person counts as an employee.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US federal minimum wage 7.25 dollars, unchanged since 2009.
  • EU floor: at most 48 hours a week on average, at least 4 weeks paid leave.
  • About 160 million children in child labour worldwide (2020 estimate).
The common mistake

Judging a country's unions by membership alone. Coverage by collective agreements can be far higher, as in France.
