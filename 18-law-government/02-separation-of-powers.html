Separation of powers. Government designed on the assumption that rulers cannot be trusted.

If one person makes the law, enforces it and judges whether you broke it, you have no protection at all. Whatever they want becomes legal, and whoever annoys them becomes guilty. The separation of powers is the old idea that the three jobs of government should be held by different people who can block each other. It is slow, frustrating and sometimes paralysing. Its defenders say that is exactly the point.

Question 1 · What is it?

Three jobs, held by different hands

Every government does three things: it makes rules, carries them out, and settles disputes about them. The separation of powers says these jobs should not all belong to the same person or body.

Chapter 01

The French aristocrat who admired England

In 1748 a French nobleman and judge, Charles-Louis de Secondat, better known as Montesquieu, published a long book called The Spirit of the Laws. He had spent time in England and thought he had found the secret of English liberty. It was not that the English were more virtuous. It was that power in England was divided. Parliament made the laws, the king and his ministers carried them out, and judges decided cases.

Montesquieu's description of England was simplified, and in some ways wrong, because ministers already sat in Parliament. But his argument travelled far beyond his facts. When the power to make laws and the power to execute them are held by the same body, he argued, there can be no liberty, because the same people may make harsh laws and then enforce them harshly. And if the power of judging is joined to either of the others, the judge becomes a legislator or an oppressor.

The three powers have standard names. The legislature makes the laws: a parliament, congress or assembly. The executive carries them out: the president or prime minister, the ministers, and the large administration underneath them. The judiciary interprets the laws and applies them to disputes: the courts. The idea of these three branches is sometimes called by its Greek-Latin name, the trias politica.

Chapter 02

Separation is not enough: checks and balances

A pure separation, where each branch stays in its own box and never touches the others, would not work. Nobody would stop a branch that overstepped. So the more important idea is checks and balances: each branch is given a few tools to restrain the others.

The American founders put this most clearly. In one of the Federalist Papers, a series of newspaper essays written in 1787 and 1788 to persuade New Yorkers to accept the new constitution, James Madison explained the logic.

"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. … If men were angels, no government would be necessary."

The Federalist No. 51 · James Madison, 1788

The point is subtle. Madison did not expect officials to be good. He expected them to want more power. So he designed a system in which each branch's desire for power would push against the others. The safety comes not from the virtue of the people in office, but from their rivalry. A president wants to act; Congress wants to control; courts want their judgments respected. Each one's self-interest guards the others.

Chapter 03

More than three branches

Modern states have added bodies that do not fit neatly into the three boxes but share the same purpose of dividing power. Many countries have an independent central bank that sets interest rates without taking orders from the government, because politicians are tempted to cut rates before elections. Most democracies have an electoral commission so that the party in power does not run the vote count. Auditors check public spending. Ombudsmen, officials who investigate complaints against the administration, exist in many countries, starting with Sweden in 1809.

Outside the state, a free press is sometimes called the "fourth estate" or fourth power, because its reporting can expose abuses the branches miss. And in a federal country, splitting power between the centre and the regions is a second, vertical separation on top of the horizontal one. The general principle is the same in every case: no single actor should be able to do everything alone.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three main designs, and an independent judiciary under all of them

Countries apply the idea in very different ways. The biggest difference is how the executive relates to the legislature: separate and fixed, joined and removable, or a mix of the two.

Chapter 04

Presidential systems: separate elections, fixed terms

In a presidential system the head of government is elected separately from the legislature and serves a fixed term. The United States is the model, and most countries in the Americas follow some version of it. The American president cannot sit in Congress and cannot dissolve it. Congress cannot remove the president simply because it disagrees with the policy.

Each branch then holds weapons against the others. The president can veto a bill, and Congress can override the veto only with a two-thirds vote in both houses. The Senate must confirm the president's choices for judges and senior officials, and approve treaties by two-thirds. Congress controls the budget. The House of Representatives can impeach the president, meaning formally accuse him of serious wrongdoing, and the Senate then holds a trial where removal needs two-thirds. No American president has ever been removed this way, although three have been impeached: Andrew Johnson, Bill Clinton and Donald Trump, the last of them twice.

A common result is divided government, when the president's party does not control Congress. Then almost nothing passes without compromise. That can force broad agreements, or it can produce deadlock, as Chapter 09 explains.

Chapter 05

Parliamentary systems: the government lives inside parliament

In a parliamentary system, the executive is not elected separately. Voters elect a parliament, and the government is formed from whoever can command a majority there. The UK, Germany, India, Japan, Canada, Italy, Spain and most of Europe work this way. The prime minister and ministers are usually members of parliament themselves.

The key tool is confidence. A government stays in office only as long as a majority of parliament supports it. If parliament passes a vote of no confidence, the government must resign or call an election. Germany adds a twist after its Weimar experience: a constructive vote of no confidence, which can remove the chancellor only if parliament elects a successor at the same time. That stops opposition parties who agree on nothing else from bringing down governments and leaving a vacuum.

In 1867 the British writer Walter Bagehot argued that the "efficient secret" of the English system was not separation but the near-complete fusion of the executive and the legislature. A prime minister with a solid majority can pass almost anything. So in a parliamentary system, the real checks come from elsewhere: the opposition, rebels inside the governing party, coalition partners, an upper chamber, the courts, and the next election.

Chapter 06

Semi-presidential systems: France's hybrid

France's Fifth Republic, created in 1958 around Charles de Gaulle, mixes the two. There is a president, directly elected by the people since 1962, with real powers over foreign policy and defence and the right to dissolve the National Assembly. There is also a prime minister who leads the government and must keep the confidence of the Assembly. This model is called semi-presidential, and it has been copied by many countries, including Portugal, Poland and several former French colonies.

Usually the president's party controls the Assembly, and the prime minister acts as the president's lieutenant. But sometimes voters elect an Assembly of the opposite camp. Then the president must appoint a prime minister from the other side, a situation the French call cohabitation. It happened three times between 1986 and 2002. During cohabitation, power swings sharply toward the prime minister at home. France reduced the presidential term from seven years to five in 2000, partly so that presidential and parliamentary elections would fall close together and cohabitation would become rare. The years after the 2024 snap election, when no camp won a majority in the Assembly, showed a different difficulty: a president facing a fragmented parliament and governments that struggled to survive.

Chapter 07

Keeping judges independent

Whatever the system, the judiciary must be independent, or the other checks collapse. A court that fears the government cannot rule against it. Independence is built through a few practical protections. Judges usually cannot be fired for their decisions, only removed for misconduct through a difficult procedure. Their pay cannot be cut to punish them. And the way they are chosen tries to stop any one politician from packing the court with loyal friends.

The methods differ. US federal judges are nominated by the president, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life. Judges of Germany's Federal Constitutional Court are elected half by each chamber of parliament with a two-thirds majority, which forces the main parties to agree, and serve a single twelve-year term. In the UK, the highest court was part of the House of Lords until 2009. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created a separate Supreme Court and an independent commission to select judges, precisely to make the separation clearer. Before that, the Lord Chancellor had been a government minister, a judge and the presiding officer of the House of Lords at the same time, which was the opposite of Montesquieu's ideal.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Stopping abuse, at the price of speed

The value of separated powers shows most clearly when a powerful person breaks the rules and is stopped. Its cost shows when urgent problems go unsolved because nobody can agree.

Chapter 08

Watergate: the system working

In June 1972, men linked to President Richard Nixon's re-election campaign were caught breaking into the Democratic Party's offices in the Watergate building in Washington. Nixon's staff tried to cover up the connection. Over the next two years, every branch played its part in exposing it. Journalists kept reporting. A Senate committee held televised hearings, which revealed that Nixon secretly recorded conversations in his office.

Nixon refused to hand over the tapes, claiming that a president's private conversations were protected. In July 1974, in United States v. Nixon, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that he must release them. Three of the justices had been appointed by Nixon himself. The tapes showed he had joined the cover-up. With impeachment by the House close and removal by the Senate likely, Nixon resigned in August 1974.

The lesson is not that the system is perfect. It is that each branch had the means and the motive to check the others, and in that case they used them. A court that the president could ignore, or a Congress controlled entirely by his loyalists, might have produced a different ending.

Chapter 09

Gridlock: the system jamming

The same design has a darker side. In the United States, if Congress and the president cannot agree on funding, parts of the federal government close. Hundreds of thousands of employees are sent home or work without pay. A shutdown that began in December 2018 lasted 35 days, the longest until then, and one in late 2025 lasted longer still. Repeated stand-offs over the debt ceiling, a legal limit on federal borrowing that Congress must raise, have brought the country close to defaulting on its debts.

Parliamentary systems rarely suffer this exact problem, because a government that cannot pass a budget usually falls and is replaced. Instead they risk the opposite. A British government with a large majority can pass major changes quickly, and the checks on it are mostly political rather than legal. Every design chooses which danger to fear more: a government that cannot act, or a government that cannot be stopped.

Chapter 10

The executive keeps growing

Across almost all democracies, one long trend is clear. The executive has gained power compared with legislatures. Modern government regulates medicines, banks, airlines, food, the internet and the environment. Parliaments cannot write every detail. So they pass broad laws and let ministries and agencies fill in the rules. This is called delegated legislation, and in many countries it now produces far more rules each year than parliament does itself.

That raises a constitutional worry. When agencies make binding rules, are they not exercising legislative power? British critics point to so-called Henry VIII clauses, named after a king who liked to govern by proclamation, which let ministers amend Acts of Parliament by regulation. In the United States, the Supreme Court in 2024 overturned a 40-year-old doctrine known as Chevron deference, under which courts generally accepted an agency's reasonable reading of an unclear law. Supporters said this restored the role of judges and Congress. Critics said it moved technical decisions from experts to courts. Both sides agreed it shifted the balance among the branches.

Presidents also act through executive orders, directives to the administration that do not need Congress. They are legal within limits, but they can be reversed by the next president, which makes policy swing back and forth. The Public administration topic looks at how this machinery of government actually operates.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Safety against efficiency

No design wins on every count. The honest comparison is between kinds of risk.

Chapter 11

What dividing power buys, and what it costs

Strengths

Protection against abuse. No single person can make, enforce and judge the law. Better decisions through scrutiny. Bills that must pass several hands get examined more carefully. Protection of minorities. A temporary majority in one place cannot do everything. Stability. Policies that survive several veto points tend to last because they had wide support.

Weaknesses

Slowness. Many veto points mean urgent reforms can stall for years. Blurred accountability. When several branches share power, each can blame the others, and voters cannot tell whom to punish. Status quo bias. Those who benefit from current arrangements need to win only one veto point to block change. Constitutional crises. Two branches with separate mandates can each claim to speak for the people.

Chapter 12

Are presidential systems more fragile?

In 1990 the Spanish-born political scientist Juan Linz published an essay called "The Perils of Presidentialism". He noticed that many Latin American presidential democracies had collapsed into military rule during the twentieth century, while most stable long-lasting democracies were parliamentary. His explanation was dual legitimacy: the president and the legislature are both elected, both claim the people's mandate, and when they clash there is no peaceful way to settle the dispute before the next election. In a parliamentary system, a deadlock simply brings down the government.

Linz's argument was influential and contested. Other researchers pointed out that presidential systems were often adopted in poorer countries with more military involvement in politics, so the system itself might not be the cause. The United States has kept a presidential system for over two centuries. Many scholars now argue that the details matter more than the label: how strong the president's legislative powers are, how parties are organised, and whether courts are independent. The safest conclusion is that no structure can rescue a democracy where the main actors no longer accept defeat.

Chapter 14

The checks that are not in any law

Studies of democracies in trouble keep returning to an uncomfortable finding. The formal rules were often fine. What failed were unwritten habits: accepting election results, not using every legal power to its maximum against opponents, treating judges' rulings as final even when they hurt. Scholars call this forbearance, the restraint of choosing not to use a power you legally have.

Separation of powers depends on forbearance more than its designers admitted. A president could appoint judges who promise loyalty. A parliamentary majority could change the size of a court. A legislature could refuse ever to confirm anyone. All of these may be legal; all would break the balance. The branches stay separate partly because the people in them choose to keep them separate. The future of the idea therefore depends less on clever institutional design than on whether voters reward or punish leaders who push the limits.

The core idea

Government has three jobs, legislative, executive and judicial, and liberty is safer when different people hold them. Pure separation is not enough: each branch needs checks on the others, so that ambition counteracts ambition. Countries do this through presidential, parliamentary or semi-presidential designs, all relying on an independent judiciary.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws: 1748.
  • US veto override and Senate removal both need two-thirds.
  • US v. Nixon, 1974: unanimous; Nixon resigned weeks later.
The common mistake

Thinking separation of powers means the branches stay apart. It works because they overlap and can block each other. In Britain, the executive and legislature are largely fused, and the checks come from elsewhere.
