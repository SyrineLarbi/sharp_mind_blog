Human rights. The claim that some things may not be done to anyone, by any government, for any reason.

For most of history, how a ruler treated his own subjects was his own business. Other states might object to a massacre, but they had no legal ground to stand on. That changed in the middle of the twentieth century, after a war in which a modern state had murdered millions of its own citizens through lawful procedures. The answer the world reached was a new kind of law: rights that belong to people simply because they are human, and that every government owes them. Whether that promise is kept is another question, and this file looks at both the promise and the record.

Question 1 · What is it?

Rights you hold because you are human

A human right is a claim every person has against the state, whatever their nationality, and which the state cannot simply take away by passing a law.

Chapter 01

Paris, December 1948

On 10 December 1948, at a meeting in Paris, the new United Nations General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Forty-eight states voted in favour, none against, and eight abstained: the Soviet bloc, South Africa and Saudi Arabia. The date is now marked every year as Human Rights Day.

The text was drafted by a commission chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, the widow of the American president. Its members came from very different traditions. The French jurist René Cassin shaped much of the structure. The Chinese philosopher and diplomat Peng Chun Chang argued for wording that was not tied to any single religion or culture. The Lebanese philosopher Charles Malik pressed for the dignity of the individual person. The draft went through many rounds of debate, line by line.

The result has thirty short articles. They cover life, liberty and security; freedom from torture and slavery; fair trials; freedom of thought, religion, expression and assembly; the right to take part in government; and also work, education, health and an adequate standard of living. Its first article says that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. The Declaration was not a treaty and was not legally binding by itself. But it became the reference point for almost everything that followed.

Chapter 02

What makes a right a human right

Human rights share four features that set them apart from ordinary legal rights.

Universal

They belong to everyone, everywhere, not only to citizens. A foreign tourist in prison has the same right not to be tortured as a national.

Inalienable

They cannot be sold, given away or removed by an ordinary law. Some may be limited, but only under strict conditions.

Owed by the state

They are mainly duties of governments: to respect rights (not violate them), protect them (stop others violating them) and fulfil them (take steps to make them real).

Indivisible

The official view, stated by a world conference in Vienna in 1993, is that civil, political, economic and social rights all depend on each other.

The third feature is easy to miss. Human rights law is mainly about what governments may and must do, not about how private people treat each other. If your neighbour insults you, that may be rude or unlawful, but it is not normally a human rights violation. If the police beat you, it is. The state can also violate rights by failing to protect people, for example by ignoring known threats of violence.

Chapter 03

A long road before 1948

The idea of limits on rulers is old. England's Magna Carta of 1215 forced a king to accept that free men could not be imprisoned except by lawful judgment. The English Bill of Rights of 1689, the American Bill of Rights of 1791 and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 each turned limits on power into written guarantees.

But these early rights were national, and they were narrow in practice. The men who wrote "all men are created equal" in 1776 included slave owners. The French declaration of 1789 did not extend equal political rights to women, a point made at the time by the playwright Olympe de Gouges, who wrote a declaration of the rights of woman in 1791. The history of human rights is largely a history of excluded groups using the language of rights to demand inclusion: enslaved people, women, colonised peoples, religious minorities, disabled people.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Treaties, limits, balancing and courts

The Declaration was turned into binding treaties. Judges then had to work out what the rights mean in practice, which mostly comes down to deciding when a right may be limited.

Chapter 04

From declaration to treaty

The Cold War split the Declaration in two. Western states emphasised civil and political rights. Communist states emphasised economic and social rights. In 1966 the UN adopted two separate treaties, both in force from 1976: the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. With the Declaration, they are called the International Bill of Human Rights. The United States ratified the first in 1992 but not the second. China ratified the second in 2001 and has signed but not ratified the first.

Chapter 05

Absolute rights and qualified rights

Not all rights work the same way. A few are absolute: they can never be limited, whatever the reason. The ban on torture is the clearest. No emergency, war or terrorist threat justifies it in law. The ban on slavery is another.

Most rights are qualified. Freedom of expression, privacy, religion and assembly can be limited, but only if the limit meets strict conditions. The European Convention on Human Rights, for example, says expression may be restricted for reasons such as national security, public safety, preventing crime, protecting health or protecting the rights of others. That is why incitement to violence can be banned while offensive opinions are generally protected.

Some treaties also allow states to derogate, meaning to suspend certain rights temporarily in a real public emergency that threatens the life of the nation, as long as they tell the other parties and go no further than strictly necessary. Absolute rights can never be suspended this way. The structure is a ladder: a hard core that never moves, and a wider ring that can bend under pressure, but only so far and only with reasons.

Chapter 06

How judges balance: the proportionality test

When a government limits a qualified right, courts in Europe, Canada, South Africa, Israel and many other places use a series of questions known as the proportionality test.

The proportionality test, step by step
  1. LEGALITY       Is the limit set out in clear, accessible law?
         | yes
  2. AIM            Does it pursue a legitimate goal (safety, health,
         | yes      others' rights...)?
  3. SUITABILITY    Does it actually help achieve that goal?
         | yes
  4. NECESSITY      Is there a less restrictive way to get there?
         | no
  5. BALANCE        Is the benefit worth the harm to the right?
         | yes
      LIMIT IS LAWFUL          a "no" at any step -> violation

The test is powerful because it forces governments to give reasons. A blanket ban on all prisoners voting, for instance, was found by the European Court of Human Rights in 2005 (Hirst v United Kingdom) to fail, because it applied automatically to everyone, whatever their crime. The court did not say prisoners must vote. It said a blunt, unexamined ban went too far. The case caused years of political argument in Britain about whether foreign judges should decide such questions.

The United States uses a different method. Its courts apply levels of "scrutiny" depending on the right involved, and its protection of free speech under the First Amendment is unusually strong. Hate speech that would be illegal in Germany or France is generally protected in America unless it incites imminent violence or is a true threat.

Chapter 07

The UN machinery

At the global level, enforcement is mostly about reporting, review and pressure, not judgment. Each major treaty has a committee of independent experts. States send it reports, the committee questions them and issues recommendations, and in many cases individuals can bring complaints if their state has accepted that procedure.

The UN Human Rights Council, created in 2006 with 47 member states, holds a Universal Periodic Review in which every UN member's record is examined by its peers in turn. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, an office created in 1993, investigates and speaks publicly. Special rapporteurs report on themes such as torture or freedom of expression.

None of these bodies can order a state to pay compensation or change a law. Their tools are publicity and persuasion. The Council itself is often criticised because its members have included states with poor rights records, which can shield each other. The UN level is a megaphone, not a court.

Chapter 08

Chapter 09

Rights at home

Most rights are protected, or not, in national courts, under national constitutions. Germany's Basic Law of 1949, written in the shadow of Nazism, begins with the words that human dignity shall be inviolable. Its constitutional court can strike down laws that violate it. The United States Supreme Court can do the same under the Bill of Rights.

The UK has a different design. Its Human Rights Act 1998 brought the European Convention into British law. Judges must interpret laws compatibly with it where possible, but they cannot strike down an Act of Parliament. Instead they can issue a declaration of incompatibility, which leaves Parliament to decide what to do. This keeps the final word with elected politicians, which is either a strength or a weakness depending on whom you trust more.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Words that became leverage

Human rights law changed what governments could say about each other, and what citizens could demand of their own government.

Chapter 10

The clause nobody expected to matter

In 1975, at Helsinki, thirty-five states including the United States and the Soviet Union signed a long agreement on European security. The Soviet side wanted recognition of post-war borders. In return it accepted a section committing all signatories to respect human rights and fundamental freedoms. It was not a treaty, and many observers thought the rights section was empty words.

Within months, citizens in the Soviet Union, Czechoslovakia, Poland and elsewhere formed groups to monitor whether their own governments were keeping the promises they had just signed. Many were arrested. But they gave Western governments and journalists a documented, agreed standard to point to, and they gave dissidents a legal vocabulary their governments had accepted. Historians of the Cold War widely see these groups as one of the forces that weakened communist rule from inside.

The pattern repeats. Human rights law rarely forces change directly. It works by giving people a standard their government has formally endorsed, and making the gap between promise and practice visible and costly.

Chapter 11

Do the treaties make a difference?

This is an empirical question, and researchers disagree. In 2002 the legal scholar Oona Hathaway published a much-discussed study finding that ratifying human rights treaties was not reliably associated with better practices, and that some countries with poor records ratified freely because they expected no consequences. In 2009 the political scientist Beth Simmons argued that treaties did improve outcomes in specific conditions, especially in countries that were partly democratic, with courts and civil society able to use them.

The careful conclusion is conditional. Treaties do little in a closed dictatorship and little in a stable democracy that already respects rights. They seem to matter most in the middle: countries in transition, where activists, lawyers and judges can turn a signature into pressure. Some long trends are also clear. The share of countries that have abolished the death penalty in law or in practice has grown to more than two-thirds, and torture, though still common, is now something governments deny rather than defend.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A moral achievement with a mixed record

Few people oppose human rights in general. The arguments are about which rights, who decides, and how they are enforced.

Chapter 13

Universal, or Western?

The most persistent debate is about universality. Critics have argued that the human rights framework reflects Western liberal values, especially individualism, and is used selectively by powerful states against weaker ones. In the early 1990s some Asian governments, in the Bangkok Declaration of 1993, stressed national and regional particularities and the priority of economic development. In 1990 the Organisation of the Islamic Conference adopted the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam, which places rights within the framework of Islamic law.

Defenders reply that the Universal Declaration was drafted by people from many traditions, as Chapter 01 showed, and that the strongest demands for rights have often come from within non-Western societies, from anti-colonial movements to democracy campaigners. They also note that the "cultural" objection is often raised by governments rather than by the people whose rights are at stake.

Both sides have a point. The charge of selectivity is often fair: powerful states criticise rivals and excuse allies. But selective enforcement is an argument for applying the standard more evenly, not necessarily for abandoning it. Sharp disagreements remain on specific issues, such as the death penalty, abortion, religious expression and sexual orientation, where countries and cultures genuinely differ and where the law itself varies widely.

Chapter 15

A promise that has to be kept by people

Human rights law was built on a hope: that writing rights down, in treaties signed by nearly every government, would make them harder to violate. The record shows that the writing alone does not protect anyone. What protects people is the use of the writing by courts, journalists, lawyers, activists and voters.

The next decades will test that in new places: against corporations as powerful as states, against surveillance systems that never sleep, and in the effects of a warming climate on people who did least to cause it. The core idea from 1948 is likely to survive, because it is very hard to argue in public that some people do not count. How seriously it is enforced will depend, as always, on whether enough people insist.

The core idea

Human rights are universal claims against the state, set out in the 1948 Declaration and binding treaties after it. A few are absolute; most are qualified and may be limited only by law, for a legitimate aim, and proportionately. Enforcement is weak at the UN, strongest in regional courts, and decisive in national courts.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Declaration adopted 10 December 1948: 48 for, 0 against, 8 abstentions.
  • Two Covenants adopted 1966, in force 1976.
  • The European Court covers 46 states.
The common mistake

Thinking every right is absolute. Most rights can be limited; the legal question is whether a particular limit is justified and proportionate.
