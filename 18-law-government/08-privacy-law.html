Privacy law. The rules that decide who may know what about you, and what they may do with it.

A hundred years ago, privacy was mostly about walls and doors. Someone who wanted to know your business had to read your letters or follow you down the street. Today the information is collected for you, by you, every time you search, pay, walk past a camera or carry a phone. Privacy law is society's attempt to put rules around that flow. It is younger than most people think, it differs sharply between Europe, America and Asia, and it is still being written as the technology moves ahead of it.

Question 1 · What is it?

A right that had to be invented, then reinvented

Privacy law grew out of two different worries. One is about being watched and exposed. The other is about losing control of information once someone holds it. Modern law tries to deal with both.

Chapter 01

Two lawyers and a new camera

In 1890 two Boston lawyers, Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, published an article in the Harvard Law Review called "The Right to Privacy". They were reacting to two new things: cheap portable cameras, which meant anyone could be photographed without posing, and popular newspapers that printed gossip about society families. Their argument was that the law already protected people against physical intrusion and damage to reputation, and that it should also protect a more basic interest, which they described as the right "to be let alone".

The article is one of the most cited in American legal history, and its pattern has repeated ever since. A new technology makes a new kind of exposure easy, and the law then scrambles to catch up. The camera, the telephone wiretap, the mainframe computer and the smartphone each produced their own wave of law.

After the Second World War, privacy also entered international human rights law. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 says no one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with their privacy, family, home or correspondence. The European Convention on Human Rights of 1950 protects, in its Article 8, the right to respect for private and family life.

Chapter 02

Secrecy versus control

It helps to separate two ideas that both get called privacy.

Privacy as secrecy

Some things should not be seen or known by others at all: your body, your home, your private letters, your medical history. The harm is intrusion or exposure. This is the older idea, and it is about keeping information hidden.

Privacy as control (data protection)

Much information about you is not secret. Your bank knows your balance and your phone company knows where you are. The question is what they may do with it: keep it, combine it, sell it, use it to judge you. This is the newer idea, and it is about fair handling.

Germany gave the second idea its clearest statement. In 1983 its Federal Constitutional Court struck down parts of a planned national census. It ruled that people have a right to informational self-determination: the right to decide, in principle, what happens to information about themselves. A person who cannot know who knows what about them starts to censor themselves. The point is not to hide everything, but to keep the person in a position to understand and influence what happens to their data.

Chapter 03

The vocabulary of data protection

Most modern privacy laws, whatever the country, use a similar set of terms. Learn these and you can read almost any of them.

TermMeaning
Personal dataAny information about an identified or identifiable person
ProcessingAnything done with that data: collecting, storing, sorting, sharing, deleting
Data subjectThe person the data is about
ControllerThe organisation that decides why and how data is processed, and carries the main legal duty
ProcessorAn organisation that handles data on the controller's instructions
Sensitive dataExtra-protected categories: health, religion, ethnic origin, sexual orientation, biometrics

If information can be linked back to you, even by combining it with other information, it usually counts as personal data. That breadth is why the law touches almost every business.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three models: European, American, and everyone in between

Countries answer the same question in different ways. Europe writes one general law that covers everyone. The United States writes separate rules for separate industries. Many others now copy Europe.

Chapter 04

The European model: one law, seven principles

Europe's first data laws were national. The German state of Hesse passed one in 1970 and Sweden passed a national one in 1973. The European Union harmonised the rules with a directive in 1995, and then replaced it with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), adopted in 2016 and in force from 25 May 2018. As a regulation, it applies directly in every member state.

The GDPR does not list forbidden uses of data one by one. Instead it sets out principles that every use must respect.

PrincipleWhat it means in plain words
Lawfulness, fairness, transparencyYou need a legal reason, and you must tell people what you are doing.
Purpose limitationCollect data for a stated purpose and do not quietly reuse it for something unrelated.
Data minimisationCollect only what you need for that purpose.
AccuracyKeep it correct and fix it when it is wrong.
Storage limitationDo not keep it longer than needed.
Integrity and confidentialityKeep it secure against loss and theft.
AccountabilityBe able to prove you did all of the above.

The last principle is the one that changed company behaviour most. Under the GDPR the burden is on the organisation to show compliance, not on the individual to prove harm. Companies now keep records of what data they hold and assess risky projects in advance. Each EU country has an independent data protection authority that investigates complaints and can fine companies up to 20 million euros or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Chapter 05

A legal reason for every use

A common misunderstanding is that European law requires consent for everything. It does not. The GDPR lists six lawful bases, and an organisation needs at least one for each use of personal data.

  • Consent: the person freely agreed, for a specific purpose, and can withdraw.
  • Contract: the data is needed to deliver what the person asked for, such as an address to ship a parcel.
  • Legal obligation: the law requires it, such as keeping tax records.
  • Vital interests: to protect someone's life, such as in a medical emergency.
  • Public task: a public authority doing its official job.
  • Legitimate interests: the organisation has a real interest that is not outweighed by the person's rights, such as basic fraud prevention.

Consent under the GDPR has to be real. It must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and a pre-ticked box does not count. An employee may not feel free to refuse their employer, so consent at work is rarely valid. "Legitimate interests" is the most flexible basis and the most argued over, because it requires a balancing test that companies and regulators often weigh differently.

Chapter 06

The rights you hold

The GDPR gives individuals a set of rights they can use directly against any organisation that holds their data.

Access

Ask for a copy of everything held about you, and why. The organisation normally has one month to reply.

Correction and erasure

Have wrong data fixed, and in some cases have data deleted, for instance when it is no longer needed.

Objection and portability

Object to some uses, including direct marketing, and take your data with you in a usable format.

There is also a right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing when it has legal or similarly serious effects, such as an automatic loan refusal, with some exceptions. And when a security breach puts people at risk, the controller must normally tell the regulator within 72 hours.

The most famous of these rights came from a court before it was written into the GDPR. In 2014, in a case brought by a Spanish man named Mario Costeja González, the EU's highest court ruled that a search engine could be required to remove links to old, outdated information about a person from results for their name. The press called it the "right to be forgotten". The information itself stays online. It simply stops coming up when someone searches the name.

Chapter 07

The American model: a patchwork

The United States has no single general privacy law at the federal (national) level. Instead it has separate laws for particular industries and kinds of data, a style lawyers call sectoral.

LawYearWhat it covers
Fair Credit Reporting Act1970Credit reports: accuracy, and who may see them
FERPA1974Student education records
HIPAA1996Health data held by doctors, hospitals and insurers
COPPA1998Online data about children under 13
Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act1999Customer data held by financial firms

Outside those areas, the main federal tool is the Federal Trade Commission, which can act against "unfair or deceptive" business practices. If a company promises in its privacy policy not to share data and then shares it, that is deceptive. In 2019 the FTC imposed a 5 billion dollar penalty on Facebook for breaking an earlier privacy agreement, by far its largest privacy penalty at the time.

The gaps are being filled by the states. California passed the California Consumer Privacy Act in 2018, in force from 2020 and strengthened by a ballot measure in 2020. It gives residents rights to know, delete and opt out of the sale of their data. By 2025 well over a dozen states had passed broadly similar laws. So an American's privacy rights depend partly on which state they live in.

The deeper difference is philosophical. In Europe, processing needs a justification. In America, business may generally use information unless a specific rule forbids it, and the main danger guarded against is the government.

Chapter 08

Privacy against the state

A separate body of law limits police and spy agencies. In the United States it rests on the Fourth Amendment to the Constitution, which forbids "unreasonable searches and seizures". In 1967, in Katz v. United States, the Supreme Court decided that bugging a public phone booth was a search, because the caller had a reasonable expectation of privacy. Protection followed the person, not the property.

Phones forced the courts to update this. In 2014 the Supreme Court ruled that police generally need a warrant to search the contents of a phone taken during an arrest. In 2018, in Carpenter v. United States, it ruled that police generally need a warrant to obtain weeks of a person's historical phone location records from the phone company. The old rule, that information handed to a third party loses protection, could not simply be applied to a record of everywhere a person had been.

In Europe, the European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg has repeatedly judged national surveillance laws against Article 8. Surveillance must have a clear legal basis, be necessary and proportionate, and face independent oversight. Across democracies, the common principle is that the state may watch, but only under law, for good reason, and with someone checking.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Data is power, and it does not stay at home

Privacy law decides how much the companies and governments that hold data can do with it. It also shapes the global internet, because data moves across borders in milliseconds while laws stop at them.

Chapter 09

The scandals that wrote the law

Two events in the 2010s changed the debate. In 2013 Edward Snowden, a contractor for the US National Security Agency, leaked documents showing the scale of American and allied electronic surveillance, including programmes that collected data from major internet companies. It made the abstract debate concrete, especially in Europe.

In 2018 it emerged that a political consulting firm, Cambridge Analytica, had obtained data on up to about 87 million Facebook users through a personality quiz app. Most of them had never used the app. Their data was collected because their friends had. The case showed that consent given by one person can expose many others, and it arrived just as the GDPR came into force. It also led to the FTC penalty described in Chapter 07.

Chapter 10

When data crosses an ocean

The GDPR forbids sending personal data out of Europe unless it will be protected to a broadly similar standard. The European Commission can declare a country "adequate", which allows free transfers. For the United States this has been a long fight, led by the Austrian activist Max Schrems.

The EU-US transfer saga, simplified
2000  Safe Harbor deal lets US firms certify they protect EU data
2013  Snowden leaks; Schrems complains about Facebook's transfers
2015  EU court strikes down Safe Harbor          ("Schrems I")
2016  Privacy Shield replaces it
2020  EU court strikes down Privacy Shield       ("Schrems II")
        reason: US surveillance law, weak remedies for Europeans
2022  US President orders new limits and a review court for EU complaints
2023  EU-US Data Privacy Framework adopted; Meta fined 1.2 bn euros
      by the Irish regulator over earlier transfers

A European privacy dispute was really a dispute about American national security law, and it could only be settled when the US changed its own surveillance rules. Critics, including Schrems, argue the latest framework still has the same weaknesses, so the legal ground could shift again.

Chapter 11

The Brussels effect

Global companies often find it simpler to apply EU rules everywhere than to build separate systems. The legal scholar Anu Bradford named this the Brussels effect: the EU sets standards that spread around the world through markets rather than treaties.

Privacy is its clearest case. Brazil's general data protection law took effect in 2020. China's Personal Information Protection Law took effect in November 2021. India passed its Digital Personal Data Protection Act in 2023. Japan, South Korea, South Africa and many others have GDPR-style laws. According to tracking by the UN trade agency UNCTAD, roughly seven in ten countries had data protection laws by the mid-2020s.

Similar words can hide different systems. China's law imposes strict duties on companies but leaves the state broad access to data. A data protection law protects people from whoever it is designed to restrain, and that is not always the government.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Real protection, real friction

Privacy law has changed how organisations treat data. It has also produced paperwork, pop-ups, and enforcement that struggles to keep up with the largest companies.

Chapter 12

What works, and what does not

FeatureStrengthWeakness
General principles (EU style)Covers new technologies without new laws; flexible.Vague in practice. Companies and regulators argue for years about what "necessary" means.
Sectoral rules (US style)Precise and well understood in health, credit and finance.Huge gaps. Data brokers and apps can fall between the laws.
Big finesGot board-level attention for the first time.Large cases take years, and fines can be small next to the profits.
Individual rightsAnyone can find out what is held about them.Few people use them, and the burden falls on the individual.
One-stop shop (one lead regulator per company in the EU)A company deals with one authority, not 27.Ireland, home to many tech firms' EU headquarters, has been criticised by other regulators as too slow.
Compliance dutiesForces organisations to know what data they hold.Costs fall hardest on small firms, which can entrench the largest ones.

The last point is a real irony. Some studies have suggested that the GDPR's compliance burden fell more heavily on smaller advertising and technology firms than on the giants, which had the lawyers to adapt. Rules meant to restrain the most powerful companies can end up strengthening them if only they can afford to comply. The size of this effect is debated.

Chapter 13

The consent problem

The cookie banner is the most visible result of European privacy law, and it is widely disliked. It comes mostly from an older EU law on electronic communications, the ePrivacy Directive, updated in 2009, which requires consent for storing tracking files on a device. Combined with the GDPR's strict idea of consent, it produced pop-ups on almost every website.

The problem is that consent works badly when choices are constant, complex and tiny. Nobody can read the privacy policies of every site they visit. Banners are often designed so that "accept all" is one bright click and refusing takes several. Regulators call these tricks dark patterns and have started fining them. Many scholars argue for fewer consent requests and firmer rules about what is never allowed. Others reply that bans remove choices some people would happily make.

Chapter 15

The hard question underneath

Every privacy law rests on the idea that information belongs, in some sense, to the person it describes. Modern data makes that idea strained. A DNA test reveals facts about relatives who never took it. An AI model may repeat facts about a person without storing their data in any form anyone can find.

That is why the field is slowly shifting from individual control toward collective rules: limits on what may be built, inferred and decided, whoever clicked "agree". The safe prediction is that the law will keep arriving after the technology, as it has since 1890.

The core idea

Privacy law protects two things: freedom from intrusion and control over data about you. Europe uses one general law (the GDPR) built on principles, a legal basis for every use, and individual rights. The US uses sector-by-sector laws, the FTC and a growing set of state laws. Separate rules limit what the state may watch.

The numbers worth knowing
  • GDPR in force from 25 May 2018; fines up to 4% of global turnover or 20 million euros.
  • Breaches reported to the regulator within 72 hours.
  • Roughly 7 in 10 countries had data protection laws by the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Thinking European law requires consent for everything. Consent is only one of six legal bases, and often not the best one.

Informational self-determination

GDPR

Controller

Processor
