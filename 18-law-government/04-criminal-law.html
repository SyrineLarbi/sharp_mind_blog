Criminal law. The only part of the law that lets the state lock you up.

Most law sorts out who owes what to whom. Criminal law is different. It decides which acts are so harmful that the whole community, through the state, will condemn and punish them. That is an enormous power: it can take away a person's liberty, and in some countries their life. So criminal law is full of protections against its own misuse. It asks not only what you did, but what you were thinking, and it demands a very high level of proof before it calls anyone guilty.

Question 1 · What is it?

A wrong against everyone, not just the victim

Criminal law defines offences, sets the punishments for them, and lays down the rules for proving them. Its defining feature is that the state, not the victim, brings the case.

Chapter 01

Why the state is the accuser

Look at how criminal cases are named. In England, a prosecution is written R v Smith: R stands for Rex or Regina, the king or queen, in whose name the case is brought. In the United States it is The People v. Smith or United States v. Smith. In France the prosecutor is called the ministère public, the public ministry. In every case the name says the same thing. A crime is treated as a wrong against the whole community, so the community's representative brings the case.

That has practical consequences. The victim of an assault cannot usually stop a prosecution by forgiving the attacker, because it is not their case to drop. The state pays for the investigation and the prosecution. And the outcome is not compensation to the victim, which is the business of civil law, but punishment: a fine paid to the state, a community sentence, or prison.

Which acts deserve this treatment? The most influential answer in liberal societies came from John Stuart Mill in 1859. His harm principle holds that the only legitimate reason to use power over someone against their will is to prevent harm to others. Their own good is not enough. No country follows Mill strictly; most still criminalise some drugs, for example, partly to protect users from themselves. But the harm principle remains the starting point for arguments about what should and should not be a crime.

Chapter 02

No crime without a law

One principle is older and even more basic. A person can be punished only for breaking a law that existed, clearly, when they acted. Lawyers express it in Latin: nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege, no crime and no punishment without law. It is called the principle of legality.

It sounds obvious, but it is one of the most important protections people have. Without it, a government could look at something you did legally last year, make it illegal today, and prosecute you for it. France wrote the principle into its Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789. It appears in Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights and in the US constitution's ban on ex post facto laws, meaning laws that punish acts after the fact.

Legality also requires laws to be clear enough that an ordinary person can know what is forbidden. A law so vague that nobody can tell whether they are breaking it hands the police and prosecutors the power to decide who is a criminal. Courts in many countries strike down criminal laws on exactly that ground.

Chapter 03

Grades of seriousness

Every system sorts crimes by how serious they are, because the seriousness decides which court hears the case, what procedure applies and what punishment is possible.

The details vary from country to country, but the pattern is universal. Minor offences are handled quickly and cheaply. The most serious crimes get the most careful procedure, because the stakes for the accused are highest.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A guilty act, a guilty mind, and proof beyond doubt

To convict someone, the prosecution must normally prove two things: that they did the forbidden act, and that they did it with the required state of mind. Then the accused may still have a defence.

Chapter 04

The act and the mind

Imagine you pick up a black umbrella at a restaurant as you leave. It is not yours. If you thought it was yours, because you own an identical one, you have taken someone else's property but you are not a thief. If you knew it was not yours and took it anyway, you are. The physical act is the same. The difference is entirely in your head.

Common law lawyers describe the two elements with Latin labels. The actus reus, the "guilty act", is the physical part: taking the umbrella, striking the blow, making the false statement. The mens rea, the "guilty mind", is the mental part: intention, knowledge or recklessness. A famous old maxim puts it this way: an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. Civil law systems use different vocabulary, such as the French distinction between the material element and the moral element of an offence, but the idea is the same.

The mental states come in levels. An influential American framework, the Model Penal Code of 1962, sets out four.

LevelMeaningUmbrella example
Purpose (intention)You aimed to bring about the resultYou wanted to take someone else's umbrella
KnowledgeYou were practically certain it would happenYou knew yours was at home
RecklessnessYou saw a real risk and took it anywayYou suspected it might not be yours and did not care
NegligenceYou did not see a risk that a reasonable person would have seenYou never looked, though yours was blue

Most serious crimes require intention or knowledge. Some, such as causing death by careless driving, can be committed through negligence. And a few minor regulatory offences are strict liability: no guilty mind is needed at all. Selling alcohol to a minor or exceeding the speed limit can be offences even if you honestly believed you were acting lawfully. Lawmakers accept that because the penalties are small and proving knowledge in every case would be impossible.

Chapter 05

One death, different crimes

The guilty mind explains why the same result, a death, can lead to very different charges. Someone who kills deliberately commits murder. In England, murder also covers killing with the intention of causing serious injury, and it carries a mandatory life sentence. Someone who kills in a sudden fight, without intending serious harm, may be guilty of manslaughter instead. A driver who kills a pedestrian through careless driving commits yet another offence. A surgeon whose patient dies despite proper care commits no crime at all.

French law similarly separates meurtre (intentional killing), assassinat (intentional killing with premeditation, the most serious), and unintentional homicide through carelessness. American states grade murder into degrees, with first-degree murder usually requiring premeditation. The law punishes not just the harm caused, but the choice that caused it. That idea, that blame follows the mind, runs through almost every legal tradition.

Chapter 06

Defences: when a guilty-looking act is not a crime

Even when the act and the mind are proved, the accused may have a defence. The main ones exist in some form almost everywhere.

Self-defence allows reasonable force to protect yourself or others. The key word is reasonable: the response must be necessary and proportionate to the threat as you honestly saw it. Duress covers acts done under a serious threat of death or injury. Insanity covers people who, because of mental illness, did not understand what they were doing or that it was wrong. The English version comes from the M'Naghten rules of 1843, laid down after a man who believed he was being persecuted shot and killed the prime minister's secretary. Many American states still use a version of them.

The limits of these defences are revealing. In 1884 an English yacht sank, and four survivors drifted in a lifeboat for weeks. Two of the men, Dudley and Stephens, killed the youngest, a seventeen-year-old cabin boy, and the survivors ate him to survive. They were rescued days later. In R v Dudley and Stephens the court ruled that necessity is not a defence to murder: no one has the right to choose whose life is worth less. They were sentenced to death, and the sentence was then reduced to six months in prison. The case is still taught in law schools because it puts the hardest question so starkly.

Age is another limit. Every system sets an age of criminal responsibility, below which a child cannot be prosecuted. It varies widely: 10 in England and Wales, 12 in Scotland under a 2019 law, 14 in Germany. France presumes that children under 13 lack the necessary understanding. These differences reflect real disagreement about when children become responsible for their acts.

Chapter 07

From arrest to verdict

A criminal case moves through stages. The police investigate and may arrest a suspect. A prosecutor decides whether there is enough evidence, and whether prosecution is in the public interest, before bringing a charge, the formal accusation. Then comes the trial, and if the accused is convicted, the sentence.

At every stage, rights protect the accused. The most important is the presumption of innocence: the accused does not have to prove anything; the prosecution must prove guilt. There is a right to silence and a right not to incriminate oneself, a right to a lawyer, and a right to know the evidence. In the United States, the 1966 Supreme Court case Miranda v. Arizona required police to warn suspects of these rights before questioning them, which is why American television detectives recite "You have the right to remain silent". Evidence obtained illegally may be excluded from trial in some systems, most strongly in the US.

Why so many protections?

The state has police, forensic laboratories, prosecutors and prisons. The accused usually has one lawyer, if any. The protections exist to balance a contest that is unequal by nature. They are not loopholes for the guilty, though they sometimes help the guilty; they are the price of protecting the innocent.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

What punishment is for, and what a society chooses to punish

Criminal law expresses a society's deepest judgments about right and wrong. How it punishes, and whom, says a lot about what that society values.

Chapter 08

Five reasons to punish

Why punish at all? Philosophers and lawmakers have given five main answers, and real systems mix them.

AimThe ideaWhat it suggests
RetributionWrongdoers deserve to suffer in proportion to the wrongPunishment should fit the crime, no more, no less
DeterrencePunishment discourages the offender and others from offendingMake punishment certain and visible
IncapacitationA person in prison cannot harm the public outsideLong sentences for the most dangerous
RehabilitationChange the offender so they do not offend againEducation, treatment, work in prison
RestorationRepair the harm to the victim and communityMeetings between offender and victim, reparation

Retribution looks backward at what the offender did; the others mostly look forward to what punishment can achieve. That mirrors the ethical split in Volume 40 between judging by duty and judging by consequences. In 1764 the Italian thinker Cesare Beccaria published On Crimes and Punishments, which argued against torture and the death penalty and claimed that the certainty of punishment deters more than its severity. Modern research largely agrees with him. Most people who commit crimes do not expect to be caught, so raising the maximum sentence changes little; raising the chance of being caught changes more.

Chapter 09

The prison question

Countries that look similar in wealth and crime rates imprison people at very different rates. For decades the United States has had one of the highest incarceration rates in the world, several times higher than most of western Europe. The increase came mainly from the 1970s to the 2000s, through longer sentences, mandatory minimum terms and the war on drugs. The rate has fallen somewhat since its peak around 2008, but remains very high by international standards.

At the other end, the Nordic countries, especially Norway, are known for short sentences and prisons designed to resemble ordinary life, on the theory that almost everyone in prison will eventually be released and should come out able to live lawfully. Norway's reoffending rates are often cited as low, though comparing reoffending across countries is tricky because they measure it differently. The debate is about which approach makes society safer over time, not only which feels like justice today.

Chapter 10

What counts as a crime keeps changing

The list of crimes is not fixed. Sex between men was a crime in England until 1967, when it was partly decriminalised, and in Germany the relevant law was not finally repealed until 1994. Suicide was a crime in England until 1961. Blasphemy remained an offence in England and Wales until 2008.

New crimes appear too. Hacking a computer was not clearly a crime in most countries until laws such as the UK's Computer Misuse Act of 1990. In 2015 England and Wales created the offence of coercive or controlling behaviour in intimate relationships, recognising that abuse can be a pattern of domination without any single violent act. Drug laws show the debate at its sharpest. In 2001 Portugal decriminalised possession of small amounts of all drugs, treating it as a health matter rather than a crime, while keeping trafficking illegal. Supporters point to reduced HIV infections and overdose deaths in the following years; critics point to problems in some cities more recently. Criminal law is a moving map of what a society currently refuses to tolerate.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Strong protection, high costs, and hard choices about error

Every criminal justice system must decide which mistakes it fears more: letting the guilty go, or punishing the innocent.

Chapter 11

What the system does well, and badly

The English jurist William Blackstone, in his Commentaries on the Laws of England in the 1760s, put the choice in a sentence that is still quoted.

"It is better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer."

Commentaries on the Laws of England · William Blackstone

Strengths

Public condemnation. A conviction states clearly that the act was wrong. Protection. Dangerous people can be removed from the community. Restraint on revenge. Victims do not have to take justice into their own hands. Safeguards. The presumption of innocence and high standard of proof protect people against the state.

Weaknesses

Expense. Prisons cost a great deal per person per year. Uneven impact. In many countries, poorer people and some minorities are arrested, charged and imprisoned at higher rates, and the reasons are debated. Damage. Prison can break family ties and job prospects, making reoffending more likely. Victims sidelined. The case belongs to the state, and victims often feel like bystanders.

Chapter 12

The death penalty

The sharpest debate in criminal law concerns the death penalty. Most countries have now abolished it in law or stopped using it in practice. All member states of the European Union and the Council of Europe have abolished it; it is a condition of EU membership. It remains in use in China, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the United States (in some states and at federal level) and a number of other countries.

Supporters argue that some crimes are so terrible that only death is proportionate, that it gives victims' families a sense of justice, and that it may deter. Opponents argue that the state should not kill, that mistakes cannot be corrected, and that the research does not show a clear deterrent effect beyond long imprisonment. The possibility of executing an innocent person is the argument that has moved most opinion over time, especially as DNA evidence has exposed wrongful convictions. This is one of the few legal questions where Europe and much of the rest of the world have taken clearly different paths.

Chapter 14

Alternatives to prison

A quieter movement is reshaping punishment for less serious offences. Restorative justice brings offenders face to face with their victims, with trained facilitators, to talk about the harm and agree on ways to repair it. It is used in New Zealand's youth justice system, which has relied on family conferences since 1989, and in programmes in many other countries. Studies generally find that victims who take part report higher satisfaction than those who go through ordinary court, and some find modest reductions in reoffending.

Other tools include electronic monitoring, community service, drug courts that combine supervision with treatment, and diversion schemes that keep first-time young offenders out of the system altogether. None of these replaces prison for violent and dangerous offenders. But the direction of debate in many countries is clear: use prison where it protects people, and use cheaper, less damaging tools where it does not. How far that shift goes will depend on politics, because promising to be "tough on crime" remains a powerful way to win votes.

The core idea

A crime is a wrong against the community, prosecuted by the state. Conviction normally needs a guilty act and a guilty mind, proved beyond reasonable doubt, under a law that existed and was clear at the time. Defences can still apply. Punishment serves several aims, from retribution to rehabilitation, which pull in different directions.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Age of criminal responsibility: 10 in England and Wales, 14 in Germany.
  • Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments: 1764. Certainty deters more than severity.
  • Blackstone's ratio: better ten guilty go free than one innocent suffer.
The common mistake

Thinking that tougher sentences are the main way to reduce crime. The chance of being caught matters more than the length of the sentence, because most offenders do not expect to be caught.
