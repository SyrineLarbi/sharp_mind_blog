International organizations. Clubs that countries build, fund and then complain about.

The United Nations, the World Health Organization, the International Monetary Fund, NATO, the European Union: they appear in the news every day, usually described as if they were actors with minds of their own. "The UN failed to act." "Brussels decided." In reality most of them can do only what their member states allow, with the money those states provide. Understanding them means understanding that tension: bodies created to solve shared problems, run by members who never fully agree on what the problems are.

Question 1 · What is it?

Permanent meeting places with rules

An international organisation is a body set up by states, usually by treaty, with its own members, rules, staff and purpose. It turns occasional diplomacy into a standing institution.

Chapter 01

The first attempt

After the First World War, the victors created the League of Nations, which began work in 1920 in Geneva. Its central idea was collective security: an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, and members would respond together, first with economic pressure and if necessary with force. It was largely the project of US President Woodrow Wilson.

The United States never joined. The Senate refused to approve the peace treaty that contained the League's founding rules. Germany joined late and left, as did Japan and Italy. In 1931 Japan seized Manchuria from China. The League investigated, condemned the invasion, and Japan simply walked out. In 1935 Italy invaded Ethiopia. The League imposed sanctions but left out oil, the one that might have mattered. The League had rules but no way to make the powerful follow them, and by the late 1930s it was irrelevant.

The people who designed the United Nations in 1944 and 1945 studied that failure closely. Their main conclusion was that a world body must keep the great powers inside, even at the cost of giving them special privileges. That decision still shapes everything the UN does.

Chapter 02

Two designs: meeting place or pooled power

Intergovernmental

Member states keep their full sovereignty. The organisation is a forum and a service: it gathers information, hosts negotiation and runs programmes. Important decisions need agreement, often unanimity or consensus, and members can usually ignore what they did not vote for. Most bodies, including the UN General Assembly, work this way.

Supranational

Members transfer some decision-making power to the organisation itself. It can adopt binding rules by majority vote, and its own court can enforce them against members. The European Union is the main example, and even it is supranational only in some areas.

Most organisations sit somewhere between the two. The key questions to ask of any of them are the same. Who are the members? How are decisions made? Are decisions binding? Who pays? And what happens to a member that ignores them? The answers usually explain more than the organisation's mission statement.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Votes, vetoes and budgets

The inner workings of these bodies are mostly about two things: who gets to decide, and who pays. The two are closely linked.

Chapter 04

Inside the United Nations

The UN Charter was signed in San Francisco in June 1945 by 50 states, with Poland signing soon after as the 51st founding member. It now has 193 member states. The Charter created six main organs.

The General Assembly is the most democratic part, in the sense that Tuvalu and China each have one vote. But its resolutions on most matters are recommendations, not law. The Secretary-General is often described as "more secretary than general": a diplomat who can draw attention, mediate and manage, but has no army and no power to compel. António Guterres of Portugal has held the post since 2017, and the choice of his successor is due in 2026.

Chapter 05

The Security Council and the veto

The Security Council is where the UN's real power sits. Under Chapter VII of the Charter it can impose binding sanctions and authorise military force. A decision needs nine of the fifteen votes, and none of the five permanent members may vote against. Those five are the United States, Russia, China, the United Kingdom and France, the main victors of 1945. Each therefore has a veto.

The veto was the price of keeping the great powers in, the lesson of the League. It means the Council can act against small states, but never against a permanent member or a state one of them protects. During the Cold War it was used often, first mostly by the Soviet Union. In recent decades Russia and the United States have used it most, Russia on Syria and Ukraine, the United States mainly on resolutions concerning Israel.

When the Council is blocked, the General Assembly can meet in emergency session and recommend action, a procedure first used in 1950 during the Korean War. Since 2022, a General Assembly rule also requires a debate whenever a veto is cast, so that the vetoing state must explain itself in public. The veto cannot be removed without the agreement of the five who hold it, which is why reform has stalled for decades.

Chapter 06

Money and votes

The UN's core budget is a few billion dollars a year, smaller than the budgets of many large cities. Members pay according to a formula based mainly on their share of the world economy. The United States is assessed the largest share, about 22% of the regular budget, followed by China. Peacekeeping has a separate budget, and many agencies depend on voluntary donations, which donors can direct toward their own priorities. In 2025 cuts and late payments by several major contributors, above all the United States, forced the UN into deep spending reductions and a reform review.

The IMF and World Bank work differently. Votes are weighted by each country's financial contribution, its quota. The United States holds about 16% of the IMF's votes, and the most important decisions need 85%, so in practice it has a veto there too. By long tradition, a European leads the IMF and an American leads the World Bank. Whoever pays tends to steer, which is efficient for raising money and uncomfortable for legitimacy.

The World Trade Organization, with around 165 members, takes decisions by consensus, meaning any member can block. That protects small countries in theory, and makes new global trade deals very hard to reach in practice.

Chapter 07

The specialised agencies

Much of the UN system's useful work happens away from the Security Council, in specialised agencies with their own members, budgets and governing bodies. The International Civil Aviation Organization sets the standards that let a plane built in one country land safely in another. The World Meteorological Organization coordinates weather data. The World Food Programme delivers food aid on an enormous scale and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2020. UNHCR, the refugee agency, protects and assists people forced to flee their countries.

These bodies have real expertise and less politics than the Council, though not none. The World Health Organization, founded in 1948, coordinated the global campaign that eradicated smallpox, but it was also heavily criticised for its slow early response to the Ebola outbreak in West Africa in 2014 and argued over during COVID-19. The United States announced its withdrawal from the WHO in January 2025. An agency's authority comes from its expertise, and its reach from its members' money and goodwill.

Chapter 08

The European Union: sovereignty pooled

The European Union is unlike any other international organisation. Its 27 members have transferred real law-making power to shared institutions. The European Commission proposes laws and enforces them. The European Parliament, directly elected, and the Council of the EU, made of national ministers, pass them. Many decisions in the Council are by qualified majority, so a country can be outvoted and still be bound. The Court of Justice in Luxembourg rules that EU law takes priority over conflicting national law.

But the pooling is partial. Foreign policy, defence and taxation still largely need unanimity. Members can leave, as the United Kingdom did in 2020. The EU is supranational on the single market and trade, and intergovernmental on war and tax. That mix explains why it can set global rules on privacy and products (see Privacy law) but often struggles to speak with one voice in a crisis.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Problems no country can solve alone

Diseases, financial crises, polluted air and shipping lanes do not respect borders. International organisations are the main tools the world has for dealing with them, and their record is mixed in instructive ways.

Chapter 09

The quiet successes

Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people in the twentieth century alone. In 1967 the WHO launched an intensified campaign to eradicate it, using vaccination around each new case to stop its spread. The last natural case was in Somalia in 1977, and in 1980 the WHO declared the disease gone. It remains the only human disease ever eradicated, and it was achieved through cooperation between rivals, including the United States and the Soviet Union, in the middle of the Cold War.

Other successes are less dramatic. The IMF lends to countries in financial crisis when no one else will, although its conditions have often been criticised as harsh. The Montreal Protocol, supported by the UN Environment Programme, is repairing the ozone layer. Civil aviation, among the safest forms of travel, runs on shared global standards. The less a problem is about power and the more it is about coordination, the better these institutions tend to perform.

Chapter 10

Peacekeeping: the record

The Charter does not mention peacekeeping. It was improvised. In 1956, during the Suez crisis, the UN sent its first armed force to supervise a ceasefire between Egypt and Israel. The model spread: lightly armed troops from neutral countries, wearing blue helmets, sent with the consent of the parties to monitor and support a peace. UN peacekeepers received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988.

The failures are well known. In Rwanda in 1994 a small UN force was left without the mandate or troops to stop a genocide. In Srebrenica in 1995 a lightly armed Dutch battalion could not protect a declared "safe area", and more than 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were murdered. Peacekeepers have also been accused of sexual abuse, and UN troops were linked to the cholera outbreak in Haiti after 2010.

Yet many researchers who study peacekeeping statistically find that, on average, countries where peacekeepers are deployed after a civil war are less likely to slide back into fighting. Peacekeeping works best when there is a peace to keep, and badly when it is sent into an active war with too few troops and an unclear mandate.

Chapter 11

Where they stall

The same features that bring states in also slow them down. The Security Council is blocked on the wars involving its permanent members. The WTO has not been able to complete a major round of global trade talks since it was founded, and its appeals body has been unable to work since 2019. Climate negotiations under the UN produce agreements, such as the Paris Agreement of 2015, whose targets are set by each country for itself.

An organisation of sovereign states cannot be stronger than its members' willingness to be bound. When the major powers broadly agree, as on smallpox or aviation, international organisations deliver. When they are rivals, the organisations become arenas for the rivalry rather than solutions to it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Indispensable and frustrating at once

Most criticism of international organisations comes in two opposite forms: that they are too weak to do anything, and that they are too powerful and unaccountable. Both can be true of the same body.

Chapter 13

Who do they represent?

The post-1945 institutions were designed when much of Africa and Asia was still colonised. The permanent members of the Security Council have not changed since then, apart from China's seat passing from Taipei to Beijing in 1971 and the Soviet seat passing to Russia in 1991. Africa, with more than fifty UN members, has no permanent seat. India, now the world's most populous country, has none. Leadership of the IMF and World Bank still follows the old transatlantic split.

Different groups draw different conclusions. Some governments, especially in the Global South, argue for reform from inside: more seats, more votes, more say. Some have also built alternatives, such as the New Development Bank set up by the BRICS group. In some richer countries, especially among nationalist movements, the criticism runs the other way: that international bodies limit national democracy and cost too much. Calls to make these bodies more representative and calls to make them less intrusive pull in opposite directions, which is part of why change is slow.

Chapter 15

A system built for one world, living in another

The international organisations of today were mostly designed between 1944 and 1949 for a world of about fifty independent states, one dominant economy and a clear set of victors. The world now has nearly four times as many states, several centres of economic power, and problems the founders never imagined, from climate change to artificial intelligence.

Bodies like these are rarely abolished. More often they decline quietly or are bypassed, while new ones grow beside them. The likely future is a patchwork: universal bodies that keep the shared plumbing working, and smaller clubs that do the ambitious work. Whether that patchwork can handle a truly global crisis is the open question. The next file looks inward, at how a single government decides what to do (see How governments create policies).

The core idea

International organisations are bodies created by states to manage shared problems. Most are intergovernmental and can do only what members allow; a few, above all the EU, are partly supranational. Their power depends on who decides, who pays, and whether decisions bind, and they work best on coordination and worst on great-power conflict.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 193 UN members; 15 on the Security Council, 5 with a veto.
  • A Council decision needs 9 votes and no permanent-member veto.
  • Smallpox, declared eradicated in 1980, is the only human disease ever wiped out.
The common mistake

Saying "the UN failed" as if it were an independent actor. Usually its members failed to agree, or one of the five used its veto.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the UN's founders gave five countries a veto, using the League of Nations as your example? If you can, you understand the central trade-off of the whole system.

The words, in plain words
International organisation
A body created by states, usually by treaty, with members, rules and staff.
Intergovernmental
A design in which states cooperate but keep full control of decisions.
Supranational
A design in which states transfer some binding decision-making to the organisation.
Collective security
The idea that members respond together to an attack on any one of them.
Security Council
The 15-member UN body that can make binding decisions on peace and security.
Veto
The power of each of the five permanent members to block a Council decision.
Secretary-General
The UN's chief administrator and top diplomat.
Specialised agency
An independent UN-linked body with a technical field, such as health or aviation.
Quota
A country's financial share in the IMF, which sets its votes and borrowing access.
Consensus
Decision-making in which nobody formally objects, so any member can block.
Qualified majority
An EU voting rule requiring a weighted majority of states and population.
Peacekeeping
UN forces deployed, with the parties' consent, to monitor and support a peace.
Minilateralism
Cooperation in small groups of like-minded states rather than universal bodies.
