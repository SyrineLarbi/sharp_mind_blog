Data Science. The craft of asking a sharp question and not fooling yourself with the answer.

Data science sounds like a branch of computing. It is closer to detective work. Somebody has a question: why are customers leaving, which treatment works, where should we build the next shop. Somewhere there is data that might answer it, usually messy, incomplete and collected for some other purpose. The data scientist's job is to get from the question to an answer that is true, useful and honestly uncertain. The code is the easy part. The hard part is judgement.

Question 1 · What is it?

Statistics, programming and subject knowledge, in one person

Data science is not new in spirit. People have answered questions with data for centuries. What is new is the volume of data, the cheapness of computing, and a job title.

Chapter 01

A map, a pump handle and a hospital chart

In 1854 cholera killed hundreds of people in a few days in Soho, London. Most doctors believed the disease spread through bad air. A physician called John Snow suspected the water. He marked each death on a street map and saw that they clustered around one public water pump on Broad Street. Households that drew water elsewhere were mostly spared. He persuaded local officials to remove the pump's handle. The outbreak was already fading by then, but his map became one of the founding documents of epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads in populations.

A few years later Florence Nightingale, back from the Crimean War, used carefully drawn charts to show that far more British soldiers had died of preventable disease in army hospitals than of battle wounds. The charts helped change military and hospital sanitation.

Neither of them had a computer. What they did is still the heart of data science. They started with a question that mattered, gathered the right data, looked at it in a way that revealed a pattern, and presented it so that people in power acted. Every modern tool is an amplifier for that sequence.

Chapter 02

Where the job title came from

The phrase "data scientist" became popular around 2008, when people building data teams at companies such as LinkedIn and Facebook needed a name for a new kind of role. In 2012 the Harvard Business Review published an article by Thomas Davenport and D. J. Patil calling it "the sexiest job of the 21st century". The line stuck, and universities launched data science degrees by the hundred.

A popular way to describe the role is a Venn diagram drawn in 2010 by Drew Conway. It shows three overlapping circles: programming skills, maths and statistics, and subject knowledge of the field in question. Programming without statistics produces confident nonsense. Statistics without programming cannot handle modern data. And both without subject knowledge answer the wrong question very precisely. The data scientist sits in the middle.

Chapter 03

The family of data jobs

As the field matured, the single title split into several roles. The names vary between companies, but the pattern is common.

RoleMain questionTypical tools
Data analystWhat happened, and why?SQL, spreadsheets, dashboards
Data scientistWhat will happen, what causes what, and what should we do?Python or R, statistics, experiments, models
Data engineerHow do we collect, store and move data reliably?Databases, pipelines, cloud platforms
Machine learning engineerHow do we run a model in a live product, at scale?Model training and deployment systems

Two terms help here. SQL (Structured Query Language) is the standard language for asking questions of a database, such as "total sales by country last month". Python and R are programming languages with large free libraries for statistics and charts. Most working data scientists use SQL every day, whatever else they use.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From a vague question to an answer you can defend

Every serious project goes through the same stages, though rarely in a straight line. Most of the important decisions happen before any model is built.

Chapter 04

The workflow

A data science project, roughly
  1. QUESTION     "Why are customers cancelling?"  -> make it precise and measurable
       |
  2. DATA         find it, get access, understand how it was collected
       |
  3. CLEAN        fix errors, missing values, duplicates, mismatched formats
       |
  4. EXPLORE      plot it, count it, look for patterns and surprises
       |
  5. MODEL        statistics or machine learning, if needed
       |
  6. CHECK        does it hold on new data? could anything else explain it?
       |
  7. COMMUNICATE  a decision-maker understands it and can act on it
       |
       +----> new questions, back to 1

The first step is the most underrated. "Why are customers cancelling?" is not yet a question data can answer. Which customers? Cancelling within what period? Compared with whom? A precise question is half the answer, because it tells you which data you need and what a good result would look like.

The last step is the second most underrated. A brilliant analysis that the decision-maker does not understand, or does not trust, changes nothing. Snow and Nightingale were great communicators as well as great analysts.

Chapter 05

Cleaning and exploring: where the time goes

Practitioners often joke that most of the job is cleaning data, and surveys of data scientists tend to agree that it takes a large share of their time. Real data is full of problems. Dates are written in three different formats. A customer appears twice under slightly different spellings. A sensor reports "0" both when the value is zero and when the sensor is broken. A field called "revenue" includes tax in one country and not in another.

These problems are not just annoying. Each cleaning decision quietly changes the answer. Delete the rows with missing values, and you may delete exactly the unhappy customers who stopped filling in forms. That is why good data scientists write down every cleaning step, so someone else can check it.

Exploring comes next. In 1977 the statistician John Tukey published Exploratory Data Analysis, arguing that you should look at data from many angles before testing any formal hypothesis. Plot every variable. Look at the extremes. Count things. Surprises at this stage are gold. Often they are errors. Sometimes they are the real discovery.

Chapter 06

Models: describing, predicting and explaining

A model is a simplified mathematical description of how some things relate to others. It is worth separating three jobs a model can do, because people mix them up constantly.

Describe

Summarise what is in the data. "Customers on the cheapest plan cancel twice as often." Useful, but it says nothing about why.

Predict

Guess an unknown value. "This customer has a 40 per cent chance of cancelling next month." The model does not need to understand causes, only patterns that repeat. This is where machine learning shines.

Explain

Say what causes what. "Raising the price caused cancellations to rise." This is the hardest job, and the one decision-makers usually want.

The key test of any predictive model is simple: does it work on data it has never seen? A model can learn the noise in its training data so well that it fails on new cases. This is called overfitting, like a student who memorised last year's exam answers and is lost when the questions change. The standard protection is to hold back part of the data, build the model without it, and test on it at the end.

Chapter 07

Correlation, causation and the experiment

Two things that move together are correlated. That does not mean one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, because hot weather drives both. A hidden third factor like the weather is called a confounder.

Confounders can even reverse a result. In 1973 the University of California, Berkeley, looked at its graduate admissions. Overall, men were admitted at a noticeably higher rate than women. But when researchers looked department by department, most departments admitted women at similar or slightly higher rates. The explanation was that women had applied in larger numbers to the most competitive departments, which rejected most applicants of either sex. A pattern that appears in every group but reverses when the groups are combined is called Simpson's paradox.

The cleanest way to find a cause is an experiment. Split people at random into two groups, change one thing for one group only, and compare. Randomness spreads the confounders evenly, so any difference is very likely due to the change. In technology companies this is called an A/B test. Large websites run many such tests at once, on button colours, prices and recommendations. When an experiment is impossible or unethical, data scientists use methods from the field of causal inference to get as close as they can. Those methods are powerful, and they always rest on assumptions that must be stated.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Better decisions, and new ways to be wrong at scale

Data science now shapes what you see online, which loans are approved, and how hospitals plan. That gives it real power to improve decisions, and real power to harm.

Chapter 08

When data beats instinct

Around 2002 the Oakland Athletics, a baseball team with one of the smallest budgets in the league, began choosing players using statistics that most scouts ignored, such as how often a batter reached base. The story, told in Michael Lewis's 2003 book Moneyball, became famous because the team competed with much richer clubs for several seasons. Within a few years almost every professional team in many sports had its own analysts.

The lesson was not that numbers always beat experts. It was that experts often hold beliefs that have never been checked, and data can check them cheaply. The same pattern repeated in retail, logistics, medicine and public services. Supermarkets plan stock from sales data. Delivery firms plan routes. Health systems predict demand for beds. Most of this is quiet, practical work that saves money and time without making headlines.

Chapter 09

When data automates old unfairness

The same methods can go badly wrong. In 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had abandoned an experimental tool for rating job applicants. It had been trained on ten years of the company's past hiring decisions, which favoured men, and it learned to mark down CVs containing words like "women's". Nobody programmed it to discriminate. It learned the pattern that was in the data.

In 2016 the news organisation ProPublica analysed COMPAS, a tool used in some US courts to predict whether defendants would reoffend. It reported that Black defendants who did not reoffend were wrongly labelled high-risk more often than white defendants. The tool's maker disputed the analysis. The debate that followed revealed something deep: there are several reasonable mathematical definitions of "fair", and researchers showed that, in general, a tool cannot satisfy all of them at once when groups have different underlying rates. Choosing between them is a moral and political choice, not a technical one.

Chapter 10

The crisis of findings that do not repeat

From the early 2010s, large projects tried to repeat well-known studies in psychology, medicine and economics. A worrying share failed to produce the same results. This became known as the replication crisis. One major cause was a set of habits sometimes called p-hacking: running many analyses and reporting the one that looks significant.

Here is why it works. A common standard says a result is "significant" if it would happen by chance less than one time in twenty. So if you test twenty unrelated things, one will probably pass by luck alone. Try different subgroups, drop a few outliers, measure the outcome three ways, and a "discovery" is almost guaranteed. The statistician Andrew Gelman calls the many hidden choices "the garden of forking paths". Often nobody is cheating on purpose.

Companies face exactly the same risk with their dashboards and A/B tests. The fixes are known: decide the analysis before seeing the results, report everything you tried, use fresh data to confirm, and be suspicious of surprising findings from small samples.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful lens that can also distort

Data science brings discipline to decisions. It also brings the risk of false precision, and of measuring only what is easy to measure.

Chapter 11

Weighing it up

Strengths

Checks beliefs against reality. Assumptions that "everyone knows" can be tested. Finds patterns humans miss in millions of records. Experiments reveal causes, not just correlations. Decisions become explainable and repeatable, rather than resting on one person's instinct. Small gains add up: a one per cent improvement across millions of transactions is a lot of money or a lot of saved time.

Weaknesses

Garbage in, garbage out. Bad or biased data gives confident wrong answers. False precision: a number with two decimal places feels truer than it is. Measures what is easy, such as clicks, rather than what matters, such as satisfaction. Privacy: useful data is often personal data. Automation of bias at scale, as Chapter 09 showed.

Chapter 12

Goodhart's law: when the number becomes the goal

The economist Charles Goodhart observed in the 1970s that a statistic used for control tends to stop working as a measure. The popular version, phrased by the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, is: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Data science makes this trap easier to fall into, because it makes measuring cheap.

Examples are everywhere. A video platform that optimises for watch time may learn to promote whatever keeps people watching, including outrage. A call centre measured on call length may hang up on difficult customers. A hospital measured on waiting times may reclassify who counts as waiting. The model does exactly what it is told, so the danger lies in what it is told. Good data teams track several measures at once, including ones that capture harm, and keep asking whether the number still reflects the real goal.

Chapter 14

What stays human

If an AI tool can write the code, clean the data and fit the model in minutes, what is left? Quite a lot, and it is the part that always mattered most. Someone must choose the question. Someone must know how the data was collected and what is missing from it. Someone must notice that a result is too good to be true. Someone must decide which definition of fairness to use, and explain the answer to people who will act on it.

There is also a new risk. When analysis becomes effortless, bad analysis becomes effortless too. An AI assistant can run fifty versions of an analysis in a minute, which is the garden of forking paths at machine speed. So the habits of good data science matter more, not less: state the question first, look at the raw data, test on fresh data, and report uncertainty honestly. The core idea

Data science combines statistics, programming and subject knowledge to turn questions into trustworthy answers. The workflow runs from a precise question through cleaning, exploring and modelling to communication. Prediction is easier than explanation, and only experiments or careful causal methods reveal what causes what.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The "one in twenty" significance standard means about one in twenty tests of pure noise will look like a finding.
  • 1854: John Snow's cholera map. 1977: Tukey's Exploratory Data Analysis.
  • 2012: "the sexiest job of the 21st century", Harvard Business Review.
The common mistake

Reading a correlation as a cause. Before believing that A causes B, ask what else could drive both, and whether anyone actually changed A and watched what happened.

Test yourself

Can you explain how a pattern can hold in every department and reverse when the departments are combined? If you can, you understand confounders, and why data needs context.
