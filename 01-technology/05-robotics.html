Robotics. Putting a computer into a body, and discovering how hard the body part is.

Science fiction taught us to imagine robots as metal people. Real robots are mostly arms bolted to factory floors, shelves on wheels in warehouses and vacuum cleaners bumping into sofas. They are very good at doing one precise thing millions of times, and still surprisingly bad at things a child does without thinking, such as folding a towel or picking up an unfamiliar object. Understanding why tells you a great deal about intelligence itself, and about which jobs are likely to change first.

Question 1 · What is it?

A machine that senses, decides and acts in the physical world

A robot is not defined by its shape. It is defined by a loop: it measures the world, works out what to do, and moves something, then measures again.

Chapter 01

A word from a play, a machine from a car plant

The word "robot" comes from a 1920 play, R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots), by the Czech writer Karel Čapek. It was based on the Czech word robota, meaning forced labour. In the play, artificial workers are manufactured to serve people and finally rebel. So from its very first use, the word carried two ideas that still shape the debate: machines that do our work, and the fear of what they might do to us.

The real thing arrived quietly. In 1961 a robotic arm called Unimate began work at a General Motors plant in New Jersey. It was designed by the inventor George Devol and sold by Joseph Engelberger, often called the father of industrial robotics. Unimate lifted hot pieces of die-cast metal and stacked them, a dangerous and unpleasant job. It had no eyes and no intelligence. It followed a recorded sequence of movements, perfectly, over and over.

That contrast is still the heart of the field. The robots that changed the economy were not clever. They were precise, tireless and placed in environments arranged so that cleverness was not needed.

Chapter 02

The robot family

Engineers usually describe a robot as a machine that can sense, think (compute) and act, and that can be reprogrammed for different tasks. A washing machine follows a fixed program and does not count; a robot arm that can weld one day and paint the next does. Here are the main kinds.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Muscles, senses and a loop that never stops correcting

Every robot combines a body that can move, sensors that measure, and a controller that compares what is happening with what should happen, hundreds or thousands of times a second.

Chapter 03

The body: joints, motors and degrees of freedom

A robot's muscles are called actuators. Most are electric motors, usually combined with gears that trade speed for strength. Very heavy machines use hydraulics, pushing oil through cylinders, as diggers do. Some soft robots use air pressure in flexible chambers, which makes them gentle enough to pick fruit.

The key measure of a robot body is its degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways it can move. A door has one (it swings). A typical industrial arm has six, which is the minimum needed to put a tool at any position and any angle within reach. Your own arm, from shoulder to wrist, has seven, and your hand adds more than twenty. Every extra degree of freedom makes a robot more capable and harder to control, which is one reason human hands are so hard to copy.

Chapter 04

The senses: from encoders to lidar

A robot first needs to know about itself. Encoders on each joint measure its exact angle. An inertial measurement unit, the same kind of chip that knows when you rotate your phone, measures tilt and acceleration, which matters for balance. Force and torque sensors feel how hard the robot is pushing, so it can insert a part without crushing it.

Then it needs to know about the world. Cameras supply rich detail but no direct sense of distance. Depth cameras add distance, often by projecting an invisible infrared pattern. Lidar spins a laser and measures how long the light takes to bounce back, building a precise 3D "point cloud" of the surroundings. Touch sensors on fingertips are improving but remain far less sensitive than human skin. Making sense of camera images is a field of its own, covered in Computer vision.

Chapter 05

Control: the loop that keeps correcting

Think about how you carry a full cup of coffee. You do not plan every muscle movement in advance. You watch the surface of the coffee, notice it tilting, and correct, continuously. That is feedback control, and it is the basic skill of every robot.

The most common controller in industry is called PID, after its three parts. It pushes in proportion to the present error, adds a correction for error that has built up over time, and damps the push if the error is shrinking fast, so the arm does not overshoot and wobble. A thermostat, cruise control and a robot joint can all use the same idea. The wider theory is in Control systems. A robot is, at its lowest level, a stack of feedback loops running very fast.

Chapter 06

Where am I? Mapping and moving

A factory arm never leaves its spot. A mobile robot has to answer two questions at once: where am I, and what does the place look like? These depend on each other. To build a map you need to know where you are, and to know where you are you need a map. The technique that solves both together is called SLAM: simultaneous localisation and mapping.

A robot vacuum is a good example. As it moves, it combines wheel measurements ("I have rolled two metres") with what its camera or lidar sees ("the wall is now 30 centimetres away"). Wheel measurements drift, because wheels slip. Sensor views are noisy. By combining them and constantly checking which version is more likely, the robot builds a usable floor plan. Then a path planner works out a route that avoids obstacles, rather like a satnav for a living room.

Chapter 07

The hardest problem: picking things up

In the 1980s the roboticist Hans Moravec pointed out an irony now called Moravec's paradox. Computers find it easy to do what adults find hard, such as arithmetic or chess, and very hard to do what a one-year-old finds easy, such as perceiving and moving. The reason is that our sensing and movement skills were refined over hundreds of millions of years of evolution, so they feel effortless and we do not notice how complex they are.

Grasping shows why. To pick up a mug from a cluttered table, a robot must find the mug among other objects, estimate its shape, including the side it cannot see, choose where to hold it, move without hitting anything, and adjust its grip as it feels the weight. A wine glass, a soft peach and a wet bar of soap all need different strategies. Cloth is worse, because it changes shape every time you touch it. In a factory, engineers remove this difficulty by making every part arrive in the same place at the same angle. In a home, nobody can do that.

Chapter 08

Teaching robots instead of programming them

Traditionally, every robot movement was programmed by an engineer. That is precise but slow, and it breaks the moment anything changes. Since the 2010s, robotics has borrowed heavily from machine learning. Three approaches stand out.

Learning in simulation. A robot can practise millions of times in a virtual world, using reinforcement learning, before trying in reality. The difficulty is the sim-to-real gap: real friction, light and materials never match the simulator exactly. Engineers randomise the simulation, changing weights, colours and friction, so the robot learns to cope with variety. This has worked especially well for walking.

Learning from demonstration. A person guides the robot, physically or by remote control, and it learns to imitate. Many labs now collect thousands of such demonstrations. Robot foundation models. Since about 2023, several companies and labs have trained large models that take camera images and a written instruction ("put the apple in the bowl") and output movements directly. They are promising, but still far less reliable than their text equivalents, because there is no internet-sized library of robot movement data to learn from.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Productivity, safety, and the question of jobs

Robots already decide how cheaply cars, phones and parcels reach you. Where they spread next, and who benefits, is one of the central economic questions of the coming decades.

Chapter 09

The factory floor, in numbers

The International Federation of Robotics, the industry's main statistics body, counted about 4 million industrial robots working in factories worldwide in 2023, with roughly half a million new ones installed that year. China alone accounted for about half of new installations. Measured by robots per worker, South Korea has led the world for years, with about one robot for every ten manufacturing workers.

Most of these robots are in car and electronics plants, because those industries make huge numbers of identical products. Robots are economical when the task repeats exactly and the volume is high. A small workshop making a different custom part every day gains far less, which is why collaborative robots, cheaper and easier to reprogram, are aimed at that market.

Chapter 10

Warehouses and operating theatres

The biggest growth outside factories has been in warehouses. In 2012 Amazon bought Kiva Systems, whose small orange robots slide under shelves and carry them to a person who picks the items. Instead of workers walking many kilometres a day between aisles, the shelves come to them. Amazon now runs hundreds of thousands of robots of various kinds. Picking individual items out of a mixed bin, however, is still often done by people, because it is the grasping problem from Chapter 07.

Surgical robots are a different story. The best known, the da Vinci system, was cleared for general surgery in the United States in 2000 and has been used in millions of operations. It is not autonomous. A surgeon sits at a console and controls it. The robot filters out hand tremor, scales large hand movements into tiny precise ones, and lets instruments enter through small cuts. It has made some keyhole operations easier, though for many procedures studies find outcomes similar to conventional keyhole surgery, at higher cost.

Chapter 11

Dull, dirty, dangerous, and what happens to jobs

Engineers have long said robots are best for jobs that are "dull, dirty or dangerous". They defuse bombs, inspect pipes and sewers, work inside damaged nuclear plants and explore other worlds. NASA's rovers have driven across Mars since 1997, controlled with delays of several minutes each way, which forces them to handle some decisions on their own.

The effect on employment is genuinely debated. A widely cited 2020 study by the economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found that US areas more exposed to industrial robots saw somewhat lower employment and wages. Studies of Germany found that robots shifted workers into other roles within firms more than they caused job losses. The overall picture most economists accept is that robots displace particular tasks and particular workers, often in specific regions, while raising productivity overall. Whether the gains are shared depends on policy, training and bargaining, not on the machines. The wider question is in Automation.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Superb in a fixed world, fragile in a messy one

The strengths and weaknesses of robots are two sides of one fact: they are only as good as the predictability of their surroundings.

Chapter 12

The balance sheet

Advantages

Precision and consistency: the same weld, to a fraction of a millimetre, thousands of times a day. Endurance: no breaks, no fatigue, night shifts at no extra cost. Safety: they take on heavy lifting, toxic fumes and dangerous places. Reach: they work on Mars, in reactors and in tiny spaces inside the body. Productivity: they lower the cost of manufactured goods for everyone.

Disadvantages

Brittleness: a slightly misplaced part or an unexpected object can stop the line. Cost: the robot, the integration, the safety systems and the engineers often cost far more than the arm itself. Poor dexterity: soft, varied or unfamiliar objects remain hard. Uneven effects on work: gains go to owners and skilled staff, losses to specific workers and towns. New risks: physical injury, hacking and, in the military, serious ethical questions.

Chapter 13

Safety: from cages to cobots

A large industrial arm moves fast and with great force. It cannot tell a car door from a human head. For decades, the answer was simple: put it in a cage, and stop it automatically if anyone opens the gate. International safety standards still require this for most heavy robots.

Collaborative robots take another route. They are lighter and slower, have rounded edges, and constantly measure the force at their joints. If they bump into something unexpected, they stop. This lets a person and a robot share a workbench. The trade-off is clear: a cobot is safer because it is weaker and slower, so it cannot match a caged arm's speed. Mobile robots add another problem, because they move through spaces where people walk, which is why many warehouses still keep robot zones and human zones apart.

Chapter 15

How to read the next robot video

Impressive robot videos now appear almost weekly. Four questions help you judge them. Was the robot acting on its own, or was a person controlling it remotely? Many demonstrations use teleoperation, which is a legitimate way to collect training data but is not autonomy. How many attempts were filmed to get this one? Was the setting arranged in advance? And how fast is it compared with a person? Many tasks are shown sped up.

The honest picture as of 2026 is this. Learning-based methods have made robots far better at walking, balancing and simple handling than they were ten years ago. The remaining gap is reliability across endless variety, and that gap closes slowly, because each new situation must be learned from physical data that is expensive to collect. The most likely future is gradual: robots spreading task by task, from warehouses to farms to hospitals, rather than a sudden arrival of machines that can do everything. The question of who controls and benefits from them will matter at least as much as what they can do.

The core idea

A robot is a sense, compute, act loop in a physical body. Its actuators move it, its sensors measure itself and the world, and feedback control corrects errors many times a second. Robots excel where the world is predictable and struggle where it is not.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The word "robot": 1920, Karel Čapek. The first industrial robot: 1961, Unimate.
  • About 4 million industrial robots at work worldwide in 2023, about half of new ones installed in China.
  • A typical industrial arm has 6 degrees of freedom; a human arm has 7.
The common mistake

Assuming that a robot that can do something hard, like a backflip, can easily do something easy, like loading a dishwasher. For robots, the "easy" things are often the hardest.

Test yourself

Can you explain why car factories were automated decades ago while home robots still mostly vacuum? If you can, you understand structured environments, grasping and Moravec's paradox.

The words, in plain words
Actuator
The part that produces movement: a motor, hydraulic cylinder or air chamber.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent ways a robot body can move.
Encoder
A sensor that measures the exact angle or position of a joint or wheel.
Lidar
A sensor that measures distance with laser pulses to build a 3D picture.
Feedback control
Constantly comparing what is happening with what should happen, and correcting.
PID controller
The most common feedback controller, combining present error, past error and rate of change.
SLAM
Simultaneous localisation and mapping: building a map while working out where you are on it.
Path planning
Finding a route or movement that reaches a goal without collisions.
Moravec's paradox
What is hard for people is often easy for machines, and the reverse.
Sim-to-real gap
The difference between how a robot behaves in simulation and in reality.
Teleoperation
A person controlling a robot remotely.
Cobot
A collaborative robot designed to work safely alongside people.
