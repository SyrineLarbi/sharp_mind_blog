Natural Language Processing. Seventy years of trying to make machines read, and three changes of mind.

Every time you search the web, see an email sent to spam, dictate a message or read a translated menu, a branch of computing called natural language processing, or NLP, is at work. Its goal is simple to state: let computers handle human language, written or spoken. It has been one of the hardest problems in computing. The field tried hand-written grammar rules first, then statistics, then neural networks, and each change of approach was a surprise. Today's chat assistants are the latest chapter of that long story, not the whole of it.

Question 1 · What is it?

The part of computing that deals with human language

"Natural" language means the languages people actually speak, such as Arabic, French or English, as opposed to programming languages designed for machines.

Chapter 01

Sixty Russian sentences and a promise

On 7 January 1954, in New York, Georgetown University and IBM gave a public demonstration. An IBM computer translated more than sixty Russian sentences into English. The sentences had been carefully chosen, and the program used a vocabulary of about 250 words and a handful of grammar rules. Newspapers were thrilled. Some people involved predicted that machine translation could be largely solved within a few years. This was the Cold War, and the US government badly wanted to read Soviet scientific papers. Money followed.

The prediction failed badly. In 1966 a US government committee, known as ALPAC, reported that machine translation was slower, less accurate and more expensive than human translation, and that there was no near prospect of useful general translation. Funding collapsed. The pattern of that first story, a dazzling demo on chosen examples followed by a hard collision with real language, would repeat several times in the field's history. It is worth remembering whenever a new system is shown off.

Chapter 03

Why language is so hard for machines

Consider the sentence "The chickens are ready to eat." Are the chickens about to have dinner, or about to be dinner? You decide in a fraction of a second, using the context. This is ambiguity, and it is everywhere in language. A word can have several meanings ("bank"). A sentence can have several structures ("I saw the man with the binoculars": who has the binoculars?). A pronoun can point to several things.

Language also depends on knowledge that is never stated. "Can you pass the salt?" is not a question about your abilities. "Great, another meeting" is often not great. To understand a simple story, you must know how the world works: that glasses break when dropped, that people get angry when insulted. Language is compressed, and the reader is expected to unpack it with common sense. Computers have no common sense of their own. Every era of NLP has been a different attempt to supply it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rules, then statistics, then neural networks

The clearest way to understand how NLP works today is to follow how it changed. Each era kept something from the last and solved a problem the last could not.

Chapter 04

The rules era, and the program that fooled its users

The first approach was to write language down as rules, as a grammar textbook does: a sentence is a noun phrase plus a verb phrase, a noun phrase is an article plus a noun, and so on, with dictionaries listing what each word means. Linguists and programmers spent decades building such systems. They worked in narrow areas, such as weather reports, and failed on ordinary text, because real language has more exceptions than rules.

The most famous program of this era was ELIZA, written by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT and described in 1966. Its best-known script imitated a psychotherapist. It used simple pattern matching: if you typed "I am sad about my mother", it might answer "Tell me more about your family". It understood nothing. Yet Weizenbaum was disturbed to find that people, including his own secretary, became emotionally involved in conversations with it and wanted privacy while using it. People readily credit fluent text with understanding, a tendency now called the ELIZA effect. It matters even more today.

Chapter 05

The statistics era: counting instead of parsing

From the late 1980s, researchers, many at IBM, tried something different. Instead of writing rules, they counted. If you have millions of sentences, you can measure how often each word follows each other word. That lets you estimate which of two possible transcriptions of a sound, or two possible translations, is more likely. A model that predicts a word from the few words before it is called an n-gram model; "n" is how many words it looks at.

For translation, IBM researchers trained on the records of the Canadian parliament, which are published in both English and French. With enough parallel text, a program could learn which French words tend to line up with which English words, without knowing any grammar. Google Translate, launched in 2006, used this statistical approach for its first decade. Statistics beat rules not because it understood more, but because it could learn from far more examples than any team could write rules for.

Chapter 06

Words as points in space

A big problem with counting is that it treats every word as unrelated to every other. "Cat" and "kitten" are as different as "cat" and "tax". The fix came from an old idea in linguistics.

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

J. R. Firth · British linguist · 1957

Words used in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. In 2013 a team at Google led by Tomas Mikolov released word2vec, a fast way to turn each word into a list of a few hundred numbers, called a word vector or embedding, learned from which words appear near which. Words with similar meanings ended up close together. More strikingly, directions in this space carried meaning. Taking the vector for "king", subtracting "man" and adding "woman" landed near "queen". Meaning had become geometry, the same idea that sits inside today's large models, as Volume 14 describes.

Chapter 07

Neural networks learn to read sentences

Word vectors handle single words. Sentences need order: "dog bites man" is not "man bites dog". In the early 2010s, researchers used recurrent neural networks, which read a sentence one word at a time and carry a running summary forward, like a reader keeping the gist in mind. An improved version called the LSTM (long short-term memory) was better at remembering words from earlier in a sentence.

In 2014 several groups showed that one network could read a whole sentence in one language and a second network could write it out in another. This became neural machine translation. In 2016 Google switched Google Translate to a neural system and reported large reductions in translation errors for several major language pairs. Users noticed the jump in fluency almost overnight. The weakness of these networks was that, reading one word at a time, they were slow to train and still lost track of long passages.

Chapter 08

The transformer and the idea of pretraining

In 2017 a team at Google published a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need". It introduced the transformer, which drops step-by-step reading. Instead, every word looks at every other word at once and decides which ones matter for its meaning, a mechanism called attention. Because all those comparisons happen in parallel, transformers can be trained on vastly more text using GPU chips. Volume 14 explains attention with an example (What a language model actually does).

The second key idea was pretraining. Instead of training a new model for each task, you first train one large model on a huge amount of general text, with a simple goal such as filling in hidden words or predicting the next word. Then you adapt it to a specific task with a little extra training. Google's BERT, released in 2018, used the fill-in-the-blank approach and was soon used to improve Google Search. OpenAI's GPT series used next-word prediction. Making those models much larger led to the chat assistants of the 2020s, whose workings and failure modes are in Volume 49. The big change was that one general model replaced hundreds of separate, task-specific systems.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Most human knowledge is stored in language

Laws, medical records, science, contracts, emails and conversations are all language. A machine that can process language well can reach almost every area of life.

Chapter 09

The invisible infrastructure of daily life

Long before chat assistants, NLP was everywhere. Search engines use it to work out what you mean, not just which words you typed, so that "cheap flights Paris next week" finds pages that never use those exact words. Spam filters classify billions of emails a day. Phone keyboards predict your next word. Customer service systems route messages to the right team. Banks and lawyers use information extraction to read thousands of contracts for specific clauses.

Speech recognition has had an especially large human impact. Automatic captions on videos and in video calls help deaf and hard-of-hearing people, and anyone in a noisy place. Dictation helps people who cannot easily type. Voice assistants arrived on phones from 2011, when Apple launched Siri. The most widely used NLP is often the least noticed, because it works well enough to be taken for granted.

Chapter 10

The languages left behind

The world has around 7,000 languages. Modern NLP learns from text, and digital text is very unevenly spread. English dominates the internet; a few dozen other languages have large amounts; thousands have very little or almost none. Researchers call these low-resource languages. Systems work much better in English than in, for example, Wolof or Quechua, and a speaker of a low-resource language gets worse search, worse translation and worse voice recognition.

There is a cost dimension too: models cut text into pieces called tokens, and languages poorly represented in training often need more tokens per word, so the same message costs more to process. Volume 14 explains this "quiet inequality" (Tokens). Efforts to close the gap include community projects that collect text and recordings, and large multilingual models; in 2022, for example, Meta released a translation model covering about 200 languages. Whether language technology helps endangered languages survive, or speeds up the shift to a few dominant ones, is an open question.

Chapter 11

How do you measure understanding?

In 1950 the mathematician Alan Turing proposed what is now called the Turing test: if a judge chatting by text cannot reliably tell a machine from a person, we should take seriously the idea that the machine thinks. ELIZA showed how easily people could be fooled in short conversations, so researchers mostly turned to narrower, measurable tests.

These are called benchmarks: fixed sets of questions or tasks with known answers, such as translation test sets, reading comprehension quizzes or collections of exam questions. They drove progress for decades. But they have weaknesses. Models improve so fast that benchmarks become too easy within a few years. Test questions can leak into the huge training data, so a model may have seen the answers. And a high score on a test of one skill does not guarantee the skill in real use. Benchmark scores are a useful signal, not a measure of understanding, which is why companies increasingly test systems on the real tasks they will perform.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fluency gained, transparency lost

Each era of NLP solved old problems and created new ones. Weighing them shows what today's systems are good for, and where older methods still win.

A practical lesson follows. The newest method is not always the right one. A bank checking whether a form contains a valid date needs a simple rule, not a large model that might occasionally improvise. Good systems often combine all three.

Chapter 13

The costs: bias, invention and scale

Because modern NLP learns from human text, it learns human prejudices too. A 2016 study of word vectors, titled "Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?", showed that the same geometry that produced "king − man + woman ≈ queen" also encoded gender stereotypes about jobs. The general mechanism is in Vol 49, Bias. Large models can also produce confident falsehoods, and fluent text makes errors harder to spot, the ELIZA effect again.

Scale brings its own problems. Cheap, fluent text generation makes spam, scams and coordinated misinformation easier to produce in any language. The same tools that help a small business write to customers can help a fraudster write a convincing phishing email. There are also effects on work: translation, transcription and some writing jobs have been changed quickly, with professionals increasingly editing machine output rather than writing from scratch. The technology lowers the cost of producing language for everyone, honest and dishonest alike.

Chapter 15

Does it understand? The live debate

In 1980 the philosopher John Searle described a thought experiment called the Chinese Room. A person who speaks no Chinese sits in a room with a huge rulebook. Chinese questions come in through a slot; following the rules, the person sends back correct Chinese answers. To people outside, the room seems to understand Chinese. Searle argued that nobody inside does, and that a computer following a program is in the same position.

The debate has returned with large language models. One side, expressed in a widely discussed 2021 paper that called such models "stochastic parrots", argues that they assemble plausible text from patterns without any grasp of meaning or of the world. The other side points out that these models can solve new problems, explain jokes and reason through unfamiliar puzzles, and argues that something like understanding may emerge from learning language at scale. Researchers genuinely disagree, and the disagreement is partly about the word "understand" itself.

For practical purposes, two things are true at once. These systems are extraordinarily useful language tools. And they are not reliable sources of truth on their own. Seventy years after the Georgetown demonstration, the field's oldest lesson still holds: judge a language system by how it handles your real text, not by its best demonstration.

The core idea

NLP lets computers handle human language. It moved from hand-written rules to statistics to neural networks. Key steps were word vectors (meaning as geometry), neural translation, and the transformer with pretraining, which led to today's large language models.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Georgetown-IBM demo 1954; ELIZA 1966; word2vec 2013; transformer 2017.
  • Google Translate went neural in 2016.
  • About 7,000 languages exist; only a small fraction have plenty of digital text.
The common mistake

Treating fluency as proof of understanding or accuracy. This is the ELIZA effect, and it has fooled people since 1966.
