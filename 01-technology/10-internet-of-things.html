Internet of Things. When the objects around you start reporting what they notice.

The first internet connected people to people through computers. The Internet of Things, usually shortened to IoT, connects objects: electricity meters, factory motors, thermostats, cattle collars, shipping containers, heart monitors and doorbells. Each one is small and does little on its own. Together they produce a constant stream of measurements about the physical world, and some of them can act on it. That makes life cheaper and more convenient. It also puts billions of poorly protected computers into homes, hospitals and power grids.

Question 1 · What is it?

Ordinary objects with a sensor, a small computer and a connection

An IoT device measures something about the physical world, sends that measurement over a network, and sometimes receives instructions to act. That is the whole idea.

Chapter 01

A drinks machine that saved a walk down the corridor

In the early 1980s, students in the computer science department of Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh had a problem. Their Coca-Cola machine was a long walk from their offices, it was often empty, and freshly loaded bottles were warm. So they fitted it with simple sensors and connected it to the department's computer, which was linked to ARPANET, the ancestor of the internet. From their desks, they could check how many bottles were in each column and how long they had been cooling.

It was a joke project, and it contained the entire idea. An ordinary object reports its state over a network, so people can decide without going to look. The phrase "Internet of Things" came later, in 1999, from Kevin Ashton, who used it in a presentation at Procter & Gamble about tagging products with radio chips (RFID) so that supply chains could track them automatically.

Chapter 02

What counts as a "thing"

A laptop or phone is not usually called an IoT device, because a person uses it directly. An IoT device is typically an object whose main job is something else, such as heating, measuring or moving, with connectivity added. Most do one narrow task. The main families look like this.

Chapter 03

How many, and why counting is hard

Estimates of the number of connected IoT devices vary widely because people count differently. Does a car with dozens of connected parts count once or many times? Does a supermarket price tag? Most industry estimates for the mid-2020s fall roughly in the range of 15 to 20 billion devices, which is more than two for every person on Earth, and growing. Treat any exact figure with suspicion.

What matters more than the total is the shape. Most IoT devices are not glamorous gadgets. They are cheap sensors in factories, meters and warehouses, which is where the money and most of the measurable benefit are. The smart speaker gets the headlines; the vibration sensor on a water pump pays the bills.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sense, connect, decide, act, on a tiny power budget

An IoT system is a chain. Each link involves a trade-off between cost, battery life, range and how much data can be sent.

Chapter 04

Anatomy of a connected device

Open a typical IoT sensor and you find four parts. A sensor measures something: temperature, motion, light, vibration, humidity, location. A microcontroller is a very small, cheap, low-power computer on a single chip that reads the sensor and decides what to send. A radio sends and receives data. And a power source, usually a battery, keeps it running. Some devices also have an actuator, such as a valve, switch or lock, so they can act as well as measure.

A gateway is a local box that gathers signals from many nearby devices and passes them on through an ordinary internet connection. Your smart-home hub, or your broadband router, can play this role. The device itself is usually the weakest computer in the chain, which matters greatly for security, as Question 4 explains.

Chapter 05

How things talk: choosing a radio

No single wireless technology suits every device. A security camera needs to send a lot of data and can be plugged in. A water meter under a street sends a few numbers a day and must run for ten years on one battery. So IoT uses a family of radios, each trading range against power and data.

For years, smart-home devices from different brands often could not work together. An industry standard called Matter, launched in 2022 with backing from Apple, Google, Amazon and many device makers, aims to fix this, so that a plug from one company works with an app from another. Adoption has been gradual.

Chapter 06

Running for years on one battery

Imagine replacing the batteries in ten thousand sensors spread across a city every few months. It would cost more than the sensors. So engineers design IoT devices to sleep almost all the time. A soil sensor might wake for a fraction of a second every fifteen minutes, take a reading, send a few bytes and go back to sleep, using almost no power in between. Done well, a small battery can last many years.

Some devices need no battery at all. Energy harvesting collects tiny amounts of power from light, heat, vibration or radio waves. The simplest example is the RFID tag in a shop label or a building access card: it has no battery and is powered by the reader's radio signal for the instant it is read. This is why power, not computing, is usually the real design limit of small IoT devices.

Chapter 07

Edge or cloud: where the thinking happens

Early IoT designs sent all raw data to the cloud, the large remote data centres run by tech companies, and did all the analysis there. That is simple but has costs. Sending data uses power and bandwidth. A round trip to a distant data centre takes time, which matters if a machine must stop instantly. And if the internet connection fails, the system stops working.

The alternative is edge computing: doing more processing on or near the device. A factory camera can check products for defects itself and send only the results. A smart doorbell can recognise a person locally rather than uploading every frame. Edge processing is faster, keeps working offline and can be more private, because raw data stays local. The trend is clearly towards splitting the work: quick decisions at the edge, long-term analysis in the cloud.

Chapter 08

From readings to decisions: predictive maintenance

The most valuable industrial use of IoT has a dull name: predictive maintenance. Traditionally, factories repaired machines either on a fixed schedule, often too early and wasteful, or when they broke, which stops production. A motor that is starting to fail usually vibrates differently, runs hotter or draws more current weeks before it breaks.

Cheap sensors can record these signals continuously. Software, often using machine learning, learns what normal looks like for each machine and raises an alert when the pattern drifts. Engineers can then replace a bearing during a planned stop rather than after a breakdown. The value is not the data itself but the decision it enables a week early. When many such sensors feed a live computer model of a whole plant, engineers call it a digital twin.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Seeing the physical world in real time

IoT matters less for any single gadget than for what it does at scale: it makes the physical world measurable, minute by minute, in a way it never was before.

Chapter 09

Meters, grids and factories

Smart electricity meters are among the largest IoT deployments in the world. Italy was one of the first countries to install them nationally, in the 2000s, and many countries in Europe, North America and Asia have since followed. A smart meter sends readings automatically, ending estimated bills and meter-reader visits. More importantly, it lets utilities see demand in near real time and offer prices that change by the hour, which helps grids absorb variable solar and wind power.

In factories, ports and mines, connected sensors track machines, goods and energy use. Shipping containers carrying food or medicine report their temperature during the whole voyage, so a spoiled cargo is detected before it is delivered. Much of IoT's economic value is invisible to consumers: fewer breakdowns, less waste, lower energy bills for companies, and in the end slightly cheaper goods.

Chapter 10

Homes and health

At home, the most useful devices tend to be the simplest: thermostats that learn schedules and turn the heating down when nobody is in, leak sensors under washing machines, and plugs that switch appliances off. Voice assistants and connected appliances are more common but their value is more debated; many owners use only a few features.

Health is where IoT can be most personal. A continuous glucose monitor is a small sensor worn on the skin that measures blood sugar every few minutes and sends it to a phone. For many people with diabetes it has replaced finger-prick tests and allows alarms when levels go dangerously low at night. Connected heart monitors and blood-pressure cuffs let doctors watch patients at home after surgery, a practice called remote patient monitoring.

Note

This section is general education, not medical advice. Decisions about medical devices belong with a doctor.

Chapter 11

Fields and cities

In farming, soil-moisture probes and weather stations allow precision agriculture: watering or fertilising only the parts of a field that need it, which saves water and money. Collars on cattle track location and activity, and can show that an animal is ill or about to give birth. In cities, sensors report full rubbish bins, free parking spaces, air quality, and leaks in water pipes, which can lose a large share of treated water in older networks.

These uses show IoT's real strength. It replaces guesses and fixed schedules with measurement: water when the soil is dry, not every Tuesday; empty the bin when it is full, not on a fixed route. The city-wide version, and the arguments about surveillance that come with it, are in Smart cities.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Efficiency for everyone, attack surface for everyone

Every connected object is both a useful sensor and a small computer that can be attacked, tracked or abandoned.

The cloud dependency is not hypothetical. Several smart-home companies have shut down or ended support for products, and some devices simply stopped working or lost their "smart" features. When you buy a connected device, you are also relying on a company's servers staying online.

Chapter 13

The security problem: the botnet made of cameras

In October 2016, large parts of the internet became unreachable for hours in North America and Europe. Sites including Twitter, Netflix and Reddit were hard to load. The cause was an attack on Dyn, a company that runs part of the internet's address system. The attack traffic came from a botnet, a network of hijacked devices, called Mirai. It was made mostly of home routers and internet-connected cameras. Mirai had taken them over by simply trying a short list of default usernames and passwords, such as "admin" and "admin", that owners had never changed.

The lesson was that a cheap device's weak security is not only its owner's problem. In 2015, two security researchers had already shown they could take control of a Jeep's steering and brakes remotely through its entertainment system, leading the maker to recall about 1.4 million vehicles. Privacy adds another layer: smart speakers, doorbells and televisions can collect detailed information about who is home and what they do.

Governments have started to respond. The United Kingdom banned universal default passwords on new consumer devices from April 2024. The European Union's Cyber Resilience Act, adopted in 2024, will require security updates and basic protections for connected products, phased in over the following years. The general legal principles are in Privacy law.

Chapter 15

Living among sensors

The direction of travel is clear even if the speed is not. Objects will keep getting cheaper sensors and better connections, and faster mobile networks such as 5G and its successors are designed partly with huge numbers of devices in mind. The interesting questions are less technical than social. Who owns the data your car, meter and television collect? Can your insurer, employer or landlord see it? How long must a manufacturer keep your device secure?

A sensible personal rule follows from everything above. Connect a device when the connection does something useful for you, change its default password, keep it updated, and prefer products from makers that promise years of support. A kettle that tells your phone it has boiled is a convenience. A kettle that has not had a security update in five years is a small open door in your home network. Like the students with their drinks machine, the best IoT saves a real walk down a real corridor.

The core idea

IoT puts a sensor, a tiny computer, a radio and a battery into ordinary objects. Data flows through gateways to the cloud, or is processed at the edge, so people and programs can act. The design limit is usually power; the biggest risk is security.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The phrase "Internet of Things": 1999, Kevin Ashton.
  • Roughly 15–20 billion connected IoT devices by the mid-2020s, depending on how they are counted.
  • The Mirai botnet of 2016 was built mostly from cameras and routers with default passwords.
The common mistake

Thinking IoT means smart speakers and fridges. Most of its value is in meters, factories and logistics, out of sight.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a street water meter uses a different radio from a home security camera? If you can, you understand the trade-off between range, data and battery life.

The words, in plain words
IoT
Internet of Things: everyday objects connected to a network so they can report and act.
Sensor
A part that measures something physical, such as heat, motion or light.
Actuator
A part that acts on the world, such as a switch, valve or lock.
Microcontroller
A tiny, cheap, low-power computer on a single chip.
Gateway
A local box that collects data from many devices and passes it to the internet.
Edge computing
Processing data on or near the device instead of in a distant data centre.
Mesh network
A network in which devices pass messages through each other to extend range.
LoRaWAN
A long-range, very low-power radio network for sending small amounts of data.
RFID
Radio tags that can be read without contact, often without a battery.
Energy harvesting
Powering a device from light, heat, vibration or radio waves.
Predictive maintenance
Using sensor data to fix machines before they break.
Botnet
A network of hijacked devices controlled by an attacker.
Matter
A smart-home standard meant to make devices from different brands work together.
