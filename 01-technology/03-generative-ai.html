Sharp Mind · Technology · Topic 03 of 20

Generative AI. Machines that learned what the world looks like, and can now invent more of it.

Most machine learning answers a question about something that exists: is this email spam, is this scan healthy? Generative AI does the opposite. It produces something that did not exist before: a picture of a lighthouse in the style of a woodcut, a voice reading a text its owner never read, a ten-second video of a street that was never filmed. The results are often astonishing, and they arrived fast, between about 2021 and 2024. They also raise hard questions about work, ownership and whether we can still believe what we see.

Question 1 · What is it?

Systems that produce new content in the style of what they learned

A generative model has studied millions of examples of something, and learned enough about their patterns to produce new examples that fit.

Chapter 01

The prize-winning painting nobody painted

In August 2022 a game designer called Jason Allen won first prize in the digital art category of the Colorado State Fair in the United States. His entry, "Théâtre D'opéra Spatial", showed robed figures in a vast, glowing hall. He had produced it with Midjourney, a tool that makes images from written descriptions, then edited and upscaled the result. When this became known, many artists were furious. Allen said he had followed the rules and spent many hours refining his prompts.

The argument that followed contained almost every question this topic raises. Was it art? Who was the author: Allen, the software company, or the thousands of artists whose work had been used to train the tool? Should it be allowed to compete with paintings that took weeks? The technology had arrived before society had any agreed answers, and in 2026 many of those questions are still open.

Chapter 02

What "generative" really means

A classifier, the kind of model described in Machine learning, learns to draw a line between categories: cat on this side, dog on that side. A generative model learns something richer. It learns what the data itself is like: which combinations of pixels look like a real face, which sequences of sound waves sound like a real voice. Statisticians call this learning the data's distribution. Once a model has it, it can draw new samples from it, the way you could invent a new, plausible face after seeing ten thousand of them.

Chapter 03

A short history in five steps

Computers have generated content for decades, from simple music programs to procedural landscapes in video games. But the modern wave has a fairly clear sequence.

2014: Ian Goodfellow and colleagues introduce generative adversarial networks (GANs), which can produce realistic small images. By 2018 and 2019, GANs could make photographs of people who do not exist. 2017: Google researchers publish the transformer, the design behind today's language models. 2020 to 2021: diffusion models appear and quickly beat GANs on image quality; OpenAI shows DALL-E, which draws from text descriptions. 2022: Midjourney opens to the public, Stable Diffusion is released with its model weights freely downloadable, and ChatGPT launches at the end of the year. 2024 onwards: convincing short video and full songs become ordinary consumer products.

The speed matters. Within about three years, making a realistic image went from a specialist skill to something anyone could do on a phone in seconds. Few technologies have spread that quickly.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From noise to picture, guided by words

Different generators use different tricks, but they share one recipe: compress huge amounts of data into a map of possibilities, then learn to travel through that map on request.

Chapter 04

The shared recipe: a map of possibilities

An ordinary photo is a few million numbers, one for each colour in each pixel. Almost all random combinations of those numbers look like grey static. Real photographs are a tiny, very structured island in that ocean of noise. A generative model's job is to learn the shape of that island.

To do this, most generators learn a compressed description called a latent space. Think of it as a map where every point stands for one possible image, and nearby points are similar images. Somewhere there is a region of "faces", and inside it a direction that means "older" and another that means "smiling". Generating means picking a point on this map and turning it back into pixels. Text works the same way, using the embeddings described in Volume 14.

Chapter 05

GANs: the forger and the detective

A GAN contains two networks that compete. The generator is a forger. It starts by producing rubbish and tries to make images that look real. The discriminator is a detective. It is shown a mix of real photos and the forger's work, and must say which is which. Each time the detective catches a fake, the forger learns from the mistake. Each time the forger succeeds, the detective learns too. Over many rounds, both get better, until the fakes are hard to tell apart from real images.

GANs produced the first startling photo-realistic faces. But they are hard to train. The competition can become unstable, and a forger often learns to produce only a few kinds of image that reliably fool the detective, a failure called mode collapse. That is one reason diffusion models took over image generation after about 2021.

Chapter 06

Diffusion: learning to clean up noise

Diffusion models use a surprisingly simple idea. Take a real photo and add a little random noise, like static on an old television. Then add a little more, and more, until after many steps nothing is left but static. That destruction is easy. Now train a neural network to do one small thing: look at a noisy image and predict the noise that was added, so it can be removed one step.

Diffusion in one picture
  TRAINING (learn to undo damage)
  photo ──+noise──► slightly noisy ──+noise──► ... ──► pure static
          the network learns: "given this noisy image, what noise was added?"

  GENERATING (run it backwards)
  pure static ──denoise──► blurry shapes ──denoise──► ... ──► new image
                    ▲ each step guided by the text prompt

To create an image, you start with pure random static and ask the network to remove noise, again and again, typically over a few dozen steps. Shapes appear, then objects, then fine detail. Because the starting static is random, every run produces a different image. A popular refinement, used by Stable Diffusion, does this work inside the compressed latent space rather than on full-sized pixels, which makes it much cheaper.

Chapter 07

How words steer the picture

Random static plus denoising would give you a random image. To get "a fox reading a newspaper in the rain", the model needs to connect words and pictures. A key step came in 2021 when OpenAI published CLIP, a model trained on hundreds of millions of images paired with their captions from the web. It learned to place an image and its description near each other in the same space.

During generation, the text prompt is converted into numbers by a model like this, and those numbers nudge every denoising step towards images that match the words. A setting often called guidance controls how strongly the prompt is obeyed. High guidance follows the words closely but can look stiff; low guidance is freer but may wander.

This explains some familiar failures. Older image models were notoriously bad at hands and at writing text inside pictures. They had learned what hands and letters roughly look like, not how many fingers a hand has or how spelling works. Newer models have improved a lot on both, mostly through more and better training data.

Chapter 08

Sound and moving pictures

Audio is a wave: a long list of numbers, often tens of thousands per second. Modern voice and music generators usually compress that wave into short audio tokens, little coded chunks of sound, and then generate them one after another, much as a language model generates words. Others use diffusion on a picture of the sound called a spectrogram. A voice-cloning system needs only a short recording, sometimes just seconds, to imitate a person's tone and accent convincingly.

Video is the hardest case because it adds time. A video model must keep the same face, the same lighting and the same physics across hundreds of frames. Early systems produced dream-like clips where objects melted or changed. Current systems treat a clip as a block of space and time and denoise it all together, which improves consistency. They still make physical mistakes, such as a glass that refills itself or a hand passing through a table, because they learn how things usually look, not the laws that make them move.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cheap creation changes work, ownership and trust

When something that cost hours and skill suddenly costs seconds, three things are disrupted at once: the people who did it, the people whose work trained it, and our ability to tell real from fake.

Chapter 09

Creative work, and the strikes of 2023

The first jobs touched were those producing large volumes of commercial images and text: stock photography, concept sketches, product shots, marketing copy, simple illustration and voice-over. Many designers now use generators as a fast sketchbook, producing fifty rough ideas before choosing one to develop by hand. For a small business, a passable logo or advert that once needed a freelancer can now be made in minutes.

Hollywood showed how contested this is. In 2023 the Writers Guild of America and the actors' union SAG-AFTRA both went on long strikes. AI was one of the central issues, alongside pay. Writers wanted guarantees that studios would not use AI to replace them or rewrite their work; actors wanted consent and payment before their faces and voices could be copied digitally. The deals that ended the strikes set some of the first detailed rules on AI in any industry, based on consent and compensation rather than a ban.

Chapter 10

Deepfakes: when seeing stops being believing

A deepfake is generated or altered media that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not. The harm comes in three main forms. The first is fraud. In early 2024 an employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup transferred roughly 25 million US dollars after a video call in which, police said, the "colleagues" and finance chief on screen were deepfakes. Voice-clone calls pretending to be a relative in trouble have become a recognised scam.

The second is abuse: by far the largest share of deepfakes found online has been sexual imagery of people, mostly women, made without consent. Many countries have passed or proposed laws specifically against this. The third is politics: fake audio or video of candidates released before elections.

There is also a subtler damage that researchers call the liar's dividend. Once everyone knows convincing fakes exist, a person caught on a genuine recording can simply claim it is fake. The cost is not only that fakes are believed, but that truths become easier to deny. Practical advice for protecting yourself is in Vol 47.

Chapter 11

Labels, watermarks and content credentials

Two technical defences are being built. Watermarking hides an invisible signal inside generated content that a detector can later find. Several large labs mark their images this way. Provenance works from the other side: a camera or editing program attaches a signed record of where an image came from and how it was changed. An industry standard called C2PA, backed by camera makers, software companies and news organisations, defines these "content credentials".

Neither is a complete answer. Watermarks can sometimes be removed by cropping, compressing or re-generating an image, and open-source models need not add them at all. Provenance only helps if most cameras and platforms adopt it. Tools that try to detect fakes by looking at them are in a constant race with better generators, and are unreliable. The likely future is one in which authentic content proves where it came from, rather than one in which fakes are always caught.

Chapter 13

Who owns it? The copyright fight

Two separate legal questions are often mixed up. The first is about training: was it lawful to copy millions of images, books and songs to train a model? From 2023, artists, photo agencies, authors, news organisations and record labels sued several AI companies. Getty Images sued Stability AI, and The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft. Companies argue that learning patterns from public material is a lawful, transformative use, in the US sense of "fair use", much as a human artist learns by looking. Creators argue that their work was copied commercially without permission to build products that compete with them. As of 2026, courts in different countries have not settled this, early rulings have pointed in different directions, and some companies have chosen to sign licensing deals instead.

The second question is about output: can you own what a generator makes? In the United States, the Copyright Office and the courts have so far held that copyright requires human authorship, so purely machine-made images are not protected, although a human's selection, arrangement or editing can be. Other countries treat this differently, and the details vary. The European Union's AI Act adds a separate duty for providers of general-purpose models to publish a summary of their training content.

Chapter 15

When the internet is full of generated content

A growing share of new images and text online is now machine-made. That raises a technical question: what happens when future models are trained on the output of earlier ones? A 2024 study published in Nature found that models trained repeatedly on their own generated data gradually lost the rare and unusual parts of the original data, a problem its authors called model collapse. How serious this will be in practice, when generated data is mixed with fresh human data, is debated.

The human question is larger. Cheap generation makes authentic, verified, human-made work more valuable, not less, in the way that mass-produced furniture made handmade pieces a mark of quality. Expect more emphasis on the person behind a piece of work, on live performance, and on proof of origin. The technology itself will keep improving. Whether it mostly widens human creativity or mostly replaces it will depend on the rules we set about consent, payment and labelling, which are being written now.

The core idea

Generative models learn what data is like and then draw new samples from it. GANs pit a forger against a detective. Diffusion models learn to remove noise, then build images out of pure static, step by step, steered by a text prompt.

The numbers worth knowing
  • GANs 2014; transformers 2017; public image and chat tools 2022.
  • A voice can be cloned from a recording of seconds, not hours.
  • The Hong Kong deepfake call fraud of 2024 cost about US$25 million.
The common mistake

Thinking the main danger is that fakes are believed. An equal danger is that real evidence can now be dismissed as fake.

Test yourself

Can you explain to a friend, without notes, how a diffusion model turns static into a picture of a fox? If you can, you understand noise, denoising and text guidance.
