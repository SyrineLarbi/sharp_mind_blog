Sharp Mind · Technology · Topic 18 of 20

Augmented & Virtual Reality. A technology that has been "five years away" for fifty years, and is finally arriving in pieces.

Put on a good virtual reality headset and stand at the edge of a virtual cliff. You know you are in your living room. Your legs do not believe you. That gap between what you know and what your body feels is the whole power of the technology. Augmented reality aims at something subtler: leaving you in the real world but adding information to it. Both have been promised as the next great computing platform many times. Both keep running into the same hard limits of optics, batteries, comfort and social acceptance. This file explains how they work, where they already earn their keep, and why the future is more likely to look like glasses than like goggles.

Question 1 · What is it?

Replacing the world, or adding to it

Virtual reality shuts out the real world and puts you somewhere else. Augmented reality keeps the real world and lays digital things on top. Between the two lies a range of mixtures.

Chapter 01

The sword hanging from the ceiling

In 1968 the computer scientist Ivan Sutherland and his student Bob Sproull built a headset that showed simple 3D wireframe shapes floating in the room. As the wearer turned their head, the shapes stayed in place. The headset was so heavy that it had to hang from a mechanical arm attached to the ceiling, which earned it the nickname "the Sword of Damocles". It was, in effect, both the first VR headset and the first AR headset.

The term "virtual reality" was popularised in the late 1980s by Jaron Lanier, whose company VPL Research sold headsets and data gloves. The equipment cost a fortune and the graphics were crude. In 1995 Nintendo's Virtual Boy, a red-and-black console you looked into, flopped within a year. Then the field went quiet for almost two decades.

What revived it was the smartphone. By the early 2010s phones had made small high-resolution screens, motion sensors and mobile processors cheap. In 2012 a young engineer called Palmer Luckey raised money on Kickstarter for a headset called the Oculus Rift. In 2014 Facebook bought Oculus for about two billion dollars. VR did not come back because of a breakthrough in VR. It came back because phones made its parts cheap.

Chapter 02

The spectrum from real to virtual

In 1994 the researchers Paul Milgram and Fumio Kishino described a "reality-virtuality continuum": a line running from the fully real world at one end to a fully virtual one at the other. The names used for the points in between are not used consistently by companies, but the ideas are clear.

TermWhat you seeExample
Augmented reality (AR)The real world, with digital information addedDirections drawn on the road through a phone camera; Pokémon Go

Apple calls its headset a "spatial computer" and Meta uses "mixed reality". These are partly marketing labels. The useful distinction is simpler: does the device let you see the real world, and does it understand that world well enough to place things in it convincingly?

Chapter 03

Presence: the feeling of being there

The goal of VR has a name among researchers: presence. It is the sense, felt by the body more than the mind, of actually being somewhere. When presence works, people flinch at virtual objects, hesitate at virtual drops, and lower their voices in virtual churches. It does not need photo-realistic graphics. Simple scenes can produce strong presence if the headset tracks movement accurately and responds without delay.

That is the key insight of the whole field. The brain judges reality mainly by whether the world responds correctly when you move, not by how detailed it looks. Get the movement right and crude graphics feel real. Get it wrong and beautiful graphics feel fake, or make you feel ill.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Track the head, draw two pictures, do it fast

A headset must know exactly where your head is, draw a slightly different image for each eye, and update both so fast that your brain never notices the delay.

Chapter 04

Two eyes, two images, and lenses

Your eyes are a few centimetres apart, so each sees the world from a slightly different angle. The brain uses that difference to judge depth. A VR headset copies this by showing a separate image to each eye, each drawn from that eye's position. This is called stereoscopic display, the same principle as 3D cinema but with a separate screen, or half a screen, per eye.

The screens sit only a few centimetres from your face, far too close to focus on. Lenses between the screen and each eye bend the light so that the image appears to be a comfortable distance away. Older headsets used flat, ridged Fresnel lenses, which are light but create glare. Newer ones use folded "pancake" lenses, which let the headset be much thinner.

Human vision covers a very wide field, more than 180 degrees from side to side. Most consumer headsets show around 100 degrees, which is why VR can feel like looking through ski goggles. Screens have also needed very high pixel density, because a magnified screen close to the eye shows its pixels clearly. Early users called this the "screen door effect", like looking through a fine mesh.

Chapter 05

Tracking: knowing where you are

The headset must track your head in six degrees of freedom, usually written 6DoF. Three are rotation: looking left and right, up and down, and tilting your head. Three are movement: stepping forward and back, side to side, and up and down. Cheap early phone-based headsets tracked only rotation, so if you leaned forward the world leaned with you, which feels wrong.

Modern headsets use inside-out tracking. Cameras on the headset watch the room and software works out how the headset is moving relative to fixed features, such as the corner of a table. At the same time it builds a rough 3D map of the room. The technique is called SLAM, simultaneous localisation and mapping, and it is the same family of methods used by robots and self-driving cars (see Autonomous vehicles). Small motion sensors fill in the gaps between camera frames. The same cameras increasingly track your hands, so you can pinch and point without controllers.

Chapter 06

The latency budget, and why VR can make you sick

When you turn your head, the image must move to match. The time between your movement and the updated picture reaching your eye is called motion-to-photon latency. Researchers and engineers generally aim to keep it below about 20 milliseconds. Above that, the world seems to swim or lag, and many people start to feel unwell. Headsets also refresh their screens 90 times a second or more, compared with 60 for many ordinary screens.

The leading explanation is sensory conflict. The inner ear, which senses balance and acceleration, says you are still. The eyes say you are moving. One popular theory is that the brain treats such a mismatch as a possible sign of poisoning and responds with nausea, but the exact cause is still debated. This is why VR games often let you "teleport" rather than glide, and why seated, slow experiences are more comfortable. Sensitivity varies a lot between people, and many adapt with practice.

A second problem is subtler. In real life, your eyes point at an object (vergence) and focus at the same distance (accommodation). In a headset, the eyes point at a virtual object that seems a metre away, but focus on a lens image at a fixed distance. This vergence-accommodation conflict contributes to eye strain on long sessions. Fixing it properly requires optics that can change focus, which remain mostly experimental.

Chapter 07

How AR shows you the real world

An AR device has an extra problem: it must show the real world as well as the digital one. There are two ways.

Video passthrough

The headset is closed, like VR. Cameras film the room and the screens show that live video with digital objects added. Apple's Vision Pro (2024) and Meta's Quest 3 (2023) work this way. The digital objects can look solid and bright, but you see the world through cameras, with a slight delay and less sharpness than your own eyes.

Optical see-through

You look through clear glass, and a tiny projector bounces images into your eye using a thin transparent layer called a waveguide. Microsoft's HoloLens and most smart-glasses designs work this way. You see the real world directly, but the digital images are fainter, especially in sunlight, and cover a small part of your view.

Either way, the device must understand the room: where the walls, tables and floor are, and ideally where your hands are, so a virtual object can sit on a real table and be hidden behind a real chair. Convincing AR is mostly a computer vision problem, and much of the progress has come from that field rather than from displays.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Quietly useful at work, still searching at home

The most valuable uses so far are not games or social worlds. They are training, design and guided work, where seeing in 3D or keeping your hands free saves time or lives.

Chapter 08

Training without the danger

Flight simulators have trained pilots for decades, long before consumer VR. They exist because practising an engine failure in a real aircraft is dangerous and practising it in a simulator is not. VR brings a cheaper version of the same idea to many other jobs: surgeons rehearsing a procedure, firefighters walking through a burning building, factory workers learning a machine before touching it, police and nurses practising difficult conversations.

The principle is that presence makes practice feel real enough to count. Your body reacts as if the situation were real, so the skill and the stress response both get trained. Medicine has also used VR in treatment. Exposure therapy, in which a person faces a feared situation gradually and safely, has good evidence for specific fears such as heights and flying, and VR makes it easier to arrange.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. People with epilepsy, balance disorders or a history of severe motion sickness should check with a doctor before extended VR use, and manufacturers set minimum ages for their headsets.

Chapter 09

Guided work and design

AR's best uses keep the hands free. A technician repairing a machine can see the next step floating beside the part, or share their view with a remote expert who draws arrows on it. Aircraft makers and car factories have used AR to guide complex wiring and assembly. Architects and engineers walk clients through buildings before they are built. Designers review a full-size virtual car rather than a clay model. These are the same ideas that the Digital twins file covers from the data side.

Phones made AR a mass-market experience without any headset. In 2016 Pokémon Go had hundreds of millions of people walking around their towns hunting cartoon creatures shown through their phone cameras. Furniture apps let you place a virtual sofa in your room. Camera filters on social media that add glasses or animal ears to faces are AR too, and are used by far more people than any headset.

Chapter 10

The metaverse bet

In October 2021 Facebook renamed itself Meta, announcing that the next stage of the internet would be a "metaverse": shared virtual spaces where people would work, shop and socialise as avatars. The company's Reality Labs division has since lost tens of billions of dollars. Its Quest headsets became the best-selling VR devices, mostly for games and fitness. The social metaverse did not take off on the scale promised, and by 2023 much of the company's attention had shifted towards AI.

Apple took a different route. The Vision Pro, launched in the US in February 2024 at 3,499 dollars, was technically impressive, with very sharp screens and eye-and-hand control. It was also heavy, expensive and short on must-have uses, and sales were modest. Google Glass, launched to developers in 2013, had shown the social problem years earlier. People disliked being near someone wearing a camera on their face, and the product was withdrawn from consumers.

The pattern across these stories is clear. The technology works. The problem is that a headset asks people to give up comfort, social contact and money, and few everyday tasks are worth that trade yet.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful illusion with a heavy price tag

The strengths come from presence and hands-free information. The weaknesses come from the hardware on your face and the data it gathers.

Chapter 12

The most personal data yet

A headset collects more intimate data than a phone. It maps your home in 3D. It records how you move your head and hands. Many track your eyes, to sharpen the image where you look, a technique called foveated rendering. Where you look, for how long, and how your pupils react can reveal what interests you, what you find attractive and how tired you are. Researchers have shown that head and hand movement alone can identify individual users with high accuracy, like a fingerprint.

Some makers keep raw eye-tracking data on the device rather than sending it to apps. Rules differ between companies and between countries, and most privacy laws were not written with such data in mind. As these devices spread, the question of who may use your gaze and movement data will matter as much as any question about the hardware.

Chapter 14

Why AI may be what AR was waiting for

For years AR had a chicken-and-egg problem. Glasses were not worth wearing without useful software, and developers would not build software without people wearing glasses. AI assistants may break that loop. A pair of glasses that can see what you see and answer "what is this plant?" or "what did that sign say?" is useful even with no display at all. Add a small display and it can show a translation, a name or a direction exactly when you need it.

The hard limits have not gone away. Glasses must be light, last all day, look normal and work in sunlight, and today no device does all four with a wide display. Expect progress in steps rather than one moment when everyone switches. There are further edges too, such as controlling devices with nerve signals at the wrist or directly with the brain, covered in Brain-computer interfaces. The safest prediction is the one this field has taught repeatedly: the version that wins will be the one people are willing to wear in public.

The core idea

VR replaces the world, and AR adds to it. Both depend on tracking your movement precisely and updating the image within about 20 milliseconds, because the brain judges reality by how the world responds to movement. The technology works. Comfort, cost, social acceptance and useful applications have been the real obstacles.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1968: Sutherland's "Sword of Damocles", the first head-mounted display of its kind.
  • Under about 20 ms motion-to-photon latency to feel comfortable.
  • About 100 degrees field of view in typical headsets, against more than 180 for human vision.
The common mistake

Assuming better graphics are what VR needs. Presence comes from accurate, fast tracking, not detail. And for AR, the barrier is mostly wearability, not imagination.

Test yourself

Can you explain why gliding through a virtual corridor makes people sick while teleporting does not? If you can, you understand sensory conflict and why latency matters.

The words, in plain words
Virtual reality (VR)
A headset that hides the real world and shows a computer-made one.
Augmented reality (AR)
Digital information added on top of your view of the real world.
Mixed reality (MR)
AR in which digital objects stay fixed in, and react to, the real room.
Extended reality (XR)
An umbrella term for VR, AR and MR together.
Presence
The bodily feeling of actually being in a virtual place.
Stereoscopic display
Showing each eye a slightly different image to create depth.
6DoF
Six degrees of freedom: tracking both rotation and movement of the head.
Inside-out tracking
Cameras on the headset itself work out its position from the room.
Motion-to-photon latency
The delay between moving your head and seeing the updated image.
Cybersickness
Nausea caused by a mismatch between what the eyes see and the inner ear feels.
Passthrough
Showing the real world inside a closed headset using live camera video.
Waveguide
A thin transparent layer in AR glasses that guides projected light into the eye.
Foveated rendering
Drawing sharp detail only where the eye is looking, to save computing power.
