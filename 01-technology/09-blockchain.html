Blockchain. A shared notebook that nobody owns and nobody can quietly rewrite.

Almost every record that matters, from your bank balance to who owns your flat, is kept by someone you trust to keep it honestly: a bank, a registry, a government. A blockchain is an attempt to keep such a record without any trusted keeper at all. Thousands of computers hold copies, follow the same rules, and agree on every change. The idea was invented to run a digital currency, Bitcoin. Since then it has been sold as the answer to almost everything. The truth is narrower and more interesting.

Question 1 · What is it?

A record kept by everyone, so no one has to be trusted

A blockchain is a type of database. What makes it unusual is not what it stores but who keeps it, and how they agree.

Chapter 01

A paper signed by nobody, and two very expensive pizzas

On 31 October 2008, a few weeks after the collapse of Lehman Brothers, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto posted a nine-page paper to an online mailing list about cryptography. It was called "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System". It described money that could be sent directly between two people over the internet, with no bank in the middle. In January 2009 the network started running. Hidden in its very first block was a newspaper headline from that day's London Times about a second bank bailout. Nobody has ever proved who Nakamoto is.

For more than a year, bitcoins had almost no value. On 22 May 2010 a programmer in Florida, Laszlo Hanyecz, paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas, one of the first purchases of a real good. At later prices, those coins were worth hundreds of millions of dollars. Fans still celebrate the date as "Bitcoin Pizza Day".

The story shows the two faces of this topic. One is a clever piece of engineering. The other is a market of speculation, fortunes and losses. The engineering and the speculation are different subjects, and much confusion comes from mixing them. This file is about the engineering.

Chapter 02

The problem it solves: copying money

Digital things are easy to copy. You can send the same photo to a hundred people and still have it. That is fine for photos and fatal for money. If a digital coin is just a file, what stops you spending it twice? This is called the double-spending problem.

The usual answer is a trusted middleman. Your bank keeps a ledger, a list of who owns what. When you pay, the bank subtracts from your line and adds to someone else's. Because there is only one official ledger, you cannot spend the same money twice. The cost is that you must trust the bank: to be honest, to stay open, and to let you use your money.

Nakamoto's idea was to replace the single keeper with a crowd. Imagine a village where every house keeps an identical notebook of every payment ever made. When someone pays, they announce it to the village, and everyone writes it down. To cheat, you would need to change most of the notebooks at once. A blockchain is that notebook, kept by thousands of computers around the world, with mathematics making forgery practically impossible.

The parts were not all new. Two researchers, Stuart Haber and W. Scott Stornetta, had described a way to chain time-stamped documents with cryptography in 1991, and Nakamoto's paper cites them. What was new was the way to make strangers agree without a leader, which the next part explains.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Fingerprints, chains and a costly race to write the next page

Four ingredients make a blockchain: digital fingerprints, blocks chained by those fingerprints, a rule for agreeing on the next block, and keys that prove ownership.

Chapter 04

Hashes: a fingerprint for any piece of data

A hash function takes any data, from one word to a whole film, and turns it into a short, fixed-length string of characters called a hash. Bitcoin uses one called SHA-256. A hash behaves like a fingerprint. The same input always gives the same hash. But change a single comma in a long document, and the hash changes completely and unpredictably.

Two more properties matter. You cannot work backwards from a hash to the original data. And it is practically impossible to find two different inputs with the same hash. So a hash lets you check that data has not been changed without storing the data twice. If the fingerprint still matches, the document is untouched.

Chapter 05

Blocks and the chain

Transactions are collected into batches called blocks. Each block contains its transactions plus one special item: the hash of the previous block. That is the "chain".

  │ Block 100   │     │ Block 101   │     │ Block 102   │
  │ txs: A→B 2  │     │ txs: C→D 5  │     │ txs: B→E 1  │
  │ prev: 9f3a… │◄────│ prev: 7c21… │◄────│ prev: e04b… │
  │ hash: 7c21… │     │ hash: e04b… │     │ hash: 51d8… │
  └─────────────┘     └─────────────┘     └─────────────┘
  change "A→B 2" to "A→B 20" in block 100
    ⇒ block 100's hash changes
    ⇒ block 101's "prev" no longer matches ⇒ 101 must be redone
    ⇒ and 102, and every block after it

This is why a blockchain is called tamper-evident. Changing one old record breaks the link to every later block. Anyone checking their own copy would see the mismatch at once. To get away with it, a cheater would have to rebuild every later block and convince the network to accept the new version. The next chapter explains why that is made very expensive.

Chapter 06

Proof of work: a lottery you enter with electricity

Who gets to write the next block? If anyone could, cheaters would flood the network with fake versions. Bitcoin's answer is proof of work. Computers called miners compete to solve a puzzle: find a number that, when added to the block and hashed, gives a hash starting with a long run of zeros. There is no shortcut. You can only guess, billions of times a second, and check.

The first miner to find a valid answer broadcasts the block. Others check it, which is easy, and add it to their copy. The winner is paid in newly created bitcoins plus fees. The network adjusts the difficulty so that, on average, a new block is found about every ten minutes, however many miners join.

The genius is economic. Rewriting history would mean redoing all that work faster than the rest of the world combined, which would require controlling more than half of the network's computing power. This is called a 51 per cent attack. On Bitcoin it would cost a vast amount in machines and electricity. On small blockchains with few miners, such attacks have actually happened.

Chapter 07

Proof of stake: a deposit instead of a race

Proof of work is secure but uses enormous amounts of electricity, by design. The main alternative is proof of stake. Instead of burning energy, participants called validators lock up some of their own coins as a deposit. The network picks validators to propose and confirm blocks, roughly in proportion to their deposits. If a validator cheats, for example by approving two conflicting blocks, part of its deposit is destroyed, which is called slashing.

Ethereum, the second-largest blockchain, switched from proof of work to proof of stake in September 2022, in an event called "the Merge". Its energy use fell by more than 99 per cent. Critics argue that proof of stake favours those who already hold the most coins. Supporters reply that proof of work favours those who can afford the most machines and cheapest power. Both systems replace trust in a person with a cost for cheating; they differ in what that cost is.

Chapter 08

Keys and wallets: ownership is a secret number

On a blockchain there are no names or accounts in the bank sense. Ownership is proved with public-key cryptography, the same family of mathematics described in Vol 14, Encryption. You have two linked numbers. The public key works like an address that others can send to. The private key is a secret that lets you sign transactions from that address. Anyone can check the signature; only the holder of the private key can make it.

A wallet is simply software or a small device that stores your private keys. This has a harsh consequence. Whoever has the private key controls the coins, and if you lose it, there is no one to call. No bank can reset your password. A large number of bitcoins are believed to be permanently lost this way. Many people therefore leave their coins with an exchange, which holds the keys for them, and that brings back the trusted middleman the system was designed to remove.

Chapter 09

Smart contracts: programs that run on the chain

Bitcoin records payments. In 2015 a network called Ethereum, proposed by the programmer Vitalik Buterin, went further: it lets people store small programs on the blockchain, called smart contracts. A smart contract runs exactly as written whenever someone triggers it. For example: "if two parties both deposit money, and a third party confirms the delivery, release the money to the seller". Nobody can stop or change it once it is running, unless its code allows it.

That strength is also the danger. In 2016 an investment fund built as a smart contract, called The DAO, held tens of millions of dollars. An attacker found a flaw in its code and drained a large share. The Ethereum community decided to rewrite history to return the funds, which split the network into two: Ethereum and Ethereum Classic. Code that cannot be changed also cannot be fixed. And smart contracts cannot see the outside world on their own; they rely on data feeds called oracles, which reintroduce someone you have to trust.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A new kind of trust, and a long list of disappointments

Blockchains have produced a trillion-dollar asset class and real tools for some people. They have also produced bubbles, frauds and many abandoned projects.

Chapter 10

What it is actually used for

Cryptocurrencies as assets. By far the biggest use. Bitcoin's total value has at times exceeded a trillion US dollars. Most of it is held as a speculative investment or a claimed "digital gold", rather than spent. That side is covered in Cryptocurrencies.

Stablecoins. Tokens designed to stay worth one US dollar, usually backed by cash and short-term government bonds held by a company. They have become a major way of moving dollars across borders quickly, especially in countries with unstable currencies. See Digital currencies.

Decentralised finance (DeFi) uses smart contracts to lend, borrow and trade without a bank. NFTs (non-fungible tokens) record ownership of a unique digital item. In March 2021 an NFT of a digital collage by the artist Beeple sold at Christie's for about 69 million dollars; the wider NFT market then fell sharply in 2022. Business uses, such as tracking goods along supply chains, were widely tested. Many were quietly dropped. TradeLens, a shipping platform from IBM and the shipping company Maersk, was closed after attracting too few participants. In practice, the most lasting uses have been those where moving value between strangers without a middleman is the whole point.

Chapter 11

Where it went wrong: the trusted middlemen came back

In November 2022 FTX, one of the world's largest cryptocurrency exchanges, collapsed within days. Customers' deposits had been secretly used by a linked trading firm. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was convicted of fraud in 2023. Years earlier, in 2014, the Tokyo exchange Mt. Gox had failed after losing a huge number of customers' bitcoins.

The lesson is not that the Bitcoin blockchain was broken. In both cases it kept working perfectly. The failures happened at the centralised edges: companies holding other people's keys, with weak oversight. Most people find managing their own keys too risky or inconvenient, so they hand them to firms, and those firms then need exactly the regulation and audits that banks have. Scams, hacks of poorly written smart contracts and "pump-and-dump" tokens have cost ordinary users many billions of dollars. Volume 38's chapter on bubbles describes the pattern of the boom-and-bust cycles.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Trust without a middleman, paid for in speed and simplicity

The fair test is not "is a blockchain good?" but "is it better than an ordinary database run by someone we already trust?"

Chapter 13

The trilemma and the energy bill

Engineers speak of a blockchain trilemma: it is hard to be fully decentralised, fully secure and fast at the same time. Bitcoin chose security and decentralisation, and pays with speed. One response is layer 2 systems built on top. The Lightning Network, for example, lets two people make many small payments between themselves off the main chain and record only the final balance on it, like running a bar tab and paying once.

The energy question is real. Estimates from researchers at the University of Cambridge have put Bitcoin's yearly electricity use in the same range as that of a medium-sized country. Supporters note that some mining uses surplus renewable power or gas that would otherwise be burned off. Critics reply that the energy is the security mechanism, so it cannot be made efficient without changing the system. Whether the cost is worth it depends on how much you value a money that no government controls, which is a political judgement more than a technical one.

Chapter 15

Four questions for any blockchain pitch

Because "blockchain" was attached to so many projects, it helps to have a simple test. First: who would otherwise keep the record, and why can they not be trusted? If the answer is "our own company", an ordinary database is cheaper and faster. Second: do several parties who distrust each other all need to write to the same record? Third: does the record need to stay unchangeable, even if everyone involved later agrees a mistake was made? Fourth: where does the real-world data come from, and who vouches for it? A blockchain can prove that a record has not changed since it was written. It cannot prove that the record was true when it was written.

If the answers are "no trusted keeper exists, several rivals must write, permanence matters and the data is born digital", a blockchain may be the right tool. Digital money between strangers passes that test. Tracking lettuces mostly does not. Seen this way, blockchain is neither a revolution in everything nor a fraud. It is a specialised tool for a specific problem: agreeing on a shared record when you cannot agree on who should keep it.

The core idea

A blockchain is a ledger copied across many computers. Hashes chain each block to the one before, so the past is tamper-evident. A consensus rule, proof of work or proof of stake, makes cheating expensive. Private keys prove ownership.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bitcoin paper 2008; network live January 2009; Ethereum 2015.
  • A new Bitcoin block about every 10 minutes.
  • Ethereum's switch to proof of stake in 2022 cut its energy use by more than 99%.
The common mistake

Thinking a blockchain makes data true. It only makes data hard to change after it is written. Garbage recorded on a blockchain is permanent garbage.

Test yourself

Can you explain why changing one old transaction would force a cheater to redo every later block? If you can, you understand hashes, chaining and proof of work together.
