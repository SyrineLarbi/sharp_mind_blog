Big Data. When the data no longer fits in one machine, everything about how you handle it has to change.

"Big data" was the buzzword of the early 2010s. The buzz faded, but the thing itself became part of the plumbing of modern life. Every search, card payment, delivery, phone signal and streamed song leaves a record. The companies and governments that collect these records found that ordinary databases could not keep up, so they invented new ways to store and process information across thousands of computers at once. Those methods made new things possible. They also produced a dangerous belief: that with enough data, you no longer need to think carefully about where it came from.

Question 1 · What is it?

Data too large, too fast or too messy for ordinary tools

There is no exact size at which data becomes "big". The useful definition is practical: data is big when the normal way of handling it stops working.

Chapter 01

The three Vs

In 2001 an analyst called Doug Laney described three ways data was becoming hard to manage. They became the standard definition of big data, known as the three Vs.

Volume

There is simply too much of it for one computer's disk or memory. A large retailer records every item in every basket in every shop, every day, for years.

Velocity

It arrives too fast to store first and study later. Card payments must be checked for fraud within a fraction of a second, while thousands more arrive.

Variety

It comes in many shapes: neat tables, but also text, photos, video, sensor readings and clicks. Most of it does not fit into rows and columns.

People later added more Vs, the most useful being veracity: how far can you trust the data? It is the V that the early enthusiasm most often forgot, and it returns throughout this file.

Chapter 02

How big is big? A ladder of units

Computers store information in bytes. One byte holds about one letter of text. Each step up the ladder below is roughly a thousand times larger than the one before.

UnitSizeTo picture it
Kilobyte (KB)A thousand bytesA short email
Megabyte (MB)A million bytesA phone photo, or a long novel as plain text
Gigabyte (GB)A billion bytesAbout an hour of standard video
Terabyte (TB)A trillion bytesA typical laptop drive in the 2020s

Zettabyte (ZB)A billion terabytesThe unit used for all the data the world creates in a year

Industry estimates put the data created, copied and consumed worldwide at over a hundred zettabytes a year in the mid-2020s. Such estimates are rough, and most of that data is copies and video, kept only briefly. But the direction is clear. The amount of data grows much faster than the number of people who can look at it, which is why so much of it is processed only by machines.

Chapter 03

Where it comes from

Three sources produce most of it. The first is transactions: purchases, bank transfers, bookings, every record a business keeps of what happened. The second is human activity online: searches, clicks, likes, messages, location pings from phones. The third, and fastest growing, is machines: sensors in factories, cars, aircraft engines and power grids, plus scientific instruments. The Large Hadron Collider at CERN produces far more raw data than could ever be stored, so electronics filter out all but a tiny fraction of collisions within microseconds. Even after that, CERN stores tens of petabytes a year.

Notice something about the first two sources. Most big data is "exhaust": it was collected to run a service, not to answer a research question. Your card payment is recorded so the shop gets paid. That it can also reveal shopping patterns is a side effect. This matters enormously for what the data can and cannot tell you.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Split the data, send the work to it, combine the results

The central trick of big data is to stop building bigger computers and start using many ordinary ones together. That sounds simple. Making it reliable was the real invention.

Chapter 04

Why one big computer was not enough

Until the early 2000s, a company with more data usually bought a bigger, more expensive server. This is called scaling up. It hits limits. The biggest machines cost a fortune, and even they cannot read data from disk fast enough. Reading a petabyte from a single disk would take years.

Google faced this problem first and hardest. It needed to store a copy of the web and rebuild its search index regularly. Its answer was scaling out: using thousands of cheap ordinary computers, linked in a cluster. With a thousand machines each reading its own disk at the same time, a job that would take years on one machine can take hours.

The catch is that cheap machines break. In a cluster of thousands, some disk or computer fails almost every day. The software had to assume failure was normal and carry on regardless. That idea, more than raw speed, is what made big data systems different.

Chapter 05

MapReduce: counting words across a thousand machines

In 2003 and 2004 Google engineers published two papers that shaped the field. The first described the Google File System, which splits huge files into chunks and keeps several copies of each chunk on different machines. If one machine dies, the copies survive. The second, by Jeffrey Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat, described MapReduce, a way to process that data in parallel.

The standard example is counting how often each word appears in billions of web pages. Doing it on one machine is hopeless. MapReduce does it in two phases.

MapReduce, counting words
  MAP: each machine works on its own chunk, in parallel
     machine 1: "the cat sat"   -> the:1  cat:1  sat:1
     machine 2: "the dog sat"   -> the:1  dog:1  sat:1
     machine 3: "the cat ran"   -> the:1  cat:1  ran:1

  SHUFFLE: send each word to the machine responsible for it
     "the" -> machine A    "cat","dog" -> machine B    "sat","ran" -> machine C

  REDUCE: each machine adds up its words
     A: the = 3      B: cat = 2, dog = 1      C: sat = 2, ran = 1

The important idea is that the work is sent to the data, not the data to the work. Moving a petabyte across a network is slow. Moving a small program to the machines that already hold the data is fast. If a machine fails halfway, the system simply reruns its piece somewhere else.

Chapter 06

Hadoop, Spark and the cloud warehouse

Google did not release its own software, but its papers were enough. Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella built a free, open-source version called Hadoop, named after a toy elephant that belonged to Cutting's son. Yahoo backed it heavily from 2006. For about a decade Hadoop was almost a synonym for big data.

MapReduce had a weakness: it wrote intermediate results to disk at every step, which made multi-step analysis slow. Around 2009 researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, led by Matei Zaharia, began building Spark, which keeps working data in memory whenever it can. For many jobs it was dramatically faster, and it largely replaced MapReduce.

Then the cloud changed the picture again. Services such as Amazon Redshift, Google BigQuery and Snowflake let a company load data into a cloud data warehouse and query it with ordinary SQL, the standard database language, while the provider handles the thousands of machines underneath. For most organisations today, big data means renting someone else's cluster, not building one.

Chapter 07

Lakes, warehouses and streams

Three storage ideas are worth knowing by name. A data warehouse holds cleaned, organised data in tables, ready for reporting. It is like a well-run library: everything catalogued, easy to find. A data lake holds raw data of every kind, in its original form, to be organised later. It is cheaper and more flexible, but without discipline it becomes what engineers call a "data swamp", full of files nobody understands. The "lakehouse" is a recent attempt to combine the two.

The third idea answers the velocity problem. Instead of storing data and processing it in batches overnight, stream processing handles each event as it arrives. A fraud system checks each card payment in milliseconds. A ride-hailing app updates prices as demand shifts. Tools such as Kafka, first built at LinkedIn, act as a central pipe that carries these event streams between systems.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

New powers, and a famous overconfidence

Big data made modern search, recommendations, fraud detection and AI possible. It also produced a set of high-profile failures with the same lesson.

Chapter 08

What it made possible

Much of daily life now runs on it. Search engines rank pages using signals from billions of queries and clicks. Recommendation systems on streaming and shopping sites learn from what millions of similar people chose. Card fraud detection compares each payment against patterns in vast histories of past payments. Navigation apps estimate traffic from the speeds of phones moving along each road. Weather forecasts combine enormous streams of satellite, balloon and ground observations every few hours.

Above all, big data made modern AI possible. The large language models described in Volume 49 were trained on a large share of the public text on the internet. Their abilities come as much from the scale of that data as from clever algorithms. Without the big data systems of the 2000s and 2010s, today's AI could not have been built.

Chapter 09

"The end of theory", and Google Flu Trends

In 2008 the editor of Wired, Chris Anderson, wrote an essay called "The End of Theory". He argued that with enough data, correlation was enough: we would no longer need models or hypotheses, because the numbers would speak for themselves. It captured the mood of the time.

The same year Google launched Google Flu Trends. It estimated flu levels in the United States from the volume of flu-related searches, and published its estimates faster than official health data. At first it matched official figures well. Then it drifted. In the 2012–13 winter it estimated roughly double the doctor visits for flu-like illness that official data later showed. A 2014 paper in Science, led by David Lazer, called the episode "big data hubris". Search habits had changed, partly because of news coverage and changes to Google's own search features, and the model had matched many search terms that were linked to winter rather than to flu. Google stopped publishing the estimates in 2015.

The lesson is precise. Size does not protect you from bias or from change. A pattern learned from exhaust data holds only as long as the behaviour that produces the exhaust stays the same, and nobody controls that behaviour.

Chapter 10

Ten million answers, one wrong result

The oldest lesson about big data comes from long before computers. In 1936 the American magazine Literary Digest mailed around ten million ballots asking readers whom they would vote for as president. About 2.4 million replied, and the magazine predicted a clear win for Alf Landon over Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won by a landslide. George Gallup, polling a much smaller but carefully chosen sample, called the result correctly.

The Digest's list came largely from sources such as telephone directories and car registrations, which in the Depression leaned towards wealthier voters, and the people who chose to reply were not typical either. A huge sample that is biased gives a precise wrong answer. A small sample chosen at random gives a rough right one. Modern big data often has the Digest's problem: social media users, smartphone owners and app customers are not the whole population. The habit of asking "who is missing?" is covered in Volume 39.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

More signal, more noise, more risk

Big data gives power to whoever holds it. That is its strength and its danger.

Chapter 12

Privacy, and why "anonymous" data often is not

Companies often say data is safe because names have been removed. Research has repeatedly shown that this protection is weak. Combine a few details, such as postcode, date of birth and sex, and a large share of people become unique. Location data is even more revealing: a handful of places where a phone spends its nights and working hours can point to one person. In big datasets, "anonymised" frequently means "not yet re-identified".

The 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal showed the political side. Data on up to 87 million Facebook users, gathered through a personality quiz app, was obtained by a political consulting firm without those users' clear consent. The same year the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) came into force. It requires a legal basis for processing personal data, gives people rights to see and delete their data, and allows large fines. Other places have followed with their own laws, and the rules vary considerably between countries. The tension between the value of data and the right to privacy is permanent, and it is a choice for each society.

Chapter 14

Why the word faded but the thing did not

Few people say "big data" now. That is a sign of success, not failure. The methods became ordinary. A small company can analyse billions of rows in a cloud warehouse without thinking of it as anything special, just as nobody now talks about "electric light". The excitement moved on to AI, which is built on top of the same foundations.

What should stay is the lesson the field learned the hard way. More data reduces random error, but it does nothing about systematic error. If your data leaves out the people who matter, or measures the wrong thing, a billion rows will only make you confidently wrong. The best data work of the next decade will still begin with old-fashioned questions: who collected this, why, who is missing, and what has changed since? Those questions are at the heart of Data science, and of judging any AI system trained on big data.

The core idea

Data is "big" when ordinary tools fail because of volume, velocity or variety. The solution was to scale out: split data across many cheap machines, send the work to the data, and assume machines will fail. Size helps with random noise but not with bias, and most big data is exhaust collected for another purpose.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Each unit is about 1,000 times the last: giga, tera, peta, exa, zetta.
  • 2004: Google's MapReduce paper. 2006: Hadoop at Yahoo.
  • 1936: about 2.4 million poll replies predicted the wrong US president.
The common mistake

Believing that enough data makes the sample representative. A biased sample does not become fair by getting bigger; it becomes more precisely wrong.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Google Flu Trends failed even though it used billions of searches? If you can, you understand exhaust data, changing behaviour and the limits of correlation.
