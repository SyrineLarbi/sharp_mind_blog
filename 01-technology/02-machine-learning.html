Machine Learning. Programs that are not written, but grown from examples.

For most of computing history, a program was a list of instructions that a person wrote by hand. Machine learning turns this around. You give the computer thousands or millions of examples, plus a way to measure its mistakes, and it adjusts itself until the mistakes get small. Nobody writes the final rules, and often nobody can read them. That one change is behind your spam filter, your bank's fraud alerts, the route your map suggests and the films a streaming service shows you first. It is also behind most of what people now call "AI".

Question 1 · What is it?

A way of building software by showing instead of telling

Machine learning is a method, not a machine. It is a way to get a computer to perform a task by learning patterns from data, instead of following rules a person wrote.

Chapter 01

The checkers player that beat its maker

In the 1950s an IBM engineer called Arthur Samuel wrote a program to play checkers (draughts). Samuel was not a strong player himself. So instead of writing down a good strategy, he let the program play thousands of games against itself and adjust how much it valued different features of a position, such as how many pieces each side had. Over time the program became better than Samuel. He is usually credited with popularising the phrase machine learning around 1959, to describe computers that improve without being explicitly programmed for each step.

The important point is what Samuel did not do. He never told the program what a good move was. He told it what winning was, and gave it a way to adjust itself. The programmer designs the learning process; the data decides the final behaviour. That division of labour is still the heart of the field seventy years later.

Compare an old-style spam filter. A person writes rules: block emails containing "free money", block emails with many exclamation marks. Spammers simply change their wording. A learning filter instead looks at millions of emails that people have marked as spam or not spam, and finds the patterns itself, including ones no human would think to write down. When spammers change tactics, you retrain it on new examples.

Chapter 02

Three ways a machine can learn

Almost everything in the field falls into one of three families. They differ in what kind of feedback the machine gets.

FamilyWhat the machine is givenWhat it learnsEveryday example
Supervised learningExamples with the right answer attached (a "label")To predict the answer for new examplesPhotos labelled "cat" or "dog"; house sales with their prices
Unsupervised learningExamples with no answers at allHidden structure: groups, unusual cases, simpler summariesSorting customers into groups with similar buying habits
Reinforcement learningA world to act in, and a reward signalWhich actions lead to more reward over timeA program learning a board game by playing it

Supervised learning is by far the most common in business, because most practical questions have the shape "given this, predict that": given a loan application, predict default; given a scan, predict a tumour. Its weakness is that someone has to supply the labels, and labelling is slow and expensive. A fourth approach, self-supervised learning, avoids that cost by hiding part of the data and asking the model to fill it in, for example the next word in a sentence. That trick is how large language models are trained (see Vol 49, What a language model actually does).

Chapter 03

What a "model" actually is

The word model sounds mysterious. It is not. A model is a mathematical function with adjustable numbers inside it. It takes an input and produces an output. The adjustable numbers are called parameters (or weights), and learning means finding good values for them.

The simplest example is a straight line. Suppose you want to predict a flat's price from its size. A line has two parameters: where it starts and how steep it is. Learning means trying different starts and slopes until the line passes as close as possible to the real sales in your data. Once you have the line, you can read off a price for a flat you have never seen.

Every famous system is this idea made enormously bigger. A spam filter might have thousands of parameters. A modern image or language model has billions. But the logic is the same: a flexible shape, adjusted until it fits the examples. Volume 49 makes the same point and is right to deflate the word "learning". There is no understanding in the human sense, only fitting.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Measure the error, nudge the numbers, repeat a billion times

Training sounds like magic and is mostly repetition. The craft lies in the data, in what you measure, and in stopping the model from memorising instead of learning.

Chapter 04

Training: walking downhill in the fog

To train a model you need three things: examples, a model with parameters, and a loss function. The loss function is a single number that measures how wrong the model is on the examples. For house prices it might be the average size of the price errors. Training means making that number as small as possible.

The standard method is called gradient descent. Imagine you are on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom of the valley. You cannot see the valley. But you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, and take another step. The "ground" is the loss, and each direction is one parameter. The gradient is simply the slope in every direction at once. It tells the model which way to nudge each parameter to reduce the error a little.

One training loop, repeated millions of times
  examples ──► model (with current parameters) ──► predictions
                    ▲                                   │
                    │                                   ▼
      nudge each parameter                  compare with right answers
      a little downhill   ◄──── gradient ◄──── loss (one number: how wrong)

For a neural network with many layers, working out the slope for every parameter is done by an algorithm called backpropagation. It passes the error backwards through the layers, assigning each parameter its share of the blame. The method was popularised in a 1986 paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams. Almost every modern neural network, however large, is still trained with gradient descent and backpropagation.

Chapter 05

Data: features, labels and the test you must not peek at

Each example is described by features: the measurable facts the model can see. For a flat, the features might be size, floor, district and age. For a photo, the features are the brightness of each pixel. The label is the answer you want predicted, such as the price or "cat".

The single most important habit in the field is splitting the data. You train on one part, the training set, and you judge the model on a separate part it has never seen, the test set. Think of a teacher who writes the final exam with different questions from the homework. If the exam used the same questions, a student could pass by memorising answers without understanding anything.

Mistakes here are common and costly. If information from the test set leaks into training, even indirectly, the model looks excellent in the lab and disappoints in the real world. A classic example: a model for a medical scan that quietly learns to recognise which hospital's scanner made the image, because one hospital treated sicker patients. It scores well, and it has learned nothing about the disease.

Chapter 06

Overfitting: the student who memorised the past papers

A very flexible model can fit its training examples perfectly, including their noise and accidents. This is called overfitting. The model has memorised instead of generalised. On new data it does badly, like a student who learned last year's answers by heart and then meets a slightly different question.

The opposite failure is underfitting: a model too simple to capture the real pattern, like trying to describe a curved road with one straight line. Good practice sits between the two. Engineers use several tools to stay there. They collect more data. They add regularisation, a penalty that discourages extreme parameter values and so keeps the model simpler. And they stop training at the point where the test score stops improving, even if the training score is still going up.

The goal of the whole exercise is generalisation: doing well on examples the model has never met. A model is only as good as its performance on new data. Everything else is a rehearsal.

Chapter 07

The toolbox: not everything is a neural network

News coverage suggests machine learning means huge neural networks. In practice, a large share of the systems running in banks, insurers and shops use older and simpler methods, because they are cheaper, faster and easier to explain.

The rule of thumb among practitioners is clear. For neat tables of numbers, tree-based methods are usually hard to beat. For raw images, audio and text, neural networks win. Choosing the simplest method that works is a mark of skill, not of being old-fashioned.

Chapter 08

Deep learning, and why it suddenly worked in 2012

Neural networks are old. The basic idea dates back to the 1950s, and backpropagation to the 1980s. For decades they were a minority interest, because they needed more data and computing power than existed. Deep learning simply means neural networks with many layers. Each layer learns slightly more abstract features than the one before: edges, then shapes, then objects.

The turning point came in 2012. A network now called AlexNet, built by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton at the University of Toronto, entered the ImageNet competition, a contest to label photos across a thousand categories. Its error rate was roughly 15 per cent, against about 26 per cent for the next best entry. That gap was enormous by the standards of the field. Three ingredients had come together at once: a large labelled dataset, graphics chips (GPUs) that could do the arithmetic in parallel, and a few training tricks.

Within a few years, deep learning had taken over image recognition, speech recognition and translation. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational work on neural networks. The brain inspiration behind them, and how loose it really is, is the subject of Neuroscience · Artificial neural networks.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The quiet engine under modern services

Most machine learning is invisible. It decides small things millions of times a day, and some big things too. Knowing how to judge it is now a basic civic skill.

Chapter 09

Where it already runs

You probably met a dozen machine learning systems before lunch today. Your phone's keyboard predicted your next word. Your bank scored each card payment for fraud in a fraction of a second. A map app estimated your arrival time from millions of past journeys. A shop or streaming service ranked what you saw first. None of these feel like "AI", which is exactly why they matter: they shape behaviour without anyone noticing.

Some uses are more dramatic. In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo, trained partly with reinforcement learning, beat the champion Lee Sedol at Go, a game long thought too subtle for computers. In 2020 the same company's AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional shapes of proteins with accuracy close to laboratory methods, a problem biologists had worked on for fifty years. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. The most valuable uses are often in science, where a good prediction saves years of experiments.

Chapter 10

How to judge a model: the accuracy trap

Suppose one card payment in a thousand is fraud. A model that says "not fraud" every single time is 99.9 per cent accurate, and completely useless. Accuracy alone tells you almost nothing when the thing you are looking for is rare. This is the base-rate problem, and it appears in medicine, security and hiring.

Practitioners use two better questions instead. Precision: of the cases the model flagged, how many were real? Recall: of the real cases, how many did the model catch? There is always a trade-off between them. A fraud system tuned to catch every fraud will also block many honest customers. One tuned never to annoy customers will miss fraud. The same dial appears in face recognition, as Volume 14 explains (Biometrics).

A useful habit

When someone says a model is "95 per cent accurate", ask three things. Accurate on what data? How rare is the thing it is detecting? And what happens to the people it gets wrong? Those three questions catch most exaggerated claims.

Chapter 11

The data is the product

Engineers in the field like to say that a model is mostly its data. The algorithms are widely published and often free. What differs between a good system and a bad one is usually the quantity, quality and relevance of the examples. A model can only learn patterns that are present in its data, including the patterns you did not want.

There is a second, subtler problem. A model assumes the future looks like the past it was trained on. When the world changes, the model does not know. This is called distribution shift. When the COVID-19 pandemic arrived in 2020, shopping, travel and spending patterns changed almost overnight, and many forecasting and fraud models built on earlier years performed badly until they were retrained. A model is a photograph of a moment. It needs to be checked and refreshed, like any other part of a working system.

This is also why the people who collect and label data matter. A large global workforce, often low-paid, labels images, transcribes audio and rates outputs. Their judgements become part of the model.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Superhuman at patterns, weak at knowing when it is wrong

The same features that make learned systems powerful make them hard to inspect, easy to fool and quick to copy old injustices.

Chapter 13

Shortcuts, opacity and the human in the loop

Models are lazy in a specific way. They find the easiest pattern that predicts the label, whether or not it is the pattern you meant. Researchers call this shortcut learning. An image model might learn that "cow" means "green grass in the background", and then fail on a cow on a beach. The hospital-scanner example in Chapter 05 is the same failure. Shortcuts are hard to spot because the model's score looks fine.

The difficulty of seeing inside a model is called the black box problem. A research area called explainable AI tries to show which inputs drove a decision. Its tools help, but they give partial answers, not full explanations. For this reason, regulators increasingly require that important automated decisions about people, such as credit, hiring or benefits, can be explained and appealed to a human. The European Union's AI Act, adopted in 2024 and coming into force in stages, treats such uses as high-risk. The bias side of this story, with its proxies and feedback loops, is in Vol 49, Bias.

The fair summary is that a model is a tool that needs supervision, not an oracle. It is excellent at what it was tested on and unreliable outside it.

Chapter 15

The limits that matter most: data, energy and trust

Three pressures will shape the next decade. The first is data. The largest models have already been trained on a large share of the public text and images available online. Where the next high-quality data comes from, whether from licensing, synthetic data made by other models, or new sensors, is an open and contested question.

The second is energy and hardware. Training and running large models needs huge data centres, and electricity has become a real constraint (Vol 14, Data centres). Efficiency research, smaller specialised models and models that run on phones are partly a response to this.

The third is trust. As models decide more, the demand grows for testing, auditing and the right to a human review. The likely future is not one in which models replace judgement. It is one in which knowing how to question a model becomes as ordinary a skill as reading a chart. The next topic shows what happens when the same methods are used not to predict, but to create.

The core idea

Machine learning builds software from examples plus a measure of error. A model is a function with adjustable parameters, tuned by gradient descent until its loss is small. What counts is generalisation: performance on data it has never seen.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The phrase "machine learning" dates from around 1959 (Arthur Samuel).
  • AlexNet, 2012: about 15 per cent error against about 26 per cent for the runner-up. The start of the deep learning era.
  • Two 2024 Nobel Prizes, physics and chemistry, went to work built on machine learning.
The common mistake

Trusting a single accuracy number. When the target is rare, high accuracy can mean a useless model. Ask about precision, recall and who gets the errors.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a model that is perfect on its training data might be a bad model? If you can, you understand overfitting, test sets and generalisation together.

The words, in plain words
Model
A mathematical function with adjustable numbers that turns an input into a prediction.
Parameters (weights)
The adjustable numbers inside a model. Training means finding good values for them.
Feature
A measurable fact about an example that the model can see, such as a flat's size.
Label
The right answer attached to a training example.
Supervised learning
Learning from examples that come with right answers.
Unsupervised learning
Finding structure, such as groups, in data with no answers.
Reinforcement learning
Learning which actions earn the most reward by trial and error.
Loss function
A single number that measures how wrong the model currently is.
Gradient descent
Reducing the loss step by step by nudging every parameter slightly "downhill".
Backpropagation
The method for working out each parameter's share of the error in a neural network.
Overfitting
Memorising the training data, noise included, so the model fails on new data.
Generalisation
Doing well on examples the model has never seen. The real goal.
Distribution shift
When the world changes so that new data no longer looks like the training data.
