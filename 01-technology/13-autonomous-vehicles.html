Sharp Mind · Technology · Topic 13 of 20

Autonomous Vehicles. Driving is easy ninety-nine times out of a hundred. The hundredth time is the whole problem.

A car that drives itself sounds like one invention. It is really four jobs done many times a second: work out where you are, see what is around you, guess what everyone else will do, and choose a safe path. Machines learned the first three quite quickly. What took twenty years, and is still not finished, is doing all four reliably in the strange situations that every driver meets only once in a long while.

Question 1 · What is it?

A car that takes over the driving task, in stages

"Self-driving" covers everything from a car that keeps its lane on a motorway to a taxi with nobody in the front seat. Those are very different machines, and the industry has a scale for telling them apart.

Chapter 01

The race nobody finished

In March 2004 the US military research agency DARPA held a race across the Mojave Desert. The prize was one million dollars. The rule was simple: no human inside the vehicle and no remote control. Fifteen robot vehicles started. None finished. The best one, built by a team from Carnegie Mellon University, travelled about 12 kilometres of a course roughly 240 kilometres long before it got stuck on a rock.

Eighteen months later DARPA ran the race again. This time five vehicles finished. The winner, a modified Volkswagen called Stanley, was built by a Stanford team led by Sebastian Thrun. Stanley won not because its hardware was better but because its software learned from data what drivable ground looked like. In 2007 an "Urban Challenge" moved the contest to a mock town with traffic, junctions and parking. Within a few years many of those engineers had been hired by Google, which started a self-driving project in 2009. That project became the company Waymo in 2016.

That is the shape of the whole field. Early progress was dramatic, and the last part has been slow.

Chapter 02

The six levels

The standard scale comes from SAE International, an engineering standards body. It runs from Level 0 to Level 5. The key question at each level is simple: who is responsible for watching the road?

LevelNameWho watches the roadReal example
0No automationThe driver, always. The car may warn, but does not steer or brake for youA basic car with a collision warning beep
1Driver assistanceThe driver. The car helps with steering or speedAdaptive cruise control that keeps a distance
2Partial automationThe driver. The car steers and controls speed, but you must supervise every secondTesla Autopilot and "Full Self-Driving (Supervised)", GM Super Cruise
3Conditional automationThe car, in narrow conditions. You may look away but must take over when askedMercedes-Benz Drive Pilot on certain motorways at limited speed
4High automationThe car, inside a defined area and conditions. No human needed thereWaymo and Chinese robotaxis in specific cities
5Full automationThe car, everywhere a human could driveNone exists

Two points in this table cause most of the confusion in the news. First, Level 2 is not self-driving, however good it feels. The human is still the driver in law and in fact. Second, the jump from 2 to 4 is not a small step. At Level 4 there is no human to rescue a mistake, so the system must handle its own failures safely, for example by pulling over.

The defined area in Level 4 has a name: the operational design domain, or ODD. It is the set of conditions the system was built and tested for. That might mean these streets, these speeds, daylight and night, but no snow. Outside its ODD a Level 4 car will not drive.

Chapter 03

Two different bets: robotaxis and driver assistance

Companies have taken two routes. The first is the robotaxi route, taken by Waymo and, in China, by companies such as Baidu (with its Apollo Go service), Pony.ai and WeRide. They start at Level 4 in one city, with expensive sensors and detailed maps, and expand slowly one area at a time.

The second route sells assistance features to millions of ordinary car owners and tries to improve them step by step towards full autonomy. Tesla is the best-known example. It relies on cameras alone and data from a very large fleet. As of 2025 Tesla had started a small robotaxi trial in Austin, Texas, with a company employee on board. Whether the camera-only bet can reach true Level 4 at scale is one of the most argued questions in the industry. Nobody can settle it yet.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sense, locate, predict, plan, act, many times a second

An autonomous vehicle runs a loop. It measures the world, places itself in it, guesses what will happen next, picks a path and moves the controls. Then it does it all again, many times every second.

Chapter 04

The senses: cameras, radar and lidar

A typical robotaxi carries many cameras, several radars and several lidars, pointed in every direction. Each is good at something different.

Cameras

Cheap and very detailed. They read traffic lights, signs, lane paint and a cyclist's hand signal. But a camera gives a flat picture, so distance must be worked out by software. Cameras also struggle with glare, darkness and heavy rain, as eyes do.

Radar

Sends out radio waves and times their echo. It measures distance and speed directly and works in fog and rain. Its picture is coarse, though. It knows something is there and how fast it moves, but not clearly what it is.

Lidar

Short for "light detection and ranging". It fires laser pulses, often hundreds of thousands per second, and measures each echo. The result is a precise 3D cloud of points.

Combining the three is called sensor fusion. The idea is that each sensor covers another's blind spot. If the camera is dazzled by a low sun, the lidar and radar still see the truck ahead. Tesla's choice to drop radar and rely on cameras only is the main exception. Its argument is that people drive with vision alone. Critics answer that the goal is to drive better than people, not as well as them, and extra senses are how you get there.

Chapter 05

Knowing where you are, to the centimetre

The GPS in your phone is accurate to a few metres. That is fine for directions and useless for staying in a lane. (Volume 12 explains how GPS works: GPS, and why it is really a clock.) So most Level 4 systems use high-definition maps. Before a robotaxi carries a passenger in a new city, the company drives every street with sensor cars and builds a detailed 3D map. It records lanes, kerbs, lights and signs.

When the car drives, it matches what its lidar sees right now against that stored map. This is called localisation. It can place the car to within a few centimetres.

The cost is that maps must be built and kept up to date, which is one reason robotaxis expand city by city.

Chapter 06

Perception and prediction: what is that, and what will it do?

Perception means turning raw sensor data into a list of things: this is a car, that is a pedestrian, that is a plastic bag blowing across the road. Nearly all of this is done by neural networks trained on millions of labelled examples. The methods are the same family described in Computer vision and Machine learning.

Seeing is not enough. A good driver also predicts. The child near the ball may run into the road. The car with its indicator on may pull out. The cyclist looking over her shoulder is about to change lane. Prediction software gives every nearby object several possible futures for the next few seconds, each with a probability. This is where much of the hardest work lies, because people do not follow rules. They wave each other through, jaywalk, and stop in odd places.

The driving loop, simplified
  SENSORS            cameras + radar + lidar + wheel speed + GPS
     |
  LOCALISE           "I am here, in this lane, on the stored map"
     |
  PERCEIVE           "car 30 m ahead, pedestrian on kerb, red light"
     |
  PREDICT            "pedestrian: 70% waits, 30% steps out in 2 s"
     |
  PLAN               choose a path and speed that is safe in ALL likely futures
     |
  CONTROL            steering angle, brake pressure, throttle
     |
     +-----> repeat, many times every second
Chapter 07

Planning, and the remote helper

The planner chooses what the car will actually do. It weighs safety first, then legality, comfort and progress. A good plan is safe across all the likely futures that prediction produced, not just the most likely one. If the pedestrian has a 30 per cent chance of stepping out, the car slows as if she might. Too cautious and it blocks traffic; too bold and it is dangerous.

Older systems split these jobs into separate modules written partly by hand. A newer approach, often called end-to-end learning, trains one large neural network to go from sensor input almost directly to driving decisions, learning from huge amounts of human driving. It can be smoother and more human-like. It is also harder to inspect: when it makes a mistake, it is harder to say why. Most companies now mix the two approaches.

There is also a human in the background. Robotaxi companies run remote assistance centres. When a car is confused, say by a police officer waving traffic through a red light, it can stop and ask a remote operator for guidance. Usually the operator answers a question ("yes, it is safe to go round this blocked lane") and the car still does the driving itself.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lives, time, cities, and a lesson about trust

The case for autonomous vehicles is mainly about safety. The story so far has also been a case study in how a technology earns, and loses, public trust.

Chapter 08

1.2 million deaths a year

According to the World Health Organization, about 1.2 million people die on the world's roads each year, based on its 2023 report. Road crashes are a leading cause of death for children and young adults.

Most crashes involve human error: distraction, tiredness, alcohol, speeding. US government analysis has often been quoted as saying human error is the "critical reason" in about 94 per cent of crashes. That number is widely misused. It describes the last event before the crash, not who was to blame, and it does not mean a machine would have avoided 94 per cent of them. But the basic point stands. A machine never drinks, never texts and never falls asleep. If it can also see well and react well, the room for saving lives is very large.

The early evidence is encouraging but not final. Waymo has published analyses covering tens of millions of driverless miles. They report substantially fewer injury crashes than human drivers on comparable roads. Independent researchers find this plausible but note that fair comparisons are hard. The honest summary for 2025 is: promising in the places where it runs, and not yet proven everywhere else.

Chapter 09

The crashes that shaped the industry

In March 2018, in Tempe, Arizona, an Uber test vehicle struck and killed Elaine Herzberg as she walked her bicycle across a road at night. It was the first known pedestrian death caused by a self-driving test car. The investigation found that the software had detected her seconds before impact but kept changing its mind about what she was. Emergency braking by the computer had been switched off, on the assumption that the safety driver would act. The safety driver was looking down at a phone.

In October 2023, in San Francisco, a pedestrian was hit by a human-driven car and thrown into the path of a robotaxi run by Cruise, a company owned by General Motors. The robotaxi braked but then tried to pull over and dragged her several metres. California suspended Cruise's permit, largely because regulators said the company had not shared the full video promptly. In December 2024 GM closed the robotaxi business.

Both stories teach the same lesson. The technical failure was only half of it. The other half was how humans and companies behaved around the machine.

Chapter 10

Time, freight and the shape of cities

The second reason is access. People who cannot drive, because of age, disability or poor eyesight, would gain independence. Freight is the other big case. Long motorway routes are relatively simple driving, and trucking has shortages of drivers in many countries. In 2025 the company Aurora began running driverless lorries on a motorway route in Texas, one of the first commercial services of its kind.

The third reason is cities. Cars spend most of their lives parked. If people used shared robotaxis instead of owning cars, cities could need much less parking. But the opposite is also possible. If riding in a car becomes cheap and pleasant, and you can sleep or work on the way, people may travel more, live further out, and fill roads with empty cars driving to their next pickup. Which outcome happens depends on pricing, taxes and planning, not on the technology. Smart-city questions like these are covered in Smart cities.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A safer driver that is strange in new ways

The benefits are real. So are the costs, and some of them are not about driving at all.

Chapter 12

The trolley problem is the wrong worry

A favourite debate asks: if a self-driving car must choose between hitting two pedestrians or swerving and killing its passenger, what should it do? In 2018 an MIT-led team published the "Moral Machine" study in the journal Nature. It collected millions of answers to such dilemmas from people around the world and found that preferences differed between cultures.

Engineers mostly agree that these clean dilemmas almost never happen in practice. Real cars do not know who is in another car or how old a pedestrian is. In almost every dangerous moment, the right action is to brake hard and stay in a predictable line. The real ethical questions are duller and more important. How safe must a system be before it is allowed on public roads: as safe as an average driver, or much safer? And who pays when it fails?

That last question is changing law. With a human driver, the driver is usually liable. With a Level 4 car, the obvious candidate is the company that built the system. Germany passed a law in 2021 allowing Level 4 vehicles in approved areas. The United Kingdom's Automated Vehicles Act 2024 says that when a car is driving itself, the authorised company, not the person sitting inside, is responsible for how it drives. In the United States rules are set largely state by state, and the picture is patchier. The general principle emerging in many places is simple: whoever is doing the driving carries the responsibility, even if that is software.

Chapter 14

The long tail, and what to watch

Self-driving has a long record of missed predictions. Around 2015 several car makers and tech companies said fully driverless cars would be common by 2020. They were not. The reason is the long tail: the endless list of rare events. A mattress falls off a lorry. A flood covers lane markings. A child in a dinosaur costume runs across the road on Halloween. Each one is rare, but there are so many of them that a car driving millions of miles meets some every day.

The useful signals to watch are concrete. Count cities and service areas, not announcements. Look for independent safety data, not company videos. Watch whether services run in snow and heavy rain. And watch the unit economics, the cost per mile including vehicles, maps, insurance and remote staff. A network that cannot make money will shrink, however clever it is. The wider family of machines that act on their own, from drones to farm robots, is the subject of Autonomous systems.

The core idea

An autonomous vehicle runs a loop: sense, localise, perceive, predict, plan, control. The SAE levels describe who must watch the road. Level 2 still needs a human, and Level 4 works without one inside a defined area. The ordinary 99 per cent of driving was solved fairly quickly. The long tail of rare events is what has taken decades.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 1.2 million road deaths a year worldwide (WHO, 2023 report).
  • 2004: no vehicle finished the first DARPA Grand Challenge. 2005: five did.
  • Six levels, 0 to 5. No Level 5 vehicle exists.
The common mistake

Treating a Level 2 system as self-driving. If the car needs you to watch the road, you are the driver, legally and practically, however smooth it feels.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a robotaxi that drives safely in Phoenix cannot simply be shipped to Oslo in January? If you can, you understand the operational design domain, maps and the long tail.

The words, in plain words
SAE levels
A six-step scale, 0 to 5, describing how much of the driving the system does.
Operational design domain (ODD)
The roads, speeds, weather and times a system is designed to handle.
Robotaxi
A driverless taxi owned and run by a company, usually at Level 4.
Lidar
A sensor that measures distance with laser pulses and builds a 3D picture of points.
Sensor fusion
Combining several kinds of sensors so each covers the others' weak spots.
HD map
A detailed 3D map of lanes, kerbs and signs, accurate to centimetres.
Localisation
Working out exactly where the car is by matching what it sees to the map.
Prediction
Estimating what each nearby road user is likely to do in the next few seconds.
End-to-end driving
One large neural network that turns sensor input more or less directly into driving actions.
Remote assistance
Human operators who give guidance to a confused vehicle from a control centre.
Long tail
The huge number of individually rare situations that together happen all the time.
