5G and 6G. Each new "G" is a fresh deal between physics, money and politics.

Every ten years or so, the mobile industry agrees a new set of rules for how phones and towers talk to each other, and gives it a number. Each generation has been sold as a revolution. Some were. 5G was promised as the network that would run driverless cars and remote surgery. In practice it mostly made phones faster and networks less crowded, while a quieter story unfolded in factories and in geopolitics. Knowing what actually changed, and why, is the best protection against the same hype when 6G arrives.

Question 1 · What is it?

A generation is a shared rulebook, not a gadget

You cannot buy "5G" the way you buy a phone. It is an agreed set of technical rules that lets equipment from hundreds of companies, in almost every country, work together.

Chapter 01

Five generations in one table

Each generation solved the main problem left by the one before. Notice that the headline feature changes each time, from voice to texts to data to video to "everything connected".

GenerationArrivedWhat it addedWhat it felt like
1GAround 1980Analogue mobile voice callsBrick-sized phones, easy to eavesdrop on
2GEarly 1990s (GSM, first launched in Finland in 1991)Digital voice, encryption, text messagesThe Nokia era, and SMS
3GEarly 2000sUsable mobile dataEmail and slow web pages on the move
4G (LTE)From about 2009–2010Fast, all-internet dataVideo, maps, ride-hailing, social media apps
5GFrom 2019 (South Korea and the US first)More capacity, lower delay, many more devicesFaster phones in crowds. Bigger changes behind the scenes

The most important row may be 4G. The smartphone economy was built on it. Uber, Instagram and streaming video on the move all needed fast, cheap mobile data. When people asked "what will 5G do?", they were really hoping for another jump like that. It is a fair test, and 5G has so far only partly passed it.

Chapter 02

Who writes the rules

Two bodies matter. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU), a United Nations agency, sets the targets a technology must meet to count as a new generation. For 5G that target document was called IMT-2020. For 6G it is IMT-2030, agreed in 2023. The ITU also coordinates which radio frequencies are used for what, worldwide.

The detailed engineering is written by 3GPP, the 3rd Generation Partnership Project. It is a partnership of standards organisations from Europe, the US, China, Japan, South Korea and India. Engineers from equipment makers, phone companies and chip designers meet there and argue, sometimes for years, over thousands of pages of specifications. They release them in numbered "releases". The first full 5G release, Release 15, was completed in 2018.

This matters for one reason that is easy to miss. A generation is a commercial and political agreement as much as a technical one. Companies fight to get their inventions into the standard, because every phone sold must then pay them patent royalties. Countries care because the firms that shape the standard tend to sell the equipment.

Chapter 03

The three promises of 5G

The ITU described three kinds of use that 5G had to serve. They are worth knowing, because each one was a different market.

Enhanced mobile broadband

Much faster data and far more capacity. The target peak speed was 20 gigabits per second, shared by everyone in a cell. This is the part ordinary phone users actually got.

Ultra-reliable low latency

Latency is the delay before a signal arrives. The target was 1 millisecond over the radio link, with extreme reliability. Imagined uses: factory robots, remote control of machines, vehicles.

Massive machine connections

Up to a million low-power devices per square kilometre: meters, sensors, trackers. This is the network side of the Internet of Things.

These were targets for the standard, not promises about your phone. Real networks deliver a fraction of the peak numbers, and the low-latency features needed upgrades many operators were slow to make.

Question 2 · How does it work?

More spectrum, smarter antennas, and a network made of software

There are only three ways to push more data through the air: use more radio frequencies, reuse them more cleverly, or put the towers closer together. 5G does all three.

Chapter 04

Spectrum: the road that data drives on

A mobile signal is a radio wave. Spectrum means the range of radio frequencies available. A useful picture is a motorway. The width of the band of frequencies you are allowed to use, called bandwidth, is like the number of lanes. More lanes carry more traffic. In 1948 the engineer Claude Shannon proved the mathematics behind this: the maximum data rate of a channel rises with its bandwidth and with how clean the signal is compared with the noise.

The difficulty is that radio waves behave differently at different frequencies. Low frequencies travel far and pass through walls, but there is little room there, and it is already crowded with TV, radio and older networks. High frequencies have lots of room but travel short distances and are easily blocked. 5G uses three broad bands.

BandRoughlyStrengthWeakness
Low bandBelow 1 GHzLong range, good indoors. Covers countrysideLittle bandwidth, so speeds similar to good 4G
Mid bandAbout 1–7 GHz, especially around 3.5 GHzThe sweet spot: decent range and big capacityNeeds more towers than low band
Millimetre waveAbout 24–50 GHzHuge bandwidth, very high speedsRange of a few hundred metres at best. Blocked by walls, trees, rain and even a hand

Mid band turned out to be the workhorse of 5G almost everywhere. Millimetre wave was the source of the most impressive speed tests and most of the disappointment. It works well in stadiums and busy squares, and poorly almost everywhere else. Governments raise large sums by auctioning spectrum licences to operators, which is part of why 5G was expensive to build.

Chapter 05

Massive MIMO and beamforming: aiming the signal

An older tower broadcasts in broad sectors, like a lighthouse lighting up a wide arc. Much of the energy goes where nobody is. A 5G mid-band antenna panel contains dozens of small antenna elements, often 64 sending and 64 receiving. This is called massive MIMO, for "multiple input, multiple output".

By timing the signal from each element very precisely, the panel can make the waves add up in one direction and cancel out in others. This is beamforming. The tower can aim a narrow beam at your phone, a different beam at your neighbour's, and reuse the same frequencies for both at the same time. It is like replacing one loudspeaker shouting at a crowd with many people whispering into individual ears.

Beamforming is the main reason 5G mid band is so much better than 4G in a crowd. It is also why 5G antennas are heavier and use more processing power. The tower is doing a great deal of arithmetic every millisecond to track where each phone is.

Chapter 06

The core: a network made of software

The antennas are only the visible part. Behind them sits the core network, the system that checks your identity, routes your data to the internet and keeps track of your phone as you move. In earlier generations the core ran on special-purpose boxes. In 5G it is designed to run as software on ordinary servers, often in data centres or the cloud.

That shift enables two ideas. The first is network slicing: running several virtual networks on the same physical one, each with its own rules. A hospital, a factory and the general public could get separate slices with different guarantees of speed and reliability. The second is edge computing, which places small data centres near the towers, so that time-critical processing happens a few kilometres away rather than hundreds.

There is a catch. Many early networks were non-standalone 5G: 5G radios bolted onto an old 4G core. That gave faster downloads but none of the slicing or very low latency. Full standalone 5G, with the new core, has spread more slowly. So when people say "5G didn't deliver", part of the answer is that in many places the full version was never switched on.

Chapter 07

Small cells and the cost of density

Higher frequencies mean shorter range, so capacity grows by adding more, smaller base stations. These small cells sit on lampposts, building walls and inside shopping centres. Each needs power, a permit and a fast link back to the network, usually a fibre-optic cable. Building them by the thousand is slow, local and expensive work.

This is the unglamorous truth behind every generation. Wireless networks are mostly wired. The radio link from your phone covers only the first few hundred metres. After that your data travels through fibre, across cities and under oceans. A country with poor fibre will have poor 5G, however good its antennas.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Capacity, factories, and a fight between great powers

The phone in your pocket is the least interesting part of the 5G story. The bigger effects were on network capacity, on industry, and on who countries trust.

Chapter 08

What 5G actually changed

The first real effect was capacity. Mobile data use keeps rising, driven mainly by video. Without 5G, 4G networks in cities would have become badly congested. With it, each tower carries far more data at a lower cost per gigabyte. Users notice this mainly as an absence: the network still works at a concert or a train station at rush hour.

The second was a surprise. In the United States in particular, operators began selling fixed wireless access: home broadband delivered over 5G to a box by the window, instead of through a cable. It became one of the fastest-growing sources of new home internet customers there. For areas where laying fibre is expensive, it is a real alternative.

The third is private 5G networks. Ports, mines, airports and factories have built their own 5G networks to connect cranes, vehicles and sensors more reliably than Wi-Fi can. This is quieter than the consumer story but is where the industrial promises are slowly coming true. By the end of 2024 there were more than two billion 5G subscriptions worldwide, according to industry reports, with China accounting for a large share.

Chapter 09

The missing "killer app"

Operators spent hundreds of billions of dollars worldwide on spectrum and equipment. They hoped customers would pay more for 5G. Mostly, they did not. People were happy with 4G speeds for the things they did on phones. Many operators ended up giving 5G away as part of the same monthly plan.

The applications used to sell 5G largely did not need it. Self-driving cars, for example, are designed to work with no network at all. A car that depended on a mobile signal to avoid a pedestrian would be dangerous (see Autonomous vehicles). Remote surgery demonstrations were real, but rare. The lesson for the next generation is that capability alone does not create demand. The 4G boom came from developers building apps nobody had predicted. That cannot be planned in a standards meeting.

Chapter 10

Huawei, and networks as national security

The Chinese company Huawei became one of the world's largest makers of mobile network equipment, alongside Ericsson of Sweden and Nokia of Finland. Its equipment was competitive and often cheaper. From around 2018, the United States argued that Chinese law could compel Chinese companies to help state intelligence, and that equipment at the heart of a network could be used for spying or sabotage. Huawei has always denied this.

The US restricted Huawei's access to American technology from 2019. Australia had already excluded it from 5G in 2018. In 2020 the United Kingdom decided to ban new Huawei 5G equipment and remove the existing kit from its networks by the end of 2027. Other countries took a range of positions, from full bans to no restrictions. Mobile networks came to be treated as strategic infrastructure, like ports and power grids. The debate also gave a push to Open RAN, an approach that defines open interfaces inside the radio network so operators can mix equipment from several suppliers rather than depending on one.

On health fears

In 2020 false claims linked 5G to COVID-19, and several masts were set on fire in the UK. Mobile signals are non-ionising radiation: they do not have enough energy to break chemical bonds or damage DNA, unlike X-rays. Exposure limits are set by international guidelines, and public exposure from masts is normally far below them. Health agencies, including the WHO, have found no established health effect from mobile signals at these levels. Research continues, as it does for any widely used technology.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A better network, at a high price

5G is clearly better engineering than 4G. Whether it was worth what it cost, and to whom, is a fairer question.

Chapter 12

The coverage gap

One disadvantage deserves its own chapter. New generations arrive first in rich cities, because that is where operators earn back their investment. Rural areas and poorer countries get them later, and some people still rely on 3G or even 2G. In 2022 a dispute in the United States showed another kind of friction: airlines warned that new 5G signals near 3.7 GHz might interfere with aircraft radio altimeters, which measure height above the ground. Operators agreed to limit power near airports while aircraft equipment was upgraded.

The broader point is that spectrum is a shared, finite resource, and every new use competes with old ones: satellites, radar, broadcasting, aviation. Much of the real work of a new generation is negotiation over who gets which frequencies, where, and at what power.

6G: designed for 2030, still being decided

As of 2025, 6G is a set of research goals and early standards work, not a product. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 14

A quieter generation, run by software and AI

Many in the industry say 6G should be a more modest, practical generation. The likely focus is new mid-band spectrum, roughly 7 to 15 GHz, which offers more room without millimetre wave's problems. Another focus is using machine learning throughout the network to manage beams, traffic and energy, an idea often called being "AI-native". Lower energy use is a stated goal, partly because power is now one of operators' biggest costs.

The most useful way to judge 6G when it comes is to ask the questions that 5G taught. Is the full version actually switched on, or just the label? Which band is being tested? Will customers pay for it, or will it simply be needed to keep up with traffic? And who builds the equipment? If those answers are clear, the rest of the marketing can be safely ignored.

The core idea

A mobile generation is a shared rulebook, set by the ITU and written by 3GPP. 5G raises capacity with more spectrum (especially mid band), beamforming antennas and a software core. Its biggest effects were on network capacity, home broadband, industry and geopolitics, not on how phones feel.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About one new generation per decade: 1G around 1980, 5G from 2019, 6G expected around 2030.
  • 5G design targets: 20 Gbit/s peak, 1 ms radio latency, 1 million devices per km².
  • More than 2 billion 5G subscriptions worldwide by the end of 2024.
The common mistake

Judging 5G by peak speed tests. Peak speeds come from millimetre wave, which covers almost nothing. The real value of a generation is capacity and reliability for everyone, not one fast test on a street corner.

Test yourself

Can you explain why higher radio frequencies give more speed but less coverage, and why that means more towers and more fibre? If you can, you understand most of what limits every generation.

The words, in plain words
Generation (G)
A family of mobile network standards, released roughly once a decade.
ITU
The UN agency that sets targets for each generation and coordinates radio frequencies.
3GPP
The industry partnership that writes the detailed technical specifications.
Spectrum
The range of radio frequencies; the "land" that wireless signals occupy.
Bandwidth
The width of the frequency band in use. More bandwidth carries more data.
Latency
The delay between sending a signal and it arriving.
Millimetre wave
Very high frequencies with huge capacity and very short range.
Massive MIMO
Antenna panels with dozens of elements that can serve many users at once.
Beamforming
Shaping radio waves into narrow beams aimed at individual devices.
Core network
The central system that handles identity, routing and mobility.
Network slicing
Running several virtual networks with different guarantees on one physical network.
Standalone 5G
5G radios with a new 5G core, rather than attached to a 4G core.
Open RAN
An approach using open interfaces so radio equipment from different suppliers can be mixed.
