Computer Vision. Teaching a machine to see what a toddler sees without trying.

You glance at a kitchen and instantly know there is a cat on the table, a cup about to fall and a door half open. You do not experience any effort. For a computer, the same photo arrives as millions of numbers, one for the brightness of each tiny dot, and nothing in those numbers says "cat". Computer vision is the field that turns those numbers into meaning. It unlocks your phone, reads parcel labels, checks factory products, helps read medical scans and guides cars. Its errors, especially with faces, have also put innocent people in police cells.

Question 1 · What is it?

Getting useful meaning out of images and video

Computer vision is the part of computing and AI that lets machines identify, locate, measure and track things in pictures and video.

Chapter 01

The summer project that took fifty years

In 1966 the Massachusetts Institute of Technology launched what it called the Summer Vision Project. A short planning memo, associated with the computer scientist Seymour Papert, proposed that a group of students could make real progress over one summer on getting a computer to recognise objects in a scene. The plan was not foolish by the standards of the time. Seeing seemed easy, because people do it effortlessly. Surely it would be simpler than playing chess.

It was not. Chess computers beat the world champion in 1997. A computer that could reliably name the objects in an ordinary photograph arrived only in the 2010s. The ease of human vision hides an enormous amount of work done by the brain, much of it in the visual cortex at the back of your head (see Neuroscience · Vision). Computer vision has spent half a century discovering how much.

Chapter 03

Why seeing is hard for a machine

To a computer, a colour photo is a grid of numbers. A 12-megapixel phone picture holds about 12 million dots, each with three numbers for red, green and blue. The task is to get from 36 million numbers to one word, "cat". The trouble is that the same cat produces completely different numbers depending on the light, the angle, the distance, the background, and whether half of it is hidden behind a chair.

Meanwhile, things that mean different things can produce nearly the same numbers: a muffin and a small dog's face, a shadow and a pothole. A useful vision system has to ignore the differences that do not matter, such as lighting, and keep the ones that do, such as the shape of an ear. Writing rules by hand for that proved hopeless. The breakthrough came from learning those rules from examples instead, as described in Machine learning.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From edges to objects, one layer at a time

Modern computer vision builds understanding in stages: tiny local patterns first, then larger shapes, then whole objects. Machines now learn those stages from data.

Chapter 04

Clues from a cat's brain

In 1959 two neuroscientists, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, recorded from single neurons in the visual cortex of cats while showing them simple patterns. They found cells that fired strongly only for a line or edge at one particular angle, in one part of the visual field. Other cells responded to more complex combinations. Their work, which earned a Nobel Prize in 1981, suggested that vision is built in a hierarchy: simple edges first, then shapes, then objects.

Early computer vision copied this idea by hand. Engineers wrote programs to find edges, corners and blobs, then built descriptions of objects from them. These "hand-crafted features" worked for narrow jobs, such as reading postcodes or finding faces in a camera's viewfinder, but broke easily outside them. The next step was to let the machine learn its own features.

Chapter 05

Convolutional networks: a magnifying glass sliding across the image

The key tool is the convolutional neural network, or CNN. Picture a small square window, perhaps three pixels by three, sliding across the whole image. At each position it checks for one simple pattern, such as a vertical edge, and records how strongly it found it. This window is called a filter. A network uses many filters at once, and it learns during training which patterns are worth looking for.

How a CNN builds up a picture
  pixels ──► layer 1: edges, colour blobs     ( | / — )
         ──► layer 2: corners, textures       ( ⌐ ░ ▒ )
         ──► layer 3: parts                   ( eye, wheel, ear )
         ──► layer 4: objects                 ( face, car, cat )
         ──► final answer with a probability: "cat 0.93, fox 0.04 ..."

Because the same filter is used everywhere, a CNN can spot an edge or an eye wherever it appears in the picture. Each layer looks at the output of the previous one, so later layers detect larger and more abstract patterns, much like Hubel and Wiesel's hierarchy. In the 1990s, a CNN designed by Yann LeCun and colleagues was used commercially to read handwritten numbers on bank cheques. The structure was right decades before there was enough data and computing power to make it great.

Chapter 06

ImageNet: the dataset that changed the field

In the late 2000s the computer scientist Fei-Fei Li and her colleagues argued that vision research was limited less by clever algorithms than by data. They built ImageNet, a collection of more than 14 million photos, each labelled with the objects it contained, using thousands of online workers paid small amounts per task. From 2010 they ran a yearly competition on a subset with a thousand categories.

In 2012 a deep CNN called AlexNet won by a huge margin, as told in Machine learning. Within a few years, the best systems matched or beat people on that particular benchmark. That needs care: beating humans on one test of labelled photos is not the same as seeing as well as a human. The systems still made strange mistakes on images unlike their training data. ImageNet itself was later criticised for offensive or biased labels in some of its people categories, which its team then removed.

Chapter 07

Beyond naming: boxes, outlines and transformers

Naming a whole picture is rarely enough. A self-driving car needs to know where each pedestrian is. Object detection systems draw a box around every object and label it. A well-known family called YOLO ("You Only Look Once"), introduced in 2015, made detection fast enough to run on live video. Segmentation goes further and labels every pixel, which surgeons and radiologists need to measure a tumour precisely.

Since 2020 a second design has joined CNNs: the vision transformer. It borrows the transformer from language models (see Natural language processing), cutting an image into small patches and treating them like words in a sentence. Another development connected pictures with language. Models trained on huge numbers of images paired with captions can match a photo to a description they were never specifically trained on. The result is that vision and language are merging: today's assistants can answer questions about a photo you send them.

Chapter 08

Seeing depth and motion

A single photo is flat. To act in the world, machines need distance. There are several ways to get it. Stereo vision uses two cameras a short distance apart, as your two eyes do, and measures how much each object shifts between the two views: near things shift more. Depth sensors project an invisible pattern of infrared light and measure how it is distorted, which is how phone face unlock builds a 3D map. Lidar measures distance with laser pulses.

Video adds motion. By comparing consecutive frames, a system can estimate which way each part of the scene is moving, a technique called optical flow. Robots and cars combine cameras with other sensors and with their own wheel or motion measurements, a practice known as sensor fusion. Whether cars can drive on cameras alone, or need lidar too, is one of the live engineering debates covered in Autonomous vehicles.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cheap, tireless eyes, from the factory to the clinic to the street

Cameras are cheap and everywhere. Once software can understand what they show, every camera becomes a sensor that can count, check, recognise and report.

Chapter 09

Everyday vision you never notice

Your phone camera is a computer vision system. When you take a photo, it detects faces to set focus and exposure, merges several quick shots to reduce noise, and blurs the background in portrait mode by estimating depth. This is called computational photography, and it is why small phone lenses produce pictures that once needed a large camera.

Elsewhere, vision reads number plates at car parks and toll roads, scans barcodes and parcel labels in sorting centres, and lets you photograph a menu and read it translated. On production lines, cameras inspect every bottle, circuit board or biscuit for defects at speeds no human inspector could keep up with. Most of the economic value is in this kind of narrow, repetitive checking, in places where the lighting and camera position can be fixed.

Chapter 10

Reading scans and retinas

Medicine depends heavily on images: X-rays, CT and MRI scans, skin photographs, microscope slides and photos of the back of the eye. In 2018 the US Food and Drug Administration authorised a system called IDx-DR to detect diabetic retinopathy, an eye disease caused by diabetes, from retinal photos without a specialist interpreting the image. It was described as the first autonomous AI diagnostic system authorised in the country. Since then, regulators have cleared hundreds of AI tools for medical imaging, most of them in radiology.

In practice, most of these tools assist rather than replace doctors: they flag suspicious areas, prioritise urgent scans or act as a second reader in breast cancer screening. Results are encouraging in many studies, but performance can drop when a tool is used with different scanners or patient groups from those it was trained on, the distribution shift problem.

Note

This section is general education, not medical advice. Diagnostic decisions belong with qualified clinicians.

Chapter 11

Faces: the most contested use

Face recognition comes in two very different forms. Verification checks that you are who you claim to be, one face against one stored template, as when you unlock a phone or pass an airport e-gate. Identification searches one face against a large database to find who it is, as when police compare a CCTV image against millions of photos. The second is far harder and far more consequential.

In 2018 the researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru published a study, "Gender Shades", testing commercial systems that guessed gender from faces. Error rates were below one per cent for lighter-skinned men and reached about a third for darker-skinned women. A large 2019 study by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology also found demographic differences in many algorithms, though the best ones had become much more accurate. In 2020 Robert Williams, a Black man in Detroit, was arrested in front of his family after a face recognition search wrongly matched him to a shoplifting video. Several similar cases have followed.

Rules differ widely. Some US cities have banned police use. The European Union's AI Act, adopted in 2024, largely prohibits real-time face identification in public spaces by police, with narrow exceptions. Other countries use it widely for public surveillance. The technical question, "how accurate is it?", and the political question, "should it be used at all, and by whom?", are separate, and both matter.

Chapter 13

Fooling the machine

In 2014 researchers including Ian Goodfellow showed something unsettling. They took a photo that a network correctly labelled as a panda, added a carefully calculated pattern of tiny changes, invisible to a human, and the network then said "gibbon" with high confidence. These are called adversarial examples. Later work showed that a few black and white stickers placed on a real stop sign could make some vision systems read it as a speed-limit sign.

The reason is that the network does not see objects the way we do. It responds to statistical patterns across many pixels, including textures and fine details people ignore. Studies have found that many image networks rely more on texture than on shape, while people rely mostly on shape. Defences exist, such as training on adversarial examples, but none is complete. For safety-critical uses, this is one reason engineers combine several kinds of sensors and keep humans in charge of important decisions.

Chapter 15

A world where every camera understands

For most of history, a camera only recorded. Someone had to watch the footage. Computer vision removes that limit. Every shop camera, doorbell, dashcam and phone can now, in principle, count, recognise and report automatically. That is the source of most of the benefits in this file, and of most of the worries.

The next steps are already visible. Systems are moving from recognising single objects to understanding whole scenes and events over time: not just "a person and a ladder", but "a person is about to fall off a ladder". Combined with robots and cars, vision is becoming the main way machines take in the physical world. The practical question for societies is no longer whether machines can see, but what they should be allowed to watch, and who decides. The technical question for engineers remains the old one from that 1966 summer: how to make machine sight as robust as a toddler's, in any light, from any angle.

The core idea

Computer vision turns grids of pixel numbers into meaning. Convolutional networks learn layered features, from edges to objects, from large labelled datasets such as ImageNet. The main tasks are classification, detection, segmentation, tracking and recognition.

The numbers worth knowing
  • MIT's "summer" vision project: 1966. Hubel and Wiesel's edge cells: 1959.
  • ImageNet: more than 14 million labelled photos.
  • "Gender Shades", 2018: errors under 1% for lighter-skinned men, about a third for darker-skinned women.
The common mistake

Thinking "beats humans on a benchmark" means "sees like a human". Vision systems can be superhuman on one test and fooled by a sticker.

The words, in plain words
Pixel
One tiny dot of an image, stored as numbers for its colour and brightness.
Feature
A useful pattern in an image, such as an edge, corner or texture.
CNN
Convolutional neural network: a network that slides learned filters across an image.
Filter
A small learned pattern detector applied to every part of an image.
Classification
Saying what an image shows.
Object detection
Finding and labelling each object in an image with a box.
Segmentation
Labelling every pixel with the object it belongs to.
OCR
Optical character recognition: reading text in images.
Vision transformer
A model that cuts images into patches and processes them like words.
Stereo vision
Estimating depth from two cameras, as two eyes do.
Sensor fusion
Combining cameras with other sensors for a more reliable picture.
Verification vs identification
Checking one face against one claim, versus searching one face against many.
Adversarial example
An image changed slightly on purpose to fool a vision system.
