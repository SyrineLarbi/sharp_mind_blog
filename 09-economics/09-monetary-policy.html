Monetary policy. Changing one interest rate to move a whole economy, slowly and indirectly.

A small committee meets about eight times a year and decides one number: the interest rate at the centre of the money system. That number then travels, over months and years, into mortgage payments, business investment, the exchange rate, share prices, hiring and, finally, inflation. Monetary policy is the craft of choosing that number, and the other tools used when the number alone is not enough.

Question 1 · What is it?

Managing the price and quantity of money

Monetary policy is what a central bank does to influence interest rates and the amount of money and credit in an economy, in order to keep inflation low and steady and, in many countries, employment high.

Chapter 01

The man who raised rates to 20%

By the late 1970s, American inflation had been high for a decade and was still climbing. Prices were rising at over 13% a year. People had come to expect it, so workers demanded big pay rises and firms raised prices in advance, which kept inflation going. In August 1979 President Carter appointed Paul Volcker to run the Federal Reserve, the US central bank.

Volcker tightened money hard. Short-term interest rates rose to around 20% by 1980 and 1981. The result was brutal: two recessions in quick succession, unemployment above 10% in 1982, and furious protests. Home builders, whose customers could no longer afford mortgages, famously mailed pieces of wood to the Fed. But inflation broke. By 1983 it was down to around 3 to 4%.

The episode set the template for the next forty years. A central bank can bring inflation down, but the main way it does so is by slowing the economy, and the cost falls on real people. It also showed that once people expect high inflation, breaking that belief is expensive. Much of modern monetary policy is designed so that such beliefs never take hold.

Chapter 02

The target and the tool

Most central banks today share a simple framework. They have a target, usually inflation of about 2% a year, measured by a consumer price index. And they have a main tool, a short-term interest rate they control, often called the policy rate. In the US it is the federal funds rate; in the euro area the ECB's deposit rate; in Britain, Bank Rate.

The policy rate is the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight, or the rate the central bank pays banks on the money they keep with it. It sounds narrow. But nearly every other rate in the economy, from car loans to company bonds, is priced off it, directly or through expectations of where it will go. Raise it, and borrowing gets dearer and saving more rewarding, so spending slows. Cut it, and the reverse.

Some central banks have a single goal, price stability. The European Central Bank is one. Others have more. The US Federal Reserve has what is called a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. In practice, every central bank cares about jobs, because a weak labour market eventually pulls inflation down anyway.

Chapter 03

Why 2%, and not zero?

New Zealand became the first country to adopt a formal inflation target, in 1990. Many others followed, and 2% became the common choice. The US Fed made it official in 2012. But why not aim for stable prices, meaning zero?

There are three standard reasons. First, price indices tend to overstate inflation slightly, because they are slow to capture better quality and new products. Second, a small positive rate gives a safety margin against deflation, a general fall in prices, which can trap an economy as people delay purchases and debts grow heavier in real terms. Third, with some inflation, interest rates are normally higher, so the central bank has more room to cut them in a recession.

The precise number 2% is a convention more than a law of nature. Some economists argue for 3% to give more room to cut. Others warn that changing the target would damage trust. The value of a target lies mostly in everyone believing it will be met.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From one rate to every price, through several pipes

The central bank controls only the start of the chain. Everything after that runs through markets, banks and human expectations, which is why the effects are powerful but slow and hard to predict.

Chapter 04

How the central bank makes its rate stick

A central bank cannot simply announce an interest rate and expect everyone to obey. It makes the rate real through its position as the bank of banks. Commercial banks hold accounts at the central bank, and the money in those accounts, called reserves, is what they use to pay each other.

Today most large central banks pay interest on those reserves. No bank will lend reserves to another bank for less than it can earn by leaving them at the central bank. So the rate paid on reserves acts as a floor under the market. Central banks also offer to lend to banks at a slightly higher rate, which acts as a ceiling. Market rates stay inside that corridor. Traditionally, central banks also bought and sold short-term government bonds, called open market operations, to adjust the supply of reserves. The plumbing is explained in Central banking.

Chapter 05

The transmission mechanism: five pipes

Once the policy rate changes, its effect reaches the real economy through several routes at once. Economists call the whole system the transmission mechanism.

The interest rate channel is the most direct: dearer loans mean fewer house purchases and less business investment. The credit channel works through banks becoming more cautious. The asset price channel works through shares and homes losing value, so people feel poorer and spend less. The exchange rate channel works because higher rates tend to attract foreign money and strengthen the currency, which makes imports cheaper. The expectations channel works through belief: if firms and workers trust that inflation will come down, they set prices and wages accordingly.

The weight of each pipe differs by country. Where most mortgages have rates that reset often, as in Britain, Australia or Sweden, a rate rise hits households fast. Where many homeowners have 30-year fixed mortgages, as in the United States, the effect on existing borrowers is much slower.

Chapter 06

Long and variable lags

Milton Friedman, the most influential monetary economist of the twentieth century, warned that monetary policy works with long and variable lags. The full effect of a rate change on inflation is often put at one to two years, and it differs each time. A central bank is therefore like someone steering a large ship: by the time the bow turns, it may be too late to stop the turn.

This forces central banks to act on forecasts of inflation, not today's figure. It also explains why they are often accused of being too late in both directions. To keep themselves disciplined, many look at simple guides. The best known is the Taylor rule, proposed by the economist John Taylor in 1993. It says the policy rate should rise when inflation is above target and when the economy is running above capacity, and fall in the opposite cases. No central bank follows it mechanically, but it is a useful benchmark for judging whether policy looks tight or loose.

Chapter 07

Words as a tool: expectations and forward guidance

Until the 1990s, many central banks were deliberately secretive. The modern view is the opposite. Because long-term interest rates depend on what markets expect short-term rates to be in the future, a central bank can move them just by explaining its plans. This is called forward guidance. "We expect to keep rates low for an extended period" can lower mortgage rates today without any change in the policy rate.

Central banks now publish forecasts, minutes of their meetings, and in the Fed's case a chart of where each committee member expects rates to go. Every phrase in a statement is parsed by markets. This makes communication a tool, and also a risk: a careless word can move billions.

Chapter 08

When the rate hits zero: unconventional tools

The policy rate has a floor. If it goes far below zero, people could withdraw cash, which pays zero. Economists call this the effective lower bound. After 2008, many central banks hit it and still needed to do more. They turned to three new tools.

Quantitative easing

The central bank creates reserves and uses them to buy large amounts of government bonds and sometimes other assets. This pushes down long-term interest rates and supports asset prices. Often called QE.

Negative rates

Charging banks for holding reserves. The ECB's deposit rate was below zero from 2014 to 2022; Japan's policy rate from 2016 to 2024. It works, but only a little below zero.

Yield curve control

Targeting a long-term rate directly, promising to buy whatever is needed to hold it. The Bank of Japan capped its ten-year rate from 2016 until 2024.

QE is the most important of the three. It does not mean handing out banknotes. The central bank swaps one asset (bonds) for another (reserves held by banks). Its main effect is to lower long-term borrowing costs and calm markets, not to flood the economy with spending money directly. That is one reason the large QE programmes after 2008 did not produce the high inflation many critics predicted at the time.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Two crises that rewrote the rulebook

For a generation after Volcker, monetary policy looked almost routine. Then came a financial crash that pushed rates to zero, and an inflation surge that pushed them up faster than at any time in forty years.

Chapter 09

2008 to 2020: the long zero

When the financial system nearly collapsed in 2008, central banks cut rates to near zero within months. The Fed, the Bank of England and later the ECB launched QE on a scale never seen before. The Fed's balance sheet, the total of assets it holds, grew from under one trillion dollars before the crisis to over four trillion by 2014, and then to almost nine trillion after the pandemic programmes of 2020.

Many economists credit these actions with preventing a second Great Depression. The financial system stabilised and a slow recovery followed. But rates stayed near zero for years, much longer than anyone expected, and inflation often ran below target. The problem of the 2010s was not too much inflation but too little. Japan had been in a similar position since the late 1990s. Central banks worried that their main tool had stopped working.

Chapter 10

2021 to 2023: the surge and the steepest hikes in decades

In 2021 prices began rising fast as economies reopened after the pandemic: supply chains were jammed, demand was boosted by savings and stimulus, and in 2022 Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent energy and food prices soaring. Inflation peaked at about 9% in the United States in mid-2022, and above 10% in the euro area and Britain that autumn.

At first, most central banks called the rise transitory, meaning a temporary effect of reopening that would fade on its own. That judgment proved wrong, or at least too slow, and it damaged their reputation. When they did act, they acted fast. The Fed raised its rate from near zero in March 2022 to over 5% by mid-2023. The ECB moved its deposit rate from minus 0.5% to 4%. The Bank of England took Bank Rate above 5%.

Inflation then fell substantially over 2023 and 2024. To the surprise of many forecasters, the United States avoided a recession during the hiking cycle, a rare soft landing. Economists still argue over how much of the fall was due to rate rises and how much to supply chains healing and energy prices dropping. Both clearly mattered. The episode showed that central banks can be wrong about the cause of inflation, but that their commitment to the target still anchored expectations. Long-term inflation expectations moved far less than in the 1970s.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fast to decide, slow to work, blunt in effect

Monetary policy has real strengths compared with budgets set by parliaments. It also has side effects that are hard to aim and easy to ignore.

Chapter 12

The side effects people argue about

Asset prices and inequality. Low rates and QE raise the price of shares, bonds and houses. People who already own those assets gain most. Critics argue that a decade of easy money widened the wealth gap. Defenders reply that the alternative, a deeper slump with more unemployment, would have hurt poorer households more. Research gives mixed answers, and the effect depends on the period measured.

Zombie firms and risk-taking. Very cheap credit may keep alive companies that would otherwise fail, and push investors into riskier assets in search of returns. When rates rise again, these risks surface, as they did with several American regional banks in early 2023, which had large losses on bonds bought when rates were low.

Central bank losses. Having bought bonds at low rates, central banks now pay higher interest on reserves than they earn on those bonds. Several, including the Fed and the Bank of England, have reported large operating losses since 2022. This does not stop them functioning, but it has become a political talking point.

Rates, rules and who really sets them

The big questions are where "normal" interest rates will settle, whether governments will lean on central banks, and how money itself might change. Sorted by confidence.

Chapter 14

The neutral rate, and why nobody can see it

Economists use the term neutral rate, sometimes written r-star, for the interest rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy. Policy is "tight" when rates are above it and "loose" when below. The trouble is that it cannot be observed. It can only be estimated, and the estimates move with saving, investment, demographics and productivity.

Before 2020, many estimates suggested the neutral rate had fallen to very low levels, partly because ageing populations save more. Since the inflation surge, some economists argue it has risen again, pushed up by large government deficits, investment in energy transition and defence, and perhaps a wave of spending on artificial intelligence. If the neutral rate is higher than before, rates will not return to the near-zero levels of the 2010s. That would matter for every borrower, every government budget, and every asset price. It is genuinely uncertain, and the answer will only become clear over years.

The other open question is political. Central banks can only ignore elections because laws let them. How they gained that freedom, and how it might be lost, is the subject of the next file.

The core idea

Monetary policy sets the short-term interest rate and, when needed, uses the central bank's balance sheet to keep inflation near target. Its effect travels through borrowing costs, credit, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations, and arrives with long and variable lags.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Most rich-country central banks target inflation of 2%.
  • Full effects take roughly one to two years.
  • US policy rates went from near zero in early 2022 to over 5% by mid-2023.
The common mistake

Thinking quantitative easing is "printing money" that people then spend. QE swaps bonds for bank reserves. It works mainly by lowering long-term rates, which is why it did not cause runaway inflation in the 2010s.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a central bank raises rates when today's inflation may already be falling? If you can, you have understood lags and forecasts together.

The words, in plain words
Policy rate
The short-term interest rate a central bank sets, from which other rates take their cue.
Inflation target
The rate of price rises a central bank aims for, usually 2% a year.
Dual mandate
A legal duty to pursue both stable prices and maximum employment, as at the US Fed.
Reserves
Money that commercial banks hold in their accounts at the central bank.
Open market operations
Central bank purchases and sales of securities to adjust the supply of reserves.
Transmission mechanism
The set of routes by which a rate change reaches spending and prices.
Forward guidance
Central bank statements about the likely future path of rates.
Effective lower bound
The point below which cutting rates further stops working or backfires.
Quantitative easing (QE)
Large-scale purchases of bonds with newly created reserves to lower long-term rates.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general price level.
Taylor rule
A simple formula linking the policy rate to inflation and the state of the economy.
Neutral rate (r-star)
The unobservable interest rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy.
Soft landing
Bringing inflation down without causing a recession.
