Exchange rates. The price of a country, quoted every second.

An exchange rate is just the price of one currency in another. But it is a price that touches almost everything: what your holiday costs, what your supermarket pays for imported food, whether a factory can sell abroad, and whether a government can repay its foreign debts. When it moves suddenly, governments fall. This file explains why currencies move, and why countries make such different choices about whether to let them.

Question 1 · What is it?

One money priced in another

An exchange rate tells you how much of one currency you need to buy one unit of another. Simple to define, it carries a surprising amount of economics inside it.

Chapter 01

Black Wednesday

In 1990 Britain joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, a system in which European currencies promised to stay within narrow bands against each other, in practice against the German mark. By 1992 the arrangement was under strain. Germany, paying for reunification, kept interest rates high. Britain was in recession and needed lower rates. Investors doubted that Britain would keep paying the price of defending the pound.

On 16 September 1992 they sold pounds in enormous quantities. The Bank of England bought pounds with its reserves and raised its interest rate from 10% to 12%, then announced a rise to 15%. It was not enough. That evening the government announced that Britain was leaving the mechanism, and the pound fell sharply. The investor George Soros became famous for having bet against the pound, reportedly making about a billion dollars.

The day became known as Black Wednesday. Its lesson is general. A fixed exchange rate is a promise, and markets will test whether a government is willing to pay the cost of keeping it. If they decide it is not, no amount of reserves may be enough. Many economists later argued that leaving was good for Britain's economy, because it freed interest rates to fall.

Chapter 02

Reading an exchange rate

Exchange rates are quoted as a pair. "EUR/USD 1.10" means one euro buys 1.10 US dollars. "USD/JPY 150" means one dollar buys 150 yen. The same rate can be written either way round, which is a constant source of confusion. Always ask: how many of which currency buy one of the other?

TermWhat it meansExample
AppreciationA currency becomes worth more in other currenciesThe euro goes from 1.10 to 1.20 dollars
DepreciationA currency becomes worth lessThe yen goes from 110 to 150 per dollar
Devaluation / revaluationThe same moves, but decided by a government with a fixed rateA central bank resets its peg lower
Nominal rateThe rate you see quoted1.10 dollars per euro
Real rateThe nominal rate adjusted for price levels in both countriesWhat a basket of goods in one country costs in the other

The real exchange rate is what matters for competitiveness. If a country's currency falls 10% but its prices rise 10% faster than its trading partners', its goods are no cheaper abroad. Economists also track effective exchange rates, which measure a currency against a weighted basket of trading partners rather than just one.

Chapter 03

Who needs currency, and how much

People exchange currency for four main reasons: to pay for imports, to travel, to invest abroad and to speculate on moves. The first two are what most people picture. The last two are far larger. The foreign exchange market traded more than seven trillion dollars a day in 2022, according to the Bank for International Settlements, many times the value of world trade in goods and services. Most of the time, exchange rates are driven far more by money moving between investments than by goods moving between countries. How that market works is covered in Foreign exchange.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three time horizons, and a menu of regimes

What moves a currency depends on how far ahead you look. Over decades, prices matter most. Over months, interest rates. Over days, fear and news. And countries choose how much of this movement to allow.

Chapter 04

The long run: what money buys

The oldest theory of exchange rates is purchasing power parity (PPP). It says that, over time, exchange rates should adjust so that the same basket of goods costs about the same everywhere. If a country has much higher inflation than its neighbours, its currency should lose value roughly in step.

Since 1986 The Economist magazine has published a light-hearted test called the Big Mac index. It compares the price of a McDonald's burger in different countries, converted into dollars. If a Big Mac is much cheaper in one country, the theory suggests its currency is "undervalued". The index is a joke with a serious point. Prices do differ a lot, because many things, like rent and haircuts, cannot be traded. Poorer countries tend to look cheap because local services cost less.

PPP works badly over months, and better over decades. Inflation is the most reliable long-run driver of a currency: countries that keep printing money see their currency sink. Over shorter periods, exchange rates can stay far from PPP for years.

Chapter 05

The medium run: interest rates and money flows

Imagine you can earn 5% a year on safe deposits in one currency and 1% in another. Money tends to flow towards the higher rate, and buying that currency pushes up its value. This is why central banks' decisions move exchange rates so strongly. When the US Federal Reserve raised rates rapidly in 2022, the dollar rose against most currencies. Japan kept rates near zero, and the yen weakened to around 160 per dollar in 2024, its weakest level since the 1980s.

Economic theory adds a twist. If one currency pays more interest, markets often expect it to weaken later, which would cancel the gain. In practice that expectation is frequently wrong, which is the basis of the carry trade, borrowing in a low-rate currency to invest in a high-rate one. It works until a sudden reversal wipes out years of gains.

Beyond interest rates, currencies respond to a country's growth prospects, its trade balance, political stability and the credibility of its central bank. A country that seems likely to grow fast and treat investors fairly attracts money, and its currency tends to rise.

Chapter 06

The short run: fear, news and safe havens

Over days and weeks, currencies move mainly on news and mood. A surprising inflation number, an election result, a war or a central banker's speech can move a major currency by several percent. Many of these moves are hard to explain even after the fact.

In a crisis, money rushes towards a few safe-haven currencies: above all the US dollar, and often the Swiss franc and the Japanese yen. This creates a paradox. Even when a crisis starts in the United States, as in 2008, the dollar often rises, because investors around the world need dollars to repay dollar debts and want the safest assets available, which are mostly in dollars.

Chapter 07

The menu of regimes: from free float to no currency at all

Every country must choose an exchange rate regime: the rules for how its currency is valued. The choices form a spectrum.

RegimeHow it worksExamples
Free floatThe market sets the rate; the central bank rarely intervenesUS dollar, euro, British pound, Japanese yen
Managed floatMostly market-driven, but the central bank intervenes to smooth movesMany emerging economies; India
Crawling peg or bandFixed, but the target is adjusted graduallyUsed by several countries with high inflation
Fixed pegThe central bank holds the rate at a set level against another currencySaudi riyal at 3.75 per dollar since 1986; Danish krone close to the euro
Currency boardEvery unit of local money must be backed by foreign reservesHong Kong dollar, pegged to the US dollar since 1983
DollarisationThe country uses another country's currencyPanama, Ecuador since 2000, El Salvador since 2001
Monetary unionSeveral countries share one currency and central bankThe euro area since 1999

Many countries officially float but intervene more than they admit. The economists Guillermo Calvo and Carmen Reinhart called this fear of floating: governments say they let the market decide, but they act whenever their currency moves sharply.

Chapter 08

The impossible trinity

One idea explains most regime choices. A country would ideally like three things: a fixed exchange rate for stability, free movement of capital for investment, and an independent monetary policy to set its own interest rates. It can have only two at once. This is the impossible trinity, or trilemma, developed from work by Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming in the 1960s.

Black Wednesday was the trinity in action. Britain had free capital flows and a fixed rate, so it had to follow German interest rates. When it wanted lower rates for its own recession, one of the three had to give, and it was the fixed rate. Every currency crisis is, at heart, a country trying to have all three.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Prices, debts, crises and the dollar

A currency move redistributes winners and losers across a whole economy. When a fixed rate breaks, it can wipe out savings, companies and governments in weeks.

Chapter 09

What a falling currency does

When a currency weakens, imports cost more. Fuel, food and components priced in dollars become dearer, which pushes up inflation. Economists call the share of a currency move that reaches consumer prices pass-through. It tends to be larger in small economies that import a lot.

At the same time, exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, which should help exporters sell more. But the improvement takes time. Contracts are already signed, and buyers take time to switch suppliers. So a fall in the currency often makes the trade balance worse at first, because imports cost more immediately, before it gets better. Drawn on a chart, this looks like a letter J, and it is called the J-curve.

The most dangerous effect is on debt. Many companies and governments in emerging economies have borrowed in dollars, because foreign lenders would not lend in local currency. Economists Barry Eichengreen and Ricardo Hausmann called this inability original sin. When the local currency falls, the burden of dollar debts rises in local terms, even though nothing else has changed. A 50% fall in the currency makes a dollar loan twice as heavy to repay. This is how currency crises turn into banking and debt crises.

Chapter 10

When pegs break: three famous cases

Thailand, 1997

The baht was pegged to the dollar while foreign money poured in. When confidence turned, Thailand ran short of reserves and let the baht float in July 1997. The crisis spread to Indonesia, South Korea and others. Indonesia's rupiah at its worst lost more than three-quarters of its value against the dollar.

Argentina, 2002

From 1991 a law fixed one peso to one dollar, which ended hyperinflation. But the peg made Argentine exports expensive and could not survive a long recession and heavy debts. It collapsed at the start of 2002, with a default, frozen bank accounts and a deep slump.

Switzerland, 2015

To stop its franc rising, the Swiss National Bank capped it at 1.20 francs per euro in 2011, buying euros without limit. On 15 January 2015 it suddenly dropped the cap. The franc surged within minutes, at one point by around 30%.

The Swiss case is instructive because it is the opposite problem. Thailand and Argentina ran out of dollars defending a weak currency. Switzerland could create unlimited francs to hold its currency down, but it decided that the euros piling up on its balance sheet had become too large a risk. A peg can fail from either side: from lack of reserves, or from the cost of piling them up.

Chapter 11

The dollar's special place

At Bretton Woods in 1944, the world's major currencies were fixed to the US dollar, and the dollar was fixed to gold at 35 dollars an ounce. That system ended when President Nixon stopped converting dollars into gold on 15 August 1971. By 1973 the major currencies were floating.

Yet the dollar's role did not shrink. It remains on one side of almost nine in ten foreign exchange trades. In the mid-2020s just under 60% of the world's official foreign exchange reserves were held in dollars, about a fifth in euros, and only a few percent in China's renminbi. Much of world trade, even between countries that are not the United States, is priced in dollars. The French politician Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, as finance minister in the 1960s, called this an "exorbitant privilege": the United States can borrow cheaply in its own currency and is less exposed to currency crises than anyone else.

The privilege has a flip side. When the Fed raises rates and the dollar strengthens, countries around the world with dollar debts feel the squeeze. American monetary policy is, in effect, monetary policy for much of the world.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fixed or floating: no free choice

There is no best exchange rate regime for every country. Each option buys something valuable and pays for it with something else.

Chapter 12

Weighing the regimes

QuestionFloating rateFixed rate or shared currency
Stability for tradeRates can swing widely, adding uncertainty for exporters and importers.Predictable prices; easier trade and investment with the anchor country.
Control of interest ratesThe central bank sets rates for domestic needs.Rates must follow the anchor country, even when they do not suit.
Absorbing shocksThe currency falls in a slump, making exports cheaper and cushioning the blow.Adjustment must come through wages and prices, which is slow and painful, as in Greece after 2010.
Fighting inflationDepends on the credibility of the domestic central bank.Can import credibility from the anchor, as Argentina did in the 1990s.
Crisis riskSharp falls are possible, but there is no peg to break.Pegs invite speculative attacks, and breaking one is often catastrophic.

Broadly, large economies with credible central banks tend to float. Small, very open economies, or countries whose own institutions are distrusted, often fix or adopt another currency. Oil exporters often peg to the dollar because their main export is priced in it. The right choice depends less on economic theory than on how much a country trusts its own institutions.

Chapter 14

What would really challenge the dollar

For a currency to replace the dollar, it would need deep and open financial markets, a large supply of safe assets for investors to hold, strong legal protection for foreign owners, and free movement of capital. The euro has much of this, but its government bond market is split across many countries. China has the scale of trade but keeps controls on capital, and foreign investors worry about legal protection.

That is why most economists expect slow change rather than sudden displacement. The bigger risk to the dollar may come from inside the United States: large deficits, political pressure on the Federal Reserve, or the frequent use of financial sanctions, which encourages other countries to build alternatives. Reserve currencies have been replaced before, as sterling gave way to the dollar in the twentieth century, but the change took decades and followed a shift in economic power. The debts that sit underneath all these currencies are the subject of the next file, Public debt.

The core idea

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. Over decades it follows inflation; over months, interest rates and money flows; over days, news and fear. Countries choose a regime from free float to shared currency, and the impossible trinity means none can have a fixed rate, free capital and its own monetary policy all at once.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The FX market traded more than 7 trillion dollars a day in 2022.
  • The dollar is on one side of almost 9 in 10 currency trades.
  • Just under 60% of official reserves were in dollars in the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Thinking a strong currency means a strong economy, and a weak one means failure. A falling currency can rescue exporters and cushion a slump; a strong one can hurt industry. The question is always: good for whom?

Test yourself

Can you explain why Britain could not keep both its fixed exchange rate and the low interest rates it needed in 1992? If you can, you have understood the impossible trinity.

Exchange rate

Devaluation
A deliberate lowering of a fixed exchange rate by the government or central bank.
Real exchange rate
The exchange rate adjusted for differences in price levels.
Purchasing power parity
The theory that exchange rates move to equalise the price of the same goods across countries.
Peg
A fixed exchange rate maintained against another currency.
Currency board
A strict peg in which local money is fully backed by foreign reserves.
Dollarisation
Using another country's currency instead of your own.
Impossible trinity
The rule that a country cannot combine a fixed rate, free capital flows and independent monetary policy.
Pass-through
The share of a currency move that shows up in domestic prices.
J-curve
The pattern in which a currency fall first worsens, then improves, the trade balance.
Original sin
A country's inability to borrow abroad in its own currency.
Safe-haven currency
A currency investors buy in times of fear, such as the dollar or Swiss franc.
