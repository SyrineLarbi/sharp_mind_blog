Sharp Mind · Economics · Topic 06 of 20

Economic growth. Two hundred years that changed more than the ten thousand before them.

For almost all of human history, the typical person lived close to subsistence. A farmer in 1700 was not much richer than a farmer in Roman times. Then, starting in northwest Europe around 1800, average incomes began to rise and kept rising, until people in rich countries today are many times richer than their ancestors. Economic growth is the name for that change. It explains why life expectancy has more than doubled, why most children now go to school, and why some countries remain poor. It also raises some of the hardest questions of our time, about the planet and about who gains.

Question 1 · What is it?

A sustained rise in what the average person can produce and consume

Economic growth means the economy produces more goods and services over time. What matters for living standards is growth per person, sustained over decades.

Chapter 01

The hockey stick

Draw a graph of average income per person over the last two thousand years and it looks like a hockey stick. A long flat handle, with small ups and downs, then a sudden turn upwards in the last two centuries. The economic historian Angus Maddison spent decades estimating these figures, and his work, continued by others since his death in 2010, is the basis of most such charts. The numbers for early centuries are rough, but the shape is not in doubt.

Why was the handle flat for so long? In 1798 the English clergyman Thomas Malthus offered an explanation. When harvests improved, people ate better, more children survived, and the population grew until food per person fell back to where it was. Gains in output were eaten by more mouths. This "Malthusian trap" describes most of history quite well. What is remarkable is that Malthus wrote just as his own country was beginning to escape it.

The escape began with the Industrial Revolution in Britain from the late eighteenth century, and spread to western Europe, North America, Japan, and eventually most of the world. Output began to grow faster than population, decade after decade. Today average income per person in the richest countries is many times what it was in 1800, and global life expectancy, which was around 30 years in 1800 by most estimates, was above 70 by the 2020s.

Chapter 02

Small rates, huge differences

Growth is measured as the yearly percentage increase in real GDP, the output measure explained in GDP. For living standards, the number to watch is real GDP per person. An economy whose population grows 2% a year needs 2% growth just to stand still.

The rates sound small, but they compound. A simple rule: divide 70 by the growth rate to get the approximate number of years it takes for income to double. (Volume 38 uses 72 for interest; both are rules of thumb.)

Yearly growth per personYears to doubleMultiple after 50 years
1%About 70About 1.6 times
2%About 35About 2.7 times
3%About 23About 4.4 times
7%About 10About 29 times

That is why economists obsess over a difference of one percentage point. Over a lifetime, the difference between 1% and 3% growth is the difference between children who are somewhat richer than their parents and children who are several times richer.

Chapter 03

The great divergence, and the catch-up

Growth did not arrive everywhere at once. The countries that industrialised first pulled far ahead in the nineteenth century, a gap historians call the Great Divergence. By the mid-twentieth century the richest countries were many times richer than the poorest.

Since then, some countries have closed much of the gap astonishingly fast. Around 1960, South Korea was a poor, largely rural country, with an income per person comparable to many countries in Africa and South Asia at the time. Over the next four decades it grew at very high rates, and today it is a high-income economy with world-leading firms in chips, ships and cars. China grew at close to 10% a year on average for three decades after its market reforms began in 1978, moving several hundred million people out of extreme poverty. Others, with similar starting points, grew slowly or not at all. Explaining why is the central question of growth economics.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Machines, ideas and the rules of the game

An economy can grow by putting more people to work, by giving them more tools, or by finding better ways to use both. Only the last can continue without limit.

Chapter 04

The recipe: labour, capital and technology

Think of an economy as a giant kitchen. Output depends on the number of cooks (labour), the ovens, knives and stock of ingredients (capital), and the recipes and methods they use (technology, in the broadest sense). Growth can come from more cooks, more ovens, or better recipes.

In 1956 the American economist Robert Solow built a model of this that won him a Nobel prize in 1987. Its key insight is diminishing returns to capital. Give one cook a second oven and output rises a lot. Give her a tenth oven and it barely changes. So an economy cannot grow forever just by saving and building more machines. Eventually each new machine adds less than the cost of maintaining it.

When Solow measured US growth, he found that more labour and more capital explained only part of it. The large remainder, now called total factor productivity or the "Solow residual", reflected better technology and organisation. The economist Moses Abramovitz famously called it "a measure of our ignorance", because it was everything the model could not explain. In the long run, growth in income per person comes mostly from getting better at turning inputs into output. That process is the subject of Productivity.

Chapter 05

Why ideas are different

An oven can only be used by one cook at a time. A recipe can be used by every cook in the world at once, and using it does not wear it out. Economists say ideas are non-rival. This is the insight behind the work of Paul Romer, who shared the 2018 Nobel prize. In his models, growth comes from the deliberate creation of new ideas by people and firms seeking profit. Because ideas can be reused endlessly, they escape diminishing returns.

That has a striking implication: more people can mean faster growth, because more minds produce more ideas, and every idea benefits everyone. It also explains why policy towards research, education, patents and the free flow of knowledge matters so much for long-run growth.

Ideas also destroy. The Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter, writing in 1942, called capitalism a process of creative destruction: new firms and technologies replace old ones, bringing growth to some and ruin to others. Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt built Schumpeter's idea into formal models in the early 1990s. They shared the 2025 Nobel prize with the economic historian Joel Mokyr, honoured for explaining how a culture of useful knowledge allowed sustained innovation to take off.

Chapter 06

Institutions: the rules that decide whether effort pays

Machines and ideas are available, in principle, to any country. Why do some use them so much better? A large body of research points to institutions: the formal and informal rules that shape economic life, such as property rights, contract enforcement, honest courts, and limits on the power of rulers.

The clearest natural experiment is Korea. North and South shared a language, culture and history until 1945. After division, the South developed a market economy that eventually became a democracy, while the North became a closed, centrally planned state. Today the South is many times richer. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson, who shared the 2024 Nobel prize, argue that the key difference is between inclusive institutions, which give broad groups a stake and protect their gains, and extractive institutions, which let a narrow elite take what others produce. Their book Why Nations Fail (2012) opens with the city of Nogales, split by the US–Mexico border, where the same people live very different lives on each side.

Critics accept that institutions matter but argue about how much, and about which comes first. China grew very fast for decades without Western-style democracy, though with secure enough rules for investors and fierce competition between regions. "Good institutions" seem to matter more than any single model of them.

Chapter 07

Why it started in Britain: a contested question

Historians still debate why sustained growth began in eighteenth-century Britain rather than in China, India or elsewhere in Europe. The main explanations are not mutually exclusive.

Coal and costs

The historian Robert Allen argues that Britain had unusually high wages and cheap coal. That made it profitable to invent machines that replaced labour with energy, like the steam engine and the spinning jenny. Elsewhere, the same inventions would not have paid.

Culture and knowledge

Joel Mokyr emphasises an "industrial enlightenment": a culture of experiment, publication and practical science, in which knowledge was shared between scientists and craftsmen.

Institutions

Others point to the political settlement after 1688, which limited the crown, protected property and built a strong system of public finance.

Trade and empire

Still others, such as Kenneth Pomeranz, stress access to coal deposits near the surface and to land and resources in the Americas, which relieved pressure on Europe's limited land.

There is no consensus. The honest answer is probably a combination, which is why the question still fills books.

Chapter 08

Catching up is easier than leading

A poor country does not need to invent the factory, the railway or the mobile phone. It can adopt them. That is why poor countries can grow much faster than rich ones, a process economists call convergence or catch-up growth. Japan after 1950, South Korea and Taiwan after 1960, and China after 1978 all grew at rates that no rich country at the frontier has managed.

But convergence is not automatic. Many countries reached middle income and then slowed down, because the easy gains, such as moving workers from farms into factories, ran out before they built the ability to innovate. Economists call this the middle-income trap, though some argue the evidence for a distinct trap is weaker than the phrase suggests. For a country at the frontier, such as the United States, growth in income per person has averaged roughly 2% a year over the long run. Moving faster than that means inventing, not just copying.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The difference between poverty and plenty

Nothing else in economics has done as much to reduce human misery. Growth also shapes politics, public finances and how people treat each other.

Chapter 09

The great escape from poverty

By the World Bank's measure, more than a third of humanity lived in extreme poverty in 1990. By 2019, before the pandemic, the share had fallen below a tenth, even as world population grew by more than two billion. The definition of the poverty line has been updated over the years, and it is very low, but the direction is not in dispute. Most of the fall came from fast growth in China, India, Indonesia, Vietnam and other Asian economies.

Growth pays for the things that make lives longer and better: clean water, vaccinations, schools and roads. No country has achieved mass prosperity without sustained growth. The economist Angus Deaton, who won the 2015 Nobel prize, called the escape from early death and deprivation "the great escape" in a book of that name, while stressing that it left many people behind.

Chapter 10

Growth and the mood of a society

Growth also changes politics. When the economy is growing, one person's gain need not be another's loss. When it stalls, life starts to feel like a contest over a fixed pie. The economist Benjamin Friedman argued in The Moral Consequences of Economic Growth (2005) that periods of rising living standards have tended to bring greater tolerance and openness, and periods of stagnation the opposite. This is a historical argument, not a law, but it fits a worry heard often today: that slow growth in rich countries since 2008 has fed anger and polarisation.

Chapter 11

Growth makes promises affordable

Pensions, health systems and public debts are all promises about the future. Growth makes them easier to keep. If the economy grows faster than the interest rate a government pays, its debt shrinks relative to GDP even without cuts, which is roughly how Britain and the United States reduced their huge debts from the Second World War. If growth is slow, the same promises require higher taxes or lower benefits. As populations age, with fewer workers supporting more retirees, the growth rate becomes one of the most important numbers in public finance. See Public debt.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A great good with real costs

Growth has transformed human life. It has also put pressure on the planet, and its gains have not been shared evenly.

Chapter 13

Green growth or degrowth?

The sharpest debate about growth today is about the planet. Two broad positions face each other.

Green growth holds that economies can keep growing while cutting emissions and resource use, by changing what is produced and how. Supporters point to evidence of decoupling: several rich countries, including the UK, Sweden and Germany, have cut their territorial carbon emissions substantially since 1990 while their economies grew. They argue that growth funds clean technology and that poorer countries need growth above all.

Degrowth advocates argue that decoupling has been too slow and too partial, especially once the emissions embedded in imported goods are counted, to meet climate targets in time. They call for rich countries to deliberately reduce resource use and to focus on well-being rather than GDP. Critics reply that shrinking economies tend to bring unemployment and political crisis, and that no democracy has chosen to shrink.

Most economists sit closer to green growth, while accepting that decoupling must speed up a great deal. The honest disagreement is less about whether growth can be cleaner than about whether it can become clean fast enough. Climate science is in Volume 30 (The Earth).

Chapter 15

The question beneath the forecasts

Behind every growth forecast lies a simple question: will people keep finding better ways of doing things, and will societies let them use those ideas? The history of the last two centuries says yes when ideas can circulate, when the rules reward effort and invention, and when those who lose from change are not left to sink. It says no when wars, closed borders or extractive rulers get in the way.

That is why the future of growth is as much about politics as about technology. Growth is not a natural law. It is a habit that some societies acquired and others can lose. The next file looks at the moments when growth goes into reverse: Recessions. The long-range picture is in The future of the global economy.

The core idea

Growth means a sustained rise in real output per person. It comes from more labour, more capital and, above all, better ideas and technology. Whether a country uses those well depends on its institutions. Small rates compound into huge differences.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Divide 70 by the growth rate to get the years it takes income to double.
  • Extreme poverty fell from over a third of humanity in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019 (World Bank).
  • China grew close to 10% a year on average for three decades after 1978.
The common mistake

Thinking growth means only more stuff. In the long run it is mostly better ways of doing things: the same inputs producing more, cleaner or more useful output.

The words, in plain words
Economic growth
A sustained increase in an economy's real output, usually measured by real GDP.
Growth per person
Growth in real GDP divided by population: the measure that tracks living standards.
Malthusian trap
The pattern in which gains in output are absorbed by population growth.
Capital
Tools, machines, buildings and infrastructure used to produce goods.
Diminishing returns
Each extra unit of an input adds less output than the one before.
Total factor productivity
The part of growth not explained by more labour or capital: better methods and technology.
Non-rival
Usable by many people at once without being used up, like an idea.
Creative destruction
New firms and technologies replacing old ones, as Schumpeter described.
Institutions
The rules of economic life: property rights, courts, contracts and limits on power.
Convergence
Poorer countries growing faster than richer ones and closing the gap.
Middle-income trap
The tendency of some countries to stall after reaching middle income.
Decoupling
Economic growth continuing while emissions or resource use fall.
