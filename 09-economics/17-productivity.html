Productivity. The dull-sounding number that decides how rich your grandchildren will be.

A worker in a rich country today earns many times what a worker in the same country earned in 1850. They do not work more hours; they work far fewer. The difference is that each hour now produces vastly more. That is productivity. It is the main reason living standards rise over the long run, and its growth has been disappointingly slow in most rich countries for two decades. This file explains what it is, where it comes from, why new technology takes so long to raise it, and what the arguments about artificial intelligence really turn on.

Question 1 · What is it?

Output per unit of effort

Productivity is a ratio: how much comes out divided by how much went in. The definition is simple. Measuring it well is not.

Chapter 01

Almost everything, in the long run

Productivity isn't everything, but in the long run it is almost everything.

Paul Krugman · The Age of Diminished Expectations, 1990

Krugman's line is quoted so often because it is right. A country's income per person can rise for a while because more people work, or because they work longer hours, or because it borrows. Each of those runs out. Only one thing can raise living standards year after year for centuries: getting more out of each hour of work.

The most common measure is labour productivity: output divided by hours worked. If a bakery makes 1,000 loaves with 100 hours of work, its productivity is ten loaves an hour. For a whole country, output is measured by GDP (see GDP), so labour productivity is GDP per hour worked. It is different from GDP per person, which also depends on how many people work and for how long. France, for example, has long had output per hour close to that of the US, but lower output per person, mainly because the French work fewer hours on average.

Chapter 02

Two kinds: more tools, or better ways

There are two ways the bakery can make more bread per hour. It can buy a bigger oven and a mixing machine. That is capital deepening: giving each worker more equipment. Or it can find a better way to use what it already has: a smarter layout, a better recipe, a better schedule. That second kind is called total factor productivity, or TFP. It is the growth in output that cannot be explained by more workers, more hours or more machines.

In 1957 the economist Robert Solow studied American growth from 1909 to 1949. He found that more capital per worker explained only a small part of the rise in output per hour. The rest, by his estimate around seven-eighths, was left over. Economists call this leftover the Solow residual. It captures technology, know-how, organisation and everything else that lets the same inputs produce more. In the long run, it is better ideas, not just more machines, that make people richer. Solow received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1987.

Chapter 03

Why it is so hard to measure

For a factory making identical bolts, productivity is easy to count. For most of a modern economy it is not. How do you measure the output of a teacher, a nurse or a lawyer? Often statisticians fall back on what those services cost, which means productivity can look flat even when quality improves.

Quality causes trouble everywhere. A phone in 2025 costs about the same as one in 2010 but does far more. Statisticians try to adjust for this, but the adjustments are imperfect. Free digital services, such as search engines, maps and messaging, add enormous value to people's lives but almost nothing to GDP, because nobody pays for them directly. Productivity statistics are best at measuring things and worst at measuring services and quality, which are a growing share of what rich economies produce.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Specialisation, technology, management and reallocation

Productivity rises through a handful of channels. The surprising part is how slowly even revolutionary technologies pass through them.

Chapter 04

The pin factory: specialisation

The first great description of productivity is in the opening chapter of Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations in 1776. Smith described a small workshop making pins. One worker drew out the wire, another straightened it, a third cut it, a fourth sharpened the point, and so on through about eighteen separate operations. Ten workers, each doing a few steps, could make about forty-eight thousand pins a day. One untrained worker doing everything alone, Smith wrote, certainly could not make twenty, and perhaps not even one.

The division of labour raises productivity because each person gets better through repetition, no time is lost switching tasks, and simple repeated steps are easier to mechanise. The same logic, scaled up to whole countries, is why trade raises productivity (see International trade). Smith also warned that a lifetime of repeating one tiny task could dull the mind of the worker who performed it. Both halves of his argument are still relevant.

Chapter 05

General-purpose technologies and the long wait

Some inventions are not just products. They change how almost everything is made. Economists call them general-purpose technologies: steam power, electricity, the internal combustion engine, computers, and possibly AI. They raise productivity enormously, but only after a long delay.

The classic example comes from the historian of technology Paul David, in a 1990 paper called "The Dynamo and the Computer". Factories began to adopt electric motors in the 1880s and 1890s, but for decades productivity barely moved. At first, owners simply replaced the single giant steam engine, which drove every machine through belts and shafts, with one giant electric motor. The real gains came only when factories were redesigned from scratch, with a small motor on each machine, single-storey layouts and workflows arranged around the product. That took a generation, and the big gains in American manufacturing came only in the 1920s.

The same pattern returned with computers. In 1987 Robert Solow remarked that you could see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics. A few years later the gains arrived. US productivity growth accelerated from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s, as businesses finally reorganised around computers and the internet. A technology raises productivity only when organisations change around it, and that is slow, expensive and full of false starts.

Chapter 06

Management: the hidden technology

Within almost every industry, the gap between the most and least productive firms is enormous. Studies of American manufacturing have found that, within the same narrowly defined industry, plants near the top produce roughly twice as much from the same inputs as plants near the bottom. That gap cannot be explained by machines alone.

From the early 2000s Nicholas Bloom, John Van Reenen and colleagues surveyed thousands of firms across many countries about basic management practices: whether they set clear targets, tracked performance, rewarded good work and dealt with poor performance. Firms with better practices were consistently more productive and more profitable. Family firms run by an eldest son, chosen by birth rather than skill, tended to score poorly. Good management behaves like a technology: it can be learned and copied, but many firms never do. OECD research has found a widening gap between frontier firms and the rest, suggesting that best practice spreads more slowly than it used to.

Chapter 07

Reallocation: letting the better firms grow

An economy's productivity rises not only when each firm improves but when workers and money move from weaker firms to stronger ones. The economist Joseph Schumpeter called the process creative destruction: new firms with better methods grow, old ones shrink or die, and the average rises. In the US, studies have found that a large share of productivity growth in retail during the 1990s came from efficient new chains replacing less efficient independent shops.

When that reallocation is blocked, productivity suffers. Chang-Tai Hsieh and Peter Klenow estimated in 2009 that if capital and labour in China and India had been allocated across factories as efficiently as in the US, manufacturing productivity there would have been far higher. Things that block reallocation include subsidies for failing firms, credit that flows to the politically connected, rules that make it hard to start or close a business, and housing costs that stop workers moving to where the good jobs are.

Chapter 08

The string quartet problem

In the 1960s the economists William Baumol and William Bowen noticed something about live music. A string quartet by Beethoven needs four musicians and about forty minutes, exactly as it did when it was written. Its productivity has not risen at all. Yet musicians' pay has risen over two centuries, because orchestras must compete with other employers whose productivity has soared.

This is Baumol's cost disease. In sectors where productivity grows slowly, such as education, healthcare, care for the elderly and live performance, costs rise faster than in the rest of the economy. That is a big part of why school fees and medical bills climb faster than the price of televisions. It is not mainly waste. It is the side effect of productivity rising fast elsewhere. And as these sectors take a growing share of spending, they pull down the economy's overall productivity growth.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Wages, public finances, and the great slowdown

Small differences in productivity growth, sustained for decades, decide whether a society feels prosperous or stuck.

Chapter 09

Small numbers, enormous differences

A handy rule: divide 70 by a growth rate to find roughly how many years it takes for something to double. At 2% a year productivity growth, output per hour doubles in about 35 years, roughly once per working life. At 1%, it takes about 70 years, roughly once per lifetime. The difference between those two rates is the difference between children expecting to be much better off than their parents and children expecting roughly the same.

Productivity matters for wages because, over long periods, pay tracks what workers produce. Firms cannot keep paying more than workers produce, and in competitive markets they cannot keep paying much less. Whether the link has weakened in recent decades is debated. In the US, some measures show a large gap opening between productivity and typical pay since the 1970s; others, adjusting for benefits and price measures, find a smaller one. Either way, without productivity growth, a sustained rise in pay is impossible. Productivity also matters for public finances: faster growth makes pensions, health care and debt far easier to pay for (see Public debt).

Chapter 10

The great slowdown after 2005

In the US, labour productivity grew at around 3% a year between the mid-1990s and the mid-2000s. From roughly 2005 until the pandemic, it grew at about half that rate. Similar slowdowns hit almost every rich country. Britain's was among the most severe: before the 2008 financial crisis its productivity had grown at around 2% a year, and in the decade after, growth was well under 1%. Economists there call it the productivity puzzle.

The slowdown is one of the most important economic facts of the century so far. Had pre-2005 growth rates continued, incomes in rich countries would be substantially higher today, and many debates about stagnant wages, strained public services and political anger would look different.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Who gains, who adjusts, and what gets lost

Over decades, productivity growth makes nearly everyone better off. In any given decade, it also displaces people, and the gains do not spread themselves evenly.

Chapter 13

Productivity is not working harder

In everyday speech "productive" means busy. In economics it means something nearly opposite: getting more for less effort. Some countries with very long working hours have low output per hour, and some with short hours have high output per hour. Longer hours can even lower productivity, because tired people make more mistakes.

That distinction matters for how gains are shared. A society can take its productivity gains as more goods, or as more free time, or as some mix. Over the twentieth century, rich countries took a large part as time: the typical working week fell from around sixty hours to around forty, and paid holidays spread. The choice between more income and more leisure is a social decision, not something productivity decides on its own. The economist John Maynard Keynes predicted in 1930 that his grandchildren's generation might work fifteen hours a week. Incomes rose roughly as he expected; hours fell far less than he guessed.

Chapter 15

The forces pulling the other way

Even if AI delivers, other trends will press on productivity. Ageing populations shift spending towards health and care, where Baumol's cost disease bites hardest (see Aging populations). The energy transition requires large investments that replace existing capacity rather than adding to output at first, which may weigh on measured productivity for a time even if it pays off later. Fragmenting trade, as countries move supply chains for security reasons, can undo some of the specialisation gains that Adam Smith described.

Policy can help at the margins: investment in research, skills and infrastructure; competition rules that let new firms challenge old ones; planning systems that let homes and offices be built where productive firms want to grow; and support that helps smaller firms adopt proven methods. Productivity is rarely raised by one big reform. It is raised by many small frictions being removed. The countries that remove them fastest will likely capture most of what the next general-purpose technology has to offer.

The core idea

Productivity is output per hour (labour productivity) or output beyond what inputs explain (total factor productivity). It rises through specialisation, technology, management and reallocation. General-purpose technologies raise it only after organisations reorganise around them, which takes decades.

The numbers worth knowing
  • At 2% a year, output per hour doubles in about 35 years; at 1%, about 70.
  • US productivity growth roughly halved after the mid-2000s.
  • Smith's pin workshop: about 48,000 pins a day from ten workers.
The common mistake

Confusing productivity with working longer or harder. It means producing more with the same or less effort, and long hours often lower it.
