Taxes. The price of the state, and the person who pays is rarely the one who writes the cheque.

Taxes are the most ordinary fact of economic life and one of the least understood. Many people believe a pay rise can push them into a higher bracket and leave them poorer. It cannot. Many believe companies pay corporate tax. In the end, only people do. This file explains what taxes are, how the main ones actually work, why every tax changes behaviour, and why the arguments about who should pay more are never settled by arithmetic alone.

Question 1 · What is it?

A compulsory payment with nothing promised in return

A tax is different from a price. You pay a price to get something. You pay a tax because the law says so, and what you get back is decided by others.

Chapter 01

Older than money, almost

The earliest written records in Mesopotamia and Egypt are largely accounts of grain, cattle and labour owed to temples and kings. Farmers along the Nile paid a share of the harvest, and officials measured the river's flood each year to estimate what the harvest, and so the tax, would be. Rome taxed its provinces and often sold the right to collect to private contractors, the "publicans" of the Bible, who kept whatever they squeezed out above the agreed sum.

Across all of this history a tax has had the same three features. It is compulsory, backed by the power of the state. It is unrequited, a word economists use to mean you get nothing specific in exchange: paying more income tax does not buy you a better road. And it funds things that are hard to sell one by one, such as defence, courts and public health. That makes a tax different from a fee, such as the charge for a passport, which pays for a specific service.

Every tax is a decision about who gives up what, enforced by law. That is why the history of taxation is so closely tied to the history of political rights. The English Magna Carta of 1215 limited the king's power to raise certain levies without the consent of his barons. Five and a half centuries later, American colonists made the same point with the slogan "no taxation without representation".

Chapter 03

How much countries take

The usual comparison is total tax revenue as a share of GDP, the country's yearly income. Across the rich countries of the OECD the average was about a third of GDP in the early 2020s. France and Denmark were near the top, at well over 40%. The United States was around a quarter. Mexico collected less than a fifth. Many low-income countries collect under 15%, which is often cited as roughly the minimum a state needs to fund basic services reliably.

The Gulf states are a special case. Oil and gas revenue, which goes to the state through ownership rather than taxation, long made broad taxes unnecessary. That has been changing. Saudi Arabia and the UAE introduced VAT in 2018, Saudi Arabia tripled its rate to 15% in 2020, and the UAE brought in a 9% corporate tax in 2023. A state that does not need to tax its citizens also needs their consent less, which is one reason political scientists study tax systems as closely as elections.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Brackets, chains of invoices, and the hidden payer

The mechanics of a tax decide who really bears it and how much harm it does. Three mechanisms explain most of what people get wrong.

Chapter 04

Marginal and average rates: the bracket myth

Most income taxes are progressive: the rate rises as income rises. But it rises in slices. Each rate applies only to the income inside its own slice, called a bracket. The rate on your last unit of income is your marginal rate. Your total tax divided by your total income is your average rate, and it is always lower than the top marginal rate you reach.

An invented tax system with three brackets
  income slice          rate      someone earning 60,000 pays
  0      - 10,000        0%       0       on the first 10,000
  10,000 - 40,000       20%       6,000   on the next 30,000
  above 40,000          40%       8,000   on the last 20,000
                                  ------
                                  14,000 total

  marginal rate = 40%   (on the last unit earned)
  average rate  = 14,000 / 60,000 = about 23%

  A raise from 40,000 to 41,000 is taxed at 40% on the extra 1,000 only.
  Take-home pay still goes up by 600.

So crossing into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay in a normal income tax. The myth survives because real systems have quirks. Some benefits are withdrawn as income rises, and some allowances are clawed back. In the UK, for example, the tax-free personal allowance is gradually withdrawn above an income of 100,000 pounds, which creates an effective marginal rate of around 60% on a slice of income. Those quirks are real. They are exceptions to the rule, not the rule.

Two other shapes exist. A flat tax charges one rate on all income above an allowance; several Eastern European countries adopted them after 1990. A regressive tax takes a larger share from poorer people. Consumption taxes are often regressive when measured against yearly income, because poorer households spend almost everything they earn while richer ones save part of it.

Chapter 05

VAT: a tax collected in small pieces

Value added tax was developed in France in the early 1950s, with the official Maurice Lauré usually credited as its designer. It is now used by more than 170 countries. The United States is the only large rich economy without one; it uses state-level sales taxes instead.

The trick is in the chain. Take a loaf of bread with a 10% VAT. The farmer sells wheat to the miller for 100 and charges 10 of VAT. The miller sells flour to the baker for 200, charges 20, and deducts the 10 already paid, sending 10 to the state. The baker sells bread for 400, charges 40, deducts 20, and sends 20. In total the state receives 40, exactly 10% of the final price. But it collected it in three small pieces, and each business had a reason to demand a proper invoice from its supplier so it could claim its deduction.

That self-checking chain is why VAT is hard to evade and why it spread so widely. It is also why VAT fraud, when it happens, tends to be large and organised, using fake companies that claim refunds and disappear.

Chapter 06

Incidence: who really pays

The law names someone to hand over the money. Economists call that the legal or statutory incidence. But the burden can move. If a tax on cigarettes is collected from the manufacturer, the manufacturer raises its prices, and smokers pay much of it. The question of who ends up worse off is the economic incidence, and it depends on who can more easily walk away.

The rule of thumb is simple. The side that can adjust least bears the most. Smokers find it hard to quit, so they bear most of a tobacco tax. Landowners cannot move their land, so they bear most of a land tax. Social security contributions split "half and half" between employer and worker are, according to most studies, borne largely by workers in the long run through lower wages, because the employer budgets for the total cost of a job.

Corporate tax is the hardest case. A company is a legal arrangement, not a person. In the end the tax is paid by some mix of its shareholders (lower profits), its workers (lower wages) and its customers (higher prices). How the burden splits between them is genuinely contested, and estimates vary widely. The answer matters, because if workers bear much of it, a corporate tax is less progressive than it looks.

Chapter 07

Every tax changes behaviour

Tax something and you get less of it. Sometimes that is the goal. Tobacco and alcohol taxes aim to reduce smoking and drinking, and the evidence that higher tobacco prices cut smoking, especially among young people, is strong. A tax designed to make people pay for harm they cause to others is called a Pigouvian tax, after the economist Arthur Pigou. A carbon tax is the classic modern example: it makes the polluter pay for the damage its emissions do to everyone.

Usually, though, the behaviour change is a side effect and a cost. A tax on work can lead some people to work less. A tax on property sales can stop people moving house. Economists call the lost activity the deadweight loss: value that neither the taxpayer nor the state gets, because the deal simply never happens. The loss grows faster than the tax rate. Roughly speaking, doubling a tax rate quadruples the deadweight loss, which is why economists generally prefer broad bases and low rates to narrow bases and high rates.

At the extreme is the Laffer curve, named after the economist Arthur Laffer, who popularised it in the 1970s. At a 0% rate the state collects nothing. At 100%, nobody bothers to earn taxable income, so the state again collects nothing. Somewhere between lies a rate that raises the most. The logic is sound. The fight is over where the peak lies. Most estimates place it well above the top income tax rates in most countries today, which means cutting those rates usually loses revenue rather than raising it. But the exact location is disputed.

Chapter 08

Collection: the quiet machine

A tax is only as good as its collection. The most important invention here is withholding: employers deduct income tax from wages before workers ever see the money. Britain's Pay As You Earn system began in 1944, and the US introduced wartime withholding in 1943. Withholding made mass income taxes possible, because the state no longer had to chase millions of individuals for a lump sum at the end of the year.

Even so, there is always a tax gap: the difference between what is owed and what is collected. It is small for wages, which are reported by employers, and large for cash businesses and complex foreign income, which are not. Compliance depends far more on what the tax office can see than on how honest people feel. That is why the next big changes in tax are coming from data, as Question 5 explains.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Consent, fairness, and a contest between countries

Taxes fund the state, shape inequality, and increasingly set countries against each other in a competition for mobile money.

Chapter 09

The deal behind the state

Taxes are what we pay for civilized society.

Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. · US Supreme Court, dissenting opinion, 1927

Holmes's sentence is carved over the entrance of the US tax authority's headquarters in Washington, and it captures one side of the bargain. Courts, police, schools, roads, pensions and public health all depend on revenue. Rich countries with high tax shares also tend to have strong public services. The direction of cause is debated, but no country has become rich and stable with a state that could not collect taxes.

The other side of the bargain is consent. People accept taxes far more readily when they believe others pay their share and the money is spent well. Research on tax morale, the willingness to pay voluntarily, finds it rises with trust in government. This is why scandals about tax avoidance by the rich, such as the leaked Panama Papers in 2016, cause such anger. They damage the sense of a shared deal.

Chapter 10

Taxes and inequality

Taxes are one of the two main ways states reduce inequality. The other is spending on transfers such as pensions and benefits. In most rich countries, the inequality of income after taxes and transfers is far lower than before them. Much of that reduction comes from the spending side, not from taxes alone. A country can have a progressive income tax and still redistribute little, or a fairly flat tax system and redistribute a lot through generous universal services, as the Nordic countries do.

Wealth is harder to tax than income. It is easy to hide, hard to value and mobile. The number of OECD countries with a yearly net wealth tax fell from about a dozen in 1990 to a handful by the late 2010s, and France replaced its broad wealth tax with a narrower tax on property in 2018. Meanwhile much of the income of the very rich comes as capital gains, which are often taxed only when an asset is sold, and sometimes not at all if it is passed on at death. The very top can face lower effective tax rates than people on ordinary salaries, a finding that drives much of today's debate (see Inequality).

Chapter 11

Profits on the move: havens and the global minimum tax

A company that sells software in Germany can book the profit in a country with a lower rate, by placing its patents and brands there and charging its German arm fees to use them. This is profit shifting. It is usually legal, which makes it avoidance rather than evasion. Avoidance uses the rules; evasion breaks them, for example by hiding income. For decades small jurisdictions with very low corporate taxes attracted huge paper profits this way, while the real work happened elsewhere.

In 2021 around 140 countries agreed, through the OECD, on a global minimum corporate tax of 15% for large multinationals. The rules began to take effect in the EU and many other countries from 2024. The United States did not adopt them, and in 2025 the G7 agreed to exempt US-based groups from key parts of the system after American objections. The agreement showed that countries can cooperate on tax, and also how fragile that cooperation is.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

No perfect tax, only different trade-offs

Every tax trades off efficiency, fairness, simplicity and how much it raises. The question is which weaknesses a society is willing to accept.

Chapter 13

What a good tax system looks like

In 1776 Adam Smith set out four maxims for taxation in The Wealth of Nations, and they are still quoted in textbooks (see The Wealth of Nations). Taxes should be in proportion to ability to pay. They should be certain, not arbitrary. They should be convenient to pay. And they should cost as little as possible to collect beyond what they raise. Modern economists add a fifth: they should distort behaviour as little as possible.

Those goals pull against each other. The fairest tax might be complex. The simplest might be regressive. The most efficient, a tax on land, is among the least popular. Tax design is the art of choosing which problems to live with. Most experts agree on a few general directions: broad bases with few exemptions, more weight on land and on pollution, and fewer special breaks, which mostly benefit those who can afford advisers. Where they disagree, often fiercely, is on how progressive the system should be. That is a question of values that evidence can inform but not settle.

Chapter 15

The pressures that will force change anyway

Two slower forces will reshape tax systems whatever politicians prefer. The first is ageing. Most rich countries rely heavily on taxes on wages to fund pensions and health. As the share of workers shrinks and the share of retirees grows, either rates on work rise, or more of the burden shifts to consumption, property and the wealth older people hold. That shift has already begun in several countries (see Aging populations).

The second is the changing shape of income. If more value is created by software, data and automated systems, and less by wages, a tax system built on wages will collect a smaller share of the economy. Proposals for "robot taxes" have been floated, but no country has adopted a serious one, partly because a robot is hard to define. The likelier path is heavier taxes on capital income and on company profits, which brings back all the problems of Chapter 11. Whatever happens, the basic question will not change: who gives up what, and do they accept it? See How work may change because of AI for the labour side.

The core idea

A tax is a compulsory, unrequited payment. Its real burden falls on whoever can adjust least, not on whoever the law names. Every tax changes behaviour, and the cost of that grows fast with the rate, so broad bases and low rates usually beat narrow bases and high rates.

The numbers worth knowing
  • OECD average tax take: about one third of GDP; the US about a quarter; France and Denmark over 40% (early 2020s).
  • VAT is used by more than 170 countries.
  • Global minimum corporate tax: 15%, agreed in 2021.
The common mistake

Believing a raise can push you into a higher bracket and cut your pay. The higher rate applies only to the income inside the new bracket, so take-home pay still rises, apart from special quirks like benefit withdrawal.
