Economic development. Why some countries escaped poverty in a lifetime, and others are still waiting.

For almost all of human history, nearly everyone was poor. Then, over two centuries, a minority of countries became rich, and the gap between them and the rest grew enormous. Since the 1960s a handful of countries have closed that gap in a single generation, while others with similar starting points have barely moved. Why? Economists have offered geography, culture, institutions, education, trade and aid as answers. This file explains what each explanation gets right, what the successful countries actually did, and why the old route to prosperity may be narrowing.

Question 1 · What is it?

Getting richer, and more than getting richer

Development means rising incomes, but it also means longer lives, more education and more choices. The two usually travel together, but not always.

Chapter 01

Two countries, one starting line

In the early 1960s South Korea was one of the poorest countries in Asia. It had been devastated by war, had few natural resources, and its income per person was similar to that of many countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Within about forty years it had become a rich industrial democracy, home to some of the world's leading makers of ships, cars, chips and phones. Many countries that were at a similar level in 1960 remain poor today.

That contrast is the central puzzle of development economics. Why can some countries multiply their income twenty-fold in two generations while others stay stuck? The question matters because the answer shapes what governments, aid agencies and investors do, and because it affects the lives of billions of people.

Chapter 02

Measuring development

The simplest measure is GDP per person, usually adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP). PPP corrects for the fact that a dollar buys far more food, rent and haircuts in Delhi than in Zurich. The World Bank sorts countries into low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income groups based on income per person.

Many thinkers found income alone too narrow. The Indian economist Amartya Sen, who won the Nobel Prize in 1998, argued that development should be judged by people's real freedoms: to live long, to be educated, to take part in the life of their community. His 1999 book was titled Development as Freedom. Building on his ideas, the Pakistani economist Mahbub ul Haq led the creation of the Human Development Index (HDI) in 1990. It combines life expectancy, years of schooling and income into one score.

The two measures mostly agree, but the gaps are revealing. Some oil-rich states score far higher on income than on health and schooling. The Indian state of Kerala, with modest income, long matched much richer places on literacy and life expectancy. Income makes good lives easier, but how it is used decides how much good it does.

Chapter 03

The great divergence, and the partial catch-up

Around 1800 the gap between the richest and poorest regions of the world was modest. Most people everywhere lived close to subsistence. Industrialisation in Britain, then Western Europe and North America, opened a gap that historians call the great divergence. By the late twentieth century the richest countries had average incomes dozens of times those of the poorest.

Since around 1990 the picture has changed, mainly in Asia. China and India together lifted many hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty. The share of humanity living in extreme poverty, on the World Bank's measure, fell from more than a third in 1990 to under a tenth by the late 2010s. But progress has been uneven. By the mid-2020s a growing share of the world's extreme poor lived in sub-Saharan Africa and in countries affected by conflict, and the pandemic set progress back by several years.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Moving people to more productive work

Development is above all a change in what a country does. Economists have identified several forces that make that change possible, or block it.

Chapter 04

From fields to factories: structural transformation

In a poor country most people work on small farms, where output per worker is low. In 1954 the economist Arthur Lewis, born in Saint Lucia and later a Nobel laureate, described development as the movement of those workers into a modern sector of factories and cities, where each worker produces far more. As long as there are many underused farm workers, factories can expand for years without wages rising much. Profits are reinvested, and the modern sector grows.

This shift is called structural transformation, and every country that became rich went through it. Farm employment falls from most of the workforce to a few per cent. Manufacturing rises and then, later, services take over. The single most reliable sign that a country is developing is people leaving low-productivity farming for higher-productivity work. The process also drives urbanisation (see Urbanization).

Chapter 05

The East Asian path

Japan after 1950, then South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, then China after 1978 all followed broadly similar routes. They pushed manufacturing, and they sold to world markets rather than only to their own people. Exporting forced firms to meet global standards of quality and price, and brought in foreign technology and know-how. They had very high saving and investment rates, invested heavily in schooling, and several carried out land reforms that spread rural wealth and raised farm output early on.

The role of the state is the contested part. Korea and Taiwan did not simply let markets decide. Governments directed credit to chosen industries, protected some from imports, and set export targets, while disciplining firms that failed to meet them. This is industrial policy. Supporters see it as essential to the success. Critics point to failures elsewhere and argue that the East Asian states succeeded despite some of their interventions, thanks to stability, education and openness. Most economists now accept that some state guidance can work when it is tied to export discipline, but disagree on how easily it can be copied.

China's path since 1978 had its own features. Reforms began with farmers being allowed to sell surplus crops, spread to special economic zones open to foreign investment, and moved gradually rather than all at once. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and became the world's largest manufacturer.

Chapter 06

Institutions: the rules that decide who invests

Across the border between the US and Mexico lies a town split in two, called Nogales. The two halves share a climate, a population with similar ancestry and the same food. Yet incomes, health and public services on the Arizona side are far better than on the Sonora side. The economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson used this example in their 2012 book Why Nations Fail to argue that the main difference between rich and poor countries is their institutions: the formal and informal rules that govern property, contracts, courts and political power.

They distinguished inclusive institutions, which protect property for most people, enforce contracts and spread political power, from extractive institutions, which let a narrow elite take what others produce. In work with Simon Johnson they linked these patterns to how different colonies were settled and ruled. The three shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2024. North and South Korea, one people divided in 1945, are the starkest example: the same culture and geography, two different systems, and an enormous gap in living standards.

A balanced view

The institutions view is influential but has critics. Some argue that its historical evidence is open to other readings, and that China grew fast for decades without inclusive political institutions. Others stress that institutions also change as countries get richer, so cause and effect run in both directions. The legacy of colonialism, through borders, extractive economies and education, is debated too (see Colonialism).

Chapter 07

Geography, disease, and the resource curse

Other economists, notably Jeffrey Sachs, have stressed geography. Landlocked countries pay more to trade. Tropical climates carry a heavier burden of diseases such as malaria, which kills mostly young children and saps the energy of adults. Poor soils and irregular rains make farming risky. These do not doom a country, but they raise the cost of every step.

A surprising pattern is the resource curse. Countries rich in oil, gas or minerals have often grown more slowly than countries without them. Several things go wrong. Resource exports push up the currency, making factories and farms uncompetitive, an effect called Dutch disease after the Netherlands' experience with natural gas in the 1960s and 1970s. Resource money can fund corruption and conflict, and a state that lives off oil does not need to tax, or listen to, its citizens. But the curse is not destiny. Botswana used diamond revenues carefully and was one of the world's fastest-growing economies for decades. Norway saved its oil money in a sovereign wealth fund now worth well over a trillion dollars. Resources help or harm depending on the institutions that manage them.

Chapter 08

Health, schooling and the demographic dividend

People are the most important input. Healthy, educated workers are more productive, adopt new methods faster and start more businesses. Educating girls has especially strong effects: women with more schooling tend to have fewer, healthier children and to invest more in their education.

That links to the demographic dividend. When child deaths fall, families eventually choose to have fewer children. For a few decades, the share of working-age adults in the population rises, while the share of dependants, children and the elderly, falls. If there are jobs for those adults, income per person can jump. Part of East Asia's rapid growth came from this window. Much of Africa is entering it now. Whether it becomes a dividend or a crisis depends on creating enough productive jobs, which brings the question back to structural transformation.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lives saved, and the arguments over how to help

Development is not an abstract number. It decides whether children survive, and outsiders have argued for decades about how they can help.

Chapter 09

What development buys

The most striking change of the last sixty years is in child survival. In 1960 close to one child in five worldwide died before the age of five. By the early 2020s the figure was below one in twenty-five. Global life expectancy rose from around half a century in 1960 to over seventy. Literacy, once a minority skill in much of the world, is now the norm among young people almost everywhere.

These gains came from a mix of rising incomes, vaccines, clean water, better farming, education for girls and cheap treatments such as oral rehydration salts for diarrhoea. Rising income and better public health reinforce each other, but countries have shown that health can improve far ahead of income when governments prioritise it. That is the most hopeful lesson of the period.

Chapter 10

The aid debate

Rich countries give official development assistance, known as aid, worth over 200 billion dollars a year in the early 2020s, though several large donors, including the US and the UK, announced deep cuts in 2025. Does it work? In the 2000s the debate polarised. Jeffrey Sachs argued that large, coordinated aid could lift the poorest countries out of a "poverty trap". William Easterly, in The White Man's Burden (2006), argued that big top-down plans had repeatedly failed, and that progress came from local searchers solving specific problems. The Zambian economist Dambisa Moyo, in Dead Aid (2009), argued that aid to governments in Africa had often fed dependence and corruption.

A different approach then reshaped the field. Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer pioneered randomised controlled trials in development: testing a specific programme, such as free bed nets or teacher incentives, by comparing randomly chosen villages that got it with those that did not. They shared the Nobel Prize in 2019. The trials showed that some cheap interventions work remarkably well, that others widely believed in do little, and that simply giving poor families cash often works better than expected. Critics reply that trials answer small questions well but say little about the big changes, like industrialisation, that made countries rich. Both views have a point: aid can save lives cheaply, but no country has been aided into prosperity.

Chapter 11

The Washington Consensus and its critics

In 1989 the economist John Williamson summarised the policy advice that Washington-based institutions such as the IMF and the World Bank were giving Latin America. It included fiscal discipline, open trade, privatisation, deregulation and secure property rights. The list became known as the Washington Consensus, and through the 1980s and 1990s many developing countries adopted versions of it, often as a condition for loans.

The results were mixed. Inflation came down and some countries grew, but growth in much of Latin America and Africa disappointed, and rapid opening sometimes triggered financial crises. Meanwhile the fastest-growing economies, in East Asia, had followed their own more gradual and state-guided paths. The economist Dani Rodrik argued that there is no single recipe: the binding constraint on growth differs from country to country, and good policy starts by finding it. Most development economists today hold some version of that view.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Different routes, different prices

Every strategy that has worked somewhere has failed somewhere else. And even successful development carries costs.

Chapter 13

The costs that come with success

Even the most successful development has a price. Fast industrialisation brought severe air and water pollution in China and elsewhere. Rapid urbanisation produced slums before it produced decent housing. Inequality often rose sharply during take-off (see Inequality). Traditional ways of life, languages and communities were disrupted. And in some cases rapid growth was achieved under authoritarian governments, which raises hard questions about whether the ends justify the means.

There is also the question of the planet. The rich countries industrialised by burning coal, oil and gas freely. Today's developing countries are asked to grow while cutting emissions, a request many see as unfair given who caused most of the warming so far (see Why this is a hard problem to solve). The fairness of the climate bargain is now a central development question, and it is fought over at every international climate meeting.

Chapter 15

Debt, climate, and a more divided world

Three pressures will shape the next decade. The first is debt. After the low-rate years, many low-income countries face heavy repayments and spend more on interest than on health (see Public debt). The second is climate change, which hits poorer, hotter countries hardest while they have the least to spend on adaptation. Rich countries pledged at the COP29 summit in 2024 to raise climate finance for developing countries to 300 billion dollars a year by 2035; many developing countries called the figure far too low.

The third is geopolitics. As the US, China and Europe compete, developing countries face both risk and opportunity. Some, such as Vietnam, India, Mexico and Morocco, have gained factories as companies shift supply chains away from China. Others risk being squeezed by tariffs and falling aid. The old route up the ladder is narrower, but it has not disappeared, and the countries with stable institutions, healthy and educated workers and good links to world markets remain the best placed to climb it (see Future of the global economy).

The core idea

Development is the move of people from low-productivity to high-productivity work, alongside better health, education and freedom. It is shaped by institutions, geography, human capital and trade. Successful countries found their own mix; no single recipe works everywhere.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Extreme poverty: from more than a third of humanity in 1990 to under a tenth by the late 2010s.
  • Child deaths before age five: about 1 in 5 in 1960, under 1 in 25 in the early 2020s.
  • Africa: roughly a quarter of the world's people by 2050 (UN projection).
The common mistake

Looking for one master cause, whether culture, aid, geography or free markets. Every single-cause story has famous exceptions, and the countries that succeeded combined several ingredients.
