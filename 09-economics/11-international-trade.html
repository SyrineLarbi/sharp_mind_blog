International trade. The idea economists agree on most, and voters often trust least.

Your phone was designed in one country, uses chips from a second, a screen from a third, and was assembled in a fourth. Trade across borders is so normal that we notice it only when it stops. Economists mostly agree that trade makes countries richer as a whole. Many voters feel it made their own town poorer. Both can be true at once, and understanding why is the key to the whole subject.

Question 1 · What is it?

Buying and selling across a border

International trade is the exchange of goods, services and ideas between people and firms in different countries. Its central puzzle was solved two centuries ago, and the solution still surprises people.

Chapter 01

Wine, cloth, and a surprising answer

In 1817 the English economist David Ricardo set out a simple example. Imagine two countries, England and Portugal, making two goods, cloth and wine. In his numbers, Portugal could make both goods with less labour than England. It was better at everything. Should Portugal trade with England at all?

Ricardo's answer was yes. What matters is not who is better in absolute terms, but who gives up less to produce each good. Portugal was much better at wine and only slightly better at cloth. England was a little worse at cloth and much worse at wine. If Portugal concentrated on wine and England on cloth, and they traded, both countries could end up with more of both goods than if each made everything for itself. This is the principle of comparative advantage.

The economist Paul Samuelson was once challenged by a mathematician to name one idea in the social sciences that was both true and not obvious. He chose comparative advantage. It is true as logic, and the fact that clever people keep finding it strange shows that it is not obvious.

Chapter 02

Comparative advantage in your own life

The idea is easier to see at the scale of two people. Suppose a lawyer types faster than her assistant. Should she do her own typing? No. Every hour she spends typing is an hour not spent on legal work, which earns far more. Her assistant is slower at typing, but gives up much less by doing it. Both are better off if the lawyer does law and the assistant types.

Opportunity cost: what you give up to make one more unit

The key term is opportunity cost: the value of the best thing you give up by choosing something else. Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not about skill. Every country, however poor or rich, has a comparative advantage in something, because it is always relatively less bad at some things than at others. That is why trade is not a contest that only the most productive countries can win.

Chapter 03

How big trade is, and what it looks like today

In the early 2020s, the world's exports of goods and services were worth roughly 30% of world GDP, up from under 15% in 1970. Most of that is goods: fuels, cars, machinery, electronics, food. Services, such as tourism, transport, finance, software and consulting, make up roughly a quarter and are growing faster.

The shape of trade has changed too. In Ricardo's world, countries traded finished products. Today a large share of trade is in parts and components moving along global value chains, in which each stage of making a product happens wherever it is cheapest or best done. A car may cross borders several times as parts before it is finished. Countries now specialise less in products and more in tasks. That makes trade more efficient, and also more fragile, as the pandemic showed.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The reasons, the patterns and the barriers

Comparative advantage is the foundation, but it is not the whole story. Size, distance, scale and government policy all shape who trades what with whom.

Chapter 04

Four reasons countries trade

Different strengths

Ricardo's comparative advantage, based on differences in technology and skill.

Different resources

Countries rich in land export food, those rich in oil export energy, those with many workers export labour-heavy goods. This is the Heckscher–Ohlin theory, named after two Swedish economists.

Economies of scale

Many products get cheaper the more you make. A world market lets one factory serve many countries, so costs fall for everyone.

Love of variety

Consumers like choice. Germany exports cars to France and imports French cars too, because people want different models.

The last two ideas explain a pattern the older theories could not: most trade happens between similar rich countries, often in similar products. Paul Krugman built them into what became known as new trade theory, work recognised with a Nobel prize in 2008. It also explains why industries cluster, why one region becomes the centre of aircraft or semiconductors, and why those positions, once won, are hard to dislodge.

Chapter 05

Gravity: size and distance still rule

One of the most reliable findings in economics is surprisingly simple. Trade between two countries rises with the size of their economies and falls with the distance between them, much as gravity depends on mass and distance. The Dutch economist Jan Tinbergen used this gravity model in the 1960s, and it has held up across decades of data.

That is why the European Union's biggest trading partners are largely each other, why Canada and Mexico trade most with the United States, and why a border between two otherwise similar places still reduces trade a lot. Despite cheap shipping and the internet, distance, language and borders still matter enormously. The world is not flat. It is merely less steep than it used to be.

Chapter 06

The tools of protection, and who really pays a tariff

Governments can restrict trade in several ways.

ToolWhat it doesExample
TariffA tax on imported goods, collected at the borderA 25% duty on imported steel
QuotaA limit on the quantity that can be importedA cap on tonnes of sugar each year
SubsidyGovernment money that lowers a domestic producer's costsSupport for farmers or chip factories
Standards and rulesProduct, safety or health rules that are hard for foreigners to meetFood safety rules, licensing requirements
Export controlsBans on selling certain goods abroadLimits on advanced chip technology

A tariff is often described as a tax "paid by the foreign country". That is mostly wrong. The tax is paid at the border by the importing firm, which usually passes much of it on to its customers. Foreign exporters may cut their prices a little to stay competitive, so they bear part of the cost. Studies of the American tariffs of 2018 and 2019 found that almost all of the cost was passed on to American buyers. How the burden splits depends on the product and the market, but the idea that a tariff is free for the country imposing it does not survive the evidence.

Chapter 07

The rules of the game: from GATT to the WTO

After the Second World War, governments built a system to stop the tariff wars of the 1930s from happening again. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was signed in 1947. Through a series of negotiating rounds, average tariffs on industrial goods in rich countries fell from double digits to a few percent. In 1995 GATT became the World Trade Organization (WTO), which by the mid-2020s had about 165 members.

Two principles sit at the core. The first is most favoured nation: a country must offer every WTO member the same terms it offers its best trading partner, with exceptions for free trade agreements. The second is national treatment: once imported goods have entered, they must be treated like domestic goods. The WTO also runs a system for settling disputes.

That system has been weakened. Its appeals court, the Appellate Body, has been unable to work since December 2019, because the United States has blocked the appointment of new judges, arguing the body overstepped its role. Meanwhile, countries have signed many regional deals instead, such as the EU's single market, the US–Mexico–Canada Agreement, and the Asia-Pacific trade pacts known as RCEP and CPTPP.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Prosperity, painful shocks and political backlash

Trade has helped lift hundreds of millions of people out of poverty. It has also hollowed out particular towns. Both facts drive politics today.

Chapter 08

1930: when the world closed its doors

In June 1930 the United States passed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act, raising duties on thousands of imported goods. More than a thousand economists signed a petition urging President Hoover to veto it. He signed it anyway. Other countries retaliated with tariffs of their own.

World trade shrank by around two-thirds in value between 1929 and the early 1930s. Most of that fall came from the Depression itself and from collapsing prices, so tariffs were not the main cause. But the spiral of retaliation made a bad situation worse and poisoned relations between countries. The lesson the post-war generation drew was that trade wars are easy to start and very hard to stop, because every country's protection invites the next one's. That lesson built the GATT.

Chapter 09

The China shock

China joined the WTO in December 2001. Over the following decade its exports exploded, and it became the world's largest manufacturer. For consumers in rich countries, this meant cheaper clothing, furniture, electronics and toys. For hundreds of millions of Chinese people, it meant factory jobs that paid far more than farming.

In 2013 the economists David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson published research on what happened to American places that made the goods China began exporting. The answer was painful. Jobs lost in those places were not quickly replaced. Wages stayed low, more people left the workforce, and the effects lasted more than a decade. Later work with colleagues estimated that competition from China cost the US around two million jobs in the 2000s, counting knock-on effects.

That is a small number next to the size of the American labour market, which gains and loses millions of jobs each year. But the losses were concentrated in specific towns, while the gains, in lower prices, were spread thinly across everyone. Concentrated pain and spread-out benefit is the political arithmetic of trade. It goes a long way to explaining the backlash of the 2010s and 2020s.

Chapter 10

Trade deficits: what they do and do not mean

A country has a trade deficit when it imports more than it exports. It sounds like losing. Often it is not. A trade deficit is the mirror image of foreigners investing in that country. If Americans buy more from the world than they sell, the extra dollars return as foreign purchases of American shares, bonds, property and companies.

At the level of a whole economy, the trade balance is mainly set by how much a country saves compared with how much it invests. A country that invests more than it saves must borrow the difference from abroad, and that shows up as a deficit. This is why tariffs on their own rarely shrink a country's overall trade deficit: they change what is imported, not the gap between saving and investment. A bilateral deficit with one partner means even less, much as you run a "deficit" with your grocer and a "surplus" with your employer.

Deficits can still matter. A country that borrows from abroad for years builds up foreign debts, and a sudden loss of foreign confidence can trigger a crisis. And the industries that shrink are real. In 2025 the United States raised tariffs on most of its trading partners to levels not seen in roughly ninety years, partly with the stated aim of cutting its deficit. Several of the measures were challenged in US courts. Their long-run effects were still being debated in 2026.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A bigger pie, unevenly sliced

The gains from trade are real and large in total. The costs are real too, and they fall on particular people.

Chapter 12

The arguments for protection that deserve a hearing

Most economists favour open trade, but they do not dismiss every argument for protection. Three are taken seriously.

Infant industries. A new industry may need temporary protection until it is big enough to compete. Alexander Hamilton argued this for the young United States in 1791. South Korea and Taiwan used forms of protection while building their industries, though they also pushed firms hard to export. Many other countries tried and got protected, inefficient industries instead. The record is mixed, and the hard part is making protection temporary.

National security. A country may reasonably want its own supply of weapons, critical medicines or advanced chips, even at a higher price. The difficulty is that "security" can be stretched to cover almost anything.

Adjustment. Even if open trade is better in the long run, sudden shocks can devastate regions. The serious answer is usually not to block trade but to help the people and places it hurts, through retraining, regional investment and income support. Most countries have done this poorly, which is part of why trade lost public support.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Security, carbon and code

Trade policy is being rewritten around three new concerns: geopolitics, climate and digital services. Sorted by how confident anyone can be.

Chapter 14

The next frontier: services you never ship

The fastest-growing part of trade does not go in containers. A radiologist in one country reads scans from another. A software team spread across three continents builds one product. A video call replaces a business trip. Services trade is harder to measure and harder to tax, and the rules for it are far less developed than the rules for goods.

As translation tools and remote work improve, more service jobs could become tradable. That could bring the gains and the pain of trade to office workers in the way factory workers felt them in the 2000s. How fast this happens is uncertain. What is fairly clear is that the next big trade debates will be about data, digital taxes and services, not only about steel and soybeans. The wider story of how all these flows connect the world is the subject of the next file, Globalization.

The core idea

Countries gain from trade by specialising according to comparative advantage, which depends on opportunity cost, not on being best. Trade makes a country richer as a whole, but the gains are spread thinly while the losses are concentrated on particular industries and places.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World exports are roughly 30% of world GDP, up from under 15% in 1970.
  • The WTO, founded in 1995, has about 165 members.
  • China joined the WTO in 2001.
The common mistake

Thinking a tariff is paid by the foreign country. It is paid at the border by the importer, and most of the cost usually reaches domestic buyers.

Can you explain why a country that is better at making everything still gains from trade? If you can, you have understood comparative advantage.
