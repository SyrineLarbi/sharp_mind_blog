Sharp Mind · Economics · Topic 16 of 20

Inequality. Falling across the world, rising inside many countries, and argued about everywhere.

Two statements are both true, and most arguments about inequality pick only one. Over the last forty years, the gap between the average person in rich and poor countries narrowed dramatically, as hundreds of millions of people in Asia left extreme poverty. Over the same years, in many rich countries, a small group at the top pulled far away from everyone else. To discuss inequality well you need to know which kind someone means, how it is measured, what drives it, and where the evidence is solid and where it is still being fought over.

Question 1 · What is it?

Unequal what, between whom?

"Inequality" covers several different gaps. Mixing them up is the most common reason arguments about it go nowhere.

Chapter 01

Income, wealth and opportunity

Imagine two neighbours. One is a surgeon of forty who earns a high salary but still has a large mortgage. The other is a retired farmer of seventy who earns little but owns land worth a fortune. Who is better off? It depends on what you measure. Income is a flow: what comes in each year from wages, profits, rents, interest and benefits. Wealth is a stock: what you own minus what you owe, at a moment in time. The two are related but far from the same.

Wealth is always much more unequal than income. Many people own almost nothing, or owe more than they own, while a few own enormous amounts. According to the World Inequality Report of 2022, the richest tenth of the world's adults owned roughly three quarters of all personal wealth, while the poorest half owned about 2%. Income was less concentrated, with the top tenth receiving about half.

The third kind is inequality of opportunity: how much your prospects depend on things you did not choose, such as your parents' income, your gender, your ethnicity or where you were born. Many people who accept large differences in outcomes object strongly to large differences in opportunity. Most real disagreements about inequality are disagreements about which of these three matters most.

Chapter 02

How it is measured

The best-known measure is the Gini coefficient, named after the Italian statistician Corrado Gini. It runs from 0, where everyone has exactly the same, to 1, where one person has everything. It is often shown as a percentage. Here is how it is built.

For income after taxes and benefits, rich countries in the 2010s and early 2020s ranged from roughly 0.25–0.28 in the Nordic countries to around 0.35 in the UK and about 0.39 in the United States. South Africa, at above 0.6, is usually listed as the most unequal large country. The Gini has a weakness: very different distributions can give the same number. So researchers also use simpler measures. The top 1% share asks what fraction of all income goes to the richest hundredth. The Palma ratio compares the income of the top 10% with that of the bottom 40%.

Chapter 03

The world picture: an elephant

Two kinds of inequality must be kept apart. Between countries: how the average Indian compares with the average American. Within countries: how rich and poor Americans compare with each other. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the gap between countries widened, as Western Europe and North America industrialised and much of the world did not. From around 1990 it began to close, mainly because of fast growth in China, India and other parts of Asia. The share of humanity in extreme poverty fell from more than a third in 1990 to under a tenth by the late 2010s.

In 2013 the economists Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanovic drew a famous chart of how incomes changed across the whole world's distribution between 1988 and 2008. Its shape looked like an elephant. The body rose high in the middle, where the new Asian middle classes saw huge gains. The trunk rose at the very top, where the global rich gained too. The dip in between, around the trunk's base, was the lower middle classes of rich countries, whose incomes barely moved.

That one picture explains much of the politics of the last decade. The winners of globalisation were mostly in Asia and at the very top. Its relative losers were concentrated in the old industrial regions of rich countries (see Globalization). Later research has refined the chart, but its broad message has held up.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Markets, technology, institutions and inheritance

Inequality does not have one cause. Several forces push it up or down, and their balance differs from country to country.

Chapter 04

The race between education and technology

The most widely accepted explanation for rising wage gaps is about skills. From the 1980s onward, computers and new production methods raised demand for workers who could use them: engineers, analysts, managers. They reduced demand for routine work, in factories and in offices, that machines could do. If the supply of skilled workers does not keep up, their pay rises relative to everyone else's. The economists Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz, in a 2008 book, described this as a race between education and technology. In the mid-twentieth century the US expanded education fast and wage gaps narrowed. When education growth slowed, technology pulled ahead and gaps widened.

A refinement, associated with David Autor and others, is job polarisation. Demand grew at both ends: for well-paid professional work and for low-paid service work that is hard to automate, such as care and cleaning. It shrank in the middle, for routine clerical and factory jobs. That hollowing helps explain why many middle-income workers felt left behind even in growing economies.

Chapter 05

Winner takes all

In 1981 the economist Sherwin Rosen published a paper called "The Economics of Superstars". His point was that some markets let the best performer serve almost everyone. A century ago, a good singer could fill one theatre a night. Recording, broadcasting and now streaming let the top singer reach hundreds of millions, while the tenth-best earns a tiny fraction as much. Small differences in talent, or luck, produce huge differences in income.

The same logic now applies far beyond entertainment. A software product, a platform or a brand can be sold worldwide at almost no extra cost per customer. The leading firm in a market can earn much of its profit, and pay its staff and owners accordingly. Scale magnifies small advantages into large fortunes. Research has found that a large share of the rise in wage inequality in several countries comes from differences between firms, with workers at the most productive firms pulling ahead, rather than only from differences within them.

Chapter 06

Institutions: the rules of the game

If technology and trade were the whole story, all rich countries would have seen the same rise in inequality. They did not. Researchers associated with the World Inequality Database, including Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, estimate that the share of pre-tax national income going to the top 1% in the United States roughly doubled between 1980 and the 2010s, to around a fifth. In France and many other continental European countries the rise was much smaller, despite access to the same technology and the same world markets.

The differences lie in institutions. Trade union membership fell further in some countries than others. Minimum wages, top tax rates, rules on executive pay, the cost and quality of education, and health systems all differ. The same global forces filter through very different national rules. That is the strongest argument that inequality is partly a political choice, not only a market outcome.

A live dispute

The size of the US rise is itself contested. Other researchers, notably Gerald Auten and David Splinter, argue that once taxes, transfers and changes in how income is reported are fully counted, the increase in the top 1% share after taxes is much smaller. Both camps agree that inequality rose in the US after 1980. They disagree about how much.

Chapter 07

Wealth, inheritance, and r greater than g

In 2013 Thomas Piketty published Capital in the Twenty-First Century, which became an unlikely bestseller. His central claim concerned wealth. When the return on capital, which he wrote as r, is larger than the growth rate of the economy, g, wealth that already exists tends to grow faster than incomes from work. Inherited fortunes then become more important over time, and a society drifts towards what he called "patrimonial capitalism", where who your parents were matters more than what you do.

Piketty's historical data, much of it collected with colleagues over many years, were widely praised. His forecast was more debated. Critics pointed out that the rich consume and divide their wealth among heirs, that returns fall as capital accumulates, and that much of the rise in wealth since 1980 came from housing, where rising prices reflect land shortages and low interest rates. Still, the role of property and inheritance in wealth inequality is now taken far more seriously than before his book. For many younger people in rich countries, whether their parents own a home now predicts their own wealth more than their salary does.

Chapter 08

Kuznets' curve, and why it broke

In 1955 the economist Simon Kuznets suggested that inequality follows an upside-down U as a country develops. It rises as people move from equal but poor farms into a growing, unequal industrial sector. It then falls as more people join that sector, education spreads and democracy pushes for redistribution. For two decades the data from rich countries seemed to fit. Inequality had fallen from the early twentieth century to the 1970s.

Then it rose again in many of them. Later research suggested the twentieth-century fall owed much to unusual shocks: two world wars and a depression that destroyed great fortunes, followed by high taxes and strong unions. Inequality does not follow an automatic path. It responds to shocks, technology and policy, which is both a warning and an opening.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Mobility, health and trust

Some inequality is the normal result of different choices, ages and luck. The debate is about when it starts to damage the things people care about.

Chapter 09

The Great Gatsby curve

In 2012 the economist Alan Krueger, then advising President Obama, presented a chart based on work by Miles Corak. On one axis was a country's income inequality. On the other was how strongly a child's income as an adult tracks their parents'. Countries with more inequality also had less mobility. Denmark, Norway and Finland sat at one end; the US, UK and Italy at the other. Krueger named it the Great Gatsby curve, after the novel about a man who reinvents himself.

The chart shows a correlation, not proof of cause. But the mechanism is easy to picture. When the rungs of a ladder are far apart, parents at the top can spend far more on their children's schooling, housing and connections, and the fall from the top is long enough that they work hard to prevent it. In the US, research led by Raj Chetty found that about 90% of children born in 1940 grew up to earn more than their parents, against about half of those born in 1980. Inequality today tends to become inequality of opportunity tomorrow. See Social mobility for the social side.

Chapter 10

Health, life expectancy and place

Richer people live longer everywhere. In a 2016 study, Chetty and colleagues found that among Americans the gap in life expectancy between the richest 1% and the poorest 1% was about 15 years for men and 10 for women. Similar, smaller gaps exist in Europe. The economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton documented rising deaths from drugs, alcohol and suicide among Americans without university degrees, which they called "deaths of despair", and linked them to the decline of stable working-class jobs and communities.

Inequality also has a geography. Prosperous cities pull ahead of former industrial towns, and the divide shows up in health, house prices and voting (see Geographic inequality). Whether inequality itself harms health, beyond the effects of poverty, is more contested. The 2009 book The Spirit Level by Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett argued that more unequal rich countries do worse on many social problems. Critics questioned the choice of countries and the methods. The debate continues.

Chapter 11

Growth, trust and politics

Economists once assumed a trade-off: more equality, less growth. Research since the 2010s, including work by IMF economists Jonathan Ostry and Andrew Berg, has suggested a more complicated picture: high inequality was associated with shorter periods of sustained growth, and moderate redistribution did not seem to harm growth on average. These are statistical associations across countries, and the issue is not settled.

The political effects worry many observers more. Where wealth is highly concentrated, it can buy influence over the rules, through lobbying, campaign finance and media ownership. People who feel the system no longer works for them lose trust in institutions. Economic inequality can turn into political inequality, and political scientists study how that cycle can feed itself.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

How much is useful, and how much is harmful

Almost no one wants perfect equality, and almost no one defends unlimited inequality. The argument is about where between them, and why.

Chapter 12

The case for some inequality, and the costs of a lot

What inequality can do

Incentives. Higher pay for scarce skills and risky ventures rewards effort, training and invention. Signals. Pay gaps tell people which skills the economy needs. Saving and investment. Those with more can save more, funding new businesses. Freedom. Many people value the right to keep what they earn and to choose different paths, and accept unequal results as the price.

What too much inequality can cost

Lost talent. Gifted children in poor families never get the chance to develop. Weaker demand. The rich spend a smaller share of income, which can slow the economy. Capture. Concentrated wealth can bend the rules in its favour. Social strain. Lower trust, resentment and polarisation. Health. Large and growing gaps in life expectancy.

Chapter 13

Poverty is not the same as inequality

A society can become more unequal while poverty falls, if everyone gets richer but the rich get richer fastest. China after 1980 is the largest example: inequality rose sharply as hundreds of millions escaped poverty. A society can also become more equal while everyone gets poorer, as in a deep economic collapse. Being clear about which one you care about prevents half the arguments.

Policy choices follow from that clarity. If the concern is poverty, the tools are growth, jobs, targeted benefits and public services. If the concern is the gap itself, the tools include progressive taxes on income and wealth, rules on pay, and limits on how money enters politics. If the concern is opportunity, the tools are early childhood support, good schools in poor areas and fair access to universities and jobs. Most countries use a mix, and the balance between them is a question of values as much as of evidence.

Chapter 15

The policy experiments to watch

Several ideas are being tested rather than just debated. Basic income, a regular cash payment with no conditions, has been tried in pilots. Finland's two-year trial from 2017 gave unemployed people a monthly payment. Participants reported better wellbeing, while the effect on employment was small. Larger cash-transfer studies in Kenya are tracking effects over a decade. None yet answers whether a full national scheme is affordable or wise.

Other directions include predistribution, which tries to make market incomes less unequal before taxes, through minimum wages, training and stronger bargaining power for workers, and baby bonds, accounts seeded by the state at birth for poorer children. On the tax side, the global minimum corporate tax and proposals for a coordinated wealth tax on billionaires continue to advance slowly (see Taxes). The honest summary is that no country has found a painless formula, but the evidence base is far richer than it was twenty years ago.

The core idea

Inequality comes in three forms: income, wealth and opportunity, and it can be measured between or within countries. Since about 1990 the between-country gap has narrowed while within-country gaps rose in many places. Technology, scale, institutions and inheritance drive it, and national rules decide how much.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Gini of disposable income: about 0.25–0.28 in the Nordics, 0.39 in the US, above 0.6 in South Africa.
  • Top tenth of adults worldwide: about three quarters of wealth (World Inequality Report 2022).
  • US children out-earning their parents: about 90% of those born in 1940, about 50% of those born in 1980.
The common mistake

Treating inequality and poverty as the same thing. Poverty can fall while inequality rises, as it did in China, and the right policies differ depending on which you care about.
