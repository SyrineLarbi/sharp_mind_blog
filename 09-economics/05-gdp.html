GDP. The most important number in economics, and the most misunderstood.

Gross domestic product is the total value of what a country produces in a year. It decides whether the news says the economy is growing or in recession. It sets how much a country can borrow, what it pays into international bodies, and how it ranks against its neighbours. Yet the number is less than a century old, it was never designed to measure happiness, and it ignores some of the work that matters most. Understanding what GDP counts, and what it does not, lets you read economic news without being fooled in either direction.

Question 1 · What is it?

One number for everything an economy makes

GDP adds up the market value of all the final goods and services produced inside a country over a period, usually a quarter or a year.

Chapter 01

Born in a depression, raised in a war

When the Great Depression struck in 1929, the US government had no reliable way of knowing how large the collapse was. There were scattered figures for steel output, freight carried and share prices, but no single measure of the whole economy. In 1932 the Senate asked for one. The job went to a young economist, Simon Kuznets, who presented estimates of national income to Congress in 1934. They showed that national income had fallen by about half between 1929 and 1932.

Kuznets himself warned against reading too much into his creation.

The welfare of a nation can, therefore, scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income as defined above.

Simon Kuznets · report to the US Congress · 1934

The Second World War then made the numbers essential. Governments needed to know how much the economy could produce for the war effort without starving civilians. Britain's accounts were built in the same years by economists working with John Maynard Keynes, including Richard Stone, who later won a Nobel prize for the work. After the war, the United Nations published a common System of National Accounts in 1953 so that countries could measure in the same way. It has been updated several times since. GDP was invented to manage depressions and wars, not to measure how good life is, and that origin explains many of its blind spots.

Chapter 02

The definition, word by word

Each word in the standard definition does work.

WordWhat it meansExample
Market valueThings are valued at the prices they sell for, so apples and haircuts can be added upA 30-euro haircut counts as 30 euros
FinalOnly goods sold to their end user count, to avoid counting the same thing twiceBread counts; the flour inside it does not, separately
Goods and servicesPhysical things and work done for people both countCars, but also teaching, banking and nursing
ProducedOnly new production counts, not resalesSelling a used car adds only the dealer's service, not the car
DomesticInside the country's borders, whoever owns the factoryA Japanese-owned car plant in England adds to UK GDP
PeriodA quarter or a year"GDP grew 0.3% in the second quarter"

The word "final" is the one people miss. If a farmer sells wheat to a miller for 10, the miller sells flour to a baker for 25, and the baker sells bread for 60, the economy has produced 60 of value, not 95. Another way to see it is to add only the value each step adds: 10 by the farmer, 15 by the miller, 35 by the baker. That also comes to 60. This value added method is how statisticians avoid double counting.

Chapter 03

GDP's cousins: GNI and GDP per person

"Domestic" means GDP counts what is produced inside the country. Gross national income (GNI) counts the income earned by a country's residents, wherever it comes from. For most countries the two are close. For some they differ a lot. Ireland hosts the European headquarters of many large multinational firms, and much of the profit counted in its GDP flows out to foreign owners. Ireland therefore publishes its own adjusted measure of national income, which is much smaller than its GDP.

The most useful version for comparing living standards is GDP per person (or per capita): GDP divided by the population. China's total GDP is far larger than Switzerland's, but the average Swiss resident has a much higher income. Total GDP tells you about economic weight; GDP per person tells you about average prosperity. Neither tells you how that income is shared, which is the subject of Inequality.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three ways of counting that should give one answer

Every euro spent is a euro earned by someone, and it pays for something produced. So GDP can be measured from spending, from income or from production.

Chapter 04

Spending, income and output

The spending approach

Add up what everyone spends on final goods and services: households' consumption, businesses' investment, government purchases, and exports minus imports. Economists write it as GDP = C + I + G + (X − M).

The income approach

Add up all the income earned from production: wages and salaries, company profits, rents and interest, plus some taxes on production. This shows how the value created is divided between workers and owners.

The output approach

Add up the value added by every industry: farming, manufacturing, construction, retail, finance, health and so on. This shows which sectors drive the economy.

In theory, all three give the same number. In practice they come from different surveys and tax records, so they never quite match, and statisticians publish a "statistical discrepancy" to close the gap. In rich countries, household consumption is usually the largest part of spending, often between about half and two thirds of GDP. That is why economists watch consumer confidence and retail sales so closely: when households stop spending, GDP usually follows.

Note that imports are subtracted. They are not bad for the economy in themselves. They are taken out because the consumption and investment figures already include imported goods, which were not produced at home. This is a common source of confusion in arguments about trade, covered in International trade.

Chapter 05

Nominal and real GDP

If an economy produces exactly the same goods as last year but every price rises 5%, nominal GDP grows 5%. Nobody is better off. To separate genuine growth from price rises, statisticians calculate real GDP, which values output at constant prices. The ratio between the two is the GDP deflator, a broad measure of inflation across everything the economy produces.

Almost every headline growth figure is real. When you read that "the economy grew 2% last year", it means real GDP. When politicians compare the size of debt or spending to GDP, they usually use nominal figures, since debts are also nominal. Mixing the two is one of the most common errors in economic arguments.

Chapter 06

Comparing countries: the hamburger problem

To compare economies you need a common currency, usually US dollars. But exchange rates move every day and do not reflect what money buys locally. A haircut in Delhi costs a fraction of one in Zurich, though it is the same service. Converting at market exchange rates makes poorer countries look poorer than their residents feel.

Economists therefore also compare GDP at purchasing power parity (PPP): adjusting for what the same basket of goods costs in each country. In 1986 The Economist magazine popularised the idea with a light-hearted "Big Mac index", comparing burger prices around the world. Official PPP figures come from a much larger price survey, the International Comparison Program, coordinated by the World Bank.

The choice changes rankings. At market exchange rates, the US economy is clearly the largest in the world. On a PPP basis, China's economy has been estimated to be larger than the US economy since the mid-2010s. Both statements are true; they answer different questions. Market rates tell you what an economy can buy on world markets, such as oil or aircraft. PPP tells you about the volume of goods and services people actually consume.

Chapter 07

Estimates, revisions and surprises

GDP is not counted like votes. It is estimated, from surveys, tax data and models, and the first estimate is published within weeks of a quarter ending, long before all the data arrive. Later revisions can change the picture, sometimes turning reported growth into a fall or the reverse.

Occasionally the changes are dramatic. In 2014 Nigeria updated the base year of its accounts, from 1990 to 2010, to include industries that had barely existed before, such as mobile phones and its large film industry. Its measured GDP nearly doubled overnight, and it overtook South Africa as Africa's largest economy. Nothing real had changed; the measuring tape had. In 2016 Ireland reported that its GDP had grown by about 26% in 2015, mainly because multinational firms had moved valuable intellectual property, such as patents, onto Irish books. The economist Paul Krugman mocked it as "leprechaun economics". When a GDP figure looks astonishing, ask first what changed in the measurement.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The denominator of almost everything

GDP is used to judge whether an economy is healthy, to size debts and budgets, and to compare nations. Many rules and targets are written as a percentage of it.

Chapter 08

A ruler for debts, deficits and promises

A debt of one trillion dollars sounds enormous. Whether it is depends on the size of the economy that must carry it. That is why government debt and deficits are almost always reported as a percentage of GDP. The EU's rules, set out in the 1992 Maastricht Treaty, use reference values of a deficit of 3% of GDP and debt of 60% of GDP. NATO members set a target in 2014 of spending 2% of GDP on defence, and in 2025 agreed to a higher goal of 5% by 2035, a figure that includes some wider security-related spending. Aid targets, climate finance pledges and health budgets are also often expressed this way.

This makes GDP politically powerful. If GDP is mismeasured, every ratio built on it is wrong too. Greece's crisis in 2009 began when a new government revealed that deficit figures had been badly understated, and part of the problem was the reliability of the official statistics. See Public debt.

Chapter 09

The size of the world, in rough numbers

According to IMF estimates, the world economy produced roughly 110 trillion US dollars of output in 2024 at market exchange rates. The United States accounted for about a quarter of that, close to 30 trillion dollars, and China for about 18 trillion. The euro area together was somewhat smaller than China at market rates. These figures shift every year with growth and exchange rates, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

The gaps per person are wider still. Average income per person in the richest countries is more than fifty times that of the poorest, even after adjusting for prices. Those gaps are the starting point for Economic development, and how countries close them is the subject of Economic growth.

Chapter 10

GDP per person tracks a lot of what people care about

Critics are right that GDP is not well-being. But it is strongly correlated with things almost everyone values. Countries with higher GDP per person tend to have longer life expectancy, lower child mortality, more years of schooling and better access to clean water and electricity. The relationship is not perfect, and above a certain level of income the gains in life expectancy flatten out. Some countries, such as Costa Rica, achieve health outcomes close to much richer ones; the United States has lower life expectancy than many countries with lower GDP per person.

Still, for poorer countries, faster GDP growth is usually the most reliable path to better lives, because it pays for schools, hospitals and infrastructure. The debate about GDP's limits is mostly a debate among rich countries, where more output no longer brings the same improvement.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant measure of output, and a poor measure of life

GDP does one job very well. The problems start when it is asked to do others.

Chapter 11

What GDP gets right and what it misses

In March 1968, at the University of Kansas, the US senator Robert F. Kennedy gave a speech that is still the most quoted criticism of national income figures. He listed what they counted, from cigarette advertising to prisons, and what they left out, from the health of children to the joy of their play. He ended with this line.

It measures everything, in short, except that which makes life worthwhile.

There are also boundary questions with no right answer. Since 2014, EU rules have asked countries to include estimates of illegal activity, such as drug dealing, where it involves voluntary market transactions. This raised measured GDP slightly in several countries and caused some public surprise. The point was consistency: in some EU countries prostitution is legal and already counted, in others it is not.

Chapter 12

The alternatives that have been tried

In 1990 the United Nations Development Programme launched the Human Development Index (HDI), designed by the Pakistani economist Mahbub ul Haq with input from Amartya Sen. It combines income with life expectancy and education. Bhutan has promoted "Gross National Happiness" since the 1970s as its guiding goal. In 2009 a commission led by Joseph Stiglitz, Amartya Sen and Jean-Paul Fitoussi, set up at the request of France's president Nicolas Sarkozy, recommended that countries measure well-being, sustainability and the distribution of income alongside GDP. New Zealand presented a "wellbeing budget" in 2019, judging spending against a wider set of indicators.

None of these has replaced GDP. The reasons are practical. Any wider measure requires choosing weights: how much is a year of life expectancy worth compared with a thousand dollars of income? GDP avoids that choice by using market prices. The common view among statisticians is therefore a "dashboard" approach: keep GDP for what it measures well, and publish other indicators beside it rather than one combined score.

Chapter 14

Faster numbers: nowcasting

Official GDP arrives weeks after the quarter ends. Central banks and investors increasingly use nowcasting: estimating current GDP from data that arrive daily, such as card payments, electricity use, shipping movements, job advertisements and online searches. During the pandemic in 2020, when the economy changed by the week, these sources showed the collapse and the recovery far faster than official figures.

Faster data have a cost: they are noisier and can be misleading when behaviour shifts, as when online shopping suddenly replaced shop visits. They supplement the official accounts rather than replace them. The lasting lesson of GDP's history is the one Kuznets gave at the start. It is an excellent measure of how much an economy produces, and a poor guide to how well its people live. Use it for the first question and look elsewhere for the second. The core idea

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period. It can be measured from spending, income or output. Real GDP removes inflation; per person GDP shows average prosperity. It measures output well and well-being poorly.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1934: Kuznets' first US national income estimates. 1953: the first UN System of National Accounts.
  • World output was roughly 110 trillion dollars in 2024 at market exchange rates.
  • EU reference values: deficit 3% and debt 60% of GDP.
The common mistake

Treating GDP as a measure of how well people live. It ignores unpaid work, inequality and environmental damage, and it counts disasters' clean-up as output.

Test yourself

A country's GDP jumps 20% in a year while wages and jobs barely change. Name two possible explanations that involve measurement rather than real growth.

The words, in plain words
GDP
Gross domestic product: the value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a period.
Final goods
Goods sold to their end user, not used up in making something else.
Value added
The value a producer adds: its sales minus what it paid for inputs.
GNI
Gross national income: income earned by a country's residents, wherever it is produced.
GDP per capita
GDP divided by population: average output per person.
Nominal GDP
GDP at current prices, including the effect of inflation.
Real GDP
GDP at constant prices, showing the change in actual output.
GDP deflator
The price index that converts nominal GDP into real GDP.
Purchasing power parity
An exchange rate adjusted for what money buys locally.
National accounts
The full set of official statistics on a country's output, income and spending.
Human Development Index
A UN measure combining income, life expectancy and education.
Nowcasting
Estimating the economy's current state from fast, frequent data.

  3. "Cutting down a forest raises GDP. Nobody subtracts the forest."
