Supply and demand. A price is a message that millions of strangers write together.

No one decides how much bread a city needs tomorrow morning. No committee counts the eaters, orders the flour and books the bakers. And yet the shelves are almost always full, and almost never overflowing. The thing that coordinates it is a number on a label. Supply and demand is the simplest model in economics, and the most useful. It explains why prices rise and fall, why shortages appear when governments fix prices, and why a frost in Brazil changes what you pay for coffee in Paris. It also has clear limits, and knowing them is half of understanding it.

Question 1 · What is it?

Two groups of people, pulling a price in opposite directions

Buyers want to pay less. Sellers want to be paid more. The price settles where the two pulls balance, and it moves every time one of them changes its mind.

Chapter 01

Nobody is in charge of the bread

In 1776 the Scottish philosopher Adam Smith published The Wealth of Nations. Its most famous idea is that people pursuing their own interest can, through trade, end up serving each other without meaning to. He put it in a sentence that is still quoted in every first-year course.

It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.

Adam Smith · The Wealth of Nations · 1776

Smith's point was not that selfishness is good. It was that a market can coordinate millions of decisions that no single mind could plan. The baker does not know you. She does not know how many people will want bread tomorrow. But she knows what sold yesterday and at what price, and that is enough.

In 1945 the Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek sharpened the idea in an essay called "The Use of Knowledge in Society". Imagine, he wrote, that a new use for tin appears somewhere, or a tin mine closes. Tin becomes scarcer and its price rises. Every factory that uses tin now economises. None of them needs to know why. The price carries the information, compressed into a single number, to everyone who needs to act on it. That is the deepest reason economists care about prices. They are not just what you pay. They are a communication system.

Chapter 02

The two curves

The model has two halves.

Demand: how much people want to buy

At a high price, few people buy. At a low price, more do. Some switch from other products, some buy more of it, some who could not afford it now can. This is the law of demand: when the price falls, the quantity people want rises, if nothing else changes. Drawn on a graph, it slopes downward.

Supply: how much sellers want to sell

At a low price, only the cheapest producers can make a profit. At a high price, more producers join, and existing ones work overtime or open new sites. This is the law of supply: when the price rises, the quantity offered rises. On a graph, it slopes upward.

The English economist Alfred Marshall made this picture famous in his textbook of 1890. People had argued for a century about whether value comes from what things cost to make or from how much people want them. Marshall said the question was like asking which blade of a pair of scissors does the cutting. Both do. Price comes from cost and desire together.

The same idea solves an old puzzle Smith raised: why is water, which keeps us alive, cheap, while diamonds, which we do not need, are expensive? Because price reflects what the next unit is worth to you, not the total value of the thing. Water is plentiful, so the next glass is worth very little. Diamonds are scarce, so the next one is worth a lot. Economists call this thinking at the margin: decisions are about one more, or one less, not all or nothing.

Chapter 03

Moving along a curve versus moving the curve

This is the distinction that most often confuses people, and it matters.

If the price of strawberries falls and you buy more, you have moved along the demand curve. Your tastes did not change; the price did. But if a newspaper reports that strawberries prevent heart disease, you will want more strawberries at every price. The whole curve has shifted. Economists keep these separate with two phrases: a change in the quantity demanded (a move along the curve, caused by price) and a change in demand (a shift of the curve, caused by something else).

When you read that "rising prices are reducing demand", stop and ask which one is meant. Usually it is the quantity demanded falling because the price rose, not people's underlying desire changing.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Where the curves cross, and how fast they react

The meeting point of the two curves is the market price. What makes the model powerful is that you can predict which way the price will move when something changes, and roughly by how much.

Chapter 04

Equilibrium: the price that clears the market

Put the two curves on one graph and they cross at one point. At that price, the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. Economists call it the equilibrium, or the market-clearing price.

Why does the price drift towards that point? Suppose the price is too high. Sellers produce more than people buy, and unsold stock piles up. To clear it they cut prices. Suppose the price is too low. Buyers want more than exists. Shelves empty, queues form, and sellers realise they can charge more. A surplus pushes the price down; a shortage pushes it up. Nobody needs to compute the equilibrium. Thousands of small reactions find it.

Real markets are rarely exactly in equilibrium. Conditions keep changing, and prices take time to adjust. But the equilibrium tells you where the market is heading, and that is usually what you need to know.

Chapter 05

Elasticity: how strongly people react

Knowing the direction is not enough. A government planning a tax on cigarettes needs to know how much smoking will fall. A shop raising its prices needs to know how many customers will leave. The measure of how strongly quantity responds to price is called elasticity.

Demand is inelastic when people keep buying almost the same amount even as the price rises. Petrol in the short run is the classic case. You still need to drive to work this week, whatever the pump says. Salt, insulin and electricity are similar. Demand is elastic when people react strongly. If one brand of cola raises its price, many drinkers simply pick another brand.

Three things make demand more elastic. Substitutes: the easier it is to switch, the more people do. Share of budget: you notice a 20% rise in rent far more than a 20% rise in matches. And time: given a few years, people facing expensive petrol buy smaller cars or move closer to work. Demand that looks rigid in the short run often bends a lot in the long run.

Supply has elasticity too. A factory can add a night shift quickly. A new copper mine can take ten years or more from discovery to first output. That is why some prices swing violently: when demand jumps for something whose supply cannot respond quickly, all the adjustment has to happen through the price.

Chapter 06

Shocks in real life: eggs, toilet paper and coffee

The model earns its keep when you use it to read the news.

In March 2020, as the pandemic began, shops in many countries ran out of toilet paper. People did not suddenly need more of it. But fear of shortages made them buy weeks' worth at once, a sudden shift in demand. Supply could not react quickly, because factories ran at full capacity and household rolls are bulky to store and ship. Many shops chose not to raise prices and limited purchases instead. The shelves emptied and stayed empty for weeks.

Eggs in the United States show the supply side. Outbreaks of avian flu in 2022 and again in early 2025 forced farms to cull many millions of laying hens. Supply fell sharply and could not recover fast, because a new flock takes months to grow. People still wanted roughly the same number of eggs for breakfast and baking. With inelastic demand and a sudden supply cut, prices rose far more than the percentage of hens lost. That is elasticity at work.

Coffee works the same way on a global scale. Brazil and Vietnam grow a large share of the world's coffee. A frost or a drought there shifts world supply, and the price change shows up months later in cafés thousands of kilometres away.

Chapter 07

Time lags, overshooting and the pork cycle

Supply often reacts to today's price with a delay. When pig prices are high, farmers breed more pigs. But those pigs reach the market a year or so later, all at once, and the price falls. Farmers then cut back, and a year later there is a shortage and the price rises again. Economists have described this "pork cycle" or "hog cycle" since the early twentieth century.

The same pattern appears wherever building new capacity takes time: ships, oil wells, office towers, chip factories. Volume 12 describes it in shipping: rates spike, everyone orders ships, and the ships arrive into a glut (Container shipping). Long lags between decision and delivery turn a smooth model into a boom and bust. The equilibrium still exists. The market just keeps overshooting it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

What happens when someone overrules the price

The most practical use of the model is predicting the side effects of policy. Fix a price, tax a product or subsidise it, and supply and demand tell you who wins, who loses and what disappears.

Chapter 08

Price ceilings: queues instead of prices

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. Its aim is usually kind: to keep essentials affordable. The model predicts a problem. If the ceiling is below the equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer. A shortage appears, and something other than money decides who gets the good. That something is often time spent queuing, personal connections, or luck.

The United States learned this in the 1970s. Price controls on petrol were in force when oil supply was disrupted in 1973 and again in 1979. Prices could not rise to the new market level, so fuel was rationed by queuing instead. Long lines formed at petrol stations, and some states let cars fill up only on odd or even days according to the number plate. The controls were removed in 1981, and the queues did not return in later oil shocks, even when prices rose steeply.

Rent control is the most argued-about case. Capping rents helps the tenants who already have a controlled flat, often a great deal. But the model predicts less new rental housing, less maintenance, and landlords converting flats to other uses. Studies in several cities have found such effects. A study of San Francisco's 1994 expansion of rent control, for example, found that it protected existing tenants but that affected landlords reduced the supply of rental housing, which pushed up rents elsewhere in the city. Most economists are sceptical of strict rent caps. Supporters reply that tenants need protection from sudden rent rises and eviction, and that milder forms, such as limits on how fast rent can rise for a sitting tenant, avoid the worst effects. The debate is about design as much as principle.

Chapter 09

Price floors, and the minimum wage debate

A price floor is a legal minimum. Farm price supports are one example: in the 1970s and 1980s the European Community bought up surplus farm output to hold prices up, and the stockpiles became known as "butter mountains" and "wine lakes". The model predicted the surplus exactly.

The minimum wage is a price floor on labour. The simple model says that if the floor is above the market wage, employers will hire fewer workers, so some people lose jobs while others earn more. For decades that was the textbook answer. Then in 1994 the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had just raised its minimum wage, with neighbouring Pennsylvania, which had not. They found no sign that employment fell in New Jersey. Card later shared the 2021 Nobel prize partly for this kind of natural experiment.

Why might the simple model fail here? One answer is that many employers have some power to set wages, because workers cannot easily switch jobs. Economists call a market with one dominant buyer a monopsony. In that case a modest minimum wage can raise pay without cutting jobs. The research since has been large and is still debated. A fair summary is that moderate minimum wages have shown small employment effects in many studies, while very high ones relative to local wages are more likely to cost jobs. Where the line falls is contested.

Chapter 10

Who really pays a tax

When a government puts a tax on petrol, the law says the seller pays it. But who bears the cost? The model's answer is surprising: it does not matter who hands the money to the state. The burden falls on whichever side is less able to walk away. Economists call this tax incidence.

If buyers cannot easily cut back (inelastic demand), sellers pass most of the tax on in higher prices. If buyers can easily switch, sellers have to absorb most of it. This is why taxes on tobacco, alcohol and fuel raise a lot of revenue: people keep buying. It is also why such taxes are heavy on poorer households, who spend a bigger share of their income on these goods.

The same logic explains surge pricing by ride-hailing apps such as Uber. When demand jumps, on a rainy Friday night for example, the price rises. That does two things at once. It tempts more drivers onto the road, and it rations the cars that exist to the people who value the ride most. Many riders hate it, and the anger is part of the story: people judge prices by fairness, not just efficiency, which is a theme of behavioral economics. But the alternative to a higher price, in a sudden shortage, is usually no car at all.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful first answer, and an incomplete last one

The model is the right place to start almost any economic question. It is the wrong place to stop when markets have few sellers, hidden information or costs that fall on outsiders.

Chapter 12

Where the simple model breaks

The textbook model assumes many buyers and sellers, a standard product, and everyone knowing the price and quality. Real markets break these assumptions in four common ways.

Market power. A monopoly is a single seller. It does not take the market price; it chooses one, and it profits by supplying less than a competitive market would. Much of competition law exists to stop this.

Externalities. An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside the deal. A factory's smoke harms neighbours who are not paid for it. The market price is too low, so too much is produced. The standard fix is to put a price on the harm, through a tax or a permit system, so that the price tells the truth again.

Hidden information. In 1970 the economist George Akerlof described the market for used cars. Sellers know whether their car is a good one or a "lemon". Buyers do not, so they offer only an average price. Owners of good cars then refuse to sell, the average quality falls, and the market can shrink or collapse. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel prize for this work. It explains why warranties, inspections and reputations matter so much.

Odd demand. For some goods, a higher price makes people want them more, because the price itself signals status or quality. Economists call these Veblen goods, after the sociologist Thorstein Veblen, who wrote about "conspicuous consumption" in 1899. Luxury handbags are the usual example. And for networks such as social media, the value to each user depends on how many others join, which can lead to one winner taking almost all.

The honest position

None of this means supply and demand is wrong. It means it is a first approximation. Good economists start with it, then ask which assumptions fail in this particular market and how much that changes the answer.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Prices set by algorithms, for one customer at a time

The laws of supply and demand will not change. What is changing is who sets prices, how often, and how much each seller knows about each buyer. Sorted by confidence.

Chapter 14

The old debate that keeps returning: fair prices in a crisis

Every crisis revives one argument. After a hurricane, the price of bottled water, generators and hotel rooms jumps. Many US states have "price gouging" laws that limit such rises during declared emergencies. Most economists dislike these laws: high prices pull supplies into the disaster area and stop early buyers hoarding. Most of the public strongly supports them: exploiting desperate people feels wrong, and in surveys people judge price rises caused by higher costs as fair but price rises caused by higher demand as unfair.

Both sides have a point. The price system is efficient, but efficiency is not the only value people hold. The likely direction is not one side winning. It is more hybrid tools: limits on how fast prices can rise, combined with direct help for the poorest, so that the price can still send its signal while nobody is priced out of necessities. Inflation is where the same tension appears at the scale of a whole economy, covered in Volume 38 (Inflation, properly understood).

The core idea

Prices form where demand (falling as price rises) meets supply (rising as price rises). Surpluses push prices down and shortages push them up, so markets drift towards equilibrium. Prices also carry information about scarcity to people who never learn its cause.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1776: Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations. 1890: Marshall's supply and demand diagram.
  • 1973 and 1979: US petrol price controls turned oil shocks into queues.
  • 1994: Card and Krueger's New Jersey study, which reopened the minimum wage debate.
The common mistake

Confusing a change in demand (the whole curve shifts) with a change in quantity demanded (a move along the curve because the price changed). The second is not people wanting less; it is people reacting to price.

Test yourself

A government caps the price of a medicine below the market price. Using the model, can you predict what happens to the queue, to new suppliers and to who ends up getting the medicine?
