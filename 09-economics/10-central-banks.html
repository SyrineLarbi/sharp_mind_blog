Central banks. The most powerful institutions most people cannot describe.

A central bank issues the notes in your wallet, sets the interest rate behind your mortgage, and stands ready to rescue the banking system when it panics. Most are run by officials nobody elected, deliberately shielded from the politicians who appoint them. That arrangement is recent, it is contested, and it rests on a bargain worth understanding: governments gave away a power they would be tempted to misuse.

Question 1 · What is it?

The bank of the government and the bank of banks

A central bank is a public institution that manages a country's currency, its money supply and its interest rates, and that acts as banker to both the government and the commercial banks.

Chapter 01

Born to pay for a war

In 1694 the English government of King William III badly needed money to fight France. Its credit was poor. A group of merchants offered a deal: they would lend the Crown 1.2 million pounds, and in return they would be allowed to form a company with special banking privileges. That company was the Bank of England. It was private, owned by its shareholders, and its first job was to finance the state.

Sweden's Riksbank is older, founded in 1668, and is usually called the oldest central bank still operating. But the Bank of England became the model. Over the next two centuries it took on new roles almost by accident. Other banks kept their reserves with it. Its notes became the most trusted in the country. And in financial panics, everyone looked to it for help.

Central banks were not designed from a blueprint. They grew out of the needs of governments and the panics of banking systems. Most were privately owned at first. Most were later nationalised; the Bank of England only in 1946. The idea that their main job is to control inflation is newer still, mostly from the 1980s and 1990s.

Chapter 02

Five jobs under one roof

Central banks differ, but most do some version of five jobs.

Many also act as the government's bank, holding its accounts, and manage the country's foreign exchange reserves, the stock of foreign currencies and gold kept for emergencies. In some countries banking supervision sits in a separate agency. The details vary a lot. The constant is that a central bank is the only institution that can create the economy's ultimate money at will.

The Fed's founding story is instructive. The United States had no central bank for most of the nineteenth century, because many Americans distrusted concentrated financial power. After a severe banking panic in 1907, which the financier J. P. Morgan helped stop by organising private bankers, Congress decided that the country could not rely on one rich man in a crisis. The Federal Reserve Act was signed in December 1913. Its unusual structure, with regional banks spread across the country, was a compromise meant to stop Wall Street or Washington from controlling it alone.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Committees, balance sheets and the rules that protect them

A central bank's power comes from three things: a balance sheet that can create money, a committee that decides how to use it, and laws that keep politicians at a distance.

Chapter 04

Lender of last resort: Bagehot's rule

Banks are fragile by design. They take deposits that customers can withdraw at any time and lend them out for years. If too many depositors want their money at once, even a healthy bank can fail. The full story is in How banks work.

In 1873 the British journalist Walter Bagehot, editor of The Economist, published Lombard Street, a book about how the Bank of England should behave in a panic. His advice is usually summarised in one line: in a crisis, the central bank should lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral. Lend freely, so that fear stops spreading. At a high rate, so that banks do not use it in normal times. Against good collateral, meaning assets the borrower pledges and loses if it fails to repay, so that the central bank helps banks that are short of cash, not ones that are already bankrupt.

This principle is still the core of crisis management. In 2008 and again in March 2020, central banks lent enormous sums to banks and, in new ways, to markets. The hardest part in a real crisis is the one Bagehot's rule takes for granted: telling a bank that is short of cash from one that is truly broke. In the middle of a panic, they can look identical.

Chapter 05

Who decides, and how

Interest rate decisions are almost always made by a committee, not one person. The aim is to balance views and avoid any single person's mistakes.

The Fed's FOMC

The Federal Open Market Committee has twelve voters: the seven governors in Washington, the president of the New York Fed, and four of the other eleven regional presidents, who rotate yearly. It meets eight times a year.

The ECB Governing Council

The six members of the Executive Board plus the governors of each euro-area national central bank. Governors vote on a rotating system so the council does not grow unwieldy.

The Bank of England's MPC

Nine members: five from inside the Bank and four outside experts appointed by the finance minister. Each member's vote is published.

Committees publish statements after each meeting and, usually a few weeks later, minutes or accounts of the discussion. Officials give speeches and testify to parliament. This openness is the price of independence: a body that answers to no voter must at least explain itself to everyone.

Chapter 06

The balance sheet that creates money

Like any bank, a central bank has a balance sheet: what it owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities). Its liabilities are special. They are banknotes and the reserves that commercial banks hold with it. Both are money. So when a central bank buys a government bond from a bank, it pays by adding reserves to that bank's account. It creates the money with a keystroke, and that money is the ultimate settlement asset of the economy.

This has a striking consequence. A central bank cannot run out of its own currency. It can make losses, as many did after 2022, when the interest they paid on reserves rose above what they earned on bonds bought earlier. But it cannot be forced to close by those losses the way a commercial bank would. In normal times central banks make profits, mostly by earning interest on assets while paying nothing on banknotes, and hand most of them to the government.

Its limit is not money, but trust. If it creates too much, the currency loses value through inflation. That is why central banks guard their reputations so carefully, and why foreign currency is different: a central bank can create unlimited amounts of its own money, but not a single dollar or euro of someone else's.

Chapter 07

What "independence" actually means

Independence does not mean a central bank can do whatever it likes. The usual distinction is between goal independence, the freedom to choose what to aim for, and instrument independence, the freedom to choose how to get there. Most modern central banks have the second but not the first. In Britain, for example, the government sets the inflation target each year, and the Bank decides what interest rate will hit it.

Independence is protected in practical ways. Officials serve long terms: Fed governors have fourteen-year terms, and ECB Executive Board members serve a single eight-year term that cannot be renewed, so they have no reason to please anyone for reappointment. They usually cannot be fired for disagreeing with the government, only for serious misconduct. And central banks are generally banned from lending directly to their own government, so they cannot be used as a cash machine. In the euro area that ban is written into the EU treaties.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Why governments tied their own hands

Handing control of money to unelected experts was a deliberate choice, based on a lesson about temptation. Its value showed in the 2012 euro crisis and in the countries where independence was later removed.

Chapter 08

The temptation problem

Before an election, a politician has a strong reason to want low interest rates. Cheap money boosts spending and jobs quickly. The cost, higher inflation, arrives later, often after the votes are counted. Tapes from the White House show that in the run-up to the 1972 election, President Nixon pressed the Fed chair, Arthur Burns, to keep money easy. Money was easy. Inflation followed in the years after.

In 1977 the economists Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott formalised a deeper version of the problem, later called time inconsistency. A government may sincerely promise low inflation. But once people have set their wages and prices on that promise, it pays to break it with a burst of easy money. People know this, so they do not believe the promise, and inflation stays high anyway. The solution is to hand the job to someone who has no such temptation. Independence is a way of making a promise believable by removing the person who might break it.

New Zealand gave its central bank a clear inflation target and operational freedom in 1989. Britain made the Bank of England independent in setting rates in 1997. The ECB was built independent from the start, modelled partly on Germany's Bundesbank, which had a strong reputation for fighting inflation. In 1993 the economists Alberto Alesina and Lawrence Summers showed that rich countries with more independent central banks had lower inflation, with no visible cost in growth. Later research has argued about causation, but the broad pattern is widely accepted.

Chapter 09

"Whatever it takes": the power of a credible sentence

By mid-2012 the euro was in serious danger. Investors feared that Spain or Italy might leave, so they demanded very high interest rates to lend to them, which made leaving more likely. On 26 July, at a conference in London, the ECB's president Mario Draghi spoke a few lines that changed the crisis.

"Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough."

Mario Draghi · President of the ECB, London, 26 July 2012

Weeks later the ECB announced a programme to buy the bonds of countries in trouble, provided they accepted conditions. The programme was never actually used. Borrowing costs for Spain and Italy fell sharply anyway. Because a central bank can create unlimited money in its own currency, a believable promise to use that power can be enough on its own. No government could have made that sentence work, because no government has an unlimited balance sheet.

Chapter 10

When politics pushes back

Turkey offers a clear modern case. Its president held the unusual view that high interest rates cause inflation, rather than curing it. Between 2019 and 2021 he removed several central bank governors who resisted cutting rates. Rates were cut while inflation rose, and by late 2022 annual inflation was above 80%. After the 2023 election a new economic team reversed course and raised rates steeply, to 50% by 2024.

Pressure is not limited to emerging economies. American presidents of both parties have criticised Fed decisions. In 2025 the US president repeatedly and publicly demanded that the Fed chair cut rates, and tried to remove one of the Fed's governors, a move that was challenged in the courts. Supporters of such pressure argue that elected leaders should not be bound by officials they cannot control. Defenders of independence warn that markets may start to demand higher long-term interest rates if they suspect monetary policy is being set for political reasons.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Expert rule, and its democratic price

Independence has a good record against inflation. It also concentrates great power in a few unelected hands, and that power has grown.

Chapter 11

The case for and against independence

For

Credibility. Promises about inflation are believed, so inflation expectations stay anchored and shocks fade faster.
Long horizon. Decisions can look past the next election.
Crisis firepower. A trusted central bank can calm markets with words, as in 2012.
Evidence. Independence is associated with lower inflation in rich countries, without obvious costs to growth.

Against

Democratic deficit. Decisions that redistribute wealth between savers and borrowers are taken by people voters cannot remove.
Groupthink. Technocrats can share the same mistaken models, as the "transitory" call in 2021 showed.
Mission creep. Since 2008 central banks have bought trillions in assets and taken on supervision, far beyond their original remit.
Coordination. Separating money from budgets can make the two pull against each other.

Chapter 12

How wide should the mandate be?

A narrow mandate, such as "keep inflation at 2%", is easy to judge and hard to abuse. But it leaves out problems that central banks are well placed to see. Should they worry about climate change, which threatens the value of bank loans and could push up food and energy prices? Should they worry about inequality, which their policies affect?

Central banks have answered differently. Several in Europe have built climate risk into how they supervise banks and, to some extent, into their operations. The Fed has taken a narrower view, arguing that climate policy is a job for elected governments. The more goals an unelected body takes on, the weaker the case that it should be left alone. That trade-off is at the heart of the debate, and both sides have serious arguments.

Chapter 14

Gold, dollars and trust

One quiet trend says something about the world central banks now operate in. Since 2022 they have been buying gold at the fastest pace in decades, around a thousand tonnes a year by industry estimates. Much of the buying is by emerging-market central banks. The trigger, many analysts believe, was the freezing of a large part of Russia's foreign reserves by Western countries in 2022. Reserves held in someone else's currency are only as safe as your relationship with that country.

This connects central banks to geopolitics in a way they have mostly avoided. Their founding purpose, more than three centuries ago, was to finance a war. Their modern purpose is to be boringly predictable about inflation. The future may ask them to be both credible guardians of money and players in a more divided world. How well they manage that will depend, as always, on whether people trust them. The core idea

A central bank is the bank of the government and of the banks. It issues the currency, sets interest rates, and acts as lender of last resort. Most are independent in how they pursue a goal set by law, because handing money to politicians invites a temptation that makes promises about inflation unbelievable.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Oldest still operating: Sweden's Riksbank, 1668; the Bank of England, 1694.
  • The Fed was founded in 1913, the ECB in 1998.
  • Bank of England independence in setting rates: 1997.
The common mistake

Thinking independence means a central bank answers to nobody. Most choose the tools, not the goal. The goal is set by law or by government, and officials must explain themselves to parliament.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Draghi's sentence in 2012 worked when no finance minister's speech could have? If you can, you understand what makes a central bank different from a government.

The words, in plain words
Central bank
A public institution that manages a country's currency, interest rates and banking system.
Lender of last resort
The central bank's role of lending to banks in a panic when no one else will.
Collateral
Assets a borrower pledges, which the lender keeps if the loan is not repaid.
Reserves
Money that banks hold in accounts at the central bank.
Mandate
The goals a central bank is legally required to pursue.
Dual mandate
A mandate with two goals, such as stable prices and maximum employment.
Goal independence
Freedom to choose what the target is.
Instrument independence
Freedom to choose how to reach a target set by others.
Time inconsistency
The problem that a promise which is sensible today becomes tempting to break tomorrow.
FOMC
The Federal Open Market Committee, which sets US interest rates.
Foreign exchange reserves
Foreign currencies and gold held by a central bank for emergencies and intervention.
Stablecoin
A private digital token designed to hold a fixed value, usually one dollar.
