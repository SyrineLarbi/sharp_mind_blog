Fiscal policy. The state's budget is also a steering wheel for the whole economy.

Every government taxes and spends, mostly just to run the country. But the size and timing of the budget also push the whole economy one way or the other. Spend more than you collect in a slump, and you can soften it. Do the same in a boom, and you can overheat it. Fiscal policy is the art of using the budget for that second job, and it is one of the most argued-about subjects in economics.

Question 1 · What is it?

Spending, taxing and borrowing, used on purpose

Fiscal policy is the government's decisions about how much to spend, how much to tax, and how to cover the gap. The idea that these choices should respond to the state of the economy is less than a century old.

Chapter 01

The lesson of the 1930s

When the Great Depression hit the United States after 1929, the common view was that a government should behave like a careful family and balance its books. In 1932, with unemployment heading towards a quarter of the workforce, Congress passed a large tax increase to close the deficit. Several European governments did similar things. The slump got deeper.

In 1936 the British economist John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. His central point was simple to state. One person's spending is another person's income. When households and firms all cut back at once, each of them is being sensible, but together they destroy the very income they are trying to protect. Keynes argued that in such moments the government should do the opposite of the crowd: spend more, or tax less, and accept a deficit until private spending recovers.

Before Keynes, a deficit was a failure. After him, a deficit in a slump could be a deliberate tool.

Chapter 02

The vocabulary of a budget

A few words do most of the work, and they are easy to mix up.

RevenueWhat the state collects, mostly taxes and social contributionsIncome tax, VAT, company tax

Primary balanceThe deficit or surplus before interest paymentsShows whether today's policy, not past debt, is in the red

The most common confusion is between the deficit and the debt. The deficit is a flow, like water running into a bath. The debt is the stock, the water already in it. A government that "halves the deficit" is still adding to the debt, only more slowly. Economists also compare both to the size of the economy, measured by GDP (gross domestic product, the value of everything produced in a year). A debt of 100 means something very different in a country that produces 50 a year and one that produces 500.

In rich countries, public spending ranges from roughly a third of GDP to more than half, with France near the top and the United States lower. That gap is mostly a political choice.

Chapter 03

Three jobs for one budget

In 1959 the economist Richard Musgrave suggested that government budgets do three separate jobs. His division is still the cleanest way to think about the subject.

Allocation

Paying for things markets provide badly or not at all: defence, courts, basic research, roads, clean air. This is about what gets produced.

Distribution

Moving income between people through taxes and transfers: pensions, child benefits, progressive income tax. This is about who gets what.

Stabilisation

Leaning against booms and slumps by changing the overall balance of the budget. This is about when, and it is what "fiscal policy" usually means in the news.

A single tax cut can help growth, hurt fairness and be badly timed, all at once. Many fiscal debates go wrong because each side is talking about a different job. This file focuses on stabilisation; taxes have their own file, Taxes.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Ripples, cushions and delays

A change in the budget does not stop where it lands. It travels through the economy, gets bigger or smaller on the way, and arrives late. Understanding those three effects is most of understanding fiscal policy.

Chapter 04

The multiplier: how one euro becomes more, or less

Imagine the government pays a builder 100 euros to repair a school roof. The builder spends part of that money at a restaurant, perhaps 60 euros, and saves the rest. The restaurant owner spends part of her 60 euros on supplies. Each round is smaller than the one before, but together they add up to more than the original 100. The ratio between the final rise in national income and the original spending is called the fiscal multiplier.

The spending ripple, simplified
 round 1   government pays builder ............ 100
 round 2   builder spends 60% ..................  60
 round 3   restaurant spends 60% of that .......  36
 round 4   supplier spends 60% of that .........  22
 ...                                             ...

 leaks that shrink it:  saving · imports · taxes · higher interest rates

The real world leaks. Some money is saved, some buys imports, some returns as tax, and a central bank worried about overheating may raise rates and cancel much of the effect. The honest answer is that the multiplier is not a fixed number. It depends on the conditions.

Most studies find multipliers between about 0.5 and 2. They are larger in a deep slump, with interest rates near zero, and when the money goes to people who spend it quickly, such as the unemployed. They are smaller in a boom, or in a small economy that imports most of what it buys.

Chapter 05

Automatic stabilisers: the policy that needs no decision

The most important fiscal policy in most rich countries is one nobody votes on each year. When a recession arrives, people lose jobs and claim unemployment benefits, so spending rises. They earn less and their companies make smaller profits, so tax receipts fall. Because income tax is usually progressive, meaning the rate rises as income rises, tax falls faster than income does. The deficit widens on its own.

These are called automatic stabilisers. They act like the suspension on a car. They do not steer, but they absorb bumps without anyone touching a control. They work in both directions: in a boom, tax revenue climbs and benefit claims fall, which cools things down. Countries with larger welfare states have larger stabilisers, which is one reason household incomes in many European economies swing less in a downturn than in the United States.

Everything else is discretionary policy: a deliberate new decision, such as a stimulus package or a temporary VAT cut. It gets the headlines; the stabilisers do more of the steady work.

Reading a deficit properly

A big deficit in a recession does not prove a government is reckless. Economists therefore estimate the structural deficit: the deficit if the economy were at normal capacity. It is a better guide, but only an estimate, because "normal capacity" cannot be observed.

Chapter 06

Three delays, and the politics inside them

Discretionary policy passes through three delays. The recognition lag: data arrive late and get revised. The decision lag: a parliament must agree. The implementation lag: projects need planning, contracts and workers. A bridge approved in a recession may be built during the next boom.

Hence the rule that stimulus should be timely, targeted and temporary: arriving during the slump, reaching people who will spend it, and not locking in permanent deficits.

The third rule is the hardest to keep. Spending programmes create supporters, and tax cuts are popular. Governments find it far easier to loosen the budget in bad times than to tighten it in good times. That one-sided pattern, repeated over decades, is a large part of why public debt in most rich countries has drifted upward since the 1970s. The full story is in Public debt.

Chapter 07

The counter-arguments: crowding out and Ricardian equivalence

Not every economist accepts that fiscal stimulus works well. Two objections matter most.

The first is crowding out. State borrowing competes with private firms for the same savings, which can push up interest rates and discourage private investment. In the extreme case, each euro of public spending simply replaces a euro of private spending. Crowding out is weak in a deep slump, when savings lie unused, and stronger in a boom.

The second is an idea revived by the American economist Robert Barro in 1974, called Ricardian equivalence after the nineteenth-century economist David Ricardo, who discussed and doubted it. The logic runs like this. If the government cuts my taxes today but borrows to do it, it will have to raise my taxes later. A fully rational household would save the whole tax cut to pay that future bill, and spending would not change at all.

Real households rarely behave like that. Many live from month to month, and studies of tax rebates generally find that a meaningful share is spent within months. But the core insight survives: people react to what they expect the government to do next, not only to what it does today.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Three crises that tested the theory

The great fiscal debates of the last twenty years were not abstract. They decided how deep recessions went, how fast jobs came back, and in one case who stayed prime minister.

Chapter 08

After 2008: stimulus, then austerity

When the global financial crisis struck in 2008, most large economies responded with stimulus. The United States passed a package of tax cuts, aid to states and public investment worth roughly 800 billion dollars in early 2009. For a few months, the Keynesian view was close to a consensus.

By 2010 the mood had turned. Deficits had ballooned, partly through automatic stabilisers and bank rescues, and Greece revealed that its true deficit was far larger than reported. Bond investors began demanding high interest rates from several euro-area countries. Governments across Europe, including the United Kingdom, shifted to austerity: cutting spending and raising taxes to reduce deficits while their economies were still weak.

Supporters argued this would restore confidence. Critics argued it would deepen the slump and worsen debt ratios, because GDP, the bottom of the fraction, would shrink. In 2013 two IMF economists, Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, published an influential study. It found that forecasters had systematically underestimated how much austerity would hurt growth. The multipliers early in the crisis had been well above the values of around 0.5 that many forecasts assumed.

Defenders of austerity reply that some countries had no choice once markets stopped lending. But the main lesson most economists drew is that cutting budgets hard in the middle of a deep slump, with interest rates already at zero, is costlier than it looks. That lesson shaped the very different response a decade later.

Chapter 09

2020: the largest peacetime fiscal response

When the pandemic shut economies in the spring of 2020, governments did not wait for the data. Within weeks they spent on a scale never seen outside wartime. The United States passed packages across 2020 and 2021 totalling roughly five trillion dollars. Britain's furlough scheme paid most of the wages of millions of workers on hold. Germany expanded Kurzarbeit, in which the state tops up the pay of employees whose hours are cut instead of letting them be fired.

The approach was different from 2008 in a key way. The aim was not to boost demand but to keep the connections between workers and firms alive while activity was frozen, so the economy could restart quickly. In that it largely succeeded: unemployment rose much less, and fell back much faster, than in previous deep recessions.

Then came inflation at forty-year highs. Economists still debate how much of it came from stimulus, especially the large American package of 2021, and how much from supply shocks and the energy spike after Russia invaded Ukraine. Most accept that both played a part.

Chapter 10

London, 2022: when the bond market says no

On 23 September 2022 the new British government announced a "mini-budget". It contained large tax cuts, including the abolition of the top rate of income tax, with no independent forecast and no plan for how they would be paid for. Within days the pound fell to a record low against the dollar, and the interest rate on long-term government bonds, called gilts, jumped at a speed rarely seen in a major economy.

Pension funds that had used borrowed money to hedge were forced to sell gilts, pushing prices down further, and the Bank of England stepped in on 28 September to stop the spiral. Within weeks the finance minister was dismissed, most of the tax cuts were reversed, and the prime minister resigned after about seven weeks in office.

A government that borrows in its own currency cannot be forced to default, but it can be forced to change course by the price lenders demand. Credibility, meaning lenders' belief that the numbers add up, is part of a country's fiscal space, the room it has to borrow.

Chapter 12

Tying the government's hands: rules and referees

To resist the temptation to spend now and pay later, many countries have adopted fiscal rules. The European Union's best-known rules, from the 1990s, set reference values of a deficit no higher than 3% of GDP and debt no higher than 60%. They were widely broken, including by France and Germany in the early 2000s, and were reformed several times, most recently in 2024 with more focus on country-specific paths for spending. Germany wrote a "debt brake" into its constitution in 2009 limiting the structural deficit. In 2025 it loosened the brake to allow more borrowing for defence and infrastructure.

Rules face a dilemma. Strict rules are credible but can force cuts at the worst moment. Flexible rules allow sensible exceptions but are easier to dodge. Most rules therefore contain escape clauses for recessions and emergencies, and the EU used its general escape clause from 2020 to 2023.

A second approach is a referee: an independent fiscal council that publishes its own forecasts, such as the US Congressional Budget Office (1974) or Britain's Office for Budget Responsibility (2010). They cannot block a budget, but they make the arithmetic harder to hide. The 2022 mini-budget came without such a forecast.

Chapter 14

The return of the interest bill

For about a decade after 2008, governments could borrow for ten years at 1% or less, and in Germany and Japan sometimes below zero. Debt seemed almost free. When inflation pushed rates up from 2022, interest took a growing slice of budgets. In the United States, net interest on the federal debt came to rival defence spending.

The key comparison is between the interest rate a government pays (r) and its economy's growth rate (g). When r is below g, debt ratios can shrink over time even with small deficits. When r rises above g, a country must run primary surpluses just to stand still. Which of the two will be higher in the 2030s is one of the most important open questions in economics, and nobody knows the answer.

The debate is likely to move from "stimulus versus austerity" towards quality: which spending raises future growth, and which taxes do the least damage.

The core idea

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending, taxes and borrowing to influence the economy. It works because one person's spending is another's income, so a change in the budget ripples outward through the multiplier. Its power varies with conditions, it arrives with delays, and it is limited by how much lenders trust the government.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Most multiplier estimates fall between about 0.5 and 2, higher in deep slumps.
  • Public spending in rich countries runs from roughly a third to over half of GDP.
  • The EU's classic reference values: deficit 3% and debt 60% of GDP.
The common mistake

Treating a government budget like a household's. A household's spending does not change its own income. A government's does, because its spending is the economy's income. That is why cutting hard in a slump can make the debt ratio worse.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the deficit grows in a recession even if the government changes nothing, and why that is usually a good thing? If you can, you understand automatic stabilisers.

The words, in plain words
Fiscal policy
Government decisions on spending, taxation and borrowing, especially as used to steady the economy.
Deficit
The amount by which spending exceeds revenue in one year.
Public debt
The total sum the government owes from all past borrowing.
Primary balance
The budget balance excluding interest payments on existing debt.
Structural deficit
The estimated deficit if the economy were at normal capacity, removing the effect of the cycle.
Fiscal multiplier
How much national income changes for each unit of extra government spending or tax cut.
Automatic stabilisers
Taxes and benefits that loosen the budget in slumps and tighten it in booms without any new decision.
Austerity
Cutting spending or raising taxes to reduce a deficit, especially when the economy is weak.
Crowding out
Government borrowing pushing up interest rates and displacing private investment.
Ricardian equivalence
The theory that people save tax cuts financed by borrowing, expecting higher taxes later.
Fiscal rule
A legal or political limit on deficits, debt or spending.
Fiscal council
An independent public body that forecasts and checks a government's budget plans.
