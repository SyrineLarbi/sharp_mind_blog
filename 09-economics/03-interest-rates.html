Interest rates. The price of time, and the most powerful number in the economy.

An interest rate is what you pay to use someone else's money now and give it back later. That sounds like a detail of banking. It is far more. The level of interest rates decides whether families can buy homes, whether firms build factories, whether governments can carry their debts and whether prices stay stable. When central banks raised rates sharply in 2022 and 2023, mortgage payments jumped, a large American bank collapsed, and inflation began to fall. This file explains where rates come from, how they travel through an economy, and why economists are still arguing about where they will settle.

Question 1 · What is it?

A price like any other, except the thing being sold is time

Lending means giving up something now for something later. Interest is the payment for that wait, for the risk of not being repaid, and for what inflation does in between.

Chapter 01

Why anyone charges interest at all

Imagine a friend asks to borrow 1,000 euros for a year. Even if you trust them completely, you give up three things. First, the use of the money: for a year you cannot spend it or invest it. Second, certainty: people lose jobs and fall ill, and some loans are never repaid. Third, value: if prices rise 3% over the year, the 1,000 euros you get back buys less than the 1,000 you lent. Interest is the payment that makes those three costs worth bearing.

People have argued about interest for as long as there has been lending. The Code of Hammurabi in ancient Babylon set maximum rates on loans of grain and silver. Medieval Christian law condemned charging interest as usury, though merchants found many ways around the ban. Islamic law still forbids riba, usually translated as interest, and Islamic banks use profit-sharing, leasing and trade-based contracts instead. These traditions share a worry that is still alive: that lenders can exploit borrowers who are desperate. Modern economies mostly allow interest but regulate it, with caps on some consumer loans in many countries.

Chapter 02

Not one rate, but a family

The news speaks of "the interest rate", but an economy has thousands. What links them is that they move together, starting from one anchor.

RateWho pays whomTypical position in the family
Policy rateThe rate a central bank sets for overnight money between itself and banksThe anchor. Everything else is built on top of it.
Interbank rateBanks lending to each other for short periodsVery close to the policy rate
Government bond yieldA state borrowing from investors for 2, 10 or 30 yearsPolicy rate plus expectations of future rates and a premium for time
Mortgage rateA household borrowing to buy a homeAbove government rates, for risk and the bank's margin
Business loan rateA company borrowing to investDepends heavily on the company's credit risk
Savings rateA bank paying you for your depositUsually below the policy rate; the gap is part of bank profit
Credit card rateYou borrowing without securityFar above the rest, often 20% or more

The difference between two rates is called a spread. Spreads tell you about risk. When investors fear that companies will fail, the spread between business borrowing and government borrowing widens. The level of rates is set mostly by the central bank and the market's view of the future. The spreads are set by risk.

Chapter 03

Nominal and real: the number on the contract and what it buys

The rate written on a loan is the nominal rate. What matters to lenders and borrowers is the real rate: the nominal rate minus inflation. The American economist Irving Fisher set out this relationship in the early twentieth century, and it carries his name.

Suppose your savings account pays 4% while prices rise 6%. Your balance grows, but its buying power falls by about 2% a year. Your real rate is negative. Now suppose a mortgage costs 3% while inflation runs at 5%. The borrower is effectively being paid to borrow, because the debt shrinks in real terms. High inflation with low rates quietly moves wealth from savers to borrowers. That is what happened across much of the rich world in 2021 and 2022, before rates caught up. Volume 16 has a short rule for this in the news: always ask whether a figure is real or nominal (How to read a number).

Question 2 · How does it work?

A central bank pulls one end of the rope, markets hold the other

Central banks control short-term rates almost exactly. Long-term rates are set by markets, which watch the central bank but also look at inflation, government debt and the global supply of savings.

Chapter 04

How a central bank actually sets a rate

A central bank such as the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank (ECB) or the Bank of England is the bank for commercial banks. Banks hold accounts with it, called reserves, and use them to settle payments with each other. Because the central bank creates those reserves, it can decide the price of borrowing and lending them.

Today most large central banks do this mainly by choosing the interest rate they pay banks on reserves. No bank will lend overnight to another bank for less than it can earn, risk-free, at the central bank. So the rate on reserves sets a floor under short-term rates across the whole system. The central bank also offers to lend to banks at a rate slightly higher, which acts as a ceiling. Short-term market rates stay inside that corridor. The details of this plumbing are in Banking · Central banking, and the wider toolkit is in Monetary policy.

From that anchor, the change spreads. Banks' own costs of funding move, so they reprice loans and deposits. A decision announced in one meeting room reaches mortgage offers within days and credit cards within weeks.

Chapter 05

Long rates: the market's guess about the future

A ten-year government bond yield is not set by any committee. It is the rate at which investors are willing to lend to the government for ten years. Roughly, it reflects three things: the average policy rate investors expect over those ten years, a premium for the uncertainty of locking money away that long (the term premium), and in some countries a premium for the risk that the government will not repay in full or will let inflation erode the debt.

This is why central banks talk so carefully. If they convince markets that rates will stay high for years, long rates rise today, even before any decision. Central banks call this forward guidance. It is also why a government that alarms investors can push up its own borrowing costs with no help from the central bank, as Chapter 10 shows.

Chapter 06

The invisible target: the neutral rate

In 1898 the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell proposed an idea that still sits at the centre of monetary policy. There is, he argued, a natural rate of interest: the rate at which the economy neither speeds up nor slows down, and inflation stays steady. If the bank rate is below it, borrowing and spending run hot and prices rise. If the bank rate is above it, the economy cools.

Modern economists call it the neutral rate, often written as "r-star" (r*). Central banks try to keep their rate above it when inflation is too high and below it when the economy is weak. The difficulty is that nobody can observe the neutral rate directly. It has to be estimated from models, and the estimates move a lot.

In 1993 the economist John Taylor offered a simple formula, now called the Taylor rule, describing how the Federal Reserve seemed to behave: start from the neutral rate, add current inflation, then raise the rate further when inflation is above target and when the economy is running above its capacity. Central banks do not follow it mechanically, but they and their critics use rules like it as a benchmark for whether policy is too loose or too tight.

Chapter 07

Forty years down, then a sudden jump

Interest rates in the rich world have followed a striking path. In the late 1970s US inflation was running near double digits. Paul Volcker, appointed to chair the Federal Reserve in 1979, pushed short-term rates to around 20% by 1980 and 1981. The result was a deep recession, with US unemployment above 10% in 1982, but inflation fell sharply and stayed lower.

From that peak, rates drifted down for about four decades. Inflation fell. So, it seems, did the neutral rate. Economists offer several reasons, and they disagree on the weights: ageing populations saving more for retirement; a large flow of savings from fast-growing Asian economies, which Ben Bernanke in 2005 called a "global saving glut"; slower productivity growth, which lowers the return on new investment; and greater demand for safe assets.

After the 2008 financial crisis, policy rates in the US, UK and euro area went to near zero. Some central banks went further. The ECB set a negative deposit rate in 2014, and the Bank of Japan did so in 2016, keeping it until 2024. A negative rate means banks pay to keep money at the central bank, an attempt to push them to lend instead.

Then inflation returned. After the pandemic and the energy shock that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine, inflation in the US and UK rose to its highest level in about forty years, and in the euro area to its highest since the euro began. From early 2022 central banks raised rates faster than at any time since the Volcker era. The Federal Reserve went from near zero in March 2022 to a range of 5.25–5.5% by July 2023. The Bank of England reached 5.25% and the ECB's deposit rate reached 4% the same year. Inflation fell back over 2023 and 2024, and central banks began cutting rates in 2024.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

One number that reaches every household, firm and government

Interest rates are the main tool for controlling inflation. They also decide who gains and loses between generations, between savers and borrowers, and between countries.

Chapter 08

How a rate rise slows an economy

When a central bank raises rates, it is trying to reduce total spending so that prices rise more slowly. The effect travels along several channels at once, which economists call the transmission mechanism.

From one decision to the whole economy

   |             |              |                |                  |

The last line matters most. Milton Friedman described monetary policy as working with "long and variable lags". A rate rise today may take a year or two to have its full effect on inflation. Central bankers are steering by looking in the mirror and guessing the road ahead. That is why they sometimes raise rates too far and cause a recession, or too little and let inflation settle in.

Chapter 09

Winners and losers

Rate changes are never neutral between people. Higher rates help those with savings in the bank and hurt those with debts on variable rates. Older households, who tend to own more savings and fewer debts, often gain. Younger households, who are trying to buy a first home, often lose. Lower rates do the reverse, and they also tend to push up the price of homes and shares, which benefits people who already own them.

How fast households feel a change depends on the mortgage system, which differs a lot by country. In the United States most home loans are fixed for 30 years, so a rate rise barely touches existing owners, though it freezes the market because owners do not want to give up a cheap loan by moving. In the UK, most mortgages are fixed for only two to five years, so higher rates reach many households within a few years. In some countries, such as Spain historically, variable-rate mortgages were common and rate changes hit monthly payments almost at once. The same central bank decision can feel mild in one country and brutal in another. More on this in Banking · Mortgages.

Chapter 10

When rates bite governments and banks

Governments borrow too, and when rates rise their interest bill grows as old debt is refinanced. Markets can also force rates up on their own. In September 2022 the UK government announced large unfunded tax cuts in a "mini-budget". Investors sold British government bonds, and long-term yields jumped within days. Some pension funds that had used borrowed money to hedge their bond positions faced sudden demands for cash and had to sell more bonds, which pushed yields higher still. The Bank of England stepped in with temporary bond purchases to stop the spiral, and the prime minister, Liz Truss, resigned the following month. The lesson was that the market, not just the central bank, decides what a government pays to borrow. Public debt has its own file: Public debt.

Banks are exposed too. When rates rise, the market value of bonds bought earlier at lower rates falls. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank, a large Californian lender to technology firms, had to sell such bonds at a loss to meet withdrawals. Word spread, depositors pulled money out very fast, and the bank failed within days. It was one of the largest bank failures in US history. A long period of low rates had hidden a risk that a fast rise exposed.

Chapter 11

Why investment and energy projects care so much

For a project that costs a lot up front and pays back slowly, the interest rate can decide whether it happens at all. Think of a wind farm, a railway or a new factory. Most of the cost is borrowed before the first euro of revenue arrives. If rates double, the cost of carrying that debt for years can wipe out the expected profit. Volume 11 notes exactly this for offshore wind, where several large projects were cancelled or renegotiated after rates rose (Wind turbines).

The same logic explains why low rates tend to raise the price of long-lived assets such as homes, land, and shares in growth companies. Their value comes from income far in the future, and a low rate makes that future income worth more today. When rates rose in 2022, many of those prices fell, especially for technology shares whose profits were expected far ahead.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A blunt but effective tool

Neither high rates nor low rates are good in themselves. Each one helps some goals and hurts others, and the right level changes with the economy.

Chapter 12

What high and low rates buy, and what they cost

SettingBenefitsCosts
Low ratesCheap borrowing supports jobs and investment in a downturn. Government debt is cheaper to carry. Recovery after a crash can be faster.Savers earn little. Home and share prices can inflate, widening wealth gaps. Weak firms survive on cheap debt. Risks build up quietly.
High ratesInflation is brought down. Savers are rewarded. Lending decisions become more careful, and bubbles are harder to start.Borrowers and homebuyers suffer. Investment falls and unemployment usually rises. Weak banks and governments can be pushed into crisis.
The rate as a toolOne lever, adjusted quickly by an independent body, influences the whole economy without new laws.It is blunt. It cannot target one region or sector, and it works with long, uncertain lags.

The bluntness is the central weakness. If inflation is driven by an energy shock, a rate rise does not produce more gas. It reduces spending everywhere until the pressure on prices eases. Critics say this makes ordinary workers pay for a supply problem. Defenders reply that if inflation is allowed to become expected, it becomes much harder and more painful to remove later, as the 1970s showed. Both views are held by serious economists, and which one is right depends on how quickly expectations shift, which is itself hard to measure. Where interest-rate policy fits among government tools is covered in Fiscal policy.

Back to near zero, or a new normal?

Chapter 14

The lessons of the zero-rate decade

The years from 2009 to 2021 were an unusual experiment. Rates near or below zero and huge bond purchases by central banks kept borrowing cheap for over a decade. Supporters credit this with preventing a repeat of the 1930s and with getting unemployment back down. Critics point to inflated asset prices, a rise in risky borrowing, and pressure on bank profits and pension funds. Both effects probably happened. The honest summary is that economists understand the benefits of very low rates better than their long-run side effects.

The 2022 to 2023 rise taught a second lesson. Many people had come to treat near-zero rates as normal and built their plans around them: households with large mortgages, banks holding long-dated bonds, governments with rising debts. The period that felt normal was the exception. Over the long sweep of history, positive real interest rates are the usual state of affairs. Anyone making a long-term plan, from a mortgage to a pension, is wise to check that it still works if rates are a few points higher than today. Who decides these rates, and how much freedom they should have, is the subject of Central banks.

The core idea

An interest rate is the price of time: a payment for waiting, for risk and for inflation. Central banks set the short-term anchor; markets set long rates from their expectations. Rates above the neutral rate slow the economy; rates below it speed it up, with lags of a year or two.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US short-term rates reached about 20% in 1980–81 under Paul Volcker.
  • The ECB went negative in 2014; the Bank of Japan kept negative rates from 2016 to 2024.
  • The Federal Reserve raised rates from near zero to 5.25–5.5% between March 2022 and July 2023.
The common mistake

Looking only at the nominal rate. A 4% savings rate during 6% inflation is a real loss. Always subtract inflation before judging whether a rate is high or low.
