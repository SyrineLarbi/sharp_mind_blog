Future of the global economy. Five slow forces you can see coming, and the shocks you cannot.

Economic forecasts are famously poor. Almost nobody predicted the 2008 financial crisis, the pandemic, or the return of inflation in 2021. Yet some features of the future are far more predictable than next year's growth rate. The people who will be working in 2050 are mostly already born. Clean energy keeps getting cheaper. Debts already taken on must be serviced. This file separates the slow, visible forces from the unpredictable shocks, explains how each one works, and sorts the possible futures by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Question 1 · What is it?

One economy of eight billion people

The "global economy" is the sum of every country's production, joined by trade, money and migration. To think about its future, start with its shape today.

Chapter 01

The shape of the world economy today

In the mid-2020s the world produced goods and services worth roughly 110 trillion US dollars a year, for a population of a little over eight billion. Measured at market exchange rates, the United States accounted for about a quarter of the total, China for about a sixth, and the European Union for about a sixth. Japan, India, the UK and others made up most of the rest.

Measured by purchasing power, which adjusts for lower prices in poorer countries, the picture changes. China's economy is then the largest in the world, and India's is the third largest. Which measure you use changes who looks biggest, and both matter. Market rates show what a country can buy abroad, such as oil, chips and weapons. Purchasing power shows how much its people actually consume (see Exchange rates).

Over the past half-century, the economic centre of gravity has moved steadily east. In 1980 the West dominated world output. Today Asia produces a large and growing share, and China alone makes roughly three tenths of the world's manufactured goods.

Chapter 02

Why forecasts fail, and what can still be known

In the late 1980s many books predicted that Japan would overtake the United States. Instead, Japan's asset bubble burst in 1990 and its economy stagnated for two decades. In the early 2000s few expected China to grow as large as it did, so fast. Short-term forecasts are worse still: recessions are very rarely forecast a year in advance. The psychologist Philip Tetlock spent two decades testing expert predictions and found that experts were often barely better than chance on long-range political and economic questions.

But not everything is equally uncertain. It helps to sort claims about the future into three kinds. Arithmetic: things that follow from facts already in place, such as how many sixty-five-year-olds there will be in 2045. Trends: things that have moved steadily for decades and have strong causes, such as the falling cost of solar power. And shocks: wars, pandemics, crises and breakthroughs that nobody can date. A sensible view of the future rests on the arithmetic, weighs the trends, and stays humble about the shocks.

Question 2 · How does it work?

How each force pushes on growth

Each force works through a different channel: how many people work, how much each produces, what energy costs, and how freely goods and money move.

Chapter 04

Demography: the arithmetic of fewer workers

An economy's output is, at its simplest, the number of people working multiplied by how much each one produces. For most of the twentieth century, the first number grew everywhere. That is ending. Fertility, the average number of children per woman, has fallen below the level of about 2.1 needed to keep a population stable in most rich countries and in China. South Korea's fell to around 0.7 in 2023, the lowest in the world. Japan's population has been shrinking for over a decade, and China's began to fall in 2022.

The United Nations projects that the world's population will peak at a little over ten billion around the 2080s. Before that, the number of working-age people will fall in much of East Asia and Europe while it rises in Africa and South Asia. A shrinking workforce means that growth must come almost entirely from higher productivity, or from immigration, or from people working longer. It also means more retirees for each worker, which presses on pensions and public budgets (see Aging populations).

Chapter 05

Technology: the productivity wildcard

If fewer workers is the problem, more output per worker is the obvious answer. That is why so much hope rests on artificial intelligence. Early studies show real gains in specific tasks such as coding, writing and customer support. Whether those add up to a lasting rise in economy-wide growth is the biggest open question in economics today, and estimates range from modest to transformative.

History suggests patience. Electricity and computers both took decades to show up in the statistics, because businesses had to reorganise around them first (see Productivity). AI may move faster because it spreads through software rather than new factories. It may also raise inequality if its gains go mostly to the firms and people who own it. The technology is likely to be big; the speed and the distribution of its gains are the real unknowns.

Chapter 06

Energy: from fuels you burn to machines you build

For two centuries economic growth ran on coal, oil and gas. Those fuels are burned once and must be bought again and again. The energy system now being built runs increasingly on solar panels, wind turbines and batteries, which are manufactured goods: you pay mostly up front, and then the energy is nearly free. Solar module prices fell by roughly nine tenths during the 2010s, and solar became the cheapest source of new electricity in much of the world (see Solar panels).

That shift has economic consequences beyond the climate. Energy turns from a commodity into a manufacturing industry, and China dominates it, making more than four fifths of the world's solar panels and most of its batteries. Countries that export oil and gas face a long-term challenge to their main source of income, which is why Gulf states such as Saudi Arabia, through its Vision 2030 plan launched in 2016, and the UAE are trying to diversify. Meanwhile, the damage from warming already locked in, through heat, floods, droughts and crop losses, will be a growing cost, falling hardest on hotter and poorer countries.

Chapter 07

Geopolitics: from efficiency to security

From about 1990 to 2008, the world economy integrated at high speed. Companies built supply chains wherever production was cheapest, and governments mostly let them (see Globalization). That logic has weakened. The pandemic exposed fragile supply chains. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 led Western countries to impose sweeping sanctions and to freeze much of Russia's central bank reserves. The US imposed controls from 2022 on exporting advanced chips and chip-making tools to China. In 2025 the US raised tariffs on imports from almost every country, lifting its average tariff rate to the highest level in many decades.

The result is often called fragmentation. Companies talk of "friend-shoring", moving production to allied countries, and "de-risking", reducing dependence on one supplier. Security is now weighed against efficiency, and the price is higher costs and somewhat slower growth. IMF economists have estimated that severe fragmentation could cut world output by several per cent in the long run, though the range of estimates is wide. Some countries, such as Vietnam, India and Mexico, gain factories as supply chains move.

Chapter 08

Debt, interest rates and the dollar

For a decade after 2008 interest rates in rich countries were near zero, and governments, firms and households borrowed cheaply. Inflation returned in 2021–22, and central banks raised rates sharply (see Monetary policy). Even as inflation eased, many economists expect rates to stay higher than in the 2010s, partly because public debts are large and still rising (see Public debt). Higher rates make every debt more expensive to carry, from mortgages to national budgets.

Overlaying this is a question about money itself. The US dollar is the world's main reserve and trading currency, making up somewhat under three fifths of central bank reserves in the mid-2020s. The freezing of Russian reserves led some countries to reduce their exposure, and central bank gold purchases rose sharply. Yet no rival currency offers the same deep, open markets and legal protections. The dollar's share may slowly erode, but a sudden replacement is unlikely without a major change in US policy or in China's willingness to open its financial system.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where growth will come from, and who will feel the change

These forces will decide which regions rise, what jobs people do, and how safe savings and pensions are.

Chapter 09

The new map of growth

For thirty years China supplied a large share of world growth. That era is ending. Its workforce is shrinking, its property sector, once a quarter or more of economic activity by some estimates, has been in crisis since the developer Evergrande defaulted in 2021, and it faces trade barriers abroad. Most forecasters expect Chinese growth to keep slowing, though from a very large base and with real strength in electric vehicles, batteries and solar.

India became the world's most populous country in 2023 and is among the fastest-growing large economies, though it still needs to create far more good jobs for its young workforce. Southeast Asia, the Gulf, and parts of Africa and Latin America are also candidates for faster growth. By 2050, Africa will be home to roughly a quarter of the world's people, which makes its success or failure in creating jobs one of the most important economic questions of the century (see Economic development).

Chapter 10

What it means for an ordinary career and savings

For workers, the likely picture is more change and more choice. Ageing societies will need many more people in health and care. The energy transition will create jobs in building, installing and maintaining equipment. Supply chains moving for security will create factory and logistics work in new places. AI will change many office jobs, raising output for some and replacing tasks for others (see How work may change because of AI).

For savers, two shifts stand out. Higher interest rates than in the 2010s make safe savings more rewarding but borrowing more expensive. And ageing means that pension systems, both public and private, will be under pressure, so people will likely need to save more and work somewhat longer (see Pensions). The best preparation for uncertainty is not a forecast but flexibility: skills that transfer, savings that can absorb a shock, and diversified investments.

Chapter 11

Shocks, and the word "polycrisis"

The 2020s so far have delivered a pandemic, a major war in Europe, an energy price spike, the highest inflation in four decades in many rich countries, and a trade war. The historian Adam Tooze helped popularise the word polycrisis for a period in which several crises overlap and feed each other. Whether the world is truly more crisis-prone or simply more connected, so that local shocks travel faster, is debated.

Shocks cannot be predicted, but they can be prepared for. Countries with low debt, credible central banks, diverse trade partners and strong public health systems recovered faster from the pandemic. Resilience, the ability to take a hit and recover, may matter more in the coming decades than squeezing out the last fraction of efficiency.

The choices behind the forecasts

The future is not just something that happens. It depends on choices about openness, growth and the planet, and each choice has winners and losers.

Chapter 13

Can the world grow and cut emissions at once?

One of the deepest debates is whether economic growth is compatible with a stable climate. Supporters of green growth point to evidence of decoupling: many rich countries have cut their emissions while their economies kept growing. The UK's emissions roughly halved between 1990 and the early 2020s, while its economy grew by about three quarters. Cheaper clean energy makes this easier every year.

Supporters of degrowth, a smaller movement mainly among some ecological economists, reply that decoupling has been too slow, that part of it came from moving heavy industry abroad, and that rich countries should aim for lower consumption rather than more output. Most economists remain unconvinced that shrinking economies is necessary or politically possible, especially for poorer countries that still need to grow. The practical debate is less about whether to grow than about how fast emissions can fall while growth continues, and who pays for the transition.

Chapter 15

How to think about the economic future

Faced with so much uncertainty, the most useful habit is to think in scenarios rather than single forecasts. Instead of asking "what will happen?", ask "what are three or four plausible futures, and what would I do in each?" Companies, governments and central banks do this because a plan that works in several futures is safer than a plan built on one confident guess.

A second habit is to update steadily. Watch the slow variables: birth rates, productivity growth, the price of clean energy, average tariff rates, interest costs as a share of budgets. They move slowly, but they move in one direction for long periods, and they tell you more about 2040 than the day's headlines (see Follow stories, not headlines). The future of the global economy will be shaped less by any single event than by forces already in motion, plus the choices people make about how to respond to them. That last part is the reason the future is still open.

The core idea

Five forces will shape the coming decades: demography, technology, energy, geopolitics and debt. Demography is nearly arithmetic; technology and energy are strong trends; geopolitics and shocks are genuinely uncertain. Rest on the arithmetic, weigh the trends, stay humble about the shocks.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World output: roughly 110 trillion dollars a year, for about 8 billion people (mid-2020s).
  • World population projected to peak at a little over 10 billion around the 2080s (UN).
  • China makes roughly three tenths of the world's manufactured goods and more than four fifths of its solar panels.
The common mistake

Trusting a single confident forecast. Long-range economic predictions are usually wrong; the slow forces are more reliable than any headline number.

Can you explain why a shrinking workforce makes productivity even more important? If you can, you understand the link between demography and growth.

The words, in plain words
Purchasing power parity
A way of comparing economies by what money actually buys in each.
Fertility rate
The average number of children a woman has over her lifetime.
Replacement level
A fertility rate of about 2.1, which keeps a population stable over time.
Dependency ratio
The number of children and retirees for each person of working age.
Electrification
Replacing fuels burned directly with electricity, ideally from clean sources.
Fragmentation
The world economy splitting into rival trading blocs.
Friend-shoring
Moving production to politically allied countries.
De-risking
Reducing dependence on a single country or supplier for critical goods.
Reserve currency
A currency central banks hold in large amounts for trade and emergencies.
Polycrisis
Several overlapping crises that make each other worse.
Decoupling
Economic growth continuing while emissions fall.
Degrowth
The view that rich economies should deliberately shrink their output for ecological reasons.
Scenario
One of several plausible futures used for planning, rather than a single forecast.
