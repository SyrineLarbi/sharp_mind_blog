Sharp Mind · Economics · Topic 04 of 20

Unemployment. A single percentage that hides very different stories.

Every month, governments publish one number that moves markets and decides elections: the unemployment rate. Behind it are people in very different situations. A graduate between two offers. A factory worker whose skills no longer match any job nearby. A waiter laid off because a recession emptied the restaurant. They need different remedies, and treating them as one problem leads to bad policy. This file explains how the number is built, why some unemployment never disappears, what joblessness does to people beyond lost income, and what may change as populations age and machines learn new tasks.

Question 1 · What is it?

Not "everyone without a job"

Being unemployed has a precise meaning. Most people without a job are not counted as unemployed at all, and understanding why is the key to reading the number.

Chapter 01

Three people without a job

Consider three adults, none of whom has paid work this week. Amira is 70 and retired. Jonas is 24, lost his job in a warehouse in March, and has sent applications every week since. Lena is 45, was laid off two years ago, applied for months, and has now stopped looking because she believes nothing is available.

By the international standard, only Jonas is unemployed. The International Labour Organization (ILO), a United Nations agency, defines an unemployed person as someone who has no job, is available to start work soon, and has actively looked for work recently, usually within the past four weeks. Amira is not looking, so she is outside the labour market. Lena has given up, so she is not counted either, even though she would take a job tomorrow. Economists call people like her discouraged workers.

This definition is not a trick. It tries to measure people who are actively competing for jobs right now. But it means the unemployment rate can fall because people give up, not because they find work. That is why careful readers always look at a second and third number.

Chapter 02

The labour force and the three numbers that matter

The adult population splits into three groups: the employed, the unemployed, and those outside the labour force (students, retirees, full-time carers, the discouraged, people too ill to work). The employed plus the unemployed form the labour force.

A country can have low unemployment and still have a problem if participation is low. For years after 2008, US unemployment fell faster than employment rose, partly because many people left the labour force. Reading the numbers together tells you whether a fall in unemployment is good news.

Chapter 03

How the number is actually measured

Most rich countries measure unemployment with a large household survey. In the United States the Current Population Survey asks about 60,000 households each month whether their members worked, and if not, whether they looked for work. The UK's Labour Force Survey and the EU's surveys follow the same ILO definitions. Because a sample is used, monthly changes of a tenth of a point are often just noise.

Many countries also publish a claimant count: the number of people receiving unemployment benefits. It is easier to collect but depends on benefit rules. If a government tightens eligibility, the claimant count falls even if nobody finds a job. Survey-based unemployment is the comparable measure; benefit counts tell you about the welfare system as much as the job market. In poorer countries, where many people work informally in family farms or street trade, the unemployment rate can look low while large numbers scrape by in poorly paid, unprotected work.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three causes, and why wages don't simply fall

If labour were sold like apples, a surplus of workers would push wages down until everyone was hired. It doesn't happen that way, and the reasons explain most of the theory.

Chapter 04

Frictional, structural and cyclical

Frictional

The time it takes to match a worker to a job. People change cities, graduate, quit to find something better. Even in a booming economy there is always some search. This kind is mostly healthy: a quick, good match beats an instant, bad one.

Structural

A mismatch between the skills or places of workers and the jobs available. When coal mines or textile mills close, their workers' skills may not fit the new jobs, which may be in another region. This kind can last years and does not go away when the economy recovers.

Cyclical

Joblessness caused by a downturn, when total spending in the economy falls and firms need fewer workers everywhere at once. It rises in recessions and falls in recoveries. This is the kind that monetary and fiscal policy can fight.

Why the split matters: if unemployment is mostly cyclical, cutting interest rates or raising government spending helps. If it is mostly structural, those tools can push up inflation without helping much, and the answer is retraining, moving support, or new industries in the affected regions. Diagnosing which kind you face is the first and hardest step of policy.

Chapter 05

Why wages don't fall to clear the market

The supply and demand model in Topic 01 predicts that a surplus of workers will lower wages until it disappears. In a recession, though, firms usually lay people off rather than cut everyone's pay. Economists call this wage stickiness, and they have found several reasons for it.

In the 1990s the American economist Truman Bewley interviewed hundreds of managers about why they did not cut wages in a recession. The most common answer was morale. A pay cut offends every worker, every day, including the best ones, who may leave. A layoff hurts a few people once, and the rest keep working. Contracts, minimum wages and union agreements add rigidity too.

Firms may also pay above the market wage on purpose. In 1914 Henry Ford roughly doubled pay at his car factories to five dollars a day. Staff turnover had been very high, and it fell sharply. The idea that higher pay can raise effort and loyalty, and reduce quitting, is called an efficiency wage. When many employers pay above the level that would clear the market, some job seekers are left waiting.

Chapter 06

The natural rate and the trade-off with inflation

In 1958 the New Zealand-born economist A. W. Phillips plotted almost a century of British data and found a pattern: when unemployment was low, wages rose fast; when it was high, they rose slowly. This became the Phillips curve, and for a time policymakers thought they could pick a point on it, buying lower unemployment with a bit more inflation.

In the late 1960s Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that this could only work briefly. Once people expected higher inflation, they would demand higher wage rises and the trade-off would vanish. There was, they argued, a natural rate of unemployment, set by frictions and structure, that policy could not push below for long without inflation speeding up. The 1970s, when high inflation and high unemployment arrived together ("stagflation"), seemed to prove them right.

Today the idea survives as the NAIRU, the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. It is useful but slippery. Like the neutral interest rate in Interest rates, it cannot be observed, and estimates have moved a great deal. In 2019 and again in 2023 US unemployment fell well below many earlier estimates of the NAIRU, reaching 3.4% in early 2023, the lowest since 1969.

Chapter 07

Okun's rule of thumb

In 1962 the economist Arthur Okun noticed that in the United States, each percentage point rise in unemployment tended to come with output about two to three percentage points below the economy's potential. Later estimates revised the ratio, and it differs by country, but the link it describes is solid: output and jobs move together, and unemployment is the human face of an economy running below capacity. The relationship also differs in an instructive way. In countries with strong job protection, such as Germany, firms tend to cut hours rather than people, so unemployment moves less for the same fall in output. The measure of output itself is the subject of the next file, GDP.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The cost is not only money

Lost output can be measured in billions. The harder costs are to health, identity, family and politics, and some of them last for decades.

Chapter 08

The village where time stopped

In 1930 the textile factory in Marienthal, a village near Vienna, closed. Almost every family lost its income. A team of researchers including Marie Jahoda and Paul Lazarsfeld lived among the villagers and published their study in 1933. They expected anger and revolt. They found something quieter: resignation. Men walked more slowly in the street and seemed to lose their sense of time. People who had unlimited free time used the public library less, not more. Clubs and political groups shrank. The researchers concluded that a job gives far more than wages. It gives a daily structure, a sense of purpose and contact with other people.

Modern research supports this. Losing a job is linked to worse mental health, higher rates of depression, and in some studies higher mortality for years afterwards. Life-satisfaction surveys find that unemployment hurts people's well-being far more than the lost income alone would predict, and that many people do not fully return to their old level even after finding new work.

Chapter 09

Scars that last

Unemployment leaves marks that outlast the downturn. Economists call this scarring. Workers who lose stable jobs in mass layoffs often earn noticeably less for many years, even after they are re-employed. Young people who graduate into a recession start on lower pay and in worse jobs, and the gap can take a decade to close.

Long spells of unemployment are worse than short ones. Skills fade, employers become suspicious of gaps in a CV, and people lose touch with the networks through which jobs are found. Economists call the risk that a temporary rise in unemployment becomes permanent hysteresis. It is one reason many economists argue that governments should act quickly and forcefully in a recession: it is cheaper to stop people losing their jobs than to bring them back later.

Chapter 10

Three episodes that shaped thinking

The Great Depression. In 1933 roughly a quarter of the US labour force was out of work, and unemployment was severe across Europe. The experience led John Maynard Keynes to argue, in 1936, that economies could get stuck with mass unemployment and that governments should fill the gap in spending. Volume 21 traces how the despair of those years fed political extremism.

Southern Europe after the euro crisis. Spain's unemployment rate passed 25% in 2013, and youth unemployment there exceeded 50%. Greece's was similar. Because these countries shared the euro, they could not lower their own interest rates or let their currency fall. The years of joblessness that followed shaped a generation's view of politics and Europe.

The pandemic of 2020. In April 2020 US unemployment jumped to 14.7%, the highest since records of this kind began after the Second World War. In much of Europe it barely rose. The difference was policy design. Germany's Kurzarbeit (short-time work) and the UK's furlough scheme paid firms to keep workers on the books at reduced hours. The US mostly let people be laid off and supported them with expanded unemployment benefits. Both approaches protected incomes; they differed in whether the link between worker and employer survived.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every remedy has a cost

Countries use very different approaches, and each one trades protection against flexibility. There is no costless way to fight unemployment.

Chapter 12

Can unemployment be too low?

It sounds strange, but zero unemployment would be a bad sign. It would mean nobody was changing jobs, graduates were not choosing carefully, and firms had no one to hire when they grew. Some frictional unemployment is the cost of a labour market that keeps matching people to better jobs.

Very low unemployment also has a cost for central banks to watch. When employers compete hard for scarce workers, wages rise quickly, and if productivity does not keep pace, prices follow. That is why central banks watch the job market so closely. The opposing view, put forward by many labour economists and some central bankers after 2019, is that a "hot" labour market brings big benefits to workers at the margins, such as people with disabilities, older workers and those with criminal records, who are hired only when employers are short of staff. How low unemployment can safely go is a judgment, not a fact, and that judgment is made by people with different priorities.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Fewer workers, smarter machines

Two big forces are pulling in opposite directions: ageing populations are shrinking the supply of workers, while automation and AI may reduce the need for some of them. Sorted by confidence.

Chapter 14

A measure built for factories, used in a gig economy

The concept of unemployment was built in the twentieth century for a world of full-time jobs with one employer. Today, many people drive for ride-hailing apps, deliver food, freelance online, or combine several part-time jobs. Someone who works ten hours a week through an app counts as employed, even if they want forty. Statistical agencies are adding new questions to capture these situations, but the headline rate captures less of the picture than it did fifty years ago.

The likely direction is more attention to the wider measures in Chapter 02: underemployment, participation and hours worked. A sensible habit for any reader of economic news is the same one this file began with. When the unemployment rate changes, ask what happened to the number of people working, and to the number who have stopped looking. Unemployment usually jumps during a recession, which is the subject of Topic 07.

The core idea

The unemployed are people without work, available and actively looking. Unemployment comes in three kinds: frictional (searching), structural (mismatch) and cyclical (downturns). Wages are sticky, so a fall in demand shows up as lost jobs rather than lower pay.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 25% of US workers were unemployed in 1933.
  • Spain passed 25% in 2013, with youth unemployment over 50%.
  • US unemployment hit 14.7% in April 2020, and 3.4% in early 2023.
The common mistake

Reading a falling unemployment rate as good news automatically. It can fall because discouraged people stop looking. Check employment and participation too.

Test yourself

A region's unemployment stays high for years after a national recovery. Is it more likely frictional, structural or cyclical, and would an interest-rate cut help?

The words, in plain words
Unemployed
Without work, available to start, and actively looking (the ILO definition).
Labour force
Everyone employed plus everyone unemployed.
Participation rate
The share of adults who are in the labour force.
Discouraged worker
Someone who wants a job but has stopped looking, so is not counted as unemployed.
Underemployment
Working fewer hours than you want, or in a job well below your skills.
Frictional unemployment
The short gap while people search for and move between jobs.
Structural unemployment
Lasting joblessness from a mismatch between workers' skills or places and the available jobs.
Cyclical unemployment
Joblessness caused by a downturn in total spending.
Sticky wages
Wages that do not fall easily, even when there are more workers than jobs.
Efficiency wage
Pay set above the market level on purpose, to raise effort and reduce quitting.
Phillips curve
The observed link between low unemployment and faster rises in wages and prices.
NAIRU
The unemployment rate below which inflation tends to speed up. It cannot be seen directly.
Hysteresis
When a temporary rise in unemployment leaves lasting damage.

  2. "The rate can fall because people give up. Always check how many are actually working."
