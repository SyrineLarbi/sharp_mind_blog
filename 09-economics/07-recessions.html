Recessions. When an economy loses its nerve, and how it gets it back.

Every few years, an economy that was growing begins to shrink. Firms stop hiring, shops cut orders, workers are laid off and spend less, and their caution causes more layoffs elsewhere. That is a recession. Most are short and mild. A few, like the Great Depression of the 1930s and the crisis of 2008, change politics and lives for a generation. Recessions are not natural disasters. They have causes, they follow patterns, and governments have learned, at real cost, how to make them shorter. They have not learned how to predict them.

Question 1 · What is it?

A broad, lasting fall in economic activity

A recession is when the economy as a whole shrinks for months rather than weeks, and the fall spreads across industries, jobs and incomes.

Chapter 01

The two-quarter rule, and why experts don't rely on it

The definition most people hear is simple: a recession is two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. Many journalists and some governments use it, and in Europe and the UK it is the common working rule. It is easy to apply and easy to understand.

It is also crude. A tiny fall in two quarters counts, while a sharp collapse in one quarter followed by a flat one does not. In the United States, recessions are dated instead by a committee of economists at the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), a private research body. It defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. It looks at jobs, incomes, spending and industrial output, not just GDP.

The difference mattered in 2022. Early estimates showed US GDP falling in the first two quarters of the year, and headlines declared a recession. But employment was growing strongly and unemployment was near a fifty-year low. The NBER did not call a recession, and later data revisions softened the GDP figures. A recession is a condition of the whole economy, not a pattern in one statistic.

Chapter 02

The business cycle, and the words around it

Economies tend to move in waves, called the business cycle. A period of growth, the expansion, reaches a peak. Activity then falls, the contraction or recession, until it reaches a trough, after which recovery begins. "Cycle" is a slightly misleading word, because the waves are not regular. Some expansions last two years, others more than ten.

An old joke captures the human side: a recession is when your neighbour loses his job; a depression is when you lose yours.

Chapter 03

How often, how long, how deep

The NBER has dated about a dozen US recessions since the end of the Second World War. Most lasted less than a year and a half, and the post-war average was around ten months. Expansions have usually lasted far longer, several years on average and more than ten years in the 2009 to 2020 expansion. Recessions are common, but in the modern era they are usually much shorter than the recoveries around them.

Depth varies even more than length. In a typical mild recession, output falls by one or two percent and unemployment rises by a couple of points. In the worst, output falls by far more and takes years to recover. A useful rule from history: recessions linked to banking crises are deeper and slower to heal than those caused by other shocks. Chapter 06 explains why.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A shock, then a spiral

Recessions usually start with a shock. They become recessions because the shock spreads, as caution in one part of the economy causes losses in another.

Chapter 04

One person's spending is another's income

Imagine a factory loses a big order and lays off a hundred workers. Those workers spend less at local shops and restaurants. The shops order less from suppliers and cut staff hours. Those people spend less too. A loss in one place travels through the economy, growing as it goes. Economists call this the multiplier: an initial fall in spending can reduce total income by more than its own size.

Fear makes it worse. When people worry about their jobs, they save more and postpone big purchases: a new car, a kitchen, a holiday. For each family this is sensible. For the economy as a whole, if everyone does it at once, total spending falls, incomes fall, and people end up no better off, sometimes worse. John Maynard Keynes described this in the 1930s; it became known as the paradox of thrift. What is prudent for one household can be harmful when every household does it together. That idea is the basis for governments spending more in a recession, covered in Fiscal policy.

Chapter 05

The main causes

No two recessions are identical, but most begin with one of five kinds of shock.

Kind of shockWhat happensExample
Financial crisisAsset prices collapse, banks lose money, lending freezes2008, after the US housing bubble burst
Supply shockA key input suddenly becomes scarce or expensiveThe oil price jump of 1973–74 after the Arab oil embargo
Policy tighteningA central bank raises rates sharply to fight inflationThe US in 1981–82 under Paul Volcker
Demand collapseSpending falls after an investment boom ends or confidence breaksThe early 2000s after the dot-com bubble
External eventA war, pandemic or disaster stops activity directlyThe pandemic lockdowns of 2020

Many recessions combine causes. Inflation rises, the central bank raises rates, and that tightening exposes weak borrowers or inflated asset prices. The interest-rate side of this story is in Interest rates.

Chapter 06

Why financial recessions last longer

In 2009 the economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published This Time Is Different, a study of financial crises over several centuries. One of its central findings is that recessions following banking crises tend to be deeper and longer, with unemployment staying high for years.

The reason is debt. After a bubble bursts, households and firms are left owing more than their assets are worth. They spend the next years paying debts down instead of spending or investing, even if interest rates are very low. The economist Richard Koo called this a balance sheet recession, describing Japan after its property and stock market bubble burst around 1990. Japan's growth was weak for much of the following decade, often called the "lost decade". At the same time, banks with heavy losses lend less, starving healthy firms of credit. A recession that damages the banks damages the system that would normally finance the recovery.

Chapter 07

The warning lights, and how reliable they are

Economists watch several indicators that have often flashed before recessions.

An inverted yield curve

Normally, lending for ten years pays more than lending for three months. When short rates rise above long rates, markets are betting that the central bank will soon have to cut, usually because of a downturn. In the US, this has preceded most recessions since the 1960s. But a long inversion from 2022 was not followed by a recession in 2023 or 2024. Details in Interest rates and markets.

The Sahm rule

Proposed by the economist Claudia Sahm in 2019: when the three-month average unemployment rate rises half a percentage point above its low of the previous twelve months, a recession has usually already begun. It is designed to trigger help quickly, not to predict far ahead. It was triggered in mid-2024 without a recession following, which Sahm herself said could happen when the labour supply grows fast.

Other signals include falling business surveys, rising claims for unemployment benefits, and falling building permits. Each has a good record and occasional false alarms. That is the honest state of recession warning: useful, not reliable.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Three recessions that rewrote the rules

The worst downturns did more than destroy output. Each one changed what economists believed and what governments were willing to do.

Chapter 08

1929–33: the Great Depression

After the US stock market crash of October 1929, the economy did not just slow. Between 1929 and 1933, US output fell by about a quarter. Thousands of banks failed, wiping out savings, and roughly a quarter of the workforce was unemployed. Prices fell year after year, which made debts harder to repay. The collapse spread worldwide through trade, finance and the gold standard, the system that tied currencies to gold and forced countries to keep rates high.

For a long time the authorities believed the downturn had to run its course. In 1963 Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued in A Monetary History of the United States that the Federal Reserve had turned a recession into a depression by letting the banking system collapse and the money supply shrink. Ben Bernanke, who studied the Depression as an academic and shared the 2022 Nobel prize partly for that work, later said on behalf of the Fed, at a 2002 event for Friedman's ninetieth birthday, that the Fed had indeed caused it and would not do it again. The lesson taken was that in a crisis, central banks must keep banks and money flowing. Countries that left the gold standard earlier, such as Britain in 1931, tended to recover sooner.

Chapter 09

2008–09: the Great Recession

A US housing bubble, fuelled by loose lending and complex mortgage-backed securities, burst from 2006. Losses spread to banks around the world. When the investment bank Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, lending between banks froze. World output fell in 2009, the first fall in decades by most measures. US unemployment reached 10% in late 2009.

This time, governments and central banks applied the lessons of the 1930s. They cut rates to near zero, bought bonds on a huge scale, rescued banks, and many governments increased spending. A repeat of the Depression was avoided. But the recovery was slow, and in the euro area a second crisis followed as several governments struggled to borrow. Anger at bank bailouts while ordinary people lost homes and jobs shaped politics across the West for the next decade. The banking side is in Financial crises.

Chapter 10

2020: the deepest and shortest

The pandemic recession was unlike any other. Governments deliberately shut large parts of the economy to slow the virus. UK output fell by about a fifth in the second quarter of 2020, the largest quarterly fall on record. US unemployment jumped to 14.7% in April. The NBER dated the US recession as lasting only two months, from February to April 2020, the shortest on its records.

The response was the largest in peacetime history. Governments paid wages through furlough schemes, sent cheques to households, and guaranteed loans to firms. Central banks cut rates and bought assets. Output recovered far faster than after 2008. The cost came later: a large rise in public debt and, together with supply disruptions and the energy shock of 2022, a burst of inflation. Economists still argue about how much of that inflation was caused by the size of the support.

Chapter 11

The cost falls unevenly

A recession that reduces GDP by 2% sounds modest. But it is not spread evenly as a 2% cut for everyone. Most people keep their jobs and lose little. A minority lose their jobs, and for them the loss can be most of their income. Young people, low-paid workers, those on temporary contracts and people in construction and manufacturing are usually hit hardest.

The damage can last. Workers laid off in recessions often earn less for years. Graduates entering a weak job market start lower and take years to catch up. Firms cancel investment and research that would have raised future output. This long shadow, which Unemployment describes as scarring, is a main reason economists argue that preventing a deep recession is worth paying a lot for.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fighting recessions, and the case that they have a use

Every tool for ending a recession has side effects, and some economists argue that downturns themselves do necessary work.

Chapter 13

Cleansing or scarring?

An older view holds that recessions are painful but useful. During a boom, weak firms survive and bad investments are made. A recession clears them out, freeing workers and capital for better uses. Schumpeter's idea of creative destruction, discussed in Economic growth, supports this "cleansing" view. Critics of large rescues add that saving every firm produces "zombie" companies that survive only on cheap credit, a worry raised about Japan in the 1990s and about Europe in the 2010s.

The opposing view, now more common, is that recessions mostly destroy, not clean. Good firms fail because credit dries up, not because they were bad. Skilled workers drift out of the labour force. Investment that would have raised productivity is cancelled. The evidence on scarring, mentioned above, is strong. Most economists accept that some reallocation happens in a recession, but think a deep recession costs far more than it cleans. The disagreement shows up mainly in how quickly and generously governments should step in.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Shorter recessions, new kinds of shock

Nobody serious thinks the business cycle has ended. The questions are what will trigger the next downturns and how much room governments have to respond. Sorted by confidence.

Chapter 15

Why nobody sees them coming

If recessions follow patterns, why are they so rarely predicted? Studies of forecasts by Prakash Loungani and colleagues at the IMF found that consensus forecasts almost never predicted a recession a year ahead, across many countries and decades. Forecasters tend to adjust only when the downturn is already under way.

There are good reasons. Recessions are triggered by shocks, and shocks are by nature unexpected. Confidence can change suddenly and then feed on itself. And forecasting a recession is costly for the forecaster: call one that does not come and you look foolish; say nothing and you are in good company. In 2008 Queen Elizabeth II, visiting the London School of Economics, asked why nobody had seen the crisis coming. Economists wrote her a letter in reply blaming a failure of collective imagination.

The practical lesson is the opposite of trying to time it. Assume a recession will come at some point, and prepare for it before you know when. For a household that means a cash buffer, as Volume 38 advises (The short list that actually matters). For a government, it means keeping room to borrow in good years. The tools themselves are the next two files: Fiscal policy and Monetary policy.

The core idea

A recession is a broad, lasting fall in economic activity. It starts with a shock and spreads because one person's spending is another's income, and because fear makes everyone cut back together. Recessions after banking crises are deeper and longer.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US output fell by about a quarter between 1929 and 1933.
  • US unemployment reached 10% in 2009 and 14.7% in April 2020.
  • The 2020 US recession lasted two months, the shortest on record.
The common mistake

Treating "two quarters of falling GDP" as the definition. Official US dating looks at jobs, income, spending and output together, and 2022 showed the difference.

Multiplier

When everyone saves more at once, total income can fall, leaving nobody better off.

A downturn in which people and firms focus on paying off debt rather than spending.
Automatic stabilisers
Benefits and taxes that adjust on their own to cushion a downturn.
Soft landing
Bringing inflation down without causing a recession.
Stagflation
Weak growth and high inflation at the same time.
Moral hazard
Taking more risk because you expect someone else to bear the losses.
Scarring
Lasting damage to workers and the economy that outlives the recession.
