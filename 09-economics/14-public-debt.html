Sharp Mind · Economics · Topic 14 of 20

Public debt. Not a household credit card, but not free money either.

Politicians love to compare a country's debt to a family's overdraft. The comparison is wrong in the ways that matter most. A government can live for centuries, it can tax, and it often prints the currency it borrows in. Families can do none of that. Yet governments do go broke, and when they do, ordinary people pay through lost pensions, closed hospitals and years of lost growth. This file explains how both things can be true at once, and the one piece of arithmetic that decides which side of the line a country is on.

Question 1 · What is it?

The total a state owes, built up one deficit at a time

Public debt is simple to define and easy to misread. Most confusion comes from mixing up two words, and from looking at the wrong number.

Chapter 01

Deficit and debt: the tap and the bathtub

Picture a bathtub. Water flows in from a tap and drains out through the plug hole. If more comes in than goes out, the level rises. Now replace the water with money. In any one year a government spends money on pensions, schools, soldiers, roads and interest. It also collects money, mostly through taxes. When spending is larger than revenue, the gap is the deficit. To cover it, the government borrows. The debt is the water level: the total of all past borrowing that has not yet been repaid.

So the deficit is a flow, measured per year, and the debt is a stock, measured at a moment in time. A government can cut its deficit sharply and still see its debt rise, because a smaller deficit is still more water going into the tub. The debt only falls in money terms when the government runs a surplus, collecting more than it spends. Few large countries do that for long.

Economists add one more distinction. The primary balance is the budget balance before interest payments. A country with a primary surplus covers all its current spending from taxes and borrows only to pay interest on old debt. That number turns out to matter a great deal, as Chapter 05 shows.

Chapter 02

Who lends to a government, and how

Governments rarely borrow from a bank the way a family takes a mortgage. They sell bonds. A bond is a written promise: lend me 100 today, and I will pay you a fixed amount of interest every year, called the coupon, and return the 100 on a set date, called maturity. Maturities range from a few weeks to thirty years or more. In the United States these are Treasuries, in the United Kingdom gilts, in Germany Bunds, in Japan JGBs.

The buyers are a wide crowd. Pension funds and insurers want safe, long-lasting income to match promises they have made to retirees. Banks hold government bonds as their safest, most easily sold asset. Foreign central banks hold them as reserves. Households buy them directly or through savings products. And since 2008 the country's own central bank has often been one of the biggest holders, because buying bonds became a main tool of monetary policy (see Monetary policy).

This leads to a point that surprises many people. Much of a country's debt is owed to its own citizens, often through their pensions. When people say "we are leaving debt to our grandchildren", they forget that the grandchildren also inherit the bonds. The real questions are who pays the taxes, who receives the interest, and how much is owed to foreigners.

Chapter 03

The number to watch: debt as a share of income

Headlines love giant figures. Global government debt passed roughly 100 trillion US dollars in 2024, according to the International Monetary Fund. But a raw number means little. A debt of one million is a crisis for a student and nothing for a large company. What matters is debt compared with the capacity to carry it. For a country, that capacity is its yearly income, measured by gross domestic product (GDP), the value of everything produced in a year (see GDP).

CountryDebt as a share of GDP, roughly, mid-2020sWhat stands out
JapanAbout 230–250% (gross)The highest among rich countries, yet borrowing costs stayed very low for decades
ItalyAbout 135–140%High debt, slow growth, a long-running concern in the euro area
United StatesAbout 100% (held by the public)Rising steadily; borrows in the world's main reserve currency
United Kingdom, FranceAbout 95–115%Both roughly doubled their ratios after 2008 and the pandemic
GermanyAbout 60–65%Low by rich-country standards, held down by a constitutional "debt brake"

Two warnings about the table. First, definitions differ: "gross" debt counts everything, "net" debt subtracts financial assets the government owns, and the US often quotes debt "held by the public", which excludes money one part of government owes another. Second, no ratio is automatically safe or dangerous. Japan carries a far higher ratio than many countries that have defaulted, while Argentina has defaulted nine times in its history, often with much lower debt than Japan's. Who you owe, in what currency, and at what interest rate matter more than the ratio alone.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rolling over, paying interest, and one piece of arithmetic

Governments almost never pay their debt back in full. They refinance it, forever. Whether that is sustainable depends on a race between two numbers.

Chapter 04

Rolling over: debt that is never repaid, only renewed

When a ten-year bond matures, a government rarely pays it from taxes. It sells a new bond and uses the money to repay the old one. This is rolling over the debt. Most rich countries have been rolling over their debt continuously for well over a century. Britain borrowed heavily to fight Napoleon, and its debt rose above twice national income in the early nineteenth century. It then spent a hundred years growing out of it, without ever paying it off in one go. In 2014 and 2015 the UK Treasury finally redeemed its last undated "perpetual" bonds, some of which traced back to the First World War and earlier.

Rolling over works only if buyers keep turning up. New bonds are sold at auctions. Investors bid, and the price they pay sets the yield, the effective interest rate the government pays. If demand is strong, yields are low. If investors worry, they demand more. A government's real constraint is not its debt today but whether lenders will refinance it tomorrow at a bearable rate. That is why the maturity of the debt matters. A country whose debt is mostly long-dated, as in the UK, feels a rise in rates slowly. A country that must refinance a large share every year feels it fast.

Chapter 05

The arithmetic that decides everything: r versus g

Here is the single most useful idea in this whole file. Because we measure debt as a share of GDP, two forces push on the ratio. Interest makes the debt grow. Economic growth makes the GDP underneath it grow. Economists write the interest rate as r and the growth rate of the economy as g, both measured in money terms, including inflation.

How the debt ratio moves from one year to the next, simplified
change in debt ratio  =  (r - g) x debt ratio   -   primary surplus

   r = average interest rate on the debt
   g = growth of GDP in money terms (real growth + inflation)

 Example: debt = 100% of GDP, primary budget balanced (no surplus)
   r = 3%, g = 5%  ->  ratio FALLS about 2 points a year, by itself
   r = 5%, g = 3%  ->  ratio RISES about 2 points a year, by itself

When r is below g, a country can run a small primary deficit forever and still see its debt ratio stay flat or fall. The economy is outgrowing the interest. When r is above g, the opposite holds. The country must run a primary surplus, meaning taxes higher than spending excluding interest, just to stop the ratio climbing. That is politically painful, because it means taxing citizens to pay bondholders.

For most of the period from the 1980s to 2021, interest rates fell, and in many rich countries r sat below g. In 2019 the economist Olivier Blanchard, in a well-known address to the American Economic Association, argued that this made public debt less costly than commonly assumed. Then inflation returned in 2021–22, central banks raised rates sharply, and the comfortable gap narrowed. The whole debate about whether debt is "too high" is really a debate about whether r will stay below g.

Chapter 06

Your own currency, or someone else's

The second deciding factor is the currency of the debt. A government that borrows in a currency its own central bank creates, as the US, the UK and Japan do, cannot be forced into default the way a company can. In an emergency the central bank can buy its bonds with newly created money. The danger for such a country is not running out of money. It is inflation, and a falling currency, if it leans on that option too hard (see Inflation, properly understood).

A government that borrows in a currency it cannot print is in a different position. Many developing countries borrow in dollars because foreign lenders will not accept their own currency. Economists Barry Eichengreen and Ricardo Hausmann called this problem "original sin". If the local currency falls, the debt grows in local terms overnight, even though nothing else changed. The Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s, the Asian crisis of 1997 and many African debt problems in the 2020s all ran through this channel.

The euro's special case

Countries in the euro area borrow in euros, but no single government controls the European Central Bank. Italy or Greece is therefore closer to a city borrowing in a national currency than to Japan. That is why the euro crisis of 2010–12 was so severe, and why it calmed only when the ECB signalled it would act. In 2012 its president Mario Draghi said the bank would do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, and yields fell sharply.

Chapter 07

Five ways big debts have come down

History offers only a short menu for shrinking a large debt. Most countries have used a mix.

RouteHow it worksHistorical example
GrowthThe economy outgrows the debt; g beats r for yearsBritain after 1815; most rich countries after 1945
Primary surplusesTaxes exceed non-interest spending, year after yearBelgium and Denmark in the 1990s; Greece after 2015
InflationPrices rise, so the fixed debt is smaller in real termsThe US and UK in the late 1940s and 1970s
Financial repressionRules push savers and banks to hold government bonds at low ratesMany rich countries from the 1940s to the 1970s
Default or restructuringLenders are paid less, later, or not at allGreece 2012; Argentina repeatedly; Sri Lanka 2022

After the Second World War, British debt was well over twice GDP and American debt held by the public was just above one times GDP. Both fell dramatically over the next thirty years. No one paid the war off. Growth, steady inflation and capped interest rates did most of the work. That combination is hard to repeat today, because savers now have more choices and central banks are committed to low inflation.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

When confidence breaks, and when interest eats the budget

Debt is mostly invisible in daily life. It becomes visible in two ways: suddenly, when lenders panic, and slowly, when interest crowds out everything else.

Chapter 08

Greece, 2010: what a debt crisis looks like from inside

In late 2009 a new Greek government revealed that the previous figures had badly understated the deficit. The true number was several times the euro's 3% limit. Investors who had been lending to Greece at rates close to Germany's suddenly demanded far more. Greece could not refinance its maturing bonds, and it could not print euros. In 2010 it accepted the first of three rescue programmes from other euro countries and the IMF.

The rescue came with conditions: deep spending cuts and tax rises, known as austerity. In 2012 private bondholders accepted losses of more than half the face value of their bonds, the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history up to then. The economy shrank by roughly a quarter from its peak. Unemployment rose above a quarter of the workforce and above half for young people. Debates still continue about how much of that pain was unavoidable and how much came from the design of the programmes. What nobody disputes is the lesson: a debt crisis is not a number on a chart, it is a sudden loss of choice, when lenders, not voters, decide the budget.

Chapter 09

London, 2022: forty-nine days

Rich countries with their own currencies are not immune to market discipline. In September 2022 the new UK government of Liz Truss announced large tax cuts without saying how they would be paid for and without the usual independent forecast. Gilt yields jumped within days. The fall in gilt prices hit pension funds that had used borrowed money in a strategy called liability-driven investment, and they were forced to sell more gilts, pushing prices down further. The Bank of England stepped in to buy long-dated gilts to stop the spiral.

Most of the tax cuts were reversed, the finance minister was replaced, and Truss resigned after about seven weeks in office. The episode became shorthand for an old idea: credibility is an asset, and it can be spent in a week. Traders who punish governments for loose budgets are sometimes called "bond vigilantes". In the 1990s a senior adviser to President Clinton, James Carville, joked that he would like to be reincarnated as the bond market, because it could intimidate everybody.

Chapter 10

The slow squeeze: the interest bill

Crises are dramatic, but the more common problem is quieter. Interest is a spending line like any other, and it competes with schools and hospitals. When rates were near zero in the 2010s, even large debts were cheap to carry. As rates rose after 2022, the bill climbed. Around 2024, US federal interest payments overtook defence spending. In May 2025 Moody's, the last of the three big rating agencies to give the US its top grade, downgraded it one notch, citing rising deficits and interest costs.

Interest is the one budget item that buys nothing new. It pays for spending that already happened. Every point of GDP spent on interest is a point not available for public services or tax cuts, and it is spent on bondholders, who are on average wealthier than taxpayers in general. That is one reason debt is a distributional question, not just an accounting one.

Chapter 11

Japan: the puzzle that refuses to break

If high debt automatically caused crises, Japan would have had one long ago. Its gross debt has been above twice GDP since the early 2010s. Yet for decades it paid some of the lowest interest rates in the world. The usual explanations are that almost all the debt is held at home, by Japanese households, banks, pension funds and above all the Bank of Japan, which owns roughly half of all government bonds. Japan also has large foreign assets, a current account surplus, and long periods of near-zero inflation.

Japan shows that debt sustainability is about the whole system, not one ratio. It does not prove that any country can copy it. As inflation and interest rates in Japan rose from 2023 onward, economists watched closely to see whether the puzzle would hold when the Bank of Japan stepped back from buying.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful tool that is easy to overuse

Borrowing lets a state act faster and smooth shocks. The risks come from how much, in what form, and for what purpose.

Chapter 13

The arguments that never end

Is there a danger threshold? In 2010 Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published a widely cited paper suggesting growth slowed sharply once debt passed about 90% of GDP. Politicians used it to justify austerity. In 2013 researchers at the University of Massachusetts, led by a graduate student, Thomas Herndon, found a spreadsheet error and other choices that weakened the result. Most economists now say high debt is associated with somewhat slower growth, but that there is no single cliff edge, and that slow growth can cause high debt as much as the reverse.

Does austerity work? One side argues that cutting deficits restores confidence and lowers interest rates. The other argues that cutting spending in a weak economy shrinks GDP so much that the debt ratio can actually rise. The evidence from the euro crisis suggests the timing matters: consolidation during a deep slump is costly, while the same cuts in a strong economy hurt far less.

Can a country with its own currency ignore debt? Supporters of Modern Monetary Theory argue that such a country is limited only by inflation, not by its debt. Most mainstream economists accept the narrow point that it cannot be forced to default, but reply that inflation and market reactions are exactly the limits that matter. This remains genuinely contested.

Chapter 15

The poorer countries' debt problem

The sharpest debt stress of the 2020s is not in Washington or Tokyo. It is in low-income countries. Zambia defaulted in 2020, Sri Lanka and Ghana in 2022. Many others spend more on interest than on health. Their lenders are now far more varied than in the 1980s: Western governments, private bondholders, and China, which became the largest bilateral lender to many developing countries. Getting all of them to agree on losses has been slow. The G20 created a "Common Framework" in 2020 to coordinate restructurings, but it has been criticised for moving at a crawl.

Two ideas are gaining ground. One is debt that adjusts to disasters, such as bonds that pause payments after a hurricane. The other is swapping debt for commitments to protect nature or cut emissions. Both exist in small numbers. Whether they scale is open. What is clear is that for poorer countries, debt is a development question, not just a fiscal one (see Economic development).

The core idea

Public debt is the stock of past deficits. It is rolled over rather than repaid. Its path depends on r versus g, the interest rate against the growth rate, and on the primary balance. Debt in your own currency carries an inflation risk; debt in someone else's carries a default risk.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Global government debt: roughly 100 trillion dollars in 2024 (IMF).
  • Japan about 2.5 times GDP; the US about 1 times (held by the public); Germany about 0.6 times, mid-2020s.
  • The euro's original limits: 60% of GDP for debt, 3% for the yearly deficit.
The common mistake

Treating a country like a household. A state lives indefinitely, taxes, and often issues its own currency, so it never needs to repay everything. But it can still lose the trust of lenders, and then it loses control of its own budget.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a country with debt at 100% of GDP can see the ratio fall without any surplus? If you can, you understand r versus g.

The words, in plain words
Deficit
The amount by which a government's spending exceeds its revenue in one year.
Debt
The total a government owes at one moment, the sum of past deficits minus surpluses.
Primary balance
The budget balance before counting interest payments.
Bond
A tradable promise to pay interest and return the borrowed amount on a set date.
Coupon
The fixed interest a bond pays each year.
Yield
The effective interest rate a buyer earns on a bond at today's price.
Rolling over
Repaying a maturing bond by selling a new one.
Debt-to-GDP ratio
Debt divided by a year's national income; the standard measure of debt burden.
r versus g
The interest rate on the debt compared with the growth rate of the economy.
Austerity
Cutting spending or raising taxes to reduce the deficit.
Default
Failing to pay lenders what was promised, on time and in full.
Restructuring
A negotiated deal in which lenders accept less, later, or at lower interest.
Original sin
A country's inability to borrow abroad in its own currency.
