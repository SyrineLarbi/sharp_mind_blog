The moments we learned something. Mostly accidents, mostly by people looking for something else.

Discovery almost never looks like the story we tell afterwards. There is rarely a flash of insight and usually a long stretch of noticing something odd and refusing to let it go. This volume is eight of those — gravity, the telescope, evolution, radioactivity, penicillin, flight, the atom and DNA — told as what they were at the time, which is mostly confusion.

Chapter 01

Gravity — the question nobody had thought to ask

The apple probably did fall, though it did not hit anyone's head. The point of the story, and Newton told a version of it himself, is not that he noticed things fall. Everyone has always noticed that.

The question he asked was stranger and better: why does the Moon not fall?

And the answer he arrived at is that it does. It is falling constantly — but it is also moving sideways fast enough that by the time it has fallen, the Earth's surface has curved away beneath it. It keeps missing. An orbit is not a thing held up; it is a permanent, beautifully judged fall.

From that came something more radical than the mechanics. Before Newton it was assumed that the heavens ran on different rules from the Earth — that was practically the definition of heavenly. Newton said no: the same rule that drops an apple holds the Moon in the sky. One set of laws, everywhere, for everything. That unification is the beginning of physics as we have it.

Chapter 02

The telescope — four points of light

The instrument was invented in the Netherlands, probably by a spectacle-maker, as a curiosity for looking at ships. Galileo heard a description, built a better one within days, and did the thing nobody else had bothered to do: he pointed it up.

What he saw dismantled a worldview in about a year. The Moon had mountains and craters, so it was a place rather than a perfect celestial sphere. Venus went through phases like the Moon, which is only possible if it circles the Sun. The Milky Way resolved into countless individual stars. And Jupiter had four small points of light that moved, night after night, around it.

That last one is the killer. Here were objects plainly going round something that was not the Earth. Whatever else was true, not everything circled us.

He published in Italian rather than Latin — a deliberate choice to reach educated ordinary people rather than only scholars — and it is part of why the trouble that followed was so serious. He was tried, forced to recant, and confined for his remaining years.

Chapter 03

Evolution — twenty years of not publishing

Darwin spent five years on a survey ship collecting specimens, noticed that finches on neighbouring islands had differently shaped beaks suited to different foods, and then spent the next twenty years mostly not writing it up.

He was not idle; he was breeding pigeons, studying barnacles, corresponding with animal breeders, and accumulating evidence, because he understood precisely how explosive the idea was and wanted it unassailable.

The idea itself is almost embarrassingly simple, which is why people miss how much work it does. Individuals vary. More are born than can survive. The variations that happen to help an individual survive and reproduce get passed on more often. Repeat for a very long time. Nobody is choosing, nothing is aiming at anything, and the result looks designed.

What finally forced publication was a letter from a younger naturalist, Alfred Russel Wallace, working in south-east Asia, who had arrived at the same idea independently and sent it to Darwin for an opinion. Their work was presented jointly, and Darwin finished his book in a year.

What he did not know

Darwin had no idea how traits were inherited. The mechanism was being worked out in the same decade by a monk counting pea plants in a monastery garden, whose results went unnoticed for thirty-five years. Mendel had the mechanism without the theory; Darwin had the theory without the mechanism. They never met, and neither knew the other's work mattered.

Chapter 04

Radioactivity — a drawer, and a cloudy week

Henri Becquerel was investigating whether minerals that glow after sunlight exposure also emitted the newly discovered X-rays. His method was to leave a mineral on a wrapped photographic plate in the sun and see whether the plate fogged.

Paris then had a cloudy week. He put the uranium salt and the wrapped plate together in a drawer and waited for sun.

When he eventually developed the plate anyway — and it is not obvious why he bothered — it was strongly fogged. No sunlight had reached it. The uranium was producing something entirely on its own, continuously, from no apparent source.

Marie and Pierre Curie took up the problem, processed tonnes of ore by hand in a leaking shed, and isolated two new elements, one of which glowed faintly blue in the dark. She kept a sample by her bed because she liked the light. Her notebooks are still too radioactive to handle without precautions, and she died of a blood disorder almost certainly caused by her work.

What it meant took longer to sink in: atoms were not permanent indivisible lumps. Some of them come apart on their own. Everything in the next chapter follows from that.

Chapter 05

Splitting the atom — the sum that did not add up

In Berlin in late 1938, two chemists bombarded uranium with neutrons expecting to make something slightly heavier. They found barium — an element about half uranium's size — and could not account for it. Chemically their result was solid; physically it seemed impossible.

They wrote to a former colleague, Lise Meitner, who had worked with them for thirty years and had recently fled Germany because she was Jewish. She read the letter over Christmas in Sweden, walked in the snow with her nephew, and worked out what had happened: the nucleus had not chipped — it had split.

She also did the accounting that mattered. The pieces together weighed very slightly less than the original, and that missing fraction had become energy. A tiny amount of mass releases an enormous amount of energy, which is the one relationship everybody can quote and almost nobody has seen a use for until this moment.

Then the detail that made it a weapon: splitting one nucleus releases neutrons, which can split others. A chain reaction. Within six years there was a bomb, and within a decade the first power stations.

Meitner refused to work on the bomb. The Nobel went to her collaborator alone.

Chapter 06

Penicillin — the mould that ruined an experiment

Alexander Fleming was untidy. He went on holiday leaving culture dishes of bacteria stacked by a window, and when he came back one was contaminated with mould that had drifted in.

Anyone would have thrown it out. What he noticed before doing so was that around the mould there was a clear ring where the bacteria had died.

He published, and very little happened for a decade — the substance was difficult to extract and unstable, and he was not a chemist. It took a team at Oxford, a war, and an enormous American industrial effort to turn it into something you could give to a patient. Their first trial patient, a policeman with an infection from a scratch, improved dramatically and then died when the supply ran out.

Before antibiotics, a scratch could kill you, childbirth was dangerous, and surgery risked infection that nothing could treat. It is probably the single intervention that has saved the most human lives, and it began with an unwashed dish.

Fleming used his Nobel lecture to warn that using too little of the drug would breed resistant bacteria. He was right, and we did it anyway.

Chapter 07

Flight — bicycle mechanics and a wind tunnel

The Wright brothers were not the best-funded people working on flight; they ran a bicycle shop. Their rival had government money and a serious scientific reputation.

Two things made the difference. First, they realised that the unsolved problem was not lift or power but control — everyone else was building machines that could get into the air and not be steered. They had watched birds twist their wingtips to bank, and built that in.

Second, when they found the published lift tables everyone was using gave results that did not match their gliders, they did not assume they were wrong. They built a small wind tunnel and measured everything again themselves, and discovered the standard tables were simply inaccurate.

The first flight lasted twelve seconds. Almost no newspaper covered it, and several that heard about it did not believe it. It took five more years of flying in public before the world accepted that it had happened.

Chapter 08

DNA — the shape that explains itself

By 1950 it was known that DNA carried inheritance, and its shape was unknown. Shape mattered, because whatever molecule carries a code has to be able to be copied.

The decisive evidence came from a technique of firing X-rays through a crystal and reading the pattern they scatter into. Rosalind Franklin, at King's College London, produced an image of remarkable quality — known as Photograph 51 — that indicated a helix and gave its dimensions.

The photograph was shown to Watson and Crick in Cambridge without her knowledge or permission. With it, and with their own model-building, they got the structure within weeks: two strands twisting round each other, with the rungs between them pairing in a fixed way, one particular base always opposite one other.

And that pairing rule is the whole thing. If the bases always pair the same way, then each strand carries the complete information to rebuild the other. Unzip the molecule and each half is a template for a new copy. The structure does not merely store the code; it explains how copying works. Their paper notes this in a single famously understated sentence.

Franklin died of ovarian cancer at thirty-seven, probably related to her X-ray work, four years before the Nobel — which is not awarded posthumously. Her contribution was described dismissively in a popular memoir for years afterwards and has only relatively recently been properly credited.

What to carry forward

How discovery actually works

Almost none of these people found what they were looking for. Becquerel wanted sunlight, Fleming wanted clean dishes, Hahn expected a heavier element. The skill is not finding the answer — it is noticing the anomaly and refusing to explain it away.

Unification is the prize

Newton's real achievement was not the mechanics but the claim that the same rules apply in the heavens and on Earth. Darwin's was that one simple process, repeated, produces apparent design. The big ideas make many separate things into one thing.

Measuring again

The Wrights' wind tunnel is the model. Everyone was using published tables that were wrong. When the data disagrees with the authority, the authority is sometimes what is broken — but you have to go and measure to find out.

And the credit

Meitner explained fission and got no Nobel. Franklin's photograph was decisive and shown without her consent. Mendel had the mechanism and was ignored for thirty-five years. The record of who discovered things is a record of who was in a position to be credited.
