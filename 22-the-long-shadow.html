The long shadow. Forty years of not quite ending the world, and the century coming apart and back together.

After 1945 two powers faced each other holding weapons that made victory meaningless, and spent two generations circling without ever fighting directly. Meanwhile the empires that had carved up the globe dissolved — sometimes by negotiation, often in blood — and a network built to survive a nuclear war quietly became the thing you are reading this on.

Part I

The standoff

Chapter 01

Allies who had nothing in common but an enemy

The United States and the Soviet Union won the war together and disliked each other thoroughly. One was a capitalist democracy, the other a one-party state that had killed millions of its own citizens in peacetime. What held them together was Germany, and when Germany was gone so was the reason.

Europe was divided along the line where the two armies had met. Stalin, whose country had been invaded twice in thirty years and had lost perhaps twenty-seven million people, wanted a belt of controlled states between himself and Germany, and installed them. The West saw an expanding tyranny swallowing countries. Both readings were defensible from where each stood, which is what made the misunderstanding so durable.

Germany itself was split, and Berlin — deep inside the Soviet zone — was split again, which made it the pressure point for the next forty years.

Chapter 02

The logic of weapons you cannot use

By the mid-1950s both sides had hydrogen bombs and were building missiles that could deliver them in half an hour. The arithmetic that followed is genuinely strange, and understanding it explains the whole period.

If either side attacks first, the other side's missiles launch before the attack lands. Both countries are destroyed. Therefore attacking first achieves nothing but your own destruction. Therefore neither attacks. The weapons keep the peace precisely because they are too terrible to use — a doctrine whose acronym, entirely intentionally, spelled MAD.

It worked, in the sense that the two never fought each other directly. It worked at the cost of a permanent low-level terror, enormous expenditure, and a series of moments when it very nearly did not work at all.

The two sides fought instead by proxy — Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, and across Africa and Latin America — in wars that were local in cause and global in sponsorship, and which killed millions of people who had no interest in the argument.

Chapter 03

Thirteen days, and the man who said no

In October 1962 an American spy plane photographed Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba, ninety miles from Florida. Kennedy's military advisers wanted air strikes and an invasion. He chose a naval blockade instead, and for thirteen days the world waited.

The public story is of two leaders stepping back, and that is broadly right — a deal was reached in which the missiles came out of Cuba and, quietly, American missiles came out of Turkey.

The part less often told happened underwater. An American destroyer was dropping practice depth charges to force a Soviet submarine to surface. The submarine, out of radio contact and with its air running out, carried a nuclear torpedo, and its captain concluded that war had begun and moved to fire. Launch required the agreement of three officers aboard. Two agreed. One, a flotilla commander named Vasili Arkhipov who happened to be on that particular boat, refused, and argued until the submarine surfaced instead.

It is as close as the species has come. The decision not to begin a nuclear war was taken by one man in a hot steel tube, and almost nobody knew his name for another forty years.

What the crisis produced

A direct telephone line between Washington and Moscow, because both sides realised they had been making decisions about the end of the world using diplomatic cables that took hours. Also the first serious arms-control treaties. Near-misses are where the safety machinery comes from — a pattern the disasters volume will repeat.

Part II

The empires dissolve

Chapter 04

India, and the line drawn in five weeks

Britain ruled a fifth of humanity and, after 1945, could no longer afford to. Independence came in 1947 — and with it, partition into India and Pakistan along religious lines.

The boundary was drawn by a British lawyer who had never been to India, working from outdated maps, in about five weeks, and its exact line was not published until after independence day. Millions of people discovered only afterwards which country their village was in.

What followed was one of the largest and fastest migrations in history: perhaps fifteen million people moving in both directions, and somewhere between several hundred thousand and two million killed in massacres, on trains, in villages where neighbours had lived together for generations. The consequences — three wars, a disputed border, two nuclear-armed states — are entirely present tense.

Gandhi, who had led the independence movement by refusing to meet violence with violence, spent the celebration fasting in an attempt to stop the killing, and was assassinated within months by a Hindu nationalist who thought him too conciliatory towards Muslims.

Chapter 05

Africa, and the borders nobody living had chosen

The European powers had divided Africa among themselves at a conference in Berlin in the 1880s at which no African was present, drawing borders with rulers across a map most of them had not visited. Those lines cut through language groups and enclosed peoples with nothing in common.

Independence came in a rush around 1960 — more than a dozen countries in a single year — and the new states inherited the lines, because the alternative was redrawing everything and fighting about it. Most also inherited economies built to extract one commodity and ship it out, administrations designed to control rather than serve, and almost no trained civil service, because colonial policy had not produced one.

Some transitions were peaceful. Others were not: Algeria's cost perhaps half a million lives, and the Congo's independence was followed within weeks by secession, foreign intervention and the murder of its first prime minister.

South Africa went the other way entirely, formalising racial separation into apartheid in 1948 and holding it for over forty years against internal resistance and international boycott, before negotiating its way out in the early 1990s — an outcome that almost nobody had believed possible without a civil war.

Chapter 06

Rwanda

In a hundred days in 1994, around eight hundred thousand people were killed in Rwanda, most of them Tutsi, most of them by neighbours, most of them with machetes and clubs.

The categories that organised the killing had been hardened by colonial administration, which had issued identity cards assigning every person to an ethnic group and favoured one over the other. Decades of politics had built on that. In the months before, a radio station had broadcast a steady stream of dehumanising propaganda, describing the target group as vermin and, in the end, giving names and locations.

A United Nations force was present and was refused reinforcement and a mandate to intervene; its commander's requests were denied. Governments that had the capacity to act avoided using the word genocide, because using it would have obliged them to act under a treaty they had signed.

It is the clearest case in modern history of a mass killing that the world watched in real time, had the means to interrupt, and chose not to.

Part III

How it ended, and what came after

Chapter 07

The night the wall opened by mistake

The Soviet system was failing economically long before it failed politically — command planning could build tanks and rockets and could not reliably supply shoes. Reform under Gorbachev was meant to save it and instead revealed how little support it had.

Through 1989 the eastern European states left one after another, mostly without bloodshed, as it became clear that Soviet tanks were not coming this time.

The Berlin Wall came down on 9 November, and the immediate cause was an administrative muddle. A spokesman announced relaxed travel rules at a press conference, was asked when they took effect, shuffled his papers, and said — incorrectly — that as far as he knew, immediately. It was broadcast live. Thousands of Berliners walked to the crossings. The guards had no orders, could not reach anyone with authority, and faced a crowd too large to stop. Eventually one commander opened the barrier on his own initiative.

People climbed the wall and attacked it with hammers. Two years later the Soviet Union voted itself out of existence.

Chapter 08

The network built to survive a war

The problem was posed in the 1960s: how do you build a communications system that keeps working when parts of it have been destroyed?

The answer was to abandon the idea of a route. Instead of a dedicated line from sender to receiver, you chop a message into small packets, label each with its destination, and let every packet find its own way across a mesh of connections, to be reassembled at the other end. If a node is gone, packets route around it. There is no centre to destroy, because there is no centre.

That design decision is why the internet has the character it has. A network with no centre is also a network with no gatekeeper, which is why it was so difficult for anyone to control once it escaped the laboratory.

The last piece arrived in 1989, when a physicist at a European research institute wanted a way for scientists to share documents and wrote a system of pages, links and addresses. He gave it away rather than patenting it. Within a decade it had rearranged commerce, publishing, politics and private life, and — as Volume 19's printing press should have taught us — it also destabilised whoever had previously decided what was true.

The pattern, one last time

Cheaper copying, collapsing authority, a period of chaos while people work out whom to trust. Gutenberg produced a century of religious war. We are perhaps thirty years into our version, and it would be unwise to assume we are through the worst of it.

Reference

What to carry forward

Why they never fought

Weapons that guaranteed mutual destruction made a first strike pointless. The peace was held by terror, at the price of proxy wars that killed millions of people with no stake in the quarrel.

How close it came

Not Kennedy and Khrushchev — a Soviet submarine officer named Arkhipov, out of contact, refusing to authorise a nuclear torpedo while two colleagues agreed to fire. Near-misses are where the safety machinery comes from.

What decolonisation inherited

Borders drawn in Berlin by people who had never visited, economies built to extract one commodity, and administrations designed to control rather than serve. Partition's line was drawn in five weeks by a man who had never been to India.

The two endings

The wall opened partly by administrative accident, on live television, because no one could reach anybody with authority. And a network designed to have no centre — so it could survive attack — turned out to have no gatekeeper either.
