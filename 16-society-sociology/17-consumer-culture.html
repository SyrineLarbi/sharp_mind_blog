Consumer culture. When what you buy starts to say who you are.

People have always bought things. What is new, in historical terms, is a society in which buying is a main way of showing who you are, a main source of everyday pleasure, and the engine that keeps the economy running. That arrangement has made billions of people materially better off than any generation before them. It has also produced mountains of waste, a constant low hum of wanting, and a planet under strain. This file explains how consumer culture arose, the psychology that drives it, and why the arguments about it are so hard to settle.

Question 1 · What is it?

A society organised around buying

Consumer culture is not the same as consumption. Everyone consumes. A consumer culture is one where goods carry meaning, and that meaning shapes identity, status and politics.

Chapter 01

The shop where you could just look

In the 1850s a Paris shop called Le Bon Marché, run by Aristide Boucicaut and his wife Marguerite, did several things that now seem obvious. Prices were fixed and marked on the goods, so you did not have to bargain. You could walk in and browse without any intention of buying. You could return what you did not want. The store held seasonal sales, put on displays and grew into a palace of goods spread over several floors.

The novelist Émile Zola set his 1883 novel Au Bonheur des Dames ("The Ladies' Paradise") in a store modelled partly on it. He saw the department store as a machine for creating desire, and he was half admiring and half horrified. That double reaction is the story of consumer culture in miniature. Shopping stopped being a chore to meet needs and became an experience and a leisure activity.

Chapter 02

Consumption, consumerism, consumer culture

Three words are often mixed up. Consumption simply means using up goods and services: eating bread, wearing shoes, taking a train. Consumerism has two meanings: the movement to protect buyers (safety rules, honest labels), and the belief that buying more is good for people and the economy. Consumer culture is the wider pattern in which goods become a language.

Think of a pair of trainers. Their use is to protect your feet. But the brand, the colour, whether they are new or vintage, and what they cost all send messages: about your age, your taste, your income, the music you like and the group you belong to. Sociologists say goods have use value (what they do) and sign value (what they mean). In a consumer culture, sign value often matters more.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Status, taste, advertising and the treadmill

Four mechanisms keep consumer culture going: goods signal status, taste marks social groups, advertising attaches meaning to products, and satisfaction fades faster than we expect.

Chapter 04

Conspicuous consumption: buying to be seen

In 1899 the American economist Thorstein Veblen published The Theory of the Leisure Class. He coined the phrase conspicuous consumption: spending that is meant to be seen, in order to display wealth and rank. A rich family's large house with servants who did nothing useful was not a waste, in Veblen's view. Showing that you could afford waste was the point.

His idea explains a puzzle in economics. Normally, when a price rises, people buy less. But for some luxury goods, a higher price makes the product more attractive, because the price itself is the message. Economists now call these Veblen goods. A handbag that costs a month's salary is partly a bag and partly a public receipt.

Status signalling has not disappeared as societies got richer. It has changed form. When almost everyone can afford a car, the signal moves to the brand, the model, the rarity, or to things that are hard to buy at all: travel to unusual places, a particular diet, time. As goods get cheaper, status hunts for what is still scarce.

Chapter 05

Taste as a badge of class

The French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu went further in Distinction (1979), based on large surveys of French tastes. He argued that what people like, from music to food to furniture, is not purely personal. It is learned from family and school, and it marks which class you come from. He called the knowledge and taste that give social advantage cultural capital.

The useful insight is that taste often works by distance. Groups with more cultural capital tend to prefer what is restrained, rare or "authentic", partly because it separates them from what is popular. When something becomes mass-market, those groups move on. This is why fashions travel and then are abandoned, and why "I don't care about brands" can itself be a carefully chosen signal. For the class side of the story, see Social class.

Chapter 06

Advertising: selling the meaning, not the thing

Early advertising mostly gave information: what the product did and what it cost. In the twentieth century, advertisers learned to sell feelings instead. A well-known example comes from Edward Bernays, a pioneer of public relations. In 1929, working for a tobacco company, he arranged for young women to smoke publicly during the New York Easter parade, and the press reported the cigarettes as "torches of freedom". A product was linked to women's liberation.

Modern branding follows the same logic. A brand is a promise and a story attached to a product so that buying it feels like joining something. Advertisers rarely sell soap; they sell being clean, attractive and admired. Online advertising added two new powers: ads targeted at individuals using data about their behaviour, and influencers whose recommendations look like advice from a friend. Volume 47 explains what data is collected to make that possible (Privacy — what is actually collected).

Chapter 07

The hedonic treadmill: why the new thing stops thrilling

In 1971 the psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell described what is now called the hedonic treadmill. People adapt quickly to improvements in their circumstances. The new phone, the bigger flat, the pay rise: each brings a burst of pleasure that fades as it becomes normal. Then the person wants the next thing, and the treadmill keeps turning.

Adaptation is not complete, and it varies by what you buy. Research on happiness tends to find that spending on experiences (trips, meals with friends, lessons) and on other people leaves a longer glow than spending on objects, and that buying back time, such as paying to avoid a hated chore, can raise wellbeing. These are average tendencies, not laws.

A contested finding

In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin pointed out that, while richer people within a country report being happier than poorer people, average happiness in some countries did not seem to rise as the country got richer over decades. This "Easterlin paradox" is still debated. Some later studies using more countries and longer data find that happiness does rise with income, though more slowly at higher levels. The safe statement is that money matters most when you have little of it.

Chapter 08

Credit, newness and planned obsolescence

Two more mechanisms keep the cycle going. The first is consumer credit: credit cards, instalment plans and "buy now, pay later" services let people consume before they have earned the money. That speeds up spending and also creates debt that some households cannot manage.

The second is planned obsolescence: designing products to need replacing. The most famous documented case is the Phoebus cartel, formed in 1924 by leading light-bulb makers, which agreed to limit the life of standard bulbs to about 1,000 hours. Today the debate is usually about softer forms: phones that are hard to repair, batteries that cannot be replaced, software updates that stop for older devices. There is also psychological obsolescence, when a working product is replaced simply because it looks out of date. Fast fashion, with new collections every few weeks, is the clearest example.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Jobs, the planet, and the self

Consumer culture is not just a matter of personal taste. Economies depend on it, the environment pays for it, and it shapes how people judge themselves.

Chapter 09

The economy runs on shopping

In the United States, household spending makes up roughly two-thirds of GDP, the total value of what the economy produces. In most rich countries the share is lower but still the largest single part. When people stop buying, factories cut shifts, shops close and workers lose jobs, which makes people buy even less. The economist John Maynard Keynes described this trap in the 1930s. It is sometimes called the paradox of thrift: saving is wise for one family, but if everyone saves at once, the whole economy shrinks.

That is why governments in recessions often try to encourage spending, and why consumer confidence surveys are watched closely. It is also why criticising consumer culture is politically awkward. Much of modern employment exists to make, move, sell and service the things people buy. See GDP for how the measure works.

Chapter 10

The material bill

Every product has a footprint: the energy, water, land and materials used to make it, move it, and dispose of it. Studies of household footprints consistently find that richer households and richer countries account for a far larger share of resource use and greenhouse-gas emissions than poorer ones. Clothing is a vivid case. Garments are worn fewer times than in the past, and large volumes of used clothing end up in landfill or are shipped abroad, where much of it cannot be resold.

The difficulty is that the environmental cost is spread out and delayed, while the pleasure is immediate and personal. No single purchase looks harmful. The harm lies in the total. This is why the debate keeps returning to systems, such as product design, repair rules and taxes, rather than to individual willpower alone. See Sustainable cities for the urban side.

Chapter 11

Identity on the shelf

For many people, buying is a way to build and express a self: the clothes of a subculture, the gear of a hobby, the furniture of a life you want to live. This is not shallow in itself. People have always used objects to mark identity, from jewellery to uniforms.

Problems appear when identity depends heavily on buying. Psychologists who study materialism, meaning a strong focus on money, possessions and image, such as Tim Kasser in The High Price of Materialism (2002), report that people who rank these goals highest tend, on average, to report lower wellbeing. The direction of cause is debated: unhappiness may push people towards buying as much as buying causes unhappiness. Social media adds a constant flow of comparison, which is discussed in Social media.

Chapter 13

The critics and the defenders

The critical tradition

Thinkers of the Frankfurt School, such as Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, argued in the 1940s that a "culture industry" produced standard pleasures that kept people passive. The economist John Kenneth Galbraith, in The Affluent Society (1958), argued that companies create the very wants they then satisfy, while public goods such as parks and schools are starved.

The defence

Defenders reply that people are not puppets. They choose, reject and reshape products, often in creative ways, and fashions are frequently started by ordinary people rather than companies. Consumer markets have also lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, and those who criticise buying are usually people who already have enough.

Both sides have a point. People are active choosers, and their choices are shaped by pressures they did not choose. A useful habit is to ask, of any purchase, which value is doing the work: the use, the sign, or simply the pleasure of buying something new.

Chapter 15

The big argument: greener buying or less buying?

The central live debate is between green growth and degrowth. Green-growth supporters argue that economies can keep growing while cutting their environmental impact, by shifting to clean energy, efficient products and services rather than stuff. They point to rich countries where emissions have fallen while GDP rose. Degrowth supporters argue that these cuts are too slow and too partial, and that rich societies must consume less material overall.

Most economists sit nearer the green-growth side, but the scale of the gap between today's trends and climate targets keeps the question alive. Meanwhile, culture itself may shift. Status can attach to new things: the well-repaired coat, the low-carbon holiday, the skill rather than the gadget. Consumer culture has changed its signals many times before, and it may change again without anyone deciding it should. For the economics, see Economic growth and Future of the global economy.

The core idea

In a consumer culture, goods carry sign value as well as use value. Buying signals status and taste, advertising attaches meaning to products, and the hedonic treadmill keeps desire renewing itself. The economy depends on this cycle, and the environment pays for it.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Household spending is roughly two-thirds of US GDP.
  • Veblen named conspicuous consumption in 1899.
  • The Phoebus cartel (1924) agreed to limit bulb life to about 1,000 hours.
The common mistake

Treating consumer culture as either pure manipulation or pure free choice. People choose actively, but inside pressures they did not design.
