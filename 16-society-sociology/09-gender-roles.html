Gender roles. The expectations attached to being a man or a woman, and why they move.

Every society has ideas about what men and women should do: who earns, who cooks, who fights, who cries, who leads, who looks after the baby. These ideas feel natural to the people who hold them. Yet they vary enormously across cultures and have changed dramatically in the last hundred years. Some of that change is widely welcomed. Some is fiercely disputed. Understanding gender roles means asking where they came from, how they are passed on, what they cost and to whom, and how much of them rests on biology.

Question 1 · What is it?

Expectations attached to sex

A gender role is the set of behaviours, tasks and traits a society expects from people because they are men or women.

Chapter 01

The day Iceland stopped

On 24 October 1975, a large majority of women in Iceland refused to work. They did not go to their jobs, and they did not cook, clean or look after children at home. Banks, factories, shops, schools and newspapers struggled to function. Fathers took their children to work and fed them sausages, which were easy to prepare. Tens of thousands of women gathered in the centre of Reykjavik.

The protest made visible something that normally goes unseen: how much of a society's work, paid and unpaid, depends on women. Five years later, Iceland elected Vigdís Finnbogadóttir, the world's first democratically elected female president. Gender roles are often invisible until they are interrupted. The day off showed that the division of labour between the sexes is not a private detail. It holds up the whole economy.

Chapter 02

Sex, gender and roles

Many social scientists distinguish sex, the biological differences between males and females such as chromosomes, hormones and reproductive organs, from gender, the social meanings attached to those differences. The British sociologist Ann Oakley made this distinction widely known in 1972. A gender role is the part of gender that concerns behaviour: what men and women are expected to do. A gender norm is an unwritten rule that enforces it, such as "boys don't cry" or "a good mother stays home".

The idea that gender is partly made by society, not simply given by nature, had been put memorably by the French philosopher Simone de Beauvoir:

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

Simone de Beauvoir · The Second Sex (1949), in English translation

Beauvoir did not deny biology. She argued that what a society builds on top of it, the meaning of being a woman, is learned and imposed. How far that is true, and how much is rooted in biology, is one of the central debates covered in Chapter 07. The distinction between sex and gender is a tool for asking which differences are fixed and which are social.

Chapter 03

How much roles vary

The content of gender roles changes from place to place, sometimes in surprising ways. In some societies, trading in the market is women's work, as in parts of West Africa, where women traders have long been powerful economic figures. In others it is mostly men's. Weaving, farming, brewing and even medicine have been assigned to men in some cultures and to women in others. Some cultures recognise more than two gender categories. South Asia's hijra communities have existed for centuries, and Nepal, India and Pakistan have given legal recognition to a third gender in the last two decades.

Some patterns do recur across most known societies. Men have nearly always done most of the fighting in war and held most formal political power. Women have nearly always done most of the care of infants. People disagree about what these recurring patterns show. Some see them as evidence of deep biological tendencies. Others see them as the result of similar pressures, such as pregnancy and breastfeeding in societies without contraception or baby formula, that no longer apply in the same way. Variation shows that roles can change. Recurrence shows that they are not arbitrary.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Economics built the roles, and socialisation passes them on

Gender roles grew out of how people made a living, and they are taught to each new generation through families, schools, media and daily life.

Chapter 04

The plough and the long shadow of farming

The Danish economist Ester Boserup noticed in 1970 that farming systems shaped women's roles. Where crops were grown with hoes, as in much of sub-Saharan Africa, women did much of the field work. Where farmers used heavy ploughs pulled by animals, which need great upper-body strength, men did the field work and women's work centred on the home.

In 2013 the economists Alberto Alesina, Paola Giuliano and Nathan Nunn tested this idea with modern data. They found that people whose ancestors farmed with the plough still hold less equal attitudes about women's roles today, and women in those societies work outside the home less. The pattern held even among migrants' children living in other countries. Gender norms can outlive the economic conditions that created them by centuries.

Chapter 05

Separate spheres, and the U-turn

Industrialisation separated the home from the workplace. In the nineteenth century, the middle-class ideal in Europe and North America became "separate spheres": men in the public world of work and politics, women in the private world of home and family. Working-class women still worked, in mills, as servants and taking in laundry, but the ideal shaped laws and expectations. Married women in many countries could not own property, sign contracts or keep their own wages until the late nineteenth or twentieth century.

The American economist Claudia Goldin, who won the Nobel Prize in 2023, showed that women's paid work followed a U-shape over economic development. It is high in farming economies, where everyone works the land. It falls as industry grows and paid work moves to factories. Then it rises again as education spreads and office and service jobs grow. Two world wars pulled women into factories temporarily. Then came a quieter revolution. Goldin and Lawrence Katz showed that the contraceptive pill, available to young unmarried women in the US from the late 1960s and 1970s, helped women plan long careers in law, medicine and business, because they could delay marriage and children. Technology, education and economics changed women's roles at least as much as argument did.

Chapter 06

How roles are learned

Gender socialisation is the process by which children learn what their society expects of their sex. It starts early. Parents may talk differently to baby girls and boys, choose different toys and clothes, and react differently to crying or risk-taking. Schools, peers, books, films and advertising add more messages. By the age of five or six, most children have firm ideas about what "boys do" and "girls do", and they police each other strictly.

In 1987 the American sociologists Candace West and Don Zimmerman described gender as something people "do" in daily interaction, not simply something they "are". Choosing what to wear, how to sit, how to speak in a meeting, who pays for dinner: each act displays gender and is judged by others. People who break expectations often face a social cost. Studies have found, for example, that women who negotiate assertively for pay are sometimes judged less likeable than men who do the same. Roles last partly because everyone, including those who dislike them, is rewarded for following them and penalised for breaking them.

Chapter 07

Nature, nurture, and the honest middle

How much of the difference between men's and women's behaviour is biological? This question is debated, sometimes bitterly. Here are the main positions as fairly as possible.

The biological emphasis

Hormones such as testosterone affect the brain before birth and at puberty. On average, men are physically stronger, more likely to take physical risks and more physically aggressive. Large studies find average differences in interests: men, on average, lean more towards working with things, women more towards working with people. Some researchers have reported that the differences in career choices are larger in more gender-equal countries, which they suggest reflects freer expression of underlying preferences.

The social emphasis

Most measured psychological differences between the sexes are small, with large overlap. Many have shrunk as roles changed, such as the gaps in maths scores. Children learn gender expectations from birth, so it is very hard to separate nature from nurture. The "gender-equal countries" findings are disputed on methods, and one prominent study had to be corrected. Even real average differences say little about any individual.

Most researchers hold some version of a middle position. Biology creates some average differences, especially in physical traits. Culture then amplifies, reduces or reshapes them, and decides what they mean for jobs and status. The scientific disagreement is about the size of each part, not about whether both exist. An average difference between groups does not tell you what any particular man or woman can do or wants.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Roles shape pay, care, power and health

Gender roles decide who earns what, who does the unpaid work, who holds power and who is at risk. Their costs fall on both sexes, in different ways.

Chapter 08

The child penalty

Across OECD countries, women working full-time earn on average roughly a tenth less than men, measured at the middle of the pay scale. The gap is much smaller than in 1970 and varies a great deal between countries. Most of it is no longer explained by women having less education. In many countries, young women now have more.

The biggest single driver, according to a wide body of research, is children. A study of Denmark by Henrik Kleven and colleagues found that women's earnings fall sharply after the first child and stay about 20 per cent lower over the long run, compared with what they would have been, while men's earnings barely change. Similar child penalties appear across many countries. Goldin argues that much of the remaining gap comes from "greedy jobs", well-paid jobs that reward long and unpredictable hours. When a couple has children, someone has to be on call at home, and it is usually the mother. The modern pay gap is less about equal pay for the same job and more about who adjusts their career after children arrive.

Chapter 09

The second shift

In 1989 the American sociologist Arlie Hochschild published The Second Shift. She found that working mothers came home from paid work to a second job of housework and childcare, while their husbands did much less. Men's share has grown since then, but the gap remains. The International Labour Organization estimated in 2018 that, worldwide, women do about three times as much unpaid care work as men.

That has knock-on effects. Women are more likely to work part-time, which pays less per hour and offers fewer promotions. They take more time out for caring, which lowers their pensions. In ageing societies they also do most of the care for elderly parents, as Aging populations describes. Globally, about half of women of working age are in the paid labour force, compared with roughly three-quarters of men, according to ILO estimates. Unpaid work is the hidden foundation under the paid economy, and it is still mostly done by women.

Chapter 10

Power, safety and survival

Women held roughly a quarter of seats in national parliaments worldwide by the mid-2020s, up from about a tenth in the mid-1990s, according to the Inter-Parliamentary Union. Rwanda has had the highest share in the world, with women holding more than half the seats in its lower house. Women remain a minority of heads of government and company chief executives in almost every country.

In some places, gender roles remain a matter of life and death. In 1990 Amartya Sen wrote that more than 100 million women were "missing" from the world's population, because of sex-selective abortion, neglect of girls and unequal access to food and care, mainly in South and East Asia. Sex ratios at birth have since improved in several countries, but the imbalance persists. Violence by intimate partners affects a large share of women worldwide. Legal rules also still differ sharply. Saudi Arabia allowed women to drive only in 2018. In Afghanistan, girls have been barred from secondary school since 2021 and women face sweeping restrictions on work and public life.

Chapter 11

The costs to men

Gender roles also carry heavy costs for men, and these have received more attention recently. Men die younger than women in almost every country; globally the gap is about five years. Part of this is biological, but much is linked to behaviour expected of men: more smoking and drinking, more dangerous jobs, more violence and less willingness to see a doctor. Worldwide, men die by suicide at more than twice the rate of women, according to the WHO, and the gap is higher in many rich countries.

Boys and young men are also falling behind in education in most rich countries, as Education explains. Many lose the role of main breadwinner that their fathers and grandfathers held, without a clear new role to replace it. Researchers such as Richard Reeves argue that this deserves attention without reversing gains for women. Rigid roles harm both sexes. Men pay in health and isolation, women in money and power.

Chapter 13

The main positions in the debate

Public argument about gender roles usually involves a few broad positions. Social conservatives, often drawing on religious traditions, see distinct roles for men and women as natural or divinely ordered, and value the family built around them. They worry that changes have weakened marriage and lowered birth rates. Liberal feminists focus on equal rights and opportunities: equal pay, equal access to education, jobs and politics, and freedom from discrimination. More radical or structural feminists argue that the system of roles itself, often called patriarchy, meaning social systems in which men hold most power, needs deep change.

In many countries a newer debate concerns gender identity: how society should treat people whose sense of their gender differs from their sex at birth. Disagreements centre on questions such as women's sports, single-sex spaces and medical treatment for children. In England, a 2024 review led by the paediatrician Hilary Cass led to tighter rules on puberty blockers for under-18s. Views differ sharply between and within countries. This file does not settle these arguments. Most people, across positions, agree that no one should be denied education, safety or a livelihood because of their sex. The disagreements start after that.

Chapter 15

The unfinished half of the revolution

Many researchers describe the change in gender roles as a revolution that is half complete. Women have moved into paid work, education and public life in large numbers. Men have moved much less into unpaid care and the home. Where both halves move together, as in the Nordic countries, couples tend to share work and care more equally, and some studies suggest birth rates fall less sharply. Where women's lives changed but men's and workplaces did not, as in parts of East Asia, women often face a hard choice between career and family, and many delay or avoid both.

The coming debates will turn on that second half. They include how to make well-paid careers compatible with care, how to help boys and men who are struggling, how far law should go in shaping private choices, and how to respect people who want traditional roles alongside those who do not. The direction of the last century was towards choice. The open question is whether societies can make the choices real for everyone, including the ones who choose differently from the majority.

The core idea

Gender roles are the expectations a society attaches to being male or female. They grew out of how people made a living, are passed on through socialisation, and shape pay, care, power and health. Biology creates some average differences; culture decides how much they matter. Their costs fall on both sexes.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Women do about three times as much unpaid care work as men worldwide (ILO, 2018).
  • Women's earnings in Denmark stay about 20% lower long-term after the first child; men's barely change (Kleven and colleagues).
  • Women held roughly a quarter of parliamentary seats worldwide by the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Treating an average difference between the sexes as if it described every man and woman. The overlap is usually large, and averages say little about individuals.
