Religion and society. Not only what people believe, but what believing together does.

Most people discuss religion as a question of truth: is it right or wrong? Sociology asks a different question, one that believers and non-believers can explore together. What does religion do in a society? It turns out to do a great deal: it builds communities, shapes moral rules, carries identities across centuries, runs schools and hospitals, inspires reform and sometimes fuels conflict. This file looks at those roles, at how states handle religion, and at why the world is becoming more secular in some regions and more religious in others at the same time.

Question 1 · What is it?

Beliefs, practices and a community around them

For a sociologist, religion is a shared system of beliefs and practices about what a group treats as sacred, together with the community that keeps them alive.

Chapter 01

Two million people in white

Every year, close to two million Muslims from around the world gather in and around Mecca for the Hajj, the pilgrimage that every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able is expected to make at least once. Men put on the same simple white garments, two pieces of unstitched cloth. A banker and a farmer, a Nigerian and an Indonesian, become hard to tell apart. Together they walk, pray and perform the same rituals over several days.

Many pilgrims describe the experience as one of the most powerful of their lives, and say it leaves them feeling part of a worldwide community. Something similar, at different scales, happens at a Christmas midnight Mass, at a Hindu festival on the banks of the Ganges, or at a Jewish family's Passover meal. Religion is not only something people think. It is something they do together, and the doing creates a bond.

The French sociologist Émile Durkheim saw this clearly. In The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) he described religion as a system of beliefs and practices about sacred things, which unite those who share them into a single moral community. He called the charged feeling of shared ritual collective effervescence: the sense of being lifted out of yourself and into the group.

Chapter 02

Believing, behaving, belonging

Researchers often describe religion along three dimensions, and people can score high on one and low on another.

Believing

Holding beliefs about God, gods, the soul, the afterlife or the meaning of life.

Behaving

Practices: prayer, worship, fasting, pilgrimage, rules on food, dress and conduct.

Belonging

Identifying with a religious group and taking part in its community.

The British sociologist Grace Davie coined the phrase "believing without belonging" for a pattern she saw in Britain: many people kept some belief in God but stopped attending church. The opposite also exists. Some people in Scandinavia remain members of the national church and use it for weddings and funerals while saying they hold few religious beliefs.

The world's religious map, according to the Pew Research Center's estimates for 2020, looks roughly like this.

"Unaffiliated" does not mean atheist. It covers people who do not identify with any religion, and many of them still believe in God or a higher power, or pray. Chinese folk practice in particular is hard to fit into these boxes.

Chapter 03

Three founders, three answers

Sociology was born in the nineteenth century partly as an attempt to understand religion in a modernising Europe. Its three founding figures gave three different answers.

ThinkerWhat religion doesKey idea
Émile Durkheim (France)Holds society togetherShared rituals and sacred symbols create moral unity.
Karl Marx (Germany)Comforts the oppressed and helps keep them quietReligion eases suffering caused by social conditions instead of changing them.
Max Weber (Germany)Shapes how people live and workReligious ideas can change economic behaviour.

"Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people."

Karl Marx · introduction to A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right · 1844

The famous last sentence is often quoted alone as an insult. Read in full, the passage is more sympathetic: Marx saw religion as a real response to real pain, while arguing that it distracted from fixing the causes. Weber's best-known argument, in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905), was that certain Protestant beliefs encouraged hard work, saving and self-discipline, which helped modern capitalism grow. Historians still debate how far Weber was right, but his method, tracing how beliefs shape behaviour, became central to sociology.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rituals, commitment, institutions and change

Religions last for thousands of years because they solve practical problems of living together. They also change, and their place in society varies hugely from country to country.

Chapter 04

Why demanding religions can be strong

Many religions ask a lot of their members: fasting during Ramadan, keeping kosher food rules, giving a tenth of income, wearing distinctive clothes, giving up whole days to worship. From the outside these can look like pure cost. Some researchers argue they work as costly signals: visible, hard-to-fake proof of commitment.

The anthropologist Richard Sosis, with Eric Bressler, studied around two hundred communes founded in the United States in the nineteenth century. Religious communes tended to last much longer than secular ones. Among the religious communes, those that demanded more of their members, in rules about food, dress, property or contact with outsiders, survived longer still. Among secular communes, more demands did not help. One interpretation is that shared sacrifice for something seen as sacred builds trust that ordinary agreements cannot. If everyone is visibly paying a price, you can believe they will not walk away.

Chapter 05

Moral communities and trust between strangers

Religions spell out moral rules and give reasons to follow them when no one is watching. Some researchers, such as the psychologist Ara Norenzayan in Big Gods (2013), have argued that belief in watchful, moralising gods helped early societies grow beyond the size where everyone knew everyone, by letting strangers who shared a faith trust each other. This idea is debated: other scholars argue that large societies came first and moralising gods followed.

Whatever the history, the practical effect is visible. Religious networks have long carried trade and credit across distances, from medieval Jewish and Muslim merchant networks to Quaker businesses in eighteenth-century Britain. Shared faith works as a form of social capital, the trust and connections described in Communities, and a congregation is one of the most durable communities most societies have.

Chapter 06

How states handle religion

Every country has to decide how religion and government relate. There are several broad models, and many countries mix them. Details vary a great deal and change over time.

ModelHow it worksExamples
Established churchOne church has an official link to the state, but other faiths are freeEngland (Church of England), Denmark
Separation, neutral stateThe state does not favour any religion and protects free exerciseUnited States (First Amendment)
Strict secularismReligion is kept out of public institutionsFrance (laïcité, based on a 1905 law)
CooperationThe state recognises several faiths and collects taxes or funds services for themGermany
Religious stateReligious law or leaders shape the stateIran, Vatican City; Saudi Arabia's legal system is based on Islamic law
State controlThe state registers, supervises and limits religious activityChina

The differences produce very different public arguments. In France, debate often concerns religious symbols in schools and public services. In the United States, it often concerns whether laws burden religious believers or favour religion. No model is neutral in every eye: what one group sees as fair separation, another sees as hostility to faith, and what one sees as respect for tradition, another sees as privilege.

Chapter 07

Secularisation: the prediction and the argument

For much of the twentieth century, many sociologists believed in secularisation theory: as societies modernise, with science, education, wealth and cities, religion would lose its public role and eventually its hold on individuals. Weber had written of the "disenchantment of the world". In Western Europe, the prediction largely came true. Church attendance in countries such as Britain, the Netherlands and Sweden fell steeply over the twentieth century.

Elsewhere it did not, or not simply. The United States remained much more religious than Europe for decades. The Iranian Revolution of 1979, the rise of evangelical Christianity in politics, and the spread of Pentecostal churches in Latin America and Africa all ran against the prediction. The American sociologist Peter Berger, once a leading secularisation theorist, wrote in 1999 that the world was "as furiously religious as it ever was" in most places, with Western Europe and a cultural elite as the main exceptions.

More recently, the picture shifted again. In the United States, the share of adults with no religious affiliation rose from under one in ten in the early 1990s to roughly three in ten in Pew's 2023–24 survey, though Pew also reported signs that the Christian decline had levelled off. Most scholars now see secularisation as real in many rich countries but not as a universal law of modernity. Where security, education and individual choice rise, religious practice often falls, but not everywhere, and not always for good.

Chapter 08

The religious world is getting younger and more southern

One reason the world is not becoming steadily less religious is demography. Religious people, on average, have more children than the unaffiliated, and the regions where religion is strongest, such as sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and South Asia, have the youngest and fastest-growing populations. Muslims are, on average, the youngest of the major groups.

The result is a shift in the centre of gravity. A growing share of the world's Christians live in sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America, and the typical Christian today is more likely to be Nigerian or Brazilian than European. Pew projected in 2015 that by 2050 the number of Muslims would come close to the number of Christians worldwide. Projections like this depend on assumptions about birth rates and conversion, and can change, but the direction is widely accepted: the future of world religion will be decided in the global South.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Reform, welfare and identity

You cannot understand politics, history or social welfare in most of the world without understanding religion's role in them.

Chapter 09

A force for change, and for keeping things as they are

Religion has often driven reform. Quakers and evangelical Christians were central to the campaign against the Atlantic slave trade in Britain (see Abolition). The American civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s was organised largely through Black churches, and its best-known leader, Martin Luther King Jr., was a Baptist minister. In Poland in the 1980s, the Catholic Church sheltered the Solidarity movement against communist rule. In South Africa, Archbishop Desmond Tutu was a leading voice against apartheid.

Religion has also defended existing orders. Religious arguments were used to justify slavery, apartheid and the exclusion of women from public life, and religious institutions have resisted many social changes. Religious identities have been part of violent conflicts, from the Thirty Years' War in seventeenth-century Europe to modern civil wars. Scholars note that in many such conflicts religion is bound up with ethnicity, land and power, so it is rarely the only cause. Religion is not a single force pushing in one direction. It is a powerful source of meaning that people use for very different ends.

Chapter 10

The welfare state before the welfare state

Long before governments ran schools, hospitals and pensions, religious bodies did much of this work. Monasteries cared for the sick and poor in medieval Europe. Islamic charitable endowments, called waqf, funded schools, hospitals, fountains and inns across the Muslim world for centuries. Giving to the poor is a core duty in many traditions: zakat is one of the five pillars of Islam, and many Christians and Jews follow traditions of tithing or charity.

The role continues today. In many countries a large share of schools, hospitals and aid organisations are run by religious groups, and in parts of Africa faith-based groups provide a significant part of health care. In rich countries, congregations are among the biggest sources of volunteers. When religious participation falls, some of this volunteering and giving falls with it, and societies have to decide what, if anything, replaces it.

Chapter 11

Religion as identity

For many people, religion is less a set of beliefs than a statement of who they are: Irish Catholic, Bosnian Muslim, Sikh Punjabi, secular Jewish. In those cases religion can stay strong even when belief weakens, because it marks a people, a history and a loyalty. It often gets stronger when a group feels threatened.

That is why religion and nationalism are so often intertwined, and why religion keeps shaping votes. In many democracies, how often people attend worship predicts how they vote better than many economic factors. The American political scientist Samuel Huntington argued in the 1990s that future conflicts would be along "civilisational" lines shaped largely by religion. Many scholars rejected this as too simple, pointing out that the bloodiest conflicts often occur within religious traditions, not between them. The safer generalisation is that religion matters most politically when it overlaps with other identities and grievances.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Benefits and costs, as different groups see them

People disagree deeply here, often because they weigh the same facts differently. The fair approach is to set out the main claims on both sides.

Chapter 12

The social ledger

AreaBenefits often citedCosts often cited
CommunityStrong, lasting congregations; support in crisis; links across generations.Strong boundaries between members and outsiders; pressure on those who leave.
Meaning and wellbeingMany studies link regular attendance with higher reported life satisfaction and lower rates of some harmful behaviours.Part of this may be the community rather than the belief, and people who are already healthy may attend more.
MoralityShared moral codes, charity, a sense of duty to others.Moral rules that some see as discriminatory, for example on gender roles or sexuality.
InstitutionsSchools, hospitals and aid organisations serving millions.Scandals of abuse and cover-up in several religious institutions have damaged trust.
PoliticsMoral voice in movements for justice and peace.Use of faith to justify conflict, persecution of minorities or restrictions on others' freedoms.

Two cautions help. First, the same religion can look entirely different in different places, depending on its history, its relation to the state and whether its members are a majority or a minority. Second, secular societies are not free of the costs above: non-religious ideologies have also produced exclusion and violence. The useful question is less "is religion good or bad?" and more "under what conditions do religious communities bring out their best?"

Research on religious freedom points in one direction here. Societies that protect freedom of religion, including the freedom to change or leave a religion, tend to have less religious conflict than those where the state favours one faith and restricts others. This is a correlation, and causes run both ways, but it is a pattern many scholars accept.

Chapter 14

The open questions

Three debates are likely to shape the coming decades. The first is about what replaces religion's social roles where it declines. If congregations shrink, what will provide weekly community, rites of passage, charity and moral education? Some point to secular groups, online communities or the state. Others doubt anything matches religion's durability; see Communities.

The second is about religious freedom and equality. In many countries, courts are asked to balance freedom of religion against laws on discrimination, for example when a religious employer's rules conflict with an employee's rights. The balance differs by country and is likely to remain contested.

The third is about technology. Religious communities already stream services, run apps for prayer times and scripture reading, and debate how to treat artificial intelligence. Some people use AI tools to ask spiritual questions. Whether this deepens faith, weakens institutions or creates new forms of belief is unknown. The safest prediction is that religion will not disappear. It will keep changing shape, as it always has, and it will remain one of the main ways billions of people make sense of their lives.

The core idea

Sociologists study what religion does: it binds communities through shared ritual (Durkheim), can comfort and pacify (Marx), and shapes economic and daily life (Weber). It has three dimensions: believing, behaving, belonging. Secularisation is real in many rich countries, but demography is making the world's religious population younger and more southern.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World, 2020 (Pew): Christians a little under 3 in 10; Muslims and the unaffiliated about a quarter each; Hindus about 15%.
  • US adults with no religious affiliation: about 3 in 10 in 2023–24, up from under 1 in 10 in the early 1990s.
  • The Hajj draws close to two million pilgrims in recent years.
The common mistake

Assuming modernisation automatically ends religion. Secularisation is a strong trend in some places, not a law of history, and "unaffiliated" is not the same as atheist.
