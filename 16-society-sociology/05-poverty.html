Poverty. The oldest human condition, and the one that changed most in a single lifetime.

For almost all of history, nearly everyone was poor by today's standards. Then, in about two centuries, and especially in the last forty years, the share of humanity living in extreme poverty collapsed. Yet hundreds of millions remain in it, and in rich countries millions of people with jobs and phones still cannot afford rent, heating or a proper diet. Both facts are true. To understand poverty you need to know what is being measured, why being poor tends to keep people poor, and which remedies have actually worked.

Question 1 · What is it?

Not having enough, but enough for what?

Poverty is a lack of the resources needed for a decent life. The hard part is deciding what "decent" means, and whether it is the same everywhere.

Chapter 01

Two kinds of being poor

Picture a farmer in rural Malawi. Her family eats one or two meals a day, mostly maize porridge. The house has a mud floor and no electricity. Water comes from a well a long walk away. If the rains fail, the family goes hungry. If a child gets malaria, the cost of the clinic may mean selling the goat that was their savings. This is extreme poverty: a daily struggle for food, shelter and basic health.

Now picture a single father in a European city. He works part-time in a warehouse. He has a phone, a television and enough food most days. But after rent he cannot afford to heat the flat properly in winter, his children cannot join school trips, and one broken washing machine puts him in debt. By the standards of the Malawian farmer he is not poor. By the standards of his own society he clearly is. Both are real poverty, but they are different problems with different remedies.

Chapter 02

Absolute and relative poverty

Absolute poverty means falling below a fixed minimum needed to survive, such as enough calories and basic shelter. The same line applies in every country and every year, adjusted only for prices. Relative poverty means having much less than is normal in your own society, enough to be shut out of ordinary life. The idea is old. Adam Smith put it precisely in 1776:

By necessaries I understand not only the commodities which are indispensably necessary for the support of life, but whatever the custom of the country renders it indecent for creditable people, even of the lowest order, to be without.

Adam Smith · The Wealth of Nations, Book V (1776)

Smith's example was a linen shirt: an eighteenth-century labourer would be ashamed to appear in public without one, though the ancient Greeks lived comfortably without linen. Today the equivalent might be a smartphone needed to apply for jobs, or the internet a child needs for homework. The British sociologist Peter Townsend built on this in 1979, defining poverty as lacking the resources to take part in the activities and living conditions that are customary in your society. Poverty is not only about survival. It is also about being able to participate.

The adjustment for local prices is called purchasing power parity (PPP). A dollar buys much more food in rural India than in New York, so incomes are converted at rates that reflect what money actually buys. The median is the middle income: half the population earns more, half earns less. One consequence of relative lines surprises people. If everyone's income doubled overnight, relative poverty would not change at all, because the line would double too. Relative poverty measures inequality at the bottom, not hunger.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Why poverty tends to keep people poor

Poverty is not just a low number in a bank account. It creates conditions that make escaping it harder, through money, health, risk and even attention.

Chapter 04

Poverty traps

A poverty trap is any situation where being poor itself causes you to stay poor. Economists describe several. A farmer who cannot afford fertiliser gets low yields, so she cannot save for fertiliser next year. A child who is underfed learns less at school, so earns less as an adult. A family with no savings must sell its tools or animals after one illness, losing its means of earning. A worker in a remote village cannot afford to move to the city, where jobs pay more.

Poor people also pay more for the same things. They buy small quantities at higher unit prices, borrow from moneylenders at very high interest, and live in areas where services are worse. Even in rich countries, people without a bank account or a good credit score pay higher fees and higher interest. Poverty is expensive, which is one reason it lasts.

Whether poverty traps are common is debated. The economists Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo argued in Poor Economics (2011) that some traps are real and others are not, and that the only way to know is to test specific situations carefully. That approach, using randomised trials like those in medicine, won them a Nobel Prize in 2019, shared with Michael Kremer.

Chapter 05

The mental tax of scarcity

In 2013 the economist Sendhil Mullainathan and the psychologist Eldar Shafir published Scarcity, which argued that being short of money uses up mental capacity. When you are constantly juggling bills, part of your mind is always busy with them. That leaves less attention for planning, self-control and learning.

In one study, sugarcane farmers in India took reasoning tests before the harvest, when they were short of money, and after it, when they had been paid. The same farmers scored noticeably worse before the harvest. Some later studies have found weaker effects, so the size is debated. But the central idea has changed how many policymakers think. Decisions that look careless from outside may be the result of a mind already overloaded by scarcity, not of bad character. This is a key reason why complicated forms and conditions in welfare systems can backfire.

Chapter 06

Individuals or structures?

The oldest argument about poverty is about blame. Is it caused mainly by individual choices, or by forces beyond individuals' control?

Individual explanations

Poverty is linked to behaviour: leaving school early, not working, family breakdown, addiction. In 1959 the anthropologist Oscar Lewis described a "culture of poverty", passed from parents to children, that made escape harder. Many people across politics believe personal responsibility matters and that help should encourage work.

Structural explanations

Poverty is caused by low wages, lack of jobs, discrimination, poor schools, high housing costs and weak safety nets. The same person would have a very different life in a different country or decade. Critics of the "culture of poverty" idea say it blamed the poor for conditions they did not create.

Most researchers now see the two as connected. Structures shape the choices people face, and choices then affect outcomes. Comparisons between countries are revealing here. Poverty rates among single parents or among people out of work differ hugely between, say, Denmark and the United States, even though behaviour in the two countries is not that different. Policy changes outcomes for the same behaviour, which shows that structure matters a great deal.

Chapter 07

Poverty in rich countries

In rich countries, poverty is rarely about starvation. It is about housing, debt, insecure work and exclusion. A growing share of the poor are the working poor: people in jobs whose wages, hours or job security are too low to keep a family above the line. Housing costs are often the decisive factor. Two families with the same income can have very different lives depending on whether they pay low social rent or high private rent.

The US official poverty line has a curious history. It was designed in the 1960s by Mollie Orshansky, a government statistician, as roughly three times the cost of a minimal food budget, because families then spent about a third of their income on food. The line is still updated for inflation but rests on that old formula. Since 2011 the US also publishes a Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts tax credits and benefits and subtracts costs such as healthcare and childcare. How you measure poverty decides how big the problem looks, and which policies appear to work.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The great escape, and the people it has not yet reached

The fall in extreme poverty is one of the biggest changes in human history. Its limits, and the damage poverty still does, are why it remains a central issue.

Chapter 08

The fastest fall in history

Economic historians estimate that in the early nineteenth century, the large majority of humanity lived in what we would now call extreme poverty. As recently as 1990, more than a third of the world's people, close to two billion, lived below the World Bank's extreme poverty line. By the early 2020s the share had fallen to under a tenth, even though the world population had grown by more than two billion.

Most of the fall came from economic growth in Asia. China alone accounts for an estimated 800 million people leaving extreme poverty since the late 1970s, through farm reforms, export manufacturing and massive investment. India, Indonesia, Vietnam and Bangladesh also cut poverty sharply. In 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in more than two decades. The decline has since resumed, but much more slowly. Growth did most of the work, but growth alone does not reach everyone.

Chapter 09

Where poverty remains

Extreme poverty is now concentrated in a narrower set of places. More than half of the world's extremely poor people live in sub-Saharan Africa, where populations are growing fast and growth per person has often been slow. Many of the rest live in countries affected by conflict or weak government, such as parts of the Sahel, Yemen, South Sudan and Afghanistan. Poverty there is not just low income but insecurity, displacement and the collapse of services.

Above the extreme line there is a much larger group. Close to half of humanity lives on less than about seven or eight dollars a day, a level at which one shock can push a family back down. The Multidimensional Poverty Index estimated in the mid-2020s that roughly 1.1 billion people were poor when health, schooling and living conditions are counted together. Climate change adds a new pressure, because droughts, floods and heat hit farmers and the urban poor first. The remaining poverty is harder to reach than the poverty that has already been overcome.

Chapter 10

What poverty does to people and societies

Poverty harms health at every age. Poor children are more likely to be stunted, meaning too short for their age because of chronic undernutrition, which affects brain development and adult earnings. Long-term stress in childhood can affect learning and health for decades. Poor adults die younger, in rich and poor countries alike. See Health inequality and Child health.

The Indian economist Amartya Sen, who won the Nobel Prize in 1998, changed how poverty is understood. He witnessed the 1943 Bengal famine as a child, and later argued that famines are usually caused not by a lack of food but by people losing the ability to buy it, for example when wages collapse or prices soar. He also observed that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that face elections and criticism act. His capability approach defines poverty as a lack of real freedom to live a life you value: to be healthy, educated, safe and able to take part in society. Income is a means. The end is what people can actually do and be.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What works against poverty, and what it costs

There is no single cure. Each remedy has evidence for it and real drawbacks.

Chapter 12

What went right, and what went wrong

What went right was mostly growth plus basic services. Countries that combined rising incomes with schools, clinics, roads and clean water cut poverty fastest. Cash transfers, once seen as naive, have become one of the best-tested tools in development. And measurement improved: household surveys, satellite images and mobile data now show where poverty is with far more precision than before.

What went wrong includes decades of aid projects that were designed far from the people they were meant to help, and structural adjustment programmes in the 1980s and 1990s, when lenders required poor countries to cut spending in return for loans. Critics argue these cuts hurt health and education. Defenders say the countries' finances left little choice. In rich countries, many welfare systems became so complex that eligible people do not claim what they are owed. The biggest lesson of the last forty years is humility: test what works, and listen to the people who are poor.

Chapter 14

The debates to watch

The first debate is about where the line should be. Critics argue that a few dollars a day is too low to mean a decent life, and that progress looks better than it is. Others answer that the extreme line is meant to measure the very worst deprivation, and that its fall is real and worth celebrating. Both points can be held at once: extreme poverty fell dramatically, and billions of people remain poor by any reasonable standard.

The second is about growth versus redistribution. In poor countries, growth remains the strongest force, which is why Economic development matters so much. In rich countries, poverty is more about how resources are shared, through wages, housing and benefits. The third is about technology and work. If automation and AI make some workers less needed, pressure for ideas like basic income will grow. If they raise productivity broadly, they could reduce poverty further. Nobody can yet say which effect will dominate. What history shows is that poverty is not fate. Its level depends on growth, institutions and choices that societies make.

The core idea

Poverty can be absolute (below a fixed survival minimum) or relative (shut out of normal life in your society). It persists through traps: lack of savings, poor health, high costs and the mental load of scarcity. Extreme poverty fell dramatically, mostly through growth in Asia, but what remains is concentrated and harder to reach.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than a third of humanity was in extreme poverty in 1990; roughly a tenth by the mid-2020s.
  • The World Bank's extreme poverty line was raised to $3.00 a day in 2025.
  • More than half of the extremely poor now live in sub-Saharan Africa.
The common mistake

Thinking poverty in rich countries "isn't real" because people own phones. Relative poverty is about exclusion, and it damages health and children's chances in measurable ways.

Can you explain why doubling everyone's income would not reduce relative poverty at all? If you can, you understand the difference between the two main ways of measuring it.

Absolute poverty
Living below a fixed minimum needed for basic survival.

The World Bank's term for living below its international line, set at $3.00 a day in 2025.

A way of converting money between countries based on what it actually buys.
Median income
The middle income: half of people earn more, half less.
Poverty trap

The mental load of constant shortage, which leaves less attention for other things.
Working poor
People in work whose income still leaves them below the poverty line.
