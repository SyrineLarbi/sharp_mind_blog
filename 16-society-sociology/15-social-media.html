Social media. A public square, a newspaper and a slot machine, all in one pocket.

In about twenty years, a handful of companies built the largest communication systems in human history. Billions of people now use them to talk to family, follow the news, find work, organise protests, sell products and argue with strangers. They did not exist when most adults alive today were born. This file explains how social media actually works, why some content spreads and some does not, what the evidence says about its effects on politics and mental health, and how governments are trying to set rules for it.

Question 1 · What is it?

Platforms where users make the content and algorithms choose who sees it

Social media are online services where people create and share content, connect with others, and receive a stream of posts chosen by the service.

Chapter 01

The protest that proved the point

In September 2006, Facebook was a site mainly for students. To see what friends were doing, you visited their profile pages one by one. Then the company launched the News Feed: a single page, updated constantly, showing what all your friends had just done. Users were furious. It felt like spying. Groups protesting against the News Feed quickly gathered hundreds of thousands of members.

There was an irony. The protest groups spread so fast precisely because the News Feed told everyone when their friends joined one. And within a short time, people were spending more time on Facebook, not less. The company added privacy controls and kept the feed. Almost every social platform since has been built around one.

The episode captured something that would define the next two decades. People often dislike what social media does to them while using it more and more. The feed was not just a feature. It turned a directory of people into a stream of content that never ends.

By 2025, industry estimates counted around five billion social media user accounts worldwide. That is not quite the same as five billion people, because many people have several accounts, but it means a clear majority of humanity uses these services. Facebook alone reports around three billion monthly users. In China, a separate set of platforms, such as WeChat and Douyin (the Chinese version of TikTok), dominates, because many Western platforms are blocked there.

Chapter 03

You are not the customer

Most social media are free to use. They make money mainly by selling advertising, targeted at users using what the platform knows about them: their interests, location, contacts and behaviour. The more time users spend, and the more the platform learns about them, the more it can earn. This is often called the attention economy: a market in which the scarce resource being competed for is human attention.

"A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

Herbert Simon · American economist and psychologist · 1971

Simon wrote this decades before social media, but it describes the business well. A common saying puts it more bluntly: if you are not paying for the product, you are the product. That is slightly unfair, because users do receive real value. But it points to the key fact. A platform's income depends on engagement, and its design choices follow from that.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Feeds, ranking and what spreads

Behind every feed is a ranking system deciding, from thousands of possible posts, which few you will see. What it rewards shapes what people post.

Chapter 04

The algorithm that picks your feed

Early feeds were mostly chronological: newest posts first. As users followed more accounts, there were too many posts to see, so platforms began to rank them. An algorithm, a set of rules run by a computer, now predicts for each post how likely you are to like, comment, share, watch to the end or stay on the app. It shows you the posts with the highest predicted value first. These systems are built with machine learning and updated constantly.

A second shift came with TikTok. Older networks mainly showed you posts from people you had chosen to follow, called the social graph. TikTok's "For You" feed shows content from anyone, chosen by what you watch, called the interest graph. It learns quickly from tiny signals, such as how long you pause on a video. Other platforms copied the model. Increasingly, what you see depends less on who you know and more on what the algorithm predicts will hold you.

Chapter 05

Why outrage and falsehood travel well

If engagement drives the feed, the question becomes: what engages people? Research has given some uncomfortable answers. A 2017 study led by the psychologist William Brady analysed hundreds of thousands of tweets about political issues. Each additional moral-emotional word, such as "evil", "shame" or "fight", was associated with roughly a 20% increase in how often a tweet was reshared.

A 2018 study in Science by Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral examined about 126,000 stories shared on Twitter between 2006 and 2017. False stories spread farther, faster and more widely than true ones. Falsehoods were about 70% more likely to be retweeted than the truth. The researchers found that automated accounts ("bots") did not explain the difference. People did. False stories tended to be more novel and surprising, and to provoke stronger emotions.

None of this means platforms set out to promote anger or lies. It means that a system tuned to maximise attention will tend to favour whatever human attention is drawn to, and we are drawn to threat, novelty and moral conflict. Platforms have since adjusted their ranking in various ways to reduce this, with mixed results.

Chapter 06

Designed to be hard to put down

Many features of social media apps are designed to keep people engaged. Infinite scroll removes natural stopping points. Notifications pull you back. Likes and follower counts make social approval visible and measurable. Pull-to-refresh, where you never know what new content will appear, works like a slot machine: psychologists have long known that rewards that come unpredictably keep people trying harder than rewards that come every time.

Social media also benefits from network effects: a service becomes more valuable the more people use it. If all your friends are on one platform, leaving it means losing contact, even if you dislike it. That is why a few large platforms dominate, and why individuals find it hard to quit even when they want to. Some former employees of large tech companies have publicly criticised these design choices; companies say they have added tools such as time limits and quieter modes.

Chapter 07

Echo chambers: less sealed than feared

In 2011 the activist Eli Pariser popularised the term filter bubble: the worry that personalised feeds would show people only what they already agreed with, sealing them into separate realities. A related idea is the echo chamber, a space where people hear only their own views repeated back.

The evidence is more mixed than the worry. Many studies find that most people's news diets are less one-sided than feared, and that the most extreme echo chambers involve a fairly small, highly active minority. In 2023 a group of academic researchers working with Meta published experiments run during the 2020 US election. Switching some users to a chronological feed, or reducing posts from like-minded sources, changed what they saw but did not measurably change their political attitudes over the few months studied.

Critics reply that short experiments on individuals cannot capture long-term effects on a whole society, such as how politicians and journalists change their behaviour to win attention online. The honest summary is that social media probably amplifies division in some ways, but it is not the sole or simple cause of polarisation, which in the United States began rising before social media existed. The debate is live.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Power, news and the young

Social media has changed how movements organise, how news reaches people, and possibly how a generation grows up. Each effect is both real and argued over.

Chapter 08

A megaphone for everyone, including the worst

Social media gave voice to people who had none. During the Arab Spring uprisings of 2010 and 2011, activists in Tunisia and Egypt used Facebook and Twitter to share videos and coordinate protests, though scholars debate how decisive the platforms were compared with long-standing grievances. The #MeToo movement, which spread in 2017, let many women publicly describe sexual harassment and abuse, and brought consequences for powerful men in several countries.

The same tools serve harm. In Myanmar, hate speech against the Rohingya Muslim minority spread widely on Facebook before and during the military's violent campaign of 2017. A UN fact-finding mission in 2018 criticised Facebook's role, and the company acknowledged it had not done enough. In 2018 it emerged that the political consulting firm Cambridge Analytica had obtained data on up to 87 million Facebook users without their clear consent. Governments and other groups also use social media for coordinated propaganda campaigns. The power to reach millions instantly is neutral; who uses it, and how platforms respond, is not.

Chapter 09

The teenage mental health debate

General education

This chapter summarises a scientific debate. It is not medical or parenting advice. Concerns about a young person's mental health should be discussed with a doctor or a qualified professional.

From around 2012, rates of reported anxiety, depression and self-harm among teenagers, especially girls, rose in the United States and several other countries. This was roughly when smartphones and social media became common among teens. The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt, in The Anxious Generation (2024), argued that the shift from a "play-based childhood" to a "phone-based childhood" was a major cause. He proposed norms such as no smartphones before high school and no social media before 16. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory warning about risks to young people's mental health.

Other researchers, such as the psychologists Candice Odgers and Amy Orben, argue that the evidence is weaker than it looks. Much of it is correlational: it shows that heavy users are more often unhappy, but not which causes which. Average effects found in large studies are small, and other factors, such as academic pressure, economic worries and changes in diagnosis, may also play a role. Many researchers in the middle think social media can harm some vulnerable young people, especially through sleep loss, social comparison and harmful content, while helping others find support. This is one of the most contested questions in social science today, and policy is moving faster than the science: Australia's ban on social media accounts for under-16s, passed in 2024, took effect in December 2025.

Chapter 10

Where people now get their news

For many people, especially under 35, social and video platforms are now the main gateway to news. The Reuters Institute's annual Digital News Report has tracked this shift for years, and reported in 2025 that in the United States, more people got news through social and video networks than through television. A growing share of that news comes not from news organisations but from individual creators and influencers.

This has upsides: more voices, faster reporting from the scene, and news reaching people who never read a newspaper. It also has costs. Advertising money that once paid for local journalism moved largely to platforms, and many local newspapers closed. Information on feeds arrives stripped of context, mixed with entertainment and ads, and ranked for engagement rather than importance. A feed is not an editor; Volume 16 explains how to build a better news habit (see Follow stories, not headlines).

Chapter 12

Who decides what can be said?

Every platform has to decide what to remove, what to label and what to leave. This is called content moderation, and it happens at huge scale, through a mix of automated systems and human reviewers. Any choice disappoints someone. Remove too little, and hate, scams and dangerous lies spread. Remove too much, and the platform is accused of censorship and political bias.

Countries handle this differently. In the United States, a 1996 law known as Section 230 largely protects platforms from being sued over what users post, and the First Amendment limits what the government can require them to remove. The European Union's Digital Services Act, fully in force since 2024, requires large platforms to assess and reduce risks such as illegal content and disinformation, and to open their systems to audits and researchers. Authoritarian governments use moderation demands to silence critics. The underlying conflict, between free expression and protection from harm, has no single correct answer; democracies weigh it differently, and details vary by country. See Technology law.

Chapter 14

From a technology to an institution

Every powerful communication technology has gone through a similar path. The printing press, as Volume 19 describes, was followed by a long period of religious conflict and pamphlet wars before societies developed norms, laws and institutions such as editors, libraries and copyright. Radio and television were followed by broadcasting regulation and public broadcasters. Social media, after about two decades, seems to be entering that phase: the period of cultural lag, described in Cultural change, in which norms and rules catch up with the machines.

What will the settled version look like? Nobody knows. Possible ingredients include clearer rights for users over their data and feeds, more transparency about how algorithms rank content, stronger protections for children, and new habits, such as the growing number of people who keep phones out of bedrooms or leave public platforms for private groups. The question is no longer whether social media changes society, but which of its changes societies choose to accept and which they decide to limit.

The core idea

Social media platforms are free services funded by advertising, so they compete for attention. Ranking algorithms show each user the posts predicted to engage them most, which tends to reward emotion, novelty and conflict. The effects on politics, news and teenage wellbeing are real but contested in size, and societies are now building rules to govern them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About five billion social media user accounts worldwide by 2025.
  • False stories were about 70% more likely to be retweeted than true ones (Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, 2018).
  • Each moral-emotional word raised resharing by roughly 20% (Brady and colleagues, 2017).
The common mistake

Blaming social media for everything, or for nothing. It amplifies some human tendencies and harms some groups more than others, and most of the strongest claims in either direction go beyond the evidence.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a platform that never decides to promote anger might still end up showing more of it? If you can, you understand engagement-based ranking.

The words, in plain words
Social media
Online services where users create and share content and connect with each other.
Feed
The constantly updated stream of posts a platform shows you.
Algorithm
A set of rules a computer follows; here, the system that ranks your feed.
Engagement
Any measurable reaction to a post: likes, comments, shares, watch time.
Attention economy
A market where companies compete for people's limited attention.
Social graph
The network of people you have chosen to connect with or follow.
Interest graph
A map of what you engage with, used to show content from anyone.
Network effect
When a service becomes more valuable the more people use it.
Filter bubble
The worry that personalised feeds show people only what they already agree with.
Echo chamber
A space where people mainly hear their own views repeated.
Misinformation
False or misleading information, shared with or without intent to deceive.
Content moderation
Platforms' decisions about what to remove, label or limit.
Creator
A person who makes content for platforms, often earning from ads or sponsors.
