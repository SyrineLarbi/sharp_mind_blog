Generational differences. Some are real, many are just age, and a few are pure marketing.

Every week brings a new headline about what "Gen Z wants", why "Millennials killed" some industry, or how "Boomers ruined" the housing market. Generations have become one of the main ways people explain social change. Some of these differences are real and important: people who grew up in different decades really did face different economies, technologies and values. But a great deal of what is blamed on generations is simply what happens to everyone at a certain age, or what happened to everyone in a certain year. This file gives you the tools to tell those apart.

Question 1 · What is it?

People born in the same years, shaped by the same times

A generation, in the social sense, is a group of people born in roughly the same period who share formative experiences. The idea is old. The labels are new.

Chapter 01

"OK boomer"

In November 2019, a 25-year-old member of New Zealand's parliament, Chlöe Swarbrick, was speaking about climate change when an older member heckled her. Without breaking stride she replied, "OK, boomer", and carried on. The clip went around the world. The phrase had already spread online as a weary reply by young people to older people's advice and criticism.

The moment showed how much generational identity had become a frame for argument. Young people felt that older generations had enjoyed cheap housing, free or cheap university and a stable climate, and then lectured them about hard work. Older people felt mocked for a world they had also struggled to build. Both sides were talking about real differences in circumstance, but both were also using a label to stand for millions of very different people.

Chapter 02

The idea of a generation

In 1928 the Hungarian-born sociologist Karl Mannheim wrote an essay called "The Problem of Generations". His key point was that being born in the same years is not enough. What makes a generation is shared experience during the formative years, roughly the late teens and twenties, when people are forming their views of the world. A war, a depression, a revolution or a new technology met at that age leaves a deeper mark than the same event met at fifty.

Mannheim also noticed that people of the same age can react to the same events in opposite ways. Young radicals and young conservatives in 1968 were the same generation, shaped by the same upheaval, but they drew different lessons. A generation shares questions more than answers.

Chapter 03

The labels, and how arbitrary they are

The most widely used labels in the English-speaking world are listed below, with the boundary years used by the Pew Research Center in the United States. Other organisations draw the lines a few years differently.

LabelBorn roughlyFormative events (US and Western Europe)

Baby Boomers1946–1964The post-war birth boom, rising prosperity, the 1960s, mass higher education
Generation X1965–1980Rising divorce, the end of the Cold War, the arrival of personal computers

Only the Baby Boom has boundaries set by an actual event: the jump in births after 1945 and the fall in the mid-1960s. The rest are conventions. The term "Millennials" was popularised by the writers William Strauss and Neil Howe in their 1991 book Generations, which argued that history moves in repeating cycles of generational types. Most historians and social scientists regard that cycle theory as unscientific, but the labels stuck. In 2023 Pew itself said it would be more careful about generational comparisons, because many supposed generational differences turn out to be something else.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Age, period and cohort: three causes that look identical

If young people today differ from older people today, there are three possible reasons. Telling them apart is the whole skill.

Chapter 04

The three effects

Imagine a survey finds that 25-year-olds use a new app far more than 60-year-olds. There are three explanations, and social scientists give each a name.

Age effect

People change as they age, whatever year they were born. Young adults move more, take more risks and adopt new things. The 60-year-olds may have been just the same at 25.

Period effect

Something happened that changed everyone at once. A pandemic, a recession or a new technology shifts all ages in the same direction, though not always by the same amount.

Cohort effect

People born in certain years carry a lasting mark from their formative years. This is a true generational difference, and it stays with them as they age.

A single survey at one moment cannot separate these, because age and birth year are perfectly linked: in 2026, every 25-year-old was born around 2001. To tell them apart, researchers need repeated surveys over many years, so they can compare people at the same age in different decades, and follow the same birth group as it ages.

How to read a generational claim
  claim: "Young people today are X"

  Were young people in 1990 also X at the same age?
     yes -->  probably an AGE effect (it will fade as they grow older)
     no  -->  Did older people today also become more X recently?
                yes -->  probably a PERIOD effect (everyone changed)
                no  -->  possibly a COHORT effect (a real generational difference)
Chapter 05

The formative years

Why would the years from about 15 to 25 matter so much? Research on attitudes supports what is sometimes called the impressionable years idea: views on politics, religion and society are most open to change in young adulthood, and tend to be more stable afterwards. This is also when people form lasting habits, music tastes and loyalties, which is why so many adults think the best music was released when they were about seventeen.

That makes the timing of big events crucial. Someone who was 20 during a war, a depression or a historic election may carry its lessons for life. Someone who was 50 during the same event is more likely to absorb it into views already formed. Generations are, in a sense, the fingerprints that history leaves on people who were young when it happened.

Chapter 06

Technology: real gaps, exaggerated claims

In 2001 the education writer Marc Prensky coined the phrase "digital natives" for young people who grew up with computers, and "digital immigrants" for their elders. The phrase was catchy and partly true: what you meet in childhood feels natural, and what you meet later feels like a tool you had to learn.

But research since has been sceptical of the strong version. Growing up with phones does not automatically make someone skilled at evaluating information, writing code or using software at work. Studies of students have found wide differences in digital skill within the same age group, often linked to family income and education. Familiarity is not the same as competence. The more important technology difference may be the environment young people grew up in: social media during adolescence, which is discussed in Chapter 11.

Chapter 07

Economic timing: born at the wrong moment

Some of the clearest cohort effects are economic. Research by the economist Lisa Kahn and others has found that people who graduate during a recession tend to earn less for many years afterwards than similar people who graduate in good times. They start in worse jobs, at lower pay, and the gap closes only slowly. Millennials in many countries entered work around the 2008 financial crisis, and many in southern Europe faced very high youth unemployment.

Housing shows a similar pattern. In many rich countries, house prices rose much faster than wages from the 1990s onward, so young adults today typically need many more years of income to buy a home than their parents did at the same age. Home ownership among young adults fell in places such as the UK and the US. When young people delay marriage, children or home ownership, part of the reason is often price, not values. That distinction matters for policy: you cannot change a generation's "attitudes" by lecturing it about thrift if the real cause is a housing shortage.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Myths, real changes and a new political divide

Generational thinking shapes how employers manage, how parents worry, how parties campaign and how countries share resources between young and old.

Chapter 08

"Kids these days": the oldest complaint

Adults have complained about the young for as long as there are records. A widely shared "quote from Socrates" complains that children love luxury, have bad manners and contradict their parents. It is almost certainly not by Socrates; it appears to come from a paraphrase written in the early twentieth century. But it survives because it sounds true to every generation of adults.

In 2019 the psychologists John Protzko and Jonathan Schooler studied why. They found that adults tend to think children today are worse in exactly the traits where the adults themselves are strong: well-read people think children read less, respectful people think children are less respectful. Part of the cause is that we compare real children today with a flattering memory of ourselves. The "kids these days" effect is partly a memory error, the kind described in Memory.

Chapter 09

Differences that do seem real

Long-running surveys do show some genuine cohort shifts, though they are usually gradual rather than sharp breaks between named generations.

AreaThe pattern in many rich countriesMainly cohort, period or age?
ReligionEach younger cohort is less religiously affiliated than the one beforeLargely cohort; people rarely become much more religious with age
ValuesMore emphasis on self-expression, tolerance and choice, less on deference and securityCohort, plus period shifts affecting everyone
Family timingLater marriage, later and fewer children, more living aloneMixed: economics, education and norms together
Risk behaviourTeenage drinking, smoking and early pregnancy have fallen in many countries since around 2000Cohort or period; causes debated
Social attitudesAcceptance of same-sex relationships rose sharplyBoth: younger cohorts more accepting, and older people changed too

The political scientist Ronald Inglehart, who helped build the World Values Survey, argued from the 1970s onward that people raised in security and prosperity shift from "materialist" priorities, such as economic and physical safety, to "post-materialist" ones, such as freedom and self-expression. His argument has held up broadly across many countries, though with exceptions. Another real cohort change is the Flynn effect, named after the researcher James Flynn: average scores on IQ tests rose by roughly three points per decade in many countries during the twentieth century, probably because of better nutrition, schooling and more abstract everyday thinking. In some countries the rise has recently stalled or reversed.

Chapter 10

Politics, wealth and the age divide

In several countries, age has become one of the strongest predictors of how people vote. In the UK's 2016 referendum on leaving the European Union, older voters were much more likely to vote Leave and younger voters to vote Remain. Similar age gaps have appeared in other elections. Whether this is a cohort effect, or the old idea that people grow more conservative with age, is debated; the evidence suggests both play some part, and their mix varies by country and era.

Wealth adds a second divide. In many rich countries, a large share of household wealth is held by people over 60, much of it in housing bought when it was cheaper. That wealth will pass to heirs over the coming decades, which means inequality between young people may increasingly depend on whether their parents own property. See Social mobility and Inequality.

Chapter 11

The smartphone generation debate

The most heated generational argument of the 2020s concerns young people's mental health. In the United States and several other countries, surveys show rising rates of reported anxiety and depression among teenagers, especially girls, from the early 2010s. The psychologist Jean Twenge, in iGen (2017), and the social psychologist Jonathan Haidt, in The Anxious Generation (2024), argue that the shift from play-based childhood to phone-based childhood, with social media at its centre, is a major cause.

Other researchers, such as the psychologist Candice Odgers, argue that the evidence is mostly correlational, that the measured effects of social media use are small on average, and that other causes, such as academic pressure, economic insecurity or changes in how distress is reported, may matter as much. This is a genuinely open scientific dispute. Meanwhile, many schools and governments have moved ahead with phone restrictions anyway. For more, see Social media.

Health note

This chapter describes a research debate. It is general education, not medical advice. Concerns about a young person's mental health should be discussed with a qualified professional.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A useful lens and a lazy stereotype

Thinking in generations can reveal how history shapes lives. It can also turn millions of different people into a cartoon.

Chapter 13

The differences inside a generation are bigger

A Millennial who grew up in a wealthy suburb in California, one from a mining town in northern England and one from Lagos share a birth decade and not much else. Even within one country, the differences between people of the same generation by class, education, region, religion and ethnicity are usually far larger than the average differences between generations.

The best use

Treat generation as one variable among several. Ask what happened to people born in these years, at what age, and whether the effect shows up in long-term data. Use it to understand circumstances rather than to judge character.

The worst use

Assume that a 24-year-old job applicant is entitled, or that a 60-year-old cannot learn new software. That is stereotyping by birth year, and in many countries age discrimination at work is illegal.

Most labels are also built from American data and exported. "Baby Boomers" makes less sense in countries where the birth boom came later or not at all, and the formative events of a Chinese, Algerian or Brazilian 40-year-old are very different from an American one. A generation is always a generation of somewhere.

Chapter 15

Fairness to generations not yet born

The newest version of the generational question is about people who do not exist yet. Decisions taken now about climate, public debt, pensions and resources will shape lives decades ahead. Some institutions have begun to give future generations a formal voice. Wales passed a Well-being of Future Generations Act in 2015 and created a commissioner to speak for them. In 2021 Germany's Constitutional Court ruled that the country's climate law did not do enough to protect the freedoms of young and future people, and the law was strengthened.

This idea of intergenerational fairness may become one of the century's big political themes, especially in ageing societies where the young are a shrinking minority. The healthiest form of the debate avoids blaming any generation for the world it was born into, and asks instead what each owes to the next. Every generation is both an heir and an ancestor. The final topic in this category, The future of society, takes that question further.

The core idea

A generation is a group shaped by shared experience in its formative years. Real generational (cohort) differences exist, but they are easily confused with age effects and period effects. Differences within a generation are usually larger than differences between them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Baby Boomers: born 1946–1964, the only label set by a real event.
  • Mannheim's essay on generations: 1928.
  • The Flynn effect: IQ scores rose about 3 points per decade in the twentieth century.
The common mistake

Seeing a difference between young and old today and calling it generational. Ask first whether the old were the same when they were young.

Test yourself

Someone says "Gen Z doesn't want to buy houses." Can you name the three possible explanations, and what evidence would tell them apart? If so, you can handle most generational claims.

The words, in plain words
Generation
People born in roughly the same years who share formative experiences.
Cohort
A group defined by a shared starting point, usually the years of birth.
Age effect
A difference caused by stage of life, which everyone passes through.
Period effect
A change that affects everyone at the same time, whatever their age.
Cohort effect
A lasting difference carried by one birth group because of when it grew up.
Formative years
Roughly the late teens and twenties, when lasting views are formed.
Digital native
A person who grew up with digital technology, a term that often overstates skill.
Post-materialism
Valuing freedom and self-expression over economic and physical security.
Flynn effect
The long rise in average IQ test scores during the twentieth century.
Scarring
The lasting earnings loss of people who start work in a recession.
Intergenerational fairness
The idea that each generation should not leave the next worse off.
Longitudinal survey
A study that asks the same questions over many years, needed to separate the three effects.
