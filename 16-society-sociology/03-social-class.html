Social class. The ranking nobody announces and almost everybody can read.

Most modern societies say they have no fixed ranks. Nobody is born a lord or a serf. Yet within a few minutes of meeting someone, people form a fairly accurate guess about their background from their accent, clothes, job, food and the words they use. Those guesses predict how long the person will live, how they vote and what their children will do. Social class is the name for this persistent ranking. It is partly about money, but not only about money, and that is why it is so hard to see clearly and so hard to escape.

Question 1 · What is it?

A rank built from money, work and respect

Social class is a group of people who share a similar position in the economy and similar life chances. It is one form of a wider pattern called stratification.

Chapter 01

Two job candidates

Two young people apply for the same job in a London law firm. Both have good degrees. One went to a private school, speaks in the accent of the professional class, plays tennis and has a parent who is a lawyer. The other grew up on a housing estate in the north of England, is the first in the family to attend university, and is unsure what to wear to the interview. On paper they are equal. In the room, the first one "fits". Studies of elite professions in Britain have found exactly this pattern: candidates from working-class backgrounds are under-represented even after accounting for qualifications.

Nothing illegal has happened. Nobody has said "we prefer the rich". But the outcome is a rank, passed from parents to children. Class works mostly through small, unspoken signals and advantages, not through formal rules. That is what makes it different from older systems of rank.

Chapter 02

Four ways societies rank people

Sociologists use the word stratification, borrowed from geology's layers of rock, for any system that ranks groups of people unequally. Class is only the most recent kind.

SystemWhat decides your rankCan you move?Examples
SlaverySome people legally own othersAlmost neverAncient Rome, the Atlantic slave trade
CasteBirth into a hereditary group, often tied to occupation and religious purityIn principle no; marriage usually within the groupThe traditional Hindu caste system of India and Nepal
EstateLegal rank: nobility, clergy, commoners, each with different rightsRarely, by royal favour or the ChurchMedieval Europe, France before 1789
ClassEconomic position: wealth, occupation, incomeYes in law; in practice, less than people thinkModern industrial and post-industrial societies

The table should not be read as a story of steady progress. Class systems still carry traces of older ranks. India's constitution of 1950 abolished "untouchability" and created reserved places in education and government jobs for disadvantaged castes, yet caste still shapes marriage, politics and work. Formal equality before the law does not end inherited advantage. It changes the form it takes.

Chapter 03

How to measure class

Researchers measure class in three main ways. Income is what you earn each year. Occupation describes the kind of work you do and the authority and security it brings. The British government uses an occupation-based scheme with categories running from higher managerial and professional jobs down to routine manual jobs. Wealth is what you own: houses, savings, shares, businesses. Wealth is usually far more unequal than income, and it is the part most easily inherited.

There is also subjective class: what people say they are. Large majorities in many countries call themselves "middle class", including people who are quite rich and people who are struggling. In Britain, many people in professional jobs still describe themselves as working class because of their family origins. Class is partly about where you stand and partly about where you feel you came from.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three thinkers, three answers, and a cycle

Almost every modern discussion of class builds on Marx, Weber or Bourdieu. Each one saw a different part of the machine.

Chapter 04

Marx: who owns, and who works

For Karl Marx, writing in the mid-nineteenth century, class was defined by one question: do you own the means of production, the factories, land and machines that create wealth, or do you have only your labour to sell? Owners, the bourgeoisie, earn profit from other people's work. Workers, the proletariat, receive a wage worth less than what they produce. Marx argued that this conflict of interest drives history, and he predicted that workers would eventually overthrow the system.

That prediction did not come true in the rich industrial countries he had in mind. Wages rose, a large middle class of clerks, managers and professionals appeared, and welfare states softened the conflict. The revolutions that did happen in his name, in Russia and China, created new hierarchies of party officials. The Yugoslav dissident Milovan Djilas called them "the new class" in 1957. Yet Marx's core insight survives in mainstream thinking: owning capital and selling labour are different positions, and the gap between them shapes how wealth is shared.

Chapter 05

Weber: class, status and power

The German sociologist Max Weber, writing in the early twentieth century, thought Marx's picture was too simple. He separated three dimensions of inequality. Class is your position in the market, including skills and education, not only ownership. Status is the respect or honour others give your group, through lifestyle, education or family name. Party is power through organisation, such as political parties and unions.

The three often go together but can come apart. A priest or a professor may have high status and modest income. A newly rich business owner may have money but be looked down on by "old money". A union leader may hold power without wealth. Weber's split explains why a plumber who earns more than a junior lecturer is still seen by many as lower in class. Class is economic, but it is lived through status.

Chapter 06

Bourdieu: three kinds of capital

The French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu gave the most influential modern account. In his 1979 book Distinction he argued that people hold three kinds of capital, meaning resources that can be used to gain advantage.

Economic capital

Money and property. The most obvious kind, and the easiest to count.

Cultural capital

Education, knowledge, manners, accent and taste: knowing which fork to use, how to talk to a teacher, which books to have read.

Social capital

Networks: who you know, and who will take your call or find your child an internship.

The key move is that the three convert into each other. Money buys a private education, which creates cultural capital, which wins a place at an elite university, which brings social capital, which leads to a well-paid job. Bourdieu also described habitus: the deep habits of taste, posture and expectation that people absorb in childhood and carry without noticing. Habitus is why the working-class candidate in Chapter 01 felt out of place. Taste looks personal, but it often works as a badge of class.

              |
              v

              |

   children's money, education, contacts  ----> loop repeats

Each link in the loop is small. Together they are powerful. Richer parents live in areas with better schools, because housing near good schools costs more. They read more to young children, pay for tutoring and know how admissions work. Their children gain internships through contacts. Then, because people tend to marry people like themselves, two advantaged families combine. Finally, wealth passes directly through gifts and inheritance, often when the child is buying a first home.

None of this requires anyone to cheat. Most class advantage is passed on by loving parents doing what parents do: helping their own children. That is why it is so durable, and why policies against it are contested. More on how much movement there really is in Social mobility.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Class reaches into the body and the ballot box

Class predicts far more than income. It shapes health, life expectancy, politics and the way people see themselves.

Chapter 08

Class gets under the skin

From 1967 the British epidemiologist Michael Marmot and colleagues studied civil servants in London, the Whitehall studies. None of these people were poor. All had access to the same free health service. Yet men in the lowest grades died at much higher rates than men in the top grades, and the risk rose step by step down the ladder. Smoking and other habits explained only part of the gap. Marmot argued that low control over one's work and life, and the chronic stress that comes with it, plays a large role.

The pattern is now called the social gradient in health. It appears almost everywhere. In the United States, a 2016 study led by the economist Raj Chetty found that the richest 1 per cent of men lived about 15 years longer than the poorest 1 per cent. Each step down the class ladder costs health, not only the bottom step.

Several routes connect class to the body, and they add up. Lower-paid jobs are more often physically dangerous, done in shifts or done standing up. Poorer neighbourhoods tend to have more air pollution, fewer safe places to exercise and more fast-food outlets. Money worries and insecure work create long-running stress, which affects sleep, blood pressure and mental health. Better-educated people also tend to act faster on new health information, such as the link between smoking and cancer, so each new medical advance can briefly widen the gap before it narrows. None of these routes alone explains the gradient. Together they explain a great deal of it.

Class also shapes how people are treated in daily life. Research on accents in Britain has found that listeners rate speakers with regional working-class accents as less intelligent and less competent than speakers of "received pronunciation", the accent associated with the upper middle class, even when they say exactly the same words. Being looked down on is not only unpleasant. It affects who is hired, believed and promoted.

For the policy side, see Health inequality.

Chapter 09

Class and politics: the great realignment

For most of the twentieth century, politics in rich democracies split roughly by class. Manual workers voted for parties of the left, such as Labour in Britain or the Social Democrats in Germany. Business owners and professionals voted for the right. That pattern has weakened a great deal.

In many countries the strongest predictor of how people vote is now education, not income. University graduates lean towards left-liberal and green parties. Voters without degrees, especially outside big cities, have moved towards conservative and populist parties. The 2016 Brexit referendum and recent US presidential elections showed this divide clearly. The economist Thomas Piketty has described a "Brahmin left", led by the highly educated, facing a "merchant right", backed by the wealthy, with many working-class voters feeling represented by neither. Analysts still debate how much this reflects economics and how much it reflects culture and identity. Class has not left politics. It has changed shape, from income to education and from economics to values.

Chapter 10

New shapes of class

The old three-part picture of upper, middle and working class fits the twenty-first century less well. In 2013 a team of British sociologists led by Mike Savage analysed a large BBC survey and proposed seven classes. They ran from an "elite" with very high wealth, through several middle groups distinguished by cultural and social capital, down to a "precariat". The word precariat, popularised by the economist Guy Standing, describes people in insecure, low-paid, short-term work with little protection, a group that gig work and zero-hours contracts have made more visible.

On a global scale, the picture looks different again. Researchers at the Brookings Institution estimated that by around 2018, for the first time, roughly half the world's people lived in households that were middle class or richer, driven mainly by growth in Asia. The economists Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanovic showed in their "elephant curve" that between 1988 and 2008 incomes grew fastest for the emerging middle classes of Asia and for the global top 1 per cent, while the working and lower middle classes of rich countries saw little gain. Class tensions in rich countries are partly a story about winners and losers of globalisation.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Is some ranking useful, and how much is too much?

Few people defend inherited privilege openly. The real debate is about how much inequality is a fair reward and how much is a stacked deck.

Chapter 11

The case for and against stratification

The functionalist case

In 1945 the American sociologists Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore argued that unequal rewards are needed to attract talented people into important and demanding jobs, such as surgery. Without higher pay and status, nobody would train for years. Many economists make a similar point about incentives for effort and risk.

The critical case

Critics answer that rewards often bear little relation to importance. Care workers and nurses are vital and poorly paid. Many top rewards come from inheritance, luck or market power, not skill. And once inequality is large, it blocks talented people from below, which wastes ability rather than rewarding it.

Chapter 12

The trap inside meritocracy

The word meritocracy, rule by the talented, was coined by the British sociologist Michael Young in his 1958 book The Rise of the Meritocracy. Most readers today miss that it was a warning. Young imagined a society that sorted people perfectly by intelligence and effort, and showed it becoming arrogant at the top and humiliated at the bottom, because the losers could no longer blame the system.

The philosopher Michael Sandel revived this argument in The Tyranny of Merit (2020). He argued that when success is seen as fully earned, the successful look down on others, and those left behind feel judged as well as poor. Defenders of meritocracy reply that the alternatives, such as promotion by family, party loyalty or connections, are worse. The ideal of merit is widely shared. The doubt is whether any real society delivers it, and what it does to people who lose.

Chapter 14

The policy arguments

Debates about reducing class gaps tend to cluster around four tools. Early years investment, such as childcare and parenting support, has wide support across politics. School reform, from more funding for poor areas to changes in university admissions, is popular but contested in detail. Taxing wealth and inheritance divides opinion sharply: supporters see it as the most direct fix, opponents warn about capital flight, double taxation and families' right to provide for their children. Labour market rules, such as minimum wages and protections for gig workers, aim at the precariat.

Whatever the policy, one lesson stands. Class is not only about the bottom. It is a whole ladder, and advantages build at every rung. A society can reduce poverty without changing class much, and it can be very unequal while almost nobody is destitute. Those are different problems, which is why the next topic, Poverty, needs its own treatment.

The core idea

Social class is a shared position in the economy that brings shared life chances. Marx stressed ownership, Weber added status and power, and Bourdieu showed how economic, cultural and social capital convert into each other and pass to children without anyone breaking a rule.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The richest 1% of US men live about 15 years longer than the poorest 1% (Chetty and colleagues, 2016).
  • Around half the world's population was middle class or richer by about 2018 (Brookings estimate).
  • Wealth is typically far more unequal than income, and it is the part most easily inherited.
The common mistake

Treating class as just income. A high-earning plumber and a low-paid lecturer may sit in different classes in most people's eyes, because class also runs through education, status and taste.

Test yourself

Can you describe how a family could pass on advantage to a child without giving them any money directly? If you can, you understand cultural and social capital.

The words, in plain words
Stratification
Any system that ranks groups of people in layers of unequal advantage.
Caste
A hereditary group into which you are born, often linked to occupation and marriage rules.
Means of production
The land, buildings, machines and tools used to produce wealth.
Bourgeoisie / proletariat
Marx's terms for the owners of capital and the workers who sell their labour.
Status
Weber's term for the social respect a group receives, separate from its wealth.
Cultural capital
Education, knowledge, manners and taste that bring advantage.
Social capital
Useful networks and relationships.
Habitus
Bourdieu's word for the deep habits of taste and behaviour absorbed in childhood.
Precariat
People in insecure, low-paid, unprotected work.
Social gradient in health
The pattern that health worsens step by step at each lower level of class.
Meritocracy
A system where rewards are supposed to follow talent and effort; first coined as a warning.
Assortative mating
Choosing a partner with a similar background, which concentrates advantage.
