Sharp Mind · Society & Sociology · Topic 02 of 20

Education. A machine for learning, sorting and belonging, and it does all three at once.

Putting thirty children of the same age in one room with one adult for twelve years is a recent and slightly strange idea. Two hundred years ago most people in the world could not read. Today almost every child starts school, and governments spend a large share of their budgets on it. Yet nobody fully agrees on what school is for. It passes on knowledge. It also teaches obedience and punctuality, ranks people for employers, and passes advantage from parents to children. Understanding education means seeing all of those jobs at once.

Question 1 · What is it?

Learning, organised by society

All humans learn. Education is the deliberate, organised part of that learning, and schooling is the form it took in the modern world.

Chapter 01

A strange room

Imagine describing a modern school to a farmer from the year 1700. Children are taken from their families for most of the day, most days of the year, from age five or six to eighteen. They are grouped by birth year, not by skill. They move between subjects when a bell rings. They are tested constantly, and the results follow them for life. The farmer would ask a reasonable question: who does the work at home while they are away?

For most of history, children learned by watching and helping adults: farming, a craft, cooking, the stories and rules of their community. Formal teaching existed, in temples, monasteries, Confucian academies and Islamic madrasas, but it reached a small elite, mostly boys. Mass schooling, the idea that every child should spend years in a classroom, is barely two centuries old. It spread across the whole world only in the second half of the twentieth century.

Chapter 02

Three words and four levels

Learning is what happens in a person's head when they gain knowledge or a skill. Education is learning that someone has organised on purpose. Schooling is education delivered through formal institutions with teachers, curricula and certificates. The three often get confused, and the confusion matters: a country can have high schooling and low learning, as Chapter 08 shows.

LevelTypical ageMain job
Early childhood (pre-school)0–5Language, play, social skills; also frees parents to work
Primary6–11Reading, writing, arithmetic, basic knowledge of the world
Secondary12–18Subjects in depth; sorting into academic and vocational tracks
Tertiary18+Universities, colleges, professional and technical training

Alongside these sit vocational education, which trains for a specific trade, and lifelong learning, the adult courses and on-the-job training that matter more as careers get longer and jobs change faster.

Chapter 03

How the whole world went to school

Prussia, the German kingdom, was among the first states to make primary school compulsory, with regulations in the 1760s. Its motives were partly religious (people should read the Bible), partly military and partly about building loyal, disciplined subjects. Other countries followed in the nineteenth century. Massachusetts passed a compulsory schooling law in 1852. England made elementary education compulsory in 1880. Japan built a national system quickly after 1872, as part of its rush to modernise.

The motives were mixed, and historians still argue about their weight. Industry needed workers who could read instructions and keep time. Nations wanted citizens who spoke one standard language and shared one story. Reformers wanted to rescue children from factories. Churches wanted believers. Mass schooling was built as much to make citizens and workers as to spread knowledge.

The results are one of the great changes in human history. Around 1800, only about one adult in ten in the world could read. By the early 2020s the figure was close to nine in ten, according to UNESCO, the United Nations body for education. The gap that remains is concentrated in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and among older women.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Teaching, signalling and sorting

Schools do several jobs at once, and the theories about education differ mainly in which job they think is the real one.

Chapter 04

The open curriculum and the hidden one

The curriculum is the official list of what pupils should learn: maths, language, science, history. But children also learn things nobody writes down. They learn to sit still, wait their turn, obey people in authority, arrive on time and accept being judged. The American education researcher Philip Jackson called this the hidden curriculum in 1968.

The French sociologist Émile Durkheim, writing around 1900, saw this as a good thing. Schools, he argued, turn children into members of a society by teaching shared rules and a sense of belonging beyond the family. In 1976 the American economists Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis gave a much darker reading. They argued that schools mirror the workplace: bells like factory whistles, grades like wages, obedience rewarded. On their view, schools prepare working-class children to accept boring jobs and orders. Both thinkers agree that schools shape character, not only knowledge. They disagree about whether that serves the child or the system.

Chapter 05

Skills or signals?

People with more education earn more. Across many countries, studies find that each extra year of schooling is linked to earnings roughly 8 to 10 per cent higher. The question is why.

Human capital

Education makes you more productive. You learn skills that employers pay for, just as a better machine produces more. This idea, developed by economists such as Theodore Schultz and Gary Becker in the 1960s, treats schooling as an investment in people. It implies that more education makes a whole country richer.

Signalling

Education shows employers what you already were: clever, persistent and willing to follow rules. The economist Michael Spence described this in 1973 and later shared a Nobel Prize. A degree is like a costly certificate. It implies that some education is a race for position, so more of it may not make a country richer.

Most economists believe both are true, and argue about the mix. Evidence for human capital: basic literacy and numeracy clearly raise productivity, and countries with higher test scores tend to grow faster. Evidence for signalling: people who finish a degree earn much more than people who complete three of its four years, even though they learned most of the same content. Economists call this the sheepskin effect, after the old material for diplomas. In 2018 the economist Bryan Caplan argued in The Case Against Education that signalling is most of the story. His view is a minority one, but it forced a useful question: how much of education is learning, and how much is ranking?

Chapter 06

How systems sort children

Every education system must decide who goes where, and when. Countries answer very differently.

ModelHow it sortsTypical examples

High-stakes examsOne national exam largely decides university entryChina (the gaokao), South Korea, France's baccalauréat in a milder form

Early tracking can give strong vocational training. Germany's apprenticeship system, which combines classroom study with paid work in a company, is admired for easing young people into jobs. Its weakness is that decisions taken at ten often reflect family background more than ability, and are hard to reverse. High-stakes exams look fair, because everyone sits the same paper, but they reward families who can pay for private tutoring, and they put intense pressure on teenagers. Every sorting method rewards some kind of advantage. The design question is which kind, and how early.

Chapter 07

What actually makes children learn

In 1966 the American sociologist James Coleman led a huge government study of schools. Its conclusion surprised everyone. Differences in school buildings, budgets and facilities explained far less of the gap in pupils' results than differences in their family backgrounds. The Coleman Report launched decades of research into what schools can and cannot do.

Later work found three things that do matter a lot. First, teachers. Pupils who have an excellent teacher for one year learn noticeably more, and some US studies have linked this to higher adult earnings. Second, the early years. Programmes that give poor children high-quality pre-school, such as the American Perry Preschool experiment of the 1960s, showed lasting benefits in adulthood. The economist James Heckman has argued that money spent in early childhood gives the highest returns of all. Third, teaching at the right level. Many children fall behind and are never caught up, because classes move on with the curriculum, not with them.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Education decides who gets ahead, and how far a country gets

Schooling shapes incomes, health, citizenship and equality. Its failures are as powerful as its successes.

Chapter 08

Schooling is not the same as learning

The world has nearly won the battle to get children into school. It has not won the battle to get them learning. In many low- and middle-income countries, children sit in classrooms for years and leave unable to read a simple paragraph. The World Bank calls this learning poverty: being unable to read and understand a short, age-appropriate text by age ten. In 2022 it estimated that about seven in ten ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries were in that position, worse than before the COVID-19 school closures.

On top of that, UNESCO estimated in the mid-2020s that around 250 million children and young people were not in school at all, many of them in sub-Saharan Africa and in conflict zones such as Afghanistan, where girls have been barred from secondary school since 2021. The global education problem has shifted from getting children through the door to making sure they learn once inside.

For comparing richer countries, the main tool is PISA, a test of fifteen-year-olds run by the OECD, a club of mostly rich countries, every three years since 2000. East Asian systems such as Singapore, Japan and parts of China usually score near the top. Finland's strong results in the early 2000s made it famous, though its scores have since declined. Results across the OECD fell in 2022, partly because of the pandemic.

Chapter 09

How school passes on advantage

Education is often described as the great equaliser. In practice it both equalises and reproduces inequality. The French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu introduced the idea of cultural capital: the knowledge, tastes, ways of speaking and confidence that children pick up at home. Middle-class children arrive at school already familiar with books, museums and the way teachers talk. Schools reward these traits as if they were pure talent.

Money adds to it. Richer parents buy homes near good state schools, pay for private schools or tutoring, and know how to work the system for university entry. In many countries, the children of graduates are several times more likely to graduate themselves. Yet schools also give millions of children from poor families a route upward that did not exist before. Both statements are true. That is why education sits at the centre of social class and social mobility.

Chapter 10

The great reversal: girls overtake boys

For most of history, girls received less education than boys, and in some places they still do. But in most rich countries and many middle-income ones, the gap has reversed. Girls now get better school grades on average, and women make up a majority of university students and graduates in most OECD countries. Boys are more likely to drop out, to be excluded and to fall behind in reading.

Educating girls has some of the strongest effects of any social policy. Women with more schooling tend to marry later, have fewer and healthier children, and earn more. Few changes have reshaped families, economies and birth rates as much as the spread of girls' education. The newer concern, raised by researchers such as Richard Reeves in the US, is how to help boys and young men who are falling behind, without reversing the gains for girls. That debate links to Gender roles.

Chapter 11

Why governments care so much

Governments in rich countries typically spend around 5 per cent of national income on education. They do it for several reasons at once. An educated workforce is more productive. Educated people tend to be healthier, live longer and depend less on welfare. Schools teach a common language and civic values, which helps hold a diverse society together. And education is a political battlefield: what history is taught, which language is used, how religion appears in school. These fights are often fierce because whoever shapes the curriculum shapes how the next generation sees its own country.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A great achievement with built-in tensions

Mass education is one of humanity's real successes. Its weaknesses come from asking one institution to do too many jobs at once.

Chapter 13

Credential inflation

When few people had a degree, a degree set you apart. As more people get one, employers start asking for degrees for jobs that did not need them before. Then a master's degree becomes the new distinction. The American sociologist Randall Collins called this the credential society in 1979. The individual still gains from more education, because without the certificate you fall behind. But the society may be running faster just to stay in the same place.

Higher education has grown dramatically. The American sociologist Martin Trow described a shift from "elite" university systems, serving a small minority, to "mass" and then "universal" systems. Worldwide, the share of young people enrolled in tertiary education is around four in ten, and much higher in rich countries. Student debt in the US and graduates working in jobs that do not use their degree have made people ask whether the expansion has gone too far. Others answer that graduates still earn much more on average, and that a more educated population benefits everyone. Both sides can point to real evidence, which is why the debate continues.

Chapter 15

The dream of a tutor for every child

In 1984 the American education researcher Benjamin Bloom reported that students taught one-to-one by a tutor performed far better than students in an ordinary class. He called it the "2 sigma problem": how to get the benefits of tutoring without the cost of a tutor for every child. Later research suggests the effect is smaller than his headline figure, but still large. AI tutors are the latest attempt to solve Bloom's problem, after radio, television, computers and online courses each promised to.

The lesson from past waves is caution. Technology helped motivated learners most and left the rest further behind. The harder questions are about purpose. If machines can write essays and solve equations, what should children learn to do themselves? Many educators argue for more emphasis on reasoning, judgement, writing as thinking and deep background knowledge, because you cannot check an answer you have no knowledge to check. The tension between education as learning, as sorting and as belonging will not disappear. It will simply be fought on new ground.

The core idea

Education does three jobs at once: it teaches skills (human capital), ranks people for employers (signalling), and socialises children into a society (the hidden curriculum). It both opens doors for poor children and passes advantage on to rich ones.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World adult literacy rose from about 1 in 10 around 1800 to nearly 9 in 10 by the 2020s.
  • Each extra year of schooling is linked to about 8–10% higher earnings.
  • About 7 in 10 ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries could not read a simple text (World Bank estimate, 2022).
The common mistake

Counting years in school as if they were learning. Enrolment is not education. Many countries have filled classrooms without filling minds.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a degree might raise your income even if you forgot everything you studied? If you can, you understand the difference between human capital and signalling.

The words, in plain words
Schooling
Education delivered through formal institutions with teachers, curricula and certificates.
Curriculum
The official list of what pupils are supposed to learn.
Hidden curriculum
The unwritten lessons of school, such as obedience, punctuality and accepting judgement.
Human capital
The skills and knowledge that make a person more productive, seen as an investment.
Signalling
The idea that qualifications mainly show employers qualities a person already had.
Sheepskin effect
The extra pay that comes from finishing a qualification, beyond the years of study.
Tracking
Sorting pupils into different schools or streams by measured ability.
Apprenticeship
Training that combines paid work in a company with classroom study.
Cultural capital
Bourdieu's term for the knowledge, tastes and manners from home that schools reward.
Learning poverty
Being unable to read and understand a simple text by age ten.
PISA
An international test of fifteen-year-olds' reading, maths and science, run by the OECD.
Credential inflation
The rising level of qualification demanded for the same jobs.
