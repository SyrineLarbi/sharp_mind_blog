Work and society. The pay cheque is only the part of a job you can see.

Ask an economist what work is for and the answer is income and output. Ask the people who lose a job and they describe something larger: a day with no shape, friends who stop calling, a question at parties they dread. Work organises our time, our relationships, our status and much of our sense of self. That is why changes in how work is done, from the factory clock to remote working and artificial intelligence, are never just economic events. This file looks at what work does for people and societies, how its organisation has changed, and what its future might demand.

Question 1 · What is it?

Effort that keeps a society going, paid or not

Work is purposeful effort that produces something others need. Most of it is paid, but a huge share, such as cooking, cleaning and caring, is not, and societies depend on both.

Chapter 01

The village that slowed down

In 1930 a large textile factory closed in Marienthal, a small industrial village near Vienna. Almost every family depended on it. Within a short time most households had no one in paid work. In 1931 and 1932 a team of young researchers, including Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld and Hans Zeisel, moved in to study what unemployment did to a community. Their book, The Unemployed of Marienthal, appeared in 1933.

They expected anger and political revolt. They found something quieter: apathy. The men had all the free time in the world, yet they used it less. Library loans fell sharply even after the fees were dropped. Club memberships declined. The researchers timed people walking through the village and found that unemployed men walked more slowly and stopped more often than employed people did. Many no longer wore watches. Days lost their shape.

Marienthal became a classic because it showed what work gives that a benefit payment does not. When work disappears, it is not only income that goes. Time, purpose and connection go with it.

Chapter 02

The hidden functions of a job

Decades later, Marie Jahoda summed up what Marienthal had taught her. Besides its obvious purpose, earning money, employment has latent functions: benefits people rarely notice until they lose them.

FunctionWhat it providesWhat happens without it
Time structureA shape to the day and weekDays blur together; sleep and routines drift
Social contactRegular contact with people outside the familyIsolation, shrinking networks
Collective purposeA sense of contributing to something largerFeeling useless
Status and identityAn answer to "what do you do?"Shame, loss of standing
Regular activityA reason to get up and be activeInertia, lower wellbeing

Not every job provides these well, and some people find them elsewhere, in caring, volunteering, study or faith. But the list explains why a good job and a decent income are not the same thing to the person who has them, and why policies that replace only income often leave people unhappy.

Chapter 03

From the season to the clock

For most of history, most people worked on the land, in households where home and work were the same place. Work followed the seasons and daylight: very hard at harvest, lighter in winter. Families worked together, and children joined in as soon as they could.

The Industrial Revolution separated home from work and put work on the clock. The British historian E.P. Thompson, in a famous 1967 essay, described how factory owners had to teach a new time-discipline: bells, fixed hours, fines for lateness, and a new idea that time is money and can be "spent" or "wasted". Over the twentieth century, most workers in rich countries moved again, from factories into offices, shops, hospitals and schools. Today roughly three-quarters or more of jobs in rich countries are in services rather than farming or industry. Each move changed not only how people earned money but how families lived, where they lived, and what they valued.

Question 2 · How does it work?

How work is divided, managed and bargained over

The way a society organises work, who does which task, under whose control, and on what terms, shapes inequality, health and politics.

Chapter 04

Dividing the work, and dividing the worker

Adam Smith opened The Wealth of Nations (1776) with a pin factory. One worker alone might make barely one pin a day, he wrote, but when the work was split into about eighteen separate steps, a small team could make thousands each. This division of labour is the source of much of modern productivity (see The Wealth of Nations).

Smith also saw a danger. A worker who spends his whole life repeating a few simple operations, he warned later in the same book, may become as "stupid and ignorant" as a human being can be. Karl Marx developed the idea into his theory of alienation: workers who do not control what they make, how they make it, or what happens to it become cut off from their work, from each other and from themselves.

Émile Durkheim, in The Division of Labour in Society (1893), took a more hopeful view. Specialisation, he argued, makes people depend on each other, and that interdependence can become a new kind of social glue, replacing the sameness that held villages together. The same division of labour can bind a society together or leave its workers feeling like parts of a machine, depending on how it is organised.

Chapter 05

Stopwatches, assembly lines and the human factor

In the early twentieth century the American engineer Frederick Winslow Taylor promised to make work efficient by studying it scientifically. In The Principles of Scientific Management (1911) he described timing each movement with a stopwatch, finding the "one best way" and paying workers to follow it exactly. Thinking moved to managers; doing stayed with workers. The approach became known as Taylorism.

Henry Ford combined these ideas with the moving assembly line, introduced at his Highland Park plant in 1913. The time to assemble a Model T car fell from more than twelve hours to about an hour and a half. The work was so monotonous that staff turnover soared, and in 1914 Ford raised pay to five dollars a day, roughly double the going rate. Fordism came to mean mass production by well-paid workers who could afford to buy what they made.

A reaction followed. Studies at the Western Electric Hawthorne plant near Chicago in the 1920s and 1930s were long read as showing that workers responded to attention and group relationships, not just pay and lighting. Later researchers have questioned that interpretation, but the "human relations" school it inspired changed management. Many modern workplaces still swing between control (measure everything) and trust (give people autonomy), and digital tools now allow a kind of Taylorism of keystrokes and delivery routes.

Chapter 06

Selling feelings: the service economy

When most work moved into services, the product often became an experience. A waiter, nurse, call centre agent or flight attendant is paid partly to show certain feelings, such as warmth, patience and calm, whatever they actually feel. The American sociologist Arlie Hochschild called this emotional labour in The Managed Heart (1983), based on research with flight attendants who were trained to smile through rude passengers and fear.

Emotional labour is real work. It can be satisfying, but constant faking of feelings is tiring and linked to stress and burnout. It is also unevenly shared: it is concentrated in jobs often done by women and often poorly paid. The service economy turned feelings into part of the product, which is why customer-facing workers often describe being exhausted by days in which they lifted nothing heavy.

Chapter 07

The standard job, and what came after it

For several decades after 1945, many workers in rich countries, especially men, had what sociologists call the standard employment relationship: one full-time employer, a permanent contract, regular hours, a pension and a path upward. It was never universal, but it shaped how societies organised family life, housing loans and welfare.

Since the 1980s, more work has become non-standard: temporary contracts, part-time work, self-employment, agency work, and, more recently, platform or "gig" work, where apps match workers with short tasks such as rides or deliveries. In the United Kingdom, "zero-hours" contracts, which guarantee no minimum hours, became a symbol of this shift. In much of the developing world, most work has always been informal: outside formal contracts, taxes and protections.

The economist Guy Standing called the growing group without security the precariat, a blend of "precarious" and "proletariat". Others argue that flexibility suits many workers, such as students, carers and people with second jobs. Both are true of different people. Flexibility chosen is freedom; flexibility imposed is insecurity. Whether gig workers are employees or independent contractors is a live legal fight in many countries.

Chapter 08

Who speaks for workers

Trade unions are organisations through which workers bargain together with employers over pay and conditions, a process called collective bargaining. They grew out of the struggles described in Volume 20 and helped win shorter hours, weekends, safety rules and pensions.

Membership has fallen in most rich countries since the 1970s and 1980s, as manufacturing shrank, work became more scattered and laws changed. The decline is far from uniform. In the Nordic countries, where unions often help run unemployment insurance, more than half of workers are typically members. In the United States, the figure is around one in ten. Many economists link falling union membership to rising wage inequality, though they disagree about how much it explains. Some newer forms of worker voice, including works councils, which give German employees a formal say in company decisions, and online organising among platform workers, are being watched closely.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Identity, health, gender and time

Work decides more than how much money people have. It shapes who they think they are, how healthy they are, how families divide their lives, and how much time anyone has left.

Chapter 09

"So, what do you do?"

In many cultures, the first question a stranger asks is about your job. Occupations carry prestige, the respect a society gives to a role, and surveys in many countries rank jobs in surprisingly similar orders, with doctors and professors near the top. Losing a job can therefore mean losing a public identity as well as an income.

Research consistently links unemployment to worse mental health, and the effect is more than just the loss of money. In the United States, the economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton described a rise in "deaths of despair", from drugs, alcohol and suicide, among middle-aged white Americans without university degrees, in their 2020 book of that name. They connected it partly to the decline of stable, respected jobs for people without degrees. When good work disappears from a community, its effects show up in health statistics years later.

General education

This chapter summarises population research, not individual advice. Anyone struggling after job loss should seek support from a doctor or local services.

Chapter 10

The work nobody pays for

Cooking, cleaning, childcare, caring for elderly parents: this work keeps every society running, and most of it is unpaid. The International Labour Organization estimated in 2018 that women perform about three-quarters of the world's unpaid care work, more than three times as much as men on average. The gap is widest in parts of North Africa, the Middle East and Asia, and narrower in Northern Europe, but it exists everywhere.

This matters for paid work too. Hochschild described the "second shift": many women in paid jobs come home to a second job of housework and care. The pay gap between men and women in many rich countries is now driven less by unequal pay for the same job and more by what happens after the arrival of children, when mothers' earnings fall and fathers' usually do not. The Harvard economist Claudia Goldin won the 2023 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences partly for research showing this. See Gender roles.

Chapter 11

The fifteen-hour week that never came

In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren". He predicted that within a century productivity would rise so much that people might need to work only around fifteen hours a week. Productivity did rise roughly as he expected. Hours fell, but nowhere near that far. Full-time workers in rich countries in the late nineteenth century worked something like 3,000 hours a year; today the figure is typically between about 1,400 and 1,800.

Why not further? People chose more goods over more leisure, new wants appeared, the rewards for long hours in top jobs grew, and work became a source of status and identity. The anthropologist David Graeber added a provocative claim in Bullshit Jobs (2018): that many people do work they privately think is pointless. He cited a 2015 YouGov poll in which 37% of British workers said their job made no meaningful contribution to the world. Later, larger surveys found much lower figures, so his claim is contested. The broader question he raised stuck: whether societies use their growing productivity well.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What work gives, and what it takes

Work is one of the main sources of wellbeing and one of the main sources of harm. Both depend on the kind of work and the terms on which it is done.

Chapter 12

The ledger of modern work

AreaWhat it givesWhat it can cost
Income and securityIndependence, a home, a pension, credit.Insecure jobs give little security, and low pay can trap people in poverty while working.
Meaning and identitySkill, pride, a role others value.Identity tied to a job makes its loss devastating; pointless-feeling work erodes morale.
Social lifeColleagues, friendships, networks.Toxic workplaces, bullying, and loneliness in remote or solo work.
HealthActivity, structure, purpose; employment generally supports mental health.Injury, stress and overwork. The WHO lists burnout as an occupational phenomenon; Japan has a word, karōshi, for death from overwork.
FlexibilityRemote and flexible work help carers and people with disabilities.The boundary between work and home can disappear; "always on" culture.

The pattern behind the table is that the same thing, a job, can be good or bad for people depending on its quality. Researchers who study job quality point to a few ingredients again and again: fair and predictable pay, some control over how the work is done, support from managers and colleagues, security, and a sense that the work matters. A bad job can be worse for mental health than no job at all, according to some studies, while a good job is one of the most reliable sources of a good life.

Chapter 14

If work shrinks, what fills the gap?

The Marienthal lesson matters for every debate about the future of work. Suppose technology does reduce the paid work available. Replacing income is the easier part: proposals range from shorter hours to wage subsidies to a universal basic income, a regular payment to everyone with no conditions. Finland tested a version in 2017 and 2018, giving about 2,000 unemployed people a monthly payment with no strings. Their wellbeing improved, and their employment rose only slightly.

Replacing the latent functions is harder. Time structure, social contact, purpose and status would need other homes: in care work that is recognised and paid, in volunteering, in learning, in community life. Some societies already value such roles highly; others give them little status. The future of work is therefore a question about values as much as technology: which activities a society decides to honour, pay for and organise its time around. That question is taken further in Human-AI collaboration.

The core idea

Work provides income and also latent functions: time structure, social contact, purpose, status and activity. Its organisation has moved from the household and the season to the factory clock, then to services, emotional labour and more non-standard jobs. Much essential work is unpaid and done mostly by women. Job quality, not just having a job, decides whether work helps or harms.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Women do about three-quarters of the world's unpaid care work (ILO, 2018).
  • Keynes predicted a 15-hour week by about 2030; annual hours have roughly halved since the late 1800s instead.
  • Ford's 1913 assembly line cut Model T assembly from over 12 hours to about 90 minutes.
The common mistake

Thinking that if you replace someone's income, you have replaced their job. Marienthal showed that the loss of structure, contact and status does damage of its own.

Test yourself

Can you name Jahoda's five hidden functions of employment and say which one a basic income would replace? If you can, you see why the future-of-work debate is about more than money.

The words, in plain words
Latent functions
The hidden benefits of a job beyond pay: structure, contact, purpose, status, activity.
Division of labour
Splitting production into specialised tasks done by different people.
Alienation
Marx's term for workers feeling cut off from their work, its product and each other.
Taylorism
Managing work by timing and standardising each movement.
Fordism
Mass production on assembly lines by well-paid workers who are also consumers.
Emotional labour
Managing your own feelings as part of a job, such as smiling at customers.
Standard employment
A permanent, full-time job with one employer and benefits.
Gig work
Short tasks arranged through apps, usually without employee status.
Informal work
Work outside formal contracts, taxes and legal protections.
Precariat
Workers with insecure jobs, unstable income and few protections.
Collective bargaining
Workers negotiating as a group, usually through a union.
Second shift
The unpaid housework and care many people, mostly women, do after paid work.
Universal basic income
A regular payment to everyone, with no conditions attached.
