The future of society. The slow forces are easier to predict than the headlines.

Predictions about the future of society have a poor record. Flying cars, the paperless office and the end of history all arrived late, partly, or not at all. But some parts of the future are already locked in. Everyone who will be 40 in 2066 has already been born. The cities of 2050 are being built now. The warming of the next few decades is largely set by gases already in the air. This file separates the slow, predictable forces from the fast, uncertain ones, explains how societies actually change, and sorts the main possibilities by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Question 1 · What is it?

The shape of life together, a generation from now

"The future of society" means the likely direction of the big structures people live inside: families, cities, work, the state, beliefs and communities. Some of those move slowly enough to forecast.

Chapter 01

Why social prediction is humbling

Around 1900, many writers imagined the year 2000. Their pictures are full of airships, moving pavements and machines doing housework. Few predicted that women would vote and work in large numbers, that families would shrink, that religion would decline in Europe, or that most people would live in cities. They imagined new machines in an unchanged society, and it was the society that changed most.

In 1970 the writer Alvin Toffler published Future Shock, arguing that change was arriving so fast that people would feel disoriented, as travellers feel culture shock. He was right about the feeling and wrong about many details. That is the usual pattern. The direction of social change is often visible decades ahead. The timing, and the way people react, are not.

Chapter 02

Slow variables and fast variables

A useful way to think about the future is to divide the forces into two kinds. Slow variables change over decades and are hard to reverse: the age structure of a population, the layout of cities, the stock of greenhouse gases, levels of education, deep values. Fast variables can change in months: elections, financial crises, wars, pandemics, viral technologies.

Slow variables are much easier to predict. A country's number of 70-year-olds in 2050 depends on people already alive today, so forecasts are fairly reliable. Fast variables are where surprises happen, and they often decide how the slow ones play out. An ageing country will have a pension problem; whether it solves it calmly or in a crisis depends on politics. Good thinking about the future starts with the slow variables and treats the fast ones as uncertain.

Question 2 · How does it work?

How societies actually change

Societies change through a few recurring engines, through the weight of past choices, and through sudden tipping points. Understanding the mechanics is more useful than any single forecast.

Chapter 04

The engines of social change

Sociologists disagree about which engine matters most, but most accept that change comes from the interaction of a few. Technology changes what is possible: the contraceptive pill changed women's careers; the smartphone changed social life. Demography changes who there is: a society of young people behaves differently from one of old people. Economics changes incentives: when service jobs replaced factory jobs, education and cities became more important. Ideas change what people think is right: the spread of human-rights thinking changed laws on everything from slavery to marriage. And shocks such as wars, pandemics and crises speed up changes already under way.

The COVID-19 pandemic is a recent example of a shock speeding up a slow trend. Remote work had grown slowly for twenty years; in 2020 it jumped in months, and in many office jobs a hybrid pattern remained afterwards. Shocks rarely create brand-new directions. They usually push societies faster along paths they were already on.

Chapter 05

Path dependence and tipping points

Two ideas explain why change is sometimes slow and sometimes sudden. Path dependence means that past choices limit future ones. A city built around cars in the 1960s cannot easily become a walking city, because its houses, shops and roads are already spread out. A pension system designed for a young population is politically hard to redesign. The past is built into concrete and into law.

Tipping points are the opposite: moments when a slow build-up suddenly becomes a rapid shift. Attitudes to smoking in public, or to same-sex marriage in many countries, changed slowly for years and then very quickly, once enough people saw the new view as normal. Social scientists think this happens when people adjust their behaviour to what they believe others think. When perceptions catch up with private views, change can cascade. Societies are often stable for a long time and then change faster than anyone expected.

Chapter 06

Forecasts versus scenarios

How should anyone plan for such an uncertain future? There are two main approaches.

Forecasting

Give a probability for a specific outcome. The psychologist Philip Tetlock spent two decades testing experts' political predictions and found, in his 2005 book Expert Political Judgment, that many did little better than chance. In later tournaments he found that some careful amateurs, nicknamed "superforecasters", did much better by updating often, thinking in probabilities and avoiding grand theories.

Scenario planning

Instead of one prediction, imagine several plausible futures and prepare for each. The oil company Shell famously developed this method in the 1970s, under planners such as Pierre Wack, and was better prepared than rivals when oil prices jumped in 1973. Scenarios are not predictions; they are rehearsals.

The two work best together. Forecasts suit near-term, well-defined questions ("will this country's population fall by 2035?"). Scenarios suit long-term, open questions ("what will work look like in 2050?"). The worst approach is the commonest one: a single confident story.

Chapter 07

Hype, and the long-run surprise

A rule of thumb often credited to the American futurist Roy Amara says that we tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run. The internet is a good example. In the late 1990s, investors expected it to transform shopping within a few years, and the dot-com bubble burst in 2000. Twenty years later, it had changed shopping, dating, news, politics and work more deeply than most of those investors imagined.

The same pattern is likely with artificial intelligence. Claims that it will transform every job within two years are probably too fast. Claims that it is a passing fad are probably too dismissive. The social effects of a technology depend on slow things: training, regulation, business models, habits and trust. Technology arrives quickly; society absorbs it slowly. Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Five questions every society will have to answer

The forces in Chapter 03 combine into a handful of hard questions. How societies answer them will decide what daily life feels like for most people alive today.

Chapter 08

Who will care for the old?

Ageing is the most certain of all social trends. In Japan, South Korea, Italy, Germany and China, the share of people over 65 is rising fast, and the number of working-age people per retiree is falling. That raises three linked questions. Who will pay for pensions? Who will pay for health care, which costs most in the last years of life? And, most personally, who will do the daily work of care, which has traditionally fallen on daughters and wives?

The answers on offer include working longer, higher taxes, more immigration of care workers, and technology such as monitoring devices and care robots, which Japan has experimented with. None is painless. Ageing turns care from a private family matter into one of the largest economic and political questions of the century. See Aging populations and Family structures.

Chapter 09

What will work be for?

Work does more than pay the rent. It structures the day, provides colleagues and status, and in most countries it is how people get pensions, health insurance and a sense of contributing. If AI and automation change many jobs, the question is not just "will there be jobs?" but "how will people get income, purpose and recognition?"

Some proposals are already being tested. Finland ran a two-year experiment from 2017 giving a basic monthly payment to unemployed people without conditions: recipients reported better wellbeing, with only small effects on employment. Trials of a four-day working week in several countries have reported positive results for many participating firms, though the firms chose to take part. A universal basic income, a regular payment to everyone, remains debated mainly on cost and on what it would do to motivation. See Work and society and How work may change because of AI.

Chapter 10

How will societies live with a warmer world?

By the early 2020s the world was about 1.2°C warmer than before industrialisation, and 2024 was the first calendar year in which the global average passed 1.5°C above that level. Much further warming over the next few decades is already locked in by gases released in the past, whatever happens next. So societies face two tasks: cutting emissions (mitigation) and preparing for the changes that are coming anyway (adaptation).

The social side of adaptation is often overlooked. Heatwaves hit older people and outdoor workers hardest. Floods and droughts affect the poor more than the rich, and can push people to move. Insurance may become unaffordable in some places, changing where people can live. Climate change is not only an environmental problem but a question of who is protected and who is left exposed.

Chapter 11

Can people still trust each other, and their institutions?

Societies run on trust: that strangers will mostly be honest, that courts will be fair, that official statistics are accurate. In many countries, surveys show trust in governments, media and political parties has fallen over recent decades, though the pattern varies widely. Researchers also report rising affective polarisation, meaning that supporters of rival parties dislike and distrust each other more, not just disagree more, particularly in the United States.

Organisations that track democracy, such as Freedom House, have reported more countries declining than improving in political rights for nearly two decades. Others caution that measuring democracy is hard and that some declines are modest. Add AI-generated fakes that make it harder to know what is real, and the ability to agree on basic facts may become one of the scarcest public goods. See Social media and When you cannot trust your eyes or ears.

Chapter 12

Where will people belong?

More people live alone than ever before in many rich countries, families are smaller, and membership of churches, unions and clubs has fallen. Governments have started to treat loneliness as a public-health issue. The UK appointed a minister with responsibility for loneliness in 2018, and Japan did the same in 2021. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory describing an "epidemic of loneliness and isolation".

At the same time, new forms of belonging keep appearing: online communities, chosen families, local mutual-aid groups, hobby clubs. The question is not whether people will belong, but whether new forms of community can provide the practical help and deep ties that older ones did. See Communities and Digital communities.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Reasons for hope, reasons for worry

Optimists and pessimists often look at the same data. Here the "advantages and disadvantages" are the strengths societies bring to the future, set against their weak points.

The optimistic case

Writers such as Hans Rosling and Steven Pinker argue that most long-run measures of human welfare have improved, and that pessimism is partly a bias: bad news is sudden and vivid, while progress is slow and quiet.

The pessimistic case

Critics answer that past progress does not guarantee future progress, that some risks such as climate change grow quietly until they are severe, and that averages can hide people left behind.

A reasonable position combines both. Things have broadly improved, things are still bad for many, and improvement is not automatic. Progress was the result of choices, and it can be reversed by choices.

Chapter 15

The future is a set of choices, not a destination

In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote an essay, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren", predicting that within a hundred years rich societies would be so productive that people might work around fifteen hours a week. He was right about the productivity: incomes in rich countries grew enormously. He was wrong about the hours, because people chose more goods, more services and more status competition instead of more leisure. Technology set the possibilities; people's choices decided the outcome.

That is the most useful lesson for thinking about the future of society. The slow forces, ageing, urbanisation, warming, are largely given. What societies do about them is not. Will ageing countries welcome migrants or build robots or ask people to work longer? Will AI's gains be widely shared or concentrated? Will trust be rebuilt? These are political and moral questions, not technical ones.

For an individual, the practical stance is simple. Learn the slow variables, because they rarely surprise. Hold forecasts about the fast ones loosely. Watch for tipping points in attitudes. And remember that every future that has ever arrived looked impossible to some people and obvious to others. The useful position is in the middle: curious, prepared, and willing to change one's mind.

The core idea

Separate slow variables (ageing, cities, climate, education), which are fairly predictable, from fast variables (elections, shocks, viral technologies), which are not. Societies change through technology, demography, economics, ideas and shocks, slowed by path dependence and sometimes accelerated by tipping points.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Since 2018, people over 65 have outnumbered children under 5 worldwide.
  • Warming reached about 1.2°C by the early 2020s; 2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5°C.
  • Extreme poverty fell from over 1 in 3 people around 1990 to under 1 in 10 before 2020.
The common mistake

Imagining new technology in an unchanged society. The biggest surprises are usually in how people live, not in the machines.

Test yourself

Can you name two slow variables and two fast variables that will shape your country in 2050, and say which are easier to predict and why? If so, you can think about the future without hype.
