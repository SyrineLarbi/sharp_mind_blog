Communities. The invisible network that decides who comes to help when things go wrong.

Ask people what they want from where they live and many say the same thing: to know their neighbours, to feel they belong, to have someone to call. That wish describes a community. It sounds soft, but it is measurable, and its effects are hard: on how long people live, how safe their streets are, how well they recover from disaster and how much they trust their government. This file explains what communities are, how they are built, why many have weakened, and what seems to bring them back.

Question 1 · What is it?

A group bound by more than being nearby

A community is a group of people linked by lasting ties, a shared sense of belonging and some common expectations. Living on the same street is not enough on its own.

Chapter 01

The town with healthy hearts

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the American doctor Stewart Wolf and colleagues studied Roseto, a small town in Pennsylvania founded by immigrants from one village in southern Italy. The men of Roseto died of heart attacks at a much lower rate than men in nearby towns, even though they smoked, ate plenty of fat and worked in hard jobs. The researchers could not find a dietary or genetic explanation that fitted.

What stood out was how the town lived. Three generations often shared one house. People stopped to talk in the street, ate together, belonged to the same church and clubs, and looked after neighbours who fell on hard times. Showing off wealth was frowned upon. Over the following decades, as younger people moved away and life in Roseto became more like the rest of America, the town's heart-attack advantage faded.

The study was small, and later researchers have debated how much of the effect was really about social life. But the "Roseto effect" became a famous example of an idea that much larger studies have since supported: the web of relationships around a person is part of their health, not just their happiness.

Chapter 02

Kinds of community

In 1887 the German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies made a distinction still used today. He contrasted Gemeinschaft ("community"), close personal ties based on kinship, place and tradition, as in a village, with Gesellschaft ("society"), looser ties based on contracts and self-interest, as in a city or a market. He feared that modern life was replacing the first with the second.

Today sociologists recognise many kinds of community, and most people belong to several at once.

What they share is more than contact. A community exists when people expect to meet again, feel some loyalty to the group, and act differently because of it.

Chapter 03

How many people can you really know?

In the 1990s the British anthropologist Robin Dunbar suggested that humans can keep up stable, meaningful relationships with only about 150 people. He based this on the link between brain size and group size in other primates, and pointed to the typical size of hunter-gatherer bands, early villages and army units. The figure became known as Dunbar's number.

It is popular and contested. Some researchers have re-analysed the data and found that the estimate could range widely, and that people differ a lot. What holds up better is the general picture: we have a small inner circle of a handful of very close people, wider circles of friends, and a large outer ring of acquaintances, and our time and attention limit how many people fit in each ring. Communities larger than a few hundred need institutions, such as churches, clubs, schools and councils, to hold them together.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Social capital: the wealth that lives between people

Communities run on trust, habits of cooperation and networks. Sociologists bundle these together under one name and have tried to measure how they grow and decline.

Chapter 04

Bonding and bridging

Social capital means the value that comes from relationships: the networks, trust and shared norms that let people cooperate. Like money, it can be invested, built up and spent. A neighbour who waters your plants is drawing on it. So is a job offer that comes through a friend.

The American political scientist Robert Putnam, whose book Bowling Alone (2000) made the idea famous, separated two kinds.

Bonding social capital

Ties within a group of similar people: family, close friends, an ethnic or religious community. It gives support and loyalty. It "helps you get by". Too much of it, without the other kind, can turn a group inward and suspicious of outsiders.

Bridging social capital

Ties across different groups: a workplace that mixes classes, a sports club that mixes ethnic backgrounds. It spreads information and tolerance. It "helps you get ahead". It is usually weaker and harder to build.

Healthy communities need both. Bonding holds people up; bridging connects them to everyone else. Mobility research shows how much bridging matters; see Social mobility · Who your friends are.

Chapter 05

The strength of weak ties

In 1973 the American sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper with a surprising title: "The Strength of Weak Ties". He had asked people in professional jobs how they found work. Many had heard about their job through a personal contact, and more often than not, the contact was an acquaintance they saw only occasionally, not a close friend.

The reason is simple once you see it. Your close friends know the same people and hear the same news as you. Acquaintances move in other circles, so they carry information you would never otherwise get. Weak ties are bridges between clusters. A community full of people who say hello at the bakery, the school gate and the bus stop is quietly building a network that can find jobs, spread warnings and pass on help.

This is why the American sociologist Ray Oldenburg, in The Great Good Place (1989), praised what he called third places. The first place is home and the second is work. Third places are cafés, pubs, barbershops, libraries and parks, where people meet regularly and informally, on neutral ground. They are where weak ties form.

Chapter 06

Collective efficacy: will anyone step in?

In 1997 the sociologist Robert Sampson and colleagues published a study of hundreds of Chicago neighbourhoods. They asked residents whether neighbours would intervene if children were skipping school or spraying graffiti, and whether people in the area could be trusted. They called the combination collective efficacy: shared trust plus a willingness to act for the common good.

Neighbourhoods with high collective efficacy had less violence, even after allowing for poverty and other differences. The point is not that residents fought crime themselves. It is that people who trust each other enforce small norms early, and that changes the whole climate of a street. It is the opposite of the bystander problem described in Volume 36, where each person waits for someone else to act.

The American political economist Elinor Ostrom showed something similar on a larger scale. Studying fisheries, forests and irrigation systems around the world, she found that local communities often manage shared resources well for centuries, without either the state or private ownership, when they have clear rules, ways to monitor and punish cheating, and a say in making the rules. She received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, the first woman to do so.

Chapter 07

Why many communities frayed

Putnam's core argument was that, in the United States, membership of clubs, unions, churches, parent associations and civic groups had fallen sharply from the 1960s onward. His title came from one detail: more Americans were bowling than before, but fewer were bowling in leagues. People still did things, but more of them did them alone.

He blamed several forces: suburbs and long commutes that left less time for local life, the rise of television as the main use of free time, the pressure of two-earner households, and above all generational change, as a very civic generation shaped by the Second World War was replaced by less joining ones. Similar declines in traditional membership have been reported in other rich countries, though not everywhere and not to the same degree.

Critics pointed out that people may simply have moved to new forms of connection, such as informal networks, single-issue campaigns and, later, online groups. That debate is still open. What is not in doubt is that the old institutions that once gathered people weekly, from churches to unions, now gather far fewer.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Health, survival and self-government

The strongest evidence for community comes from the moments when it is missing: in illness, in disaster and in politics.

Chapter 08

Loneliness as a health risk

General education

This chapter describes research on populations. It is not medical advice. If loneliness or low mood is affecting your health, talk to a doctor or a trusted support service.

In 2010 the psychologist Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues combined the results of 148 studies following more than 300,000 people. People with stronger social relationships had about a 50% greater likelihood of survival over the study periods than those with weaker ones. The size of the effect was comparable to well-known risks such as smoking, though comparisons like that are approximate.

Governments took notice. The United Kingdom appointed a minister for loneliness in 2018, and Japan did the same in 2021. In 2023 the US Surgeon General, Vivek Murthy, issued a public advisory describing an "epidemic of loneliness and isolation", and the World Health Organization launched a commission on social connection. Loneliness is now treated as a public health issue, not only a private sadness. It is also important to separate two things: being alone (a fact) and feeling lonely (a painful gap between the connection you have and the connection you want).

Chapter 09

Who dies in a heat wave

In July 1995 a heat wave hit Chicago. Over about a week, more than 700 people died beyond what would normally be expected. Most were elderly, poor and living alone. The sociologist Eric Klinenberg studied the disaster in his book Heat Wave (2002) and noticed something striking.

Some neighbourhoods with similar levels of poverty had very different death rates. In areas with busy streets, local shops and active churches, people checked on elderly neighbours and brought them to cool places. In areas where shops had closed, streets were empty and crime made people afraid to open their doors, isolated people died alone in hot apartments. The heat was the same. The social fabric was not.

Disaster researchers, notably the political scientist Daniel Aldrich, have reported the same pattern after earthquakes and floods in Japan, India and the United States: places with stronger social ties tend to recover faster than places with more money but fewer connections. Neighbours, not emergency services, are usually the first responders.

Chapter 10

The schools of democracy

In the 1830s the French writer Alexis de Tocqueville travelled across the United States and published Democracy in America. He was struck by how readily Americans formed associations for every purpose, from building a school to running a charity. He saw these groups as places where ordinary people learned to cooperate, argue, compromise and lead: the practical training for self-government.

Modern research broadly supports his intuition. Regions and countries with higher social trust tend to have more effective governments and lower corruption, although it is hard to separate cause and effect. Putnam's earlier study of Italian regions, Making Democracy Work (1993), argued that regions with long traditions of civic associations had better-run local governments. Democracy needs places where strangers practise trusting each other in small things before they are asked to trust each other in large ones.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The warmth and the walls

Strong communities protect their members. The same strength can exclude, pressure and suffocate.

SupportHelp in illness, childcare, job loss and grief.Help comes with obligations, and refusing them can be costly.
NormsSmall problems are corrected early; streets feel safe.Pressure to conform; gossip and shaming of those who differ.

Social capital is not always good for society. A mafia family, a gang or a corrupt network has plenty of bonding social capital. So do groups that unite mainly against others. The question is never just "how strong is this community?" but "who does its strength serve, and who is kept out?"

Chapter 12

The freedom of being nobody

For many people, leaving a close-knit community was a liberation. A medieval German saying held that "city air makes you free", because a serf who lived in a chartered town for a year and a day could escape his lord. In modern times, young people, religious and sexual minorities, and women escaping narrow roles have often moved to cities precisely for their anonymity.

The German sociologist Georg Simmel, writing in 1903 about the big city, described the cool, reserved attitude of city dwellers as a defence against too much stimulation, and noted that it also gave a new kind of personal freedom. The loss of close community is real, but so is what it bought: the freedom to choose your own life without your neighbours' approval. The challenge is to have connection without control.

Chapter 14

What seems to work

One idea gaining ground is social prescribing. In England, the National Health Service has employed "link workers" since 2019 who can connect patients with community groups, such as walking clubs, gardening projects, choirs or volunteering, alongside medical care. Evaluations are promising but mixed, and researchers say more rigorous evidence is needed.

Other lessons repeat across studies. People build ties through regular, repeated, low-stakes contact around a shared activity, not through one-off events or campaigns telling them to be friendly. Libraries, sports clubs, community gardens, religious congregations and parents' groups do this naturally. Communities also last when people have a real say, as Ostrom found: being consulted and trusted with decisions makes them invest.

The deeper change may be cultural. Earlier generations inherited communities. Many people today must choose and build them, and that takes effort that busy lives make hard. Community is becoming less a place you are born into and more a practice you keep up, like exercise: easy to neglect, and noticed most when it is gone.

The core idea

A community is a group bound by lasting ties, belonging and shared expectations. Its power comes from social capital: bonding ties that support members and bridging ties that connect groups. Communities with high trust and collective efficacy are healthier, safer and better governed, but strong communities can also exclude and pressure.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Stronger social relationships: about 50% higher likelihood of survival (Holt-Lunstad, 2010, 148 studies).
  • Dunbar's number: about 150 stable relationships, a popular but contested estimate.
  • Chicago, 1995: more than 700 excess deaths in a week-long heat wave, unevenly spread by neighbourhood.
The common mistake

Assuming community is always good. Bonding without bridging can produce closed, suspicious groups, and some of the tightest networks are criminal ones.

Test yourself

Can you explain why an acquaintance is more likely than a close friend to tell you about a job? If you can, you understand weak ties and why bridging matters.
