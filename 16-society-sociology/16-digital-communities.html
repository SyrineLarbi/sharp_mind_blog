Sharp Mind · Society & Sociology · Topic 16 of 20

Digital communities. Strangers who keep coming back, until they are no longer strangers.

A woman with a rare disease finds three hundred people who have it too. A teenager learns to code from people on another continent who will never meet him. A few hundred thousand volunteers write the largest encyclopedia in history for free. None of this is the noisy feed most people picture when they hear "the internet". It is something older and quieter: groups of people who return to the same place, learn each other's names, and build rules together. This file explains what turns an online crowd into a community, how those communities run themselves, and why they can be both a lifeline and a trap.

Question 1 · What is it?

A group with memory, rules and a reason to return

Not every online crowd is a community. The difference is repetition: the same people, in the same place, over time, with shared norms.

Chapter 01

Before the feed: the bulletin boards

In 1985 a small group in the San Francisco Bay Area, including the writer Stewart Brand, started a dial-up discussion system called The WELL. People connected their home computers through a telephone line and typed messages into topic areas: parenting, music, politics, the Grateful Dead. It was slow, text-only and expensive. It was also, for many members, the first time they had friends they had met through a screen. Members later described raising money for a sick member, attending each other's weddings, and mourning members who died.

The writer Howard Rheingold, an early member, wrote about it in his 1993 book The Virtual Community. His point was simple and, at the time, surprising. People could form real attachments with people they had never seen, as long as they met repeatedly in a shared space. Around the same years, Usenet, a worldwide system of discussion groups started by graduate students in North Carolina around 1980, was hosting thousands of topic groups, each with its own regulars and its own etiquette.

So online community did not begin with Facebook. Trolls, moderators and flame wars were all already there.

Chapter 02

What makes a crowd a community

Sociologists have argued for a century about what a "community" is. A useful working definition has four parts. A community has members (people who recognise each other), a shared purpose or interest, repeated interaction over time, and norms, meaning unwritten and written rules about what is acceptable.

By this test, the comments under a viral video are usually not a community. The people there will never meet again, and nobody remembers what anyone said. A subreddit for people learning Japanese, a Discord server for a small video game, or a long-running forum for diabetics usually is one. The regulars know each other's histories. Newcomers are welcomed, corrected and sometimes told off.

The key ingredient is memory. A community remembers who helped, who lied and who broke the rules. Without memory, reputation cannot form, and without reputation, trust cannot form. That is why the design question "can people see who you are and what you did last month?" turns out to be so important.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Architecture, moderators, and the quiet majority

Online communities look spontaneous, but they run on design choices and on the unpaid work of a small number of people. Change either, and the community changes.

Chapter 04

The software is the architecture

A city's layout shapes how people meet. Wide squares invite crowds; narrow streets invite neighbours. Online, the software plays the same role. The legal scholar Lawrence Lessig made this point in 1999 with a short phrase: "code is law". What the software allows and forbids governs behaviour as firmly as any written rule.

Compare three designs. A threaded forum keeps conversations organised by topic and keeps them for years, so it rewards careful, searchable answers. A chat server such as Discord shows a fast-moving stream, so it rewards presence and banter but buries old knowledge. A ranked feed shows each person a different order of posts chosen by an algorithm, so it rewards whatever gets quick reactions. Same people, different buildings, different behaviour.

Chapter 05

Moderation: the unpaid government

Every lasting community has someone who removes spam, settles fights and enforces the rules. On big platforms there are paid content moderators. But most of the work in communities is done by volunteer moderators: people who run a subreddit, a Facebook group or a forum section for free, often for years.

Their tools are simple. They write rules, delete posts, warn members, and ban persistent offenders. The hard part is judgement. Is a sharp joke harassment? Is a heated argument a healthy debate or the start of a feud? Good moderation is mostly invisible, because it stops problems before most members see them. Bad moderation is very visible: either the space fills with abuse, or members feel they are ruled by a small clique.

An old idea that fits

The political economist Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel prize in economics in 2009, studied how real communities manage shared resources such as fishing grounds and irrigation canals without either a government or private owners. She found that successful groups had clear boundaries, rules made by the people affected, monitoring, gradual penalties and cheap ways to settle conflicts. Researchers of online communities often use her list, because a well-run forum is a shared resource in exactly this sense.

Chapter 06

The 90-9-1 rule: most people only watch

Visit almost any online community and you will notice that a few names appear everywhere. This is normal. A rough rule of thumb, popularised by the usability researcher Jakob Nielsen in 2006, says that in many communities about 90% of people only read, 9% contribute now and then, and 1% create most of the content. The exact numbers vary a lot, and some communities are more balanced. But the shape, a long tail of quiet readers behind a small core, appears again and again.

The quiet readers are called lurkers, and they are not useless. They are the audience that makes contributing feel worthwhile, and many of them benefit greatly: the patient who never posts but reads every answer. The risk lies at the other end. When a community depends on a handful of very active members, it is fragile. If they burn out, quarrel or leave, the community can empty in weeks.

Chapter 07

Reputation, names and masks

In 1993 the magazine The New Yorker printed a cartoon by Peter Steiner: a dog at a computer telling another dog, "On the Internet, nobody knows you're a dog." It captured the promise and the danger of online identity. You can be anyone, so you can speak freely. And so can a liar.

Communities solve this with reputation systems. Stack Overflow, the programming question site launched in 2008, gives points when other users find your answers useful, and unlocks powers such as editing other people's posts as your score rises. Reddit's "karma" works in a looser way. Online marketplaces show seller ratings. Each turns past behaviour into a visible signal, which is the online version of a village knowing who is reliable.

There is a real trade-off between real names and pseudonyms (stable invented names). Real names can reduce some abuse, but they expose people who need privacy: someone discussing a mental illness, a sexual identity, or a political view that is dangerous where they live. Pseudonyms that persist over time give much of the benefit of reputation without the risk. What hurts communities most is not the mask but the disposable mask: accounts that cost nothing to create and nothing to throw away.

Chapter 08

Weak ties, strong ties, and newcomers

In 1973 the sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper called "The Strength of Weak Ties". He found that people often heard about new jobs not from close friends but from acquaintances. Close friends know the same things you know. Acquaintances connect you to other worlds. Digital communities are extraordinarily good at producing weak ties at scale: the stranger who answers your question about a visa, a bug or a symptom.

Some communities also produce strong ties, the kind of friendship where people call each other in a crisis. That usually needs smaller groups, longer time and some move to private channels or real meetings. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar has argued that humans can keep up only around 150 meaningful relationships. Whatever the exact figure, the idea explains why big communities split into smaller sub-groups, and why the heart of any large forum is a few dozen regulars.

Every community also has to handle newcomers. Early internet users still talk about the "Eternal September": each September, a wave of new university students joined Usenet and had to be taught its etiquette; in 1993, when a big commercial service opened Usenet to its customers, the wave never stopped. Communities that close the door to newcomers grow old and die. Communities that open it without teaching norms lose what made them good.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

What strangers can build, and who they can save

Digital communities have produced some of the most useful public goods of the past forty years, and some of the most worrying social movements. Both come from the same mechanism.

Chapter 09

Wikipedia and open source: proof that it can work

When Wikipedia launched in January 2001, many experts thought an encyclopedia anyone could edit would collapse into vandalism. Instead, the English edition alone grew to close to seven million articles by the mid-2020s, with hundreds of other language editions alongside it. It works because it is a community, not just a website. Volunteers watch pages, revert vandalism within minutes, argue on discussion pages, and follow a large body of rules such as "neutral point of view" and "cite reliable sources".

Open-source software is the other great example. The Linux operating system, started by Linus Torvalds in 1991 as a hobby, now runs most of the world's web servers and, in modified form, every Android phone. It was built by a community of programmers, many of them later paid by companies, who coordinate through mailing lists and code-review tools. These projects show that strangers, given good rules and visible reputations, can produce goods worth billions without a traditional employer.

Chapter 10

Belonging for people who are rare where they live

If you are the only person in your town with a particular illness, interest or identity, the internet can be the difference between isolation and belonging. Parents of children with rare genetic conditions have used online groups to share treatment experiences, find specialists and even help organise research. People with chronic illness exchange practical tips that no leaflet contains. Emigrants keep their language and traditions alive in diaspora groups.

This is perhaps the deepest change. For most of history, your community was decided by where you were born. Digital communities let people choose their community by what they share, not by where they happen to live. For many people in small or hostile places, including young people who feel different from those around them, that choice has been described as life-changing.

Health note

Patient communities can be a great source of support and lived experience. They are general education, not medical advice. Treatment decisions belong with a qualified professional.

Chapter 11

The same machinery, pointed the wrong way

Everything that makes a support group powerful can make an extremist group powerful too. Belonging, shared language, in-jokes, a sense that outsiders do not understand: these bind any community. Researchers have documented online spaces where conspiracy theories, harassment campaigns or violent ideologies were spread and reinforced. Volume 36 explains the underlying psychology: once people feel part of an "us", they judge "them" more harshly (Us and them).

How strong this effect is remains contested. The popular idea of the "echo chamber" says people online hear only views they already hold. Several large studies have found that most people's media diets are more mixed than that picture suggests, and that strong online segregation is concentrated among a smaller, highly engaged minority. Other researchers argue that the minority is exactly the problem, because it is the one most likely to act. The honest summary is that online communities can deepen and speed up radicalisation in people already moving that way, while the evidence that they turn ordinary people extreme on a large scale is weaker.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Reach without roots, roots without reach

Digital communities trade some of the strengths of local life for strengths local life never had. Whether the trade is good depends on what a person needs.

Chapter 13

Can online ties replace offline ones?

In 2000 the political scientist Robert Putnam published Bowling Alone, arguing that Americans had been withdrawing from clubs, unions, churches and neighbourhood groups since the 1960s, and that this loss of "social capital" (the trust and networks that let people cooperate) was harmful. The book was written before most online communities existed at scale, and people have argued ever since whether the internet makes the problem worse or offers a partial cure.

The research points in both directions. Online contact seems to work best when it adds to offline relationships: friends who also chat, neighbours who also share a group. It seems to work less well as a full substitute, especially for physical help, touch and the kind of trust that comes from showing up in person. A balanced view is that digital communities are excellent at breadth and information, and weaker at depth and care, with many exceptions on both sides.

The optimistic reading

Digital communities reconnect people who were already isolated, give a voice to minorities, and let knowledge flow to anyone who asks. For many, they created social capital where there was none.

The pessimistic reading

They let people sort themselves into groups of the like-minded, and offer a thin, always-available form of company that can crowd out the harder, richer work of local relationships.

Chapter 15

Who owns the town square?

The live debate is about ownership. Most digital communities live on platforms owned by companies, and the community has no legal claim to the space it built. When Yahoo closed the web-hosting service GeoCities in the United States in 2009, millions of personal pages disappeared, though archivists rescued many. When Google closed its social network Google+ to consumers in 2019, its communities had to move or die. In June 2023, thousands of Reddit communities went dark in protest against changes to how outside apps could access the site, a rare moment when volunteer moderators acted like a union.

The pattern is clear. A community is built by its members, but on rented land. In the coming years, expect more tools for exporting a community's history and member list, more interest in community-owned infrastructure, and more debate about whether large platforms owe the communities they host any duties at all. The answer will shape whether digital communities become lasting institutions, like clubs and churches, or remain tenants who can be evicted at any time.

One more trend is quieter but may matter most. As artificial intelligence makes it cheap to fake people, proof of being a real, consistent human may become the scarcest thing a community offers. Small groups where members know each other, or have met in person, may become more valuable precisely because they are hard to fake.

The core idea

A digital community is an online group with members, a shared purpose, repeated interaction and norms. Its behaviour is shaped by software design, held together by reputation and run largely by volunteer moderators. The same bonds that make support groups powerful also make extremist groups powerful.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The WELL began in 1985; Rheingold named "the virtual community" in 1993.
  • The rough 90-9-1 rule: most people read, few post, a tiny core writes most content.
  • English Wikipedia had close to seven million articles by the mid-2020s, written by volunteers.
The common mistake

Treating "the internet" as one thing. A ranked feed, a chat server and a threaded forum are different buildings, and the same people behave differently in each.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the comments under a viral video are usually not a community, while a small forum for a rare illness usually is? If you can, you understand the role of memory and repetition.
