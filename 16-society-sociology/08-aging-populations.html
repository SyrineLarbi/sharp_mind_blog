Aging populations. A triumph of survival that every system was built without.

Humanity is getting older, and it has never happened before. For all of history, children far outnumbered the old. Now, in a growing list of countries, there are more people over 65 than under 15, and more funerals than births. This is the result of two of the best things that ever happened to humans: most babies now survive, and most adults live into old age. But pensions, health systems, labour markets and families were all designed for a young population. Adjusting them is one of the central tasks of this century.

Question 1 · What is it?

When the average age keeps rising

Population ageing means that older people make up a growing share of a population. It is a change in the shape of society, not just in the number of old people.

Chapter 01

A village with a busy clinic and an empty school

Picture a mountain village in rural Japan, Italy or South Korea. The primary school closed a few years ago because there were not enough children. The bus to town runs twice a day, mostly carrying people to medical appointments. Many houses are empty, their owners dead and their children living in the city. The busiest building is the clinic. The local shop sells more incontinence products than baby food. The few working-age people are often care workers, some of them migrants from abroad.

Villages like this exist in many rich countries, and big cities are slowly following the same path. Population ageing is not a future problem. In much of East Asia and Europe it is already the ordinary state of things. In Japan, people aged 65 and over made up close to three in ten of the population by the mid-2020s, the highest share of any large country.

Chapter 02

How ageing is measured

Demographers, the people who study populations, use a few simple measures. The median age is the age that splits the population in half. The share aged 65 and over is the most common headline figure. The UN calls a society "ageing" when that share passes 7 per cent, "aged" at 14 per cent and "super-aged" at 20 per cent. The old-age dependency ratio compares the number of people aged 65 and over with the number of working age, usually 15 or 20 to 64. It is a rough guide to how many workers support each retiree.

Chapter 03

Two causes: fewer births, longer lives

Populations age for two reasons. The first and most powerful is falling fertility. When each generation of children is smaller than the one before, the young become a smaller share of the whole. The second is longer life. Global life expectancy at birth rose from about 46 years in 1950 to about 73 years by the mid-2020s, according to UN estimates. Most of the early gain came from children surviving infancy. More recent gains come from older people living longer, thanks to better treatment of heart disease, less smoking and better living conditions.

A third factor is timing. After the Second World War, many countries had a baby boom, a burst of high birth rates that lasted until the 1960s in Europe and North America. That large generation is now retiring. Its passage through the age structure is like a large meal moving through a snake. Ageing is mostly a story about births, not deaths, though people often assume the opposite.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A pyramid turns into a column, and every system feels it

The age shape of a population drives pensions, health spending and care needs. Each of those systems has its own way of absorbing, or failing to absorb, the change.

The chart is called a population pyramid, because in a young country it looks like one. In an old country it becomes a column, and sometimes narrower at the bottom than in the middle. Once fertility is low for a long time, the change has momentum: the small generations of children become small generations of parents, who have even fewer children. Even if birth rates rose tomorrow, it would take decades to change the shape. That is why demographic forecasts for 2050 are among the most reliable forecasts in social science. Most of the people who will be old in 2050 are already alive.

Chapter 05

How pensions feel the strain

When Otto von Bismarck's Germany introduced one of the first state old-age pensions in 1889, it was paid from age 70, an age that relatively few workers reached. Today people in rich countries often spend twenty years or more in retirement. Pensions are paid in two main ways.

Pay-as-you-go

Today's workers pay contributions or taxes, which are used directly to pay today's pensioners. Most state pensions work this way, including in Germany, France and the US Social Security system. It is a promise between generations. It works well when workers greatly outnumber retirees, and comes under pressure when the ratio falls.

Funded

Each worker, or their employer, saves into a fund that is invested, and the pension is paid from what has built up. Australia, the Netherlands and Chile rely heavily on this model. It is less exposed to the ratio of workers to retirees, but more exposed to investment returns, fees and inflation.

Governments facing a falling ratio have four basic levers: raise contributions, cut benefits, raise the retirement age, or grow the workforce, through higher employment of women and older people or through immigration. Every lever is unpopular with someone. The arithmetic of pensions is simple. The politics is not.

Chapter 06

What ageing does to health systems

Older people use more healthcare, on average. But the pattern is more complicated than "older populations cost more". Health economists have found that much of a person's lifetime health spending is concentrated in the last year or two before death, at whatever age that happens. On that view, a longer life partly delays the expensive period rather than adding to it. Growth in health spending is driven more by new technology, rising wages for health workers and higher expectations than by ageing alone. Ageing still matters, but it is one driver among several.

What does change is the type of care. Older patients often live with several long-term conditions at once, such as diabetes, heart failure and arthritis. Doctors call this multimorbidity. Health systems built around single diseases and hospital specialists handle it badly. Patients bounce between departments, take many drugs that interact, and end up in hospital beds they could have avoided with better community care. Dementia is a growing part of the challenge. The World Health Organization estimated that about 57 million people lived with dementia worldwide in 2021, with around 10 million new cases a year. Ageing populations push health systems away from hospitals and towards primary care, home care and coordination. See Primary healthcare.

Chapter 07

Who provides long-term care

Long-term care means help with daily tasks such as washing, dressing, eating and moving, over months or years. It sits awkwardly between health and social services, and in many countries it is poorly funded. Most of it is still given unpaid by family members, mostly wives and daughters, as Family structures describes. As families shrink and women work, that supply shrinks too.

Some countries have created dedicated insurance. Germany introduced compulsory long-term care insurance in 1995 and Japan in 2000. Both pay for home help and care homes, funded by contributions from workers and, in Japan, also from people over 40. Other countries, such as the UK, rely on a mix of means-tested public support and private payment, and debates about reform have run for decades. Much paid care work is done by migrants: women from the Philippines, Eastern Europe, Latin America and elsewhere care for older people in Italy, the Gulf, the US and East Asia. The care of the old in rich countries increasingly depends on the young of poorer ones.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Economies, politics and the meaning of old age all shift

Ageing changes how fast economies grow, how governments spend, who wins elections and what it means to be old.

Chapter 08

The economic effects

A shrinking working-age population means fewer workers, unless more people work or work longer. Japan's working-age population has been falling since the mid-1990s. China's began falling in the 2010s. Fewer workers tend to mean slower growth, unless each worker becomes more productive. They also mean labour shortages in some sectors, which can raise wages and push employers towards automation.

Public finances feel ageing on both sides. Spending on pensions, health and care rises, while the tax base of workers grows slowly or shrinks. Many rich countries already spend a large share of their budgets on people over 65. Some economists also argue that ageing societies save more and invest less, which helped keep interest rates low in the 2010s. Others dispute how strong that effect is. Ageing does not doom an economy, but it removes a tailwind that rich countries enjoyed for decades. For the debt side, see Public debt.

Chapter 09

The politics of age

Older people vote at higher rates than younger people in most democracies, and in ageing societies they are a growing share of the electorate. That gives them political weight. Pension reforms are among the most fought-over policies anywhere. In France, a 2023 reform raising the minimum pension age from 62 to 64 was pushed through without a final vote in the National Assembly and set off months of mass protests. Its future remained politically contested afterwards.

Some commentators frame this as a conflict between generations: older homeowners with secure pensions against younger renters with insecure jobs. Others warn that the framing is too simple. Many older people are poor, especially older women with broken work histories, and families transfer large sums from old to young through gifts and inheritance. Inequality within each generation is often bigger than inequality between generations.

Chapter 10

Getting old before getting rich

Japan and Western Europe became rich first and aged later, so they had time to build pensions and health systems. Many middle-income countries are ageing much faster and at lower incomes. China's one-child policy, in force from about 1980 to 2015, sped up the change. Its population began shrinking in 2022. In 2025 China began gradually raising its retirement ages, which had been among the lowest in the world, at 60 for men and 50 or 55 for women.

Thailand, Vietnam, Brazil and Iran are among other countries ageing quickly. The phrase used for them is "getting old before getting rich". Many have large informal workforces, people who work without contracts and do not pay into pension schemes, so large numbers of older people will have no pension at all. In the opposite situation, young countries in Africa and South Asia have a chance at a demographic dividend: a period when the working-age share is high, which can speed up growth if there are jobs, education and good policy.

Chapter 11

What it means to be old

Old age itself is changing. A 70-year-old today in a rich country is, on average, far healthier than a 70-year-old fifty years ago. Many demographers argue that we should measure age by remaining years of life, not years since birth. By that measure, people "become old" much later than they used to.

Attitudes have not kept up. The American psychiatrist Robert Butler coined the word ageism in 1969 for prejudice against people because of their age. Ageism shows up in hiring, in health care, where older patients may be treated less aggressively, and in everyday language. Loneliness is another serious issue. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory describing loneliness and social isolation as a public health concern, with risks for heart disease, dementia and early death. Older people living alone, especially after the death of a partner, are among the most affected. How a society treats its old people is becoming a question for the majority, not a minority.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A success with a bill attached

Population ageing is the result of progress. Its costs are real, but they depend heavily on how societies respond.

Chapter 13

Time bomb, or manageable shift?

The alarmed view

Dependency ratios are rising fast. Workforces are shrinking. Pension and care promises were made on assumptions that no longer hold. Without big changes, taxes will rise sharply, benefits will fall, or debt will soar. The phrase "demographic time bomb" captures this view.

The calmer view

Dependency ratios based on fixed ages exaggerate the problem, because people now work longer and stay healthy longer. Higher employment of women and older people, better productivity and moderate immigration can absorb much of the shift. Fewer children also reduces some costs.

Both views rest on real evidence. The difference is mostly about whether policy adapts. Countries that raised retirement ages gradually, linked them to life expectancy (as Denmark and others have done), and expanded employment of older workers face a much smaller problem than countries that delayed. The demographics are largely fixed. The consequences are not.

Chapter 15

How societies are adapting

The most promising responses share a theme: they treat ageing as a change to plan for, not a crisis to survive. The WHO launched a Decade of Healthy Ageing for 2021 to 2030, which pushes health systems towards prevention, community care and age-friendly cities. The idea of compression of morbidity, put forward by the American physician James Fries in 1980, is the goal: if illness can be pushed into a shorter period at the end of life, longer lives need not mean more years of disability. Evidence on whether this is happening is mixed and varies by country.

Other adaptations are practical. Workplaces can offer flexible and part-time work for older people. Housing can be designed so people stay at home longer. Cities can add benches, step-free transport and safer crossings. Immigration can fill gaps in the workforce, though it is politically divisive and cannot by itself reverse ageing, because migrants also grow old. The countries that cope best will be those that stop thinking of age 65 as the end of usefulness. For how health systems might change, see Future healthcare systems.

The core idea

Populations age mainly because of falling birth rates, and also because of longer lives. The change has strong momentum. It strains pay-as-you-go pensions, shifts health systems from acute hospital care towards long-term and chronic care, and changes economies and politics. How painful it is depends on how societies adapt.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 1 in 10 people worldwide are 65 or over; in Japan, close to 3 in 10 (mid-2020s).
  • Global life expectancy rose from about 46 in 1950 to about 73 by the mid-2020s.
  • About 57 million people lived with dementia in 2021 (WHO estimate).
The common mistake

Thinking ageing is caused mainly by people living longer. Falling births are the bigger driver. A country with long lives but steady births ages far less.
