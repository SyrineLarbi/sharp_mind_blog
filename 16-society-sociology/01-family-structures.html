Family structures. The oldest institution, and the one that changes fastest when nobody is looking.

Most people think of "the family" as something fixed, the natural background against which everything else changes. In fact it is one of the most flexible arrangements humans have. Who lives together, who counts as kin, who marries whom, who raises the children and who looks after the old: all of these vary enormously between societies and shift within a single lifetime. The shape of the family follows the economy, the law and the birth rate. Once you see that, many arguments about "the decline of the family" look different.

Question 1 · What is it?

A household, a kin network and a set of rules

"Family" means three different things at once: the people you live with, the people you are related to, and the rules a society uses to decide who belongs to whom.

Chapter 01

Two breakfasts

Picture two breakfasts on the same morning. In a house in a small town in southern India, a grandmother, her two married sons, their wives and five children share one kitchen. The money from the sons' jobs goes into one pot. Decisions about a child's school or a daughter's marriage are taken by the older generation. In a flat in Stockholm, a woman in her thirties eats alone. She lives by herself, as do roughly four in ten households in Sweden. Her parents live two hours away. Her closest daily contact may be a friend or a colleague.

Both are normal in their own society. Neither is a failed version of the other. The family is not one natural shape that some societies keep and others lose. It is a set of arrangements that people adjust to their circumstances. Sociology, the study of how people live together in groups, treats the family as an institution, a stable pattern of rules and roles, like a school or a market. Institutions last, but they are not frozen.

Chapter 02

The vocabulary: household, family, kin

Three words are worth keeping apart. A household is the people who live under one roof and share daily resources such as food and rent. A family is a group joined by marriage, birth, adoption or a lasting partnership, whether or not they live together. Kin is the wider web of relatives a society recognises. Statistics usually measure households, because they are easy to count. That is why many headlines about "the family" are really about who shares a front door.

Societies also differ in how they trace descent. In patrilineal systems, such as traditional China or much of the Arab world, you belong to your father's line and wives join their husband's family. In matrilineal systems, such as the Minangkabau of Indonesia or the Akan of Ghana, property and belonging pass through the mother's side. Most modern Western societies are bilateral: both sides count equally. These rules decide who inherits, who you may marry and who must care for you.

Chapter 03

The myth of the "traditional" family

A common story says that people in the past lived in large extended households, and that industrialisation shrank them into small nuclear units. For north-western Europe, this story is mostly wrong. From the 1960s the English historian Peter Laslett and his colleagues at Cambridge studied old parish records. They found that English households had been small and mainly nuclear for centuries before the first factories, with an average of fewer than five people.

Another historian, John Hajnal, described a "European marriage pattern" in 1965. West of a rough line from Trieste to St Petersburg, people married late, often in their mid-twenties, and a sizeable share never married at all. Young people commonly worked as servants in other households before they married and set up on their own. Further east and in most of Asia, marriage was earlier and almost universal, and couples more often lived with the husband's parents.

The point is not that one region was "more traditional". Every region had its own tradition, and the 1950s suburban family with a working father and a full-time housewife was a short and unusual period, not the ancient norm. In most of history, farm families worked together, and women's work was essential to survival.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Families do jobs, and the jobs keep moving

The family's shape follows the work it has to do. When the economy, the birth rate or the law changes what the family needs to do, its shape follows.

Chapter 04

What families are for

Sociologists usually list five functions. Families produce children and decide who may have them. They socialise them, which means teaching language, habits and values long before any school does. They are economic units that pool income and share costs. They care for the sick, the young and the old. And they place people in society: your family's wealth, religion, ethnicity and name shape your starting point.

In the mid-twentieth century, the American sociologist Talcott Parsons argued that the small nuclear family suited industrial society well. It was mobile, so it could move to where the jobs were. It left schooling, healthcare and production to other institutions and kept two core jobs: raising children and giving adults emotional stability. This approach, called functionalism, explains institutions by the needs they serve. Critics replied that it described one kind of middle-class American family as if it were universal, and that it treated the wife's unpaid role at home as a natural fit rather than a choice imposed on her.

Chapter 05

The economy redraws the household

On a farm, the household is a workplace. Children are extra hands from an early age, and old parents still own the land, which gives them authority. When work moves to factories and offices, three things change. Children become a cost instead of a source of labour, because they need years of schooling. Young adults earn their own wages, so they no longer need their parents' land or permission to marry. And the home separates from the workplace, which for a time split the roles of "breadwinner" and "homemaker".

The economist Gary Becker, who won the Nobel Prize in 1992, built a theory of the family on this kind of reasoning. In his view, couples gain from specialisation, like two countries that trade: one partner focuses on paid work and the other on the home. That model explained the mid-century family well. It explained the next fifty years less well. As women gained education and well-paid work, the gains from specialisation shrank. The rise of women's employment is probably the single biggest force behind modern family change. It affects when people marry, how many children they have, how housework is divided and whether an unhappy marriage can be left.

Chapter 06

The demographic engine

Two long-term trends change family shape from below: fewer births and longer lives. The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current rates. A rate of about 2.1 keeps a population stable over time, without migration. Worldwide, it fell from about five in the 1960s to about 2.3 by the early 2020s. In many rich countries it is well below two. South Korea reached about 0.7 in 2023, the lowest in the world.

Demographers call the result the beanpole family. Each generation is small, but because people live longer, three or four generations are alive at the same time. A child today may have no siblings and no cousins, yet four grandparents and even great-grandparents. That changes who is available to give care and who needs it, a theme taken up in Aging populations.

Chapter 07

Law and policy set the walls

The state decides what counts as a marriage, who can divorce, who inherits and who gets benefits. Small legal changes have large effects. In 1969 California passed the first modern no-fault divorce law, which let a marriage end without proving that one partner had done wrong. Most Western countries followed within two decades. Divorce rates in the US rose sharply in the 1960s and 1970s, peaked around 1980 and have fallen since. Economists still debate how much of the rise the law caused and how much it simply recorded a change that was already happening.

In 2001 the Netherlands became the first country to allow same-sex marriage. By 2025 more than thirty countries had followed, mostly in Europe and the Americas. In many other countries same-sex relationships remain unrecognised or are criminalised. Opinion on this differs sharply between regions and religious traditions.

The most dramatic example of policy shaping the family is China's one-child policy, enforced from about 1980 to 2015. It sharply cut births, often through coercion, and produced a generation of only children and a strong imbalance of boys over girls, because many families preferred a son. China then allowed two children in 2016 and three in 2021. Births kept falling anyway. Governments find it far easier to push fertility down than to pull it back up.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Children, inequality and care all run through the family

The family is where most children learn, where most care happens, and where advantages pass from one generation to the next. When its shape changes, all three change with it.

Chapter 08

The second demographic transition

In 1986 the demographers Ron Lesthaeghe and Dirk van de Kaa gave a name to a set of changes they saw spreading across Europe: the second demographic transition. (The first was the fall in death and birth rates that came with modernisation.) The second includes later marriage, more cohabitation, more divorce, more children born outside marriage, more people living alone, and fertility below replacement. They linked it to a shift in values towards individual choice and self-fulfilment.

The East Asian row explains a lot. Where having a child outside marriage is rare and marriage is demanding, especially for women who are expected to give up work, many people delay or skip both. That is one reason the lowest birth rates in the world are found in societies where family norms changed least, not most.

Chapter 09

Does family structure affect children?

This is one of the most debated questions in the field. On average, children who grow up with two stable, married parents do better on school results, income and mental health than children of single or divorced parents. Almost all researchers accept that pattern. They disagree about why.

The structure explanation

Two adults bring more money, more time and more supervision. Breakups bring moves, new schools and stress. On this view, the structure itself helps, and policy should support marriage and stability.

The selection explanation

People who marry and stay married already tend to have more education, income and stability. Some of the gap is about who forms which family, not the family form itself. On this view, money and support matter more than the marriage certificate.

The best studies try to separate the two, for example by following the same children before and after a change. The American sociologist Sara McLanahan spent decades on this question. Her work suggests both effects are real. A finding that most researchers now share is that instability, meaning repeated changes in who lives in the home, seems to matter more than any one structure. A stable single mother may do better for a child than a series of new partners. A high-conflict marriage can harm children more than a calm divorce.

Chapter 10

The marriage divide

In the United States and several other rich countries, marriage has become a marker of class. In the mid-twentieth century almost everyone married, whatever their income. Today, people with a university degree marry at higher rates and divorce less. People with less education are more likely to cohabit, to have children outside marriage and to see relationships break up. The sociologist Andrew Cherlin has described marriage as having become a "capstone": something you do once your life is in order, rather than the foundation you build it on.

This matters because families pass on advantage. Two graduates who marry each other pool two high incomes and invest heavily in their children. Economists call partners choosing people like themselves assortative mating. It existed before, but when both partners earn, it widens the gap between households. The family, in other words, is one of the main channels of social class and social mobility.

Chapter 11

Who looks after whom

Across the world, most care is still given by family members, and most of it by women. That includes the care of babies, the sick, the disabled and the old. It is unpaid, so it rarely appears in economic statistics, but it is enormous. When families shrink and women work outside the home, the question of who does this work does not disappear. It moves: to paid carers, often migrant women from poorer countries; to state services; or back onto one exhausted relative.

The sandwich generation is the name for people, usually in their forties or fifties, who are caring for children and ageing parents at the same time. As people have children later and live longer, more people find themselves in that position. The family remains the default welfare system, even in rich countries with large welfare states.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every arrangement trades security against freedom

There is no family form without costs. Each one protects some people and constrains others.

Chapter 13

What went right, and what went wrong

The gains of the last century are real. People in most countries can choose their partner, rather than having one chosen. Women can leave violent marriages and own property in their own name. Children are no longer labelled "illegitimate" in law in most of Europe and the Americas. People who were once excluded, such as same-sex couples in many countries, can form recognised families. Much of what is called the "breakdown" of the family is also the arrival of choice.

The costs are real too. Freedom to leave is also freedom to be left. Breakups fall hardest on children and on the partner with less income, usually the mother. Fewer siblings and children mean fewer people to care for you in old age. And the benefits of stable families now flow mostly to people who are already better off. Conservatives tend to stress the costs and the value of marriage; progressives tend to stress the gains and the need for support for all family forms. Both are describing parts of the same picture.

Chapter 15

The debates to watch

Three arguments will shape family policy in the coming years. The first is about birth rates. Some see very low fertility as a crisis for pensions and economies and want governments to act. Others argue that the answer lies in making work and parenthood compatible, through childcare, parental leave and fairer sharing of housework, rather than in urging people to have children. Evidence from the Nordic countries and France suggests that such support helps, but it has not reversed the decline anywhere.

The second is about who the law recognises: cohabiting partners, same-sex parents, children born through surrogacy, and people with more than two legal parents. Countries are moving in different directions, and religious and cultural traditions weigh heavily. The third is about care: whether the work families once did unpaid will be paid for by the state, bought in the market, or left on the shoulders of daughters. That is less a question about family than about what kind of society people want. The family will keep changing shape. What it will not lose is its role as the place where most people first learn who they are.

The core idea

The family is an institution, not a fixed shape. It produces and raises children, pools resources, gives care and places people in society. When the economy, the birth rate and the law change what it needs to do, its shape changes too.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World fertility fell from about 5 children per woman in the 1960s to about 2.3 in the early 2020s; South Korea was near 0.7 in 2023.
  • About 4 in 10 births in the EU and the US are outside marriage; in Japan, only a few per cent.
  • English households were mostly nuclear, with fewer than five people on average, centuries before industrialisation.
The common mistake

Treating the 1950s breadwinner-and-housewife family as the ancient, natural norm. It was a brief exception, and most families in history relied on everyone's work.
