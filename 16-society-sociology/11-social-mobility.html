Social mobility. How much of your life was decided by the postcode you were born in.

Most modern societies make a promise: work hard and you can do better than your parents, wherever you started. Social mobility is the measure of whether that promise is kept. It turns out to be one of the most revealing numbers a country has. It shows how fair a society is, how much talent it wastes, and why so many people believe, rightly or wrongly, that the game is rigged.

Question 1 · What is it?

Movement up and down the ladder

Social mobility is the movement of people between positions in society, measured by income, job, education or class. The interesting question is how much your starting point decides your finishing point.

Chapter 01

Nine in ten, then one in two

In 2017 a team led by the American economist Raj Chetty asked a simple question. Of all the Americans born in a given year, what share grew up to earn more than their parents had at the same age, after adjusting for inflation? For children born in 1940, the answer was about nine in ten. For children born in 1980, it was about one in two.

Nothing in the finding says that those born in 1980 worked less hard. The difference came from the economy around them. The 1940 generation grew up during a long boom in which growth was fast and its gains were spread widely. By the 1980s growth was slower and a larger share of it went to people at the top. The researchers calculated that most of the decline came from that unequal sharing, rather than from slower growth alone.

This is what social mobility research does. It turns a vague feeling, "it was easier for my parents", into something that can be measured, explained and argued about with evidence.

The difference between absolute and relative mobility matters most. In a fast-growing economy, almost everyone can be better off than their parents even if nobody changes rank. That is absolute mobility. Relative mobility is harder: for one person to climb a rank, someone else must fall one. Relative mobility is a zero-sum game; absolute mobility is not. A zero-sum game is one where one side's gain is exactly another side's loss. Much confusion in public debate comes from mixing the two.

Chapter 03

Measuring the stickiness

Economists often sum up relative mobility with one number, the intergenerational elasticity of earnings. It sounds technical, but the idea is simple. It tells you how much of a parent's income advantage tends to show up in the child's income. A value of 0 means parents' income tells you nothing about the child's. A value of 1 means the gap passes on completely.

Estimates by the Canadian economist Miles Corak, among others, put this number at roughly 0.15 to 0.2 in Denmark, Norway and Finland, and close to 0.5 in the United Kingdom, the United States and Italy. In plain terms: in the US, about half of a father's income advantage over the average tends to pass to his son; in Denmark, less than a fifth. Exact figures vary by study and method.

Another common tool is a transition table. You divide parents and children into five equal income groups, called quintiles, and ask what share of children born in each group end up in each. In a perfectly mobile society, a child from the bottom fifth would have a 20% chance of reaching the top fifth. In US data, the figure is well below that, around 7 to 8% in Chetty's estimates.

The Great Gatsby curve

When countries are plotted with inequality on one axis and the stickiness of income across generations on the other, they line up: more unequal countries tend to have less mobility. The American economist Alan Krueger named this pattern the "Great Gatsby curve" in 2012, after the novel about a self-made man who cannot buy his way into old money. The link is a correlation, and economists still debate how much is cause and effect.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The channels that carry advantage

Parents pass on advantage through many pipes at once. Some are obvious, such as money. Others, such as friendships and neighbourhoods, were only measured properly in the last decade.

Chapter 04

Money, time, knowledge and luck

Richer parents can buy more: better housing, tutoring, music lessons, healthier food, and the safety net of a family loan when a child's first business fails. But money is only one pipe. Parents also pass on time (reading at bedtime, help with homework), knowledge of how systems work (which school to choose, how to write a university application), and confidence (the sense that places like universities are "for people like us").

The French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu called this last group cultural capital: the tastes, manners, ways of speaking and knowledge that institutions reward but do not openly teach. A child who has heard dinner-table debates about books can perform well in an interview that looks neutral but quietly tests exactly those skills.

There is also health. Poor children are more likely to suffer illness, stress and poor nutrition, which can affect learning. And there are genes. Twin and adoption studies show that some of the similarity between parents and children is inherited, but how much, and how genes and environment interact, is still debated. Most researchers now agree that inheritance, money and environment all matter, and that they are tangled together.

Chapter 05

Education: ladder and filter

Education is the main official route up. In most rich countries, a university degree still brings a substantial earnings advantage over a lifetime. Expanding schooling, as many countries did after 1945, gave millions of working-class children their first chance at professional jobs.

But education can also preserve advantage. Gaps between rich and poor children appear before they start school and often widen afterwards. Better-off families can buy homes near good schools, pay for private schools, or afford tutoring. At the top, the effect can be stark. A 2017 study by Chetty's team found that dozens of elite American colleges, including several in the Ivy League, had more students from the richest 1% of families than from the poorest 60%.

The sociologist's word for this double role is reproduction: institutions that seem to reward talent can end up reproducing the existing class structure. Education is both the strongest ladder and a filter that favours those who start nearer the top. Which role dominates depends on how a country funds and organises its schools; see Education.

Chapter 06

Place: the neighbourhood you grow up in

In the 1990s the US government ran an experiment called Moving to Opportunity. Families in public housing in five cities were chosen by lottery, and some received vouchers to move to lower-poverty neighbourhoods. Early results were disappointing: parents' earnings barely changed.

Twenty years later, Chetty and colleagues looked at the children. Those who moved before about age 13 earned around a third more as adults than similar children who stayed, and were more likely to go to college. Children who moved as teenagers gained little. The benefit grew with each year a child spent in the better neighbourhood. Place, in other words, works like a slow dose.

Using tax records, the same researchers mapped mobility for every neighbourhood in the United States. They found huge differences between places only a few miles apart. Areas with more two-parent families, less segregation, better schools and more community groups tended to produce more upward mobility. Similar maps are now being built for other countries.

Chapter 07

Who your friends are

In 2022, Chetty's team published two papers in the journal Nature using anonymised data on billions of friendships from Facebook. They measured what they called economic connectedness: how many friends from higher-income backgrounds a lower-income person has. It turned out to be one of the strongest predictors of upward mobility found so far, stronger than school quality or job availability in their analysis.

Why would friends matter? Friends pass on information about jobs and colleges. They shape what seems normal to aim for. They can make an introduction. And the research found two reasons why cross-class friendships are rare: people of different classes often do not meet (they live, study and work apart), and even when they meet, they tend to befriend people like themselves. Mixing places, such as schools, sports clubs and churches, matter only if they actually lead to friendships across the divide.

Chapter 08

Sticky floors and glass floors

Advantage is sticky at both ends. At the bottom, a sticky floor holds people back: poor health, debt, a criminal record or caring duties make it hard to take the first step. At the top, a glass floor protects children of well-off families who do less well at school from falling far. Their families' networks, money and second chances catch them.

How sticky is it over the very long run? The British economist Gregory Clark tracked rare surnames over many generations, for example in England and Sweden, and argued in The Son Also Rises (2014) that underlying status persists far more than single-generation studies suggest, across centuries. His method and conclusions are contested. Critics argue that surname groups hide a lot of individual movement. The debate is a useful reminder: how much mobility you find depends on what you measure and over how long.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fairness, wasted talent and political trust

Mobility is where economics meets morality. It shapes whether people accept inequality, how much talent a society uses, and how they vote.

Chapter 09

The promise that makes inequality bearable

Many people accept large income gaps if they believe the race is fair: if the rich earned their place and anyone could follow. That belief is called meritocracy, rule by merit. The word itself was coined as a warning. The British sociologist Michael Young introduced it in a 1958 satire, The Rise of the Meritocracy, imagining a future in which people sorted by test scores become an arrogant new upper class and those left at the bottom have no excuse but their own "failure".

The philosopher Michael Sandel revived the argument in The Tyranny of Merit (2020). He argued that telling people the system is fair can make losing feel like a personal verdict, which breeds resentment. Defenders of meritocracy reply that the alternative, rewards by birth or connection, is worse. Both sides agree that if mobility is low, the meritocratic story becomes hard to defend.

Chapter 10

Lost Einsteins

In 2019 economists including Alex Bell and Raj Chetty linked US patent records with tax data. They found that children from the richest 1% of families were about ten times more likely to become inventors than children from families below the median income. Among children with similar high maths scores at school, the gap was still large.

The authors called the missing inventors "lost Einsteins": people with the ability to innovate who never got the exposure. Children who grew up near inventors, or whose parents worked in technical fields, were far more likely to invent, and often in the same field. Low mobility is not only unfair to individuals. It shrinks the pool of talent that the whole economy can draw on.

Chapter 11

What people believe about mobility

Beliefs about mobility shape politics as much as the real numbers do. A 2018 study by the economists Alberto Alesina, Stefanie Stantcheva and Edoardo Teso surveyed people in the United States and several European countries. Americans tended to be too optimistic about the chance of a poor child rising to the top, while many Europeans were too pessimistic. People who believed mobility was high were less supportive of redistribution through taxes and benefits.

Perceptions also explain anger. When large groups, such as young people unable to buy homes their parents could afford, believe the ladder has been pulled up, trust in institutions falls. Some analysts link this to support for anti-establishment parties, though the causes of such votes are debated. A society can survive inequality more easily than the belief that effort no longer pays.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Weighing a society that moves

Almost everyone says they want more mobility. Looking closely, high mobility has costs as well as benefits, and some policies that raise it are hard sells.

The table shows why the debate is political. The tools that most reliably raise mobility, such as high-quality early childhood education, mixed neighbourhoods and schools, and taxes on large inheritances, all ask the comfortable to give something up. Mobility from the bottom is popular. The matching downward mobility at the top is not.

There is also a quieter point. A society where everyone strives to climb can be exhausting. Some countries with high mobility, such as the Nordic ones, also have smaller gaps between rungs, so falling a rung matters less. Narrowing the distance between rungs may matter as much as making it easier to climb them.

Chapter 14

The policy debate to watch

Three policy ideas recur in mobility debates, and each has strong support and serious critics. The first is early childhood investment: high-quality care and education before age five, which several long-term studies suggest pays off for disadvantaged children. The cost is high and benefits take decades to show. The second is opening up housing: building more homes in high-opportunity areas and helping families move there, as Moving to Opportunity suggested. Local residents often resist. The third is taxing inheritances and large gifts to slow the passing of wealth. Supporters see it as fairness. Opponents see it as punishing families for saving and as double taxation.

Underneath them all is the same question. Should a society aim to make the race fairer, or to make the gap between winners and losers smaller? Most countries try some of both. The mobility evidence of the last decade suggests the two are linked more closely than people once thought: where the rungs are far apart, they are harder to climb.

The core idea

Social mobility measures how far your starting point decides your finishing point. Absolute mobility asks whether you are better off than your parents; relative mobility asks whether you changed rank. Advantage passes through money, time, cultural capital, education, neighbourhood and friendships. More unequal countries tend to have less mobility.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US children out-earning their parents: about 90% of those born in 1940, about 50% of those born in 1980.
  • Intergenerational elasticity: roughly 0.15–0.2 in Nordic countries, close to 0.5 in the US and UK.
  • Top-1% children were about 10 times as likely to become US inventors as below-median children.
The common mistake

Confusing "everyone got richer" with "people moved up". Absolute gains can be large while relative positions barely change, and the reverse.

Can you explain why moving a child to a better neighbourhood at age 8 helps much more than at age 16? If you can, you understand why place works as an exposure effect.
