Population growth. The explosion was really a fall in deaths, and it is ending on its own.

For almost all of human history, the number of people on Earth grew so slowly that nobody alive would have noticed. Then, in about two hundred years, it went from one billion to eight. Most people assume that happened because families got bigger. Mostly it did not. It happened because far fewer children died. And now, in country after country, families are shrinking faster than anyone predicted. This file explains the mechanism, the fears it produced, the mistakes made in its name, and why the next big population debate may be about too few people rather than too many.

Question 1 · What is it?

A curve that stayed flat for millennia, then shot up

Population growth is the change in the number of people over time. Its modern history is one of the most dramatic curves in any dataset.

Chapter 01

The eight-billionth person

The United Nations chose 15 November 2022 as the symbolic day the world reached eight billion people. Nobody can know which baby was actually the eight-billionth; the date is an estimate from statistical models. But the milestone was real, and it came only about eleven years after seven billion.

The surprise lies in the deeper history. Around 10,000 years ago, when farming began, there were perhaps a few million humans. It took until roughly 1800 to reach the first billion. Population grew, but famine, war and above all disease kept cancelling the gains. A family might have six or seven children and see only two or three reach adulthood. Then the brakes came off.

The world's growth rate reached about 2% a year in the late 1960s, the fastest in history, and has fallen roughly by half since. Each new billion still arrives quickly because the base is so large, but the acceleration is over.

Chapter 03

The measures demographers use

Demography is the study of populations. Its basic arithmetic is simple. A population grows when births plus people arriving exceed deaths plus people leaving. For the world as a whole, migration cancels out, so only births and deaths matter.

The single most useful number is the total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have over her life if today's birth rates held. A TFR of about 2.1 is called replacement level in rich countries: each couple replaces itself, plus a little extra because some children die young and slightly more boys than girls are born. Where child deaths are common, replacement is higher.

Growth rates also hide a trick of compounding. A rough rule, the rule of 70, says you divide 70 by the yearly growth rate to get the doubling time. At 2% a year, a population doubles in about 35 years. At 0.5%, it takes about 140. Small differences in rate make huge differences over a lifetime, which Volume 38 explains for money (Interest, and the astonishing effect of compounding).

Question 2 · How does it work?

The demographic transition: deaths fall first, births follow

Almost every country that has modernised has gone through the same sequence. The gap in time between its two steps is what produces a population boom.

Chapter 04

Why people stopped dying young

Estimates suggest average life expectancy at birth was only about 30 years in 1800, almost everywhere. That low average did not mean adults died at 30. It meant huge numbers of babies and young children died, pulling the average down. Today global life expectancy is above 70.

The change came from several directions. Clean water and sewers cut diseases such as cholera and typhoid. Vaccines, starting with smallpox, and later antibiotics reduced deadly infections. Better nutrition made children more resistant to illness. Simple knowledge, such as washing hands and boiling water, spread. In Europe these changes took over a century. In much of Asia, Latin America and Africa after 1945 they arrived within decades, because the knowledge and the medicines already existed.

Food supply kept pace thanks to two inventions in particular. The Haber-Bosch process, developed in Germany in the 1910s, made synthetic nitrogen fertiliser. The "Green Revolution" of the 1960s, led by scientists such as Norman Borlaug, spread high-yielding wheat and rice varieties. Death rates fell first, and fell fast. Birth rates did not follow at once.

Chapter 05

The four stages

Demographers in the twentieth century, notably Warren Thompson and Frank Notestein, described the pattern now called the demographic transition.

In stage 1, both births and deaths are high, so population barely grows. In stage 2, deaths fall but births stay high, so population surges. In stage 3, births start to fall too. In stage 4, both are low and growth almost stops. Many demographers add a stage 5, which much of Europe and East Asia has now entered, in which births fall below deaths and population starts to shrink.

The model is a description, not a law. Countries move through it at different speeds, and the reasons vary. But it explains the big picture: the population explosion was the gap between two falls.

Chapter 06

Why families got smaller

Why do birth rates eventually fall? Researchers point to a cluster of causes that reinforce each other, and they argue about the weight of each.

Children survive

When parents can expect their children to live, they no longer need extra births as insurance. Fertility decline almost always follows a fall in child deaths.

Girls go to school

Educated women tend to marry later, work for pay and have fewer children. Women's education is one of the strongest predictors of lower fertility found in research.

Cities change the economics

On a farm, children work. In a city, they cost money for housing and schooling. Urbanisation makes children more expensive and less economically useful.

Contraception and ideas

Modern contraception lets couples act on their choices. Television, migration and changing norms spread the idea that small families are normal.

The speed can be startling. In Iran, fertility fell from more than six children per woman in the early 1980s to about two by the early 2000s. Bangladesh, a poor and mostly rural country, went from around six in the 1970s to near two today, helped by a large family-planning programme and community health workers. Fertility can fall fast even without high incomes, once child survival, education and access to contraception improve together.

Chapter 07

Momentum: why growth continues after families shrink

Here is a puzzle. Suppose a country's fertility dropped to replacement level overnight. Its population would still keep growing for decades. Why? Because of population momentum. A country that has just come through a boom has an enormous number of young people. Even with only two children each, there are so many parents that births exceed deaths among the much smaller older generation.

Momentum explains a large part of the growth still expected this century. The Swedish doctor and statistician Hans Rosling liked to point out that the number of children in the world had more or less stopped growing: we had reached "peak child". The extra billions still to come are mostly adults: today's children growing up and living longer.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Two centuries of fear, prediction and policy

Population has been one of the most argued-about subjects in modern history. The arguments led to great achievements and to serious abuses.

Chapter 08

Malthus and the race between mouths and food

In 1798 the English clergyman Thomas Robert Malthus published An Essay on the Principle of Population. His argument was that population tends to grow geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8…) while food supply grows only arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4…). So any improvement in living standards would be eaten up by more births, until famine, disease or misery pushed numbers back down.

For most of history before him, Malthus was roughly right: gains in food did produce more people rather than richer people. That is why economists speak of a Malthusian trap. He was wrong about what came next. The industrial and agricultural revolutions raised food output faster than population, and then, crucially, people chose to have fewer children. He did not foresee that prosperity itself would lower birth rates.

Chapter 09

The Population Bomb, and a famous bet

In 1968, at the peak of the growth rate, the biologist Paul Ehrlich published The Population Bomb. It warned that hundreds of millions would starve in the 1970s and that it might already be too late to prevent mass famine. The book sold millions of copies and shaped a generation's view.

The mass famines it predicted did not happen on that scale, largely because of the Green Revolution. In 1980 the economist Julian Simon challenged Ehrlich to a bet: Ehrlich chose five metals, and if their prices rose over ten years, which would suggest growing scarcity, he would win. By 1990 all five had fallen in real terms, and Simon won. Ehrlich's defenders note that over other ten-year periods he would have won, and that his wider environmental concerns were not refuted by one bet. The episode is still used by both sides: as proof that human ingenuity beats scarcity, and as a warning against reading too much into short-term prices.

Chapter 10

When fear became coercion

Population fear had a dark side. In India, during the state of emergency of 1975 to 1977, a sterilisation campaign led to millions of operations, many of them coerced, especially among poor men. The backlash was so strong that it damaged family planning in India for years. In China, the one-child policy, introduced around 1980 and replaced by a two-child limit in 2016, was enforced with fines, pressure and, in many documented cases, forced abortions and sterilisations. It also contributed to a large surplus of men, because many families preferred sons. How much of China's fertility fall it caused, compared with economic change, is debated.

The turning point in international policy came at the UN conference on population in Cairo in 1994. Governments agreed that population policy should be built on individual rights, women's health and voluntary access to contraception, not on targets. The lesson most experts drew is that the most effective path to lower fertility is also the most humane one: child survival, girls' education and freely chosen family planning.

Chapter 11

The demographic dividend, and where growth remains

When fertility falls, a country passes through a window in which it has many working-age adults and relatively few children and elderly people to support. If those adults have jobs, savings rise and the economy can grow fast. This is called the demographic dividend. Economists believe it explains part of East Asia's rapid growth from the 1960s to the 1990s. It is an opportunity, not a guarantee: without jobs, a large young population can mean unemployment and unrest.

Today most of the world's remaining population growth is in sub-Saharan Africa, where average fertility is still above four children per woman, and in parts of South Asia and the Middle East. India became the world's most populous country in 2023, overtaking China, whose population began to fall in 2022. Where the dividend is captured, and where it is missed, will shape the global economy for the rest of the century. See Demographic change for the regional picture.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

More people: more problems, or more problem-solvers?

Population growth is neither good nor bad in itself. Its effects depend on how fast it happens, where, and with what institutions.

Chapter 13

Is population the problem? The I = PAT argument

In the early 1970s Paul Ehrlich and the physicist John Holdren proposed a simple equation: Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology. Environmental damage depends on how many people there are, how much each one consumes, and how damaging the technology used to produce it is. The equation is a way to organise the argument, not a precise measurement.

"Population matters"

Every extra person adds demand for food, energy and space. Even modest consumption, multiplied by billions, is a lot. Slower growth makes every environmental goal easier to reach.

"Consumption matters more"

The richest billion people account for a far larger share of emissions than the poorest several billion, where most growth now happens. Blaming population can shift attention from rich countries' consumption to poor women's bodies.

Both points can be true at once. Population size matters, but how people live matters at least as much, and the policies that slow population growth well are the same ones that improve lives anyway. Consumption is discussed in Consumer culture.

Chapter 15

The new worry: too few babies

Half a century after The Population Bomb, the anxiety in many countries has reversed. South Korea's fertility rate fell to about 0.72 children per woman in 2023, the lowest in the world, meaning each generation is barely a third the size of its parents' if nothing changes. Global fertility was about 2.3 in the mid-2020s, against about five in the 1950s. Most of Europe, East Asia and the Americas are below replacement.

Governments have tried cash bonuses, longer parental leave, cheaper childcare and housing support. The evidence so far suggests these policies help families have the children they already wanted, but rarely push fertility back to replacement. People cite costs, housing, work pressure and changing ideas about what a good life looks like. The live debates are now about how societies adapt: older workforces, more immigration, automation, and pensions designed for fewer workers. Those consequences are the subject of Aging populations.

The deepest point is the one this whole file has been heading towards. Population growth was never a runaway process. It was a transition, a temporary gap between falling deaths and falling births. The gap is closing. The question for the coming century is not how to stop growth, but how to live well on the other side of it.

The core idea

Population exploded because death rates fell before birth rates. As child survival, education, cities and contraception spread, families shrink: the demographic transition. Momentum keeps numbers rising for a while, but growth is slowing everywhere and has reversed in many countries.

The numbers worth knowing
  • One billion around 1800; eight billion in 2022.
  • Growth peaked at about 2% a year in the late 1960s.
  • Replacement fertility is about 2.1; UN projections (2024) put the peak near 10.3 billion in the 2080s.
The common mistake

Thinking the boom came from bigger families. It came from fewer deaths, and the growth still ahead comes mostly from today's young people growing up and living longer.

Can you explain why a country whose fertility drops to 2.1 tomorrow would still grow for decades? If so, you understand population momentum.
