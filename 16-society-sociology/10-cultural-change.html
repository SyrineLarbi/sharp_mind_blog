Cultural change. Nothing moves for years, and then everyone remembers always believing it.

Culture feels permanent from the inside. The way people greet each other, what counts as polite, who is allowed to marry whom: these seem like facts of nature until you look at your grandparents' lives. Then you notice that much of what felt fixed has moved, sometimes within one generation. This file explains what culture is, how it changes, why the change is often slow and then sudden, and why it matters so much to politics, business and ordinary family life.

Question 1 · What is it?

The shared rules nobody wrote down, and how they move

Culture is everything a group learns and passes on that is not in its genes. Cultural change is any lasting shift in that shared inheritance.

Chapter 01

The ashtray on the office desk

Picture an office in Dublin in 1990. There is an ashtray on most desks. People smoke in meetings, in pubs, on buses and in hospital waiting rooms. Nobody thinks this is strange. Non-smokers may dislike it, but asking someone to put out a cigarette in a pub would feel rude.

On 29 March 2004, Ireland became the first country to ban smoking in all enclosed workplaces, pubs included. Critics predicted that the law would be ignored, because the pub was close to the heart of Irish social life. It was not ignored. Within a few years, lighting a cigarette indoors had become something most people would not even consider. Many other countries followed.

The story shows the three parts of almost every cultural change. There was a slow build-up (decades of medical evidence on the harm of smoking). There was a trigger (a law). And then there was a fast flip in what felt normal. The law did not just change behaviour. It changed what people expected of each other, and that is what made the change stick.

Chapter 02

The layers of a culture

Sociologists, the scientists who study how societies work, break culture into layers. They change at very different speeds.

LayerWhat it isExampleTypical speed of change
Artefacts and fashionsObjects, styles, slang, musicClothes, phone apps, popular songsMonths to a few years
PracticesHabits and routinesHow people shop, eat, date, payYears to a decade
NormsUnwritten rules about what is acceptableSmoking indoors, swearing on televisionAbout a decade to a generation
ValuesDeep beliefs about what is good and importantObedience versus independence in childrenA generation or more
InstitutionsOrganised, lasting structures built on valuesMarriage law, schools, religionSlow, often in sudden jumps

A norm is an expectation about behaviour that people enforce on each other, usually with small signals: a frown, a joke, an invitation not sent. A value is the deeper belief that norms rest on. You can change a norm quickly with a law or a scandal. Values usually change only as people grow up in different conditions from their parents.

Chapter 03

Two kinds of change: drift and shift

Some cultural change is drift. Nobody plans it and nobody notices it until it is done. Languages drift this way: English speakers of 1500 would struggle to understand English today, yet no one ever decided to change it. Names, recipes and manners drift too.

Other change is a shift: a fast, visible break, often fought over. The end of legal racial segregation in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s, the spread of women's right to vote in the twentieth century (see Votes for women), and the legalisation of same-sex marriage in many countries since 2001 are shifts. They had long roots, but the visible change came in a burst. Most real change is both: a long drift that suddenly crosses a line and becomes a shift.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Engines, carriers and tipping points

Something has to push a culture, someone has to carry the new idea, and at some point enough people have to switch at once. Each step has been studied.

Chapter 04

The five engines

Culture rarely changes because someone wins an argument. It changes because the conditions of life change, and old habits stop fitting. The main engines are these.

Technology

The contraceptive pill (available in many Western countries from the 1960s) changed when women married, how long they studied and whether they worked. The printing press, cars and smartphones each reshaped daily life and then values.

Economy

When most people farm, large families and obedience to elders make sense. When most people work in offices and cities, education and independence pay. Moving from one economy to the other moves values with it.

Contact

Trade, migration, travel and media carry ideas between groups. Sociologists call this diffusion: the spread of a practice from one culture to another, as tea, Christianity, jeans and sushi all spread.

Demography and shocks

Who is born, who dies and who moves changes the mix of views in a society. Wars, pandemics and revolutions can compress decades of change into a few years.

A fifth engine is deliberate effort: social movements, campaigns, laws and religious revivals. These matter, but they mostly succeed when the other engines have already prepared the ground.

In 1922 the American sociologist William Ogburn described what he called cultural lag. Material things, such as machines, change faster than the rules and beliefs around them. The gap causes trouble until the norms catch up. Car traffic arrived before traffic laws. Social media arrived before anyone agreed on its etiquette. Much of the friction people feel in a fast-changing society is cultural lag.

Chapter 05

New people, or people who changed their minds?

A society's views can change in two ways. Individuals can change their minds. Or older people with older views can die, and younger people with new views can replace them. Researchers call the second process generational replacement (or cohort replacement; a cohort is a group born around the same time).

For deep values, replacement does most of the work. People's core values are largely set in their teens and early twenties and then change slowly. That is why the political scientist Ronald Inglehart, who studied value change for half a century, predicted that changes in values would show up decades after changes in living conditions.

But some attitudes move far faster than replacement allows. In the United States, the polling company Gallup found that only about 4% of Americans approved of marriage between Black and white people in 1958. By 2021, about 94% did. Support for same-sex marriage rose from about 27% in 1996 to about 70% in the early 2020s. That is too fast to be explained by deaths and births alone. Many people changed their own minds, often because they came to know someone personally who was affected. Psychologists have long found that personal contact with members of another group tends to reduce prejudice, especially under conditions of equal status.

Chapter 06

Tipping points and the secret majority

Norms are strange things. People often follow a norm not because they personally believe in it, but because they think everyone else does. Psychologists call it pluralistic ignorance when most people privately reject a norm while wrongly believing that most others accept it. Everyone keeps quiet, so the norm looks stronger than it is.

An influential economics study published in 2020 looked at young married men in Saudi Arabia. Most of them privately supported women working outside the home, but they underestimated how many other men agreed. When researchers told some of them the true figure, those men became more willing to help their wives look for work. The norm was weaker than it looked, and information alone moved it.

This explains why change can be sudden. If a norm is held up mostly by mistaken beliefs about others, it can collapse once people see the true picture, as on the day a law, a court case or a public event makes private views visible. In a 2018 experiment, the sociologist Damon Centola and colleagues had groups of people agree on names for things online. When a committed minority of roughly a quarter pushed a new name, the whole group often switched to it. Below that size the minority usually failed. The exact threshold in real societies varies and is debated, but the lesson holds: a committed minority can flip a norm long before it becomes a majority.

Chapter 07

How a new idea spreads: the S-curve

In 1962 the American scholar Everett Rogers published Diffusion of Innovations, a study of how new practices spread, from farming methods to medical habits. He found the same shape again and again. Adoption starts slowly, speeds up and then levels off. Plotted over time, it makes an S-shaped curve.

Rogers sorted adopters into groups: a few innovators, then early adopters (often respected local figures who others watch), then the early and late majority, then the people slowest to change. The key insight is about the early adopters. An idea spreads when people who are trusted and well connected take it up, not just when it is good. Ideas cross from group to group through people who belong to both. That is why migrants, students abroad, traders and, today, online creators so often carry change.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Values shape votes, markets and family life

Cultural change is not a side story to politics and economics. It often explains them.

Chapter 08

The map of the world's values

Since 1981 the World Values Survey, a long-running international research project, has asked people in about a hundred countries the same questions about religion, family, work, trust and politics. Ronald Inglehart and Christian Welzel used the answers to draw a cultural map with two main dimensions.

DimensionOne endOther end
Traditional versus secular-rationalReligion, national pride, respect for authority and the family are centralLess emphasis on religion and authority; more acceptance of divorce and abortion
Survival versus self-expressionEconomic and physical security come first; lower trust in strangersPriority on freedom, tolerance, participation and quality of life

Inglehart's main argument, first made in his 1977 book The Silent Revolution, was that security breeds self-expression. People who grow up without fear of hunger or war take survival for granted and start to care more about freedom and choice. He called these "post-materialist" values. Countries that grew richer and safer generally moved towards self-expression over the following decades, though at different speeds and from different starting points. Religious and historical heritage still shapes where each country sits on the map.

Chapter 09

Backlash: when change moves faster than some people

Change does not affect everyone at the same pace. In many countries, younger, urban and university-educated people have moved quickly towards self-expression values, while older, rural and less-educated people have moved less. When a society's official culture, in its media, schools and laws, moves to the new values, those who still hold the old ones can feel like strangers in their own country.

Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart argued in Cultural Backlash (2019) that this feeling helps explain the rise of populist parties in Europe and the United States. Other researchers put more weight on economic causes, such as lost industrial jobs, or on immigration. The relative weight of culture and economics in the populist wave is a live and contested debate, and most scholars think both matter. What is clear is that fast cultural change creates winners and losers, and the losers vote.

Chapter 10

Culture in the office and the market

In the late 1960s and 1970s the Dutch researcher Geert Hofstede analysed surveys of IBM employees in dozens of countries. He described national differences in, among other things, power distance (how much people accept that power is unequal) and individualism (whether people see themselves first as individuals or as members of groups). His framework is still taught in business schools, and it is also criticised for treating nations as uniform and for relying on one company's staff.

For anyone who works across borders, the practical lesson is simple. A manager who invites open disagreement may look strong in one culture and weak in another. A customer who says "maybe" may mean "no". Products fail when they assume one culture's habits are universal. And because cultures change, yesterday's national stereotype is often today's mistake: young professionals in Seoul, Lagos and Riyadh in 2026 may have more in common with each other than with their grandparents.

A last reason cultural change matters is personal. Families now often contain three or four generations raised in very different conditions. Many arguments at the dinner table are not about facts but about norms that moved between one generation's youth and the next. Seeing them that way can make them easier to manage (see Generational differences).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Adaptation has a price

A culture that never changed could not survive new conditions. A culture that changed constantly could not give anyone a stable sense of who they are.

Chapter 11

What change gives, and what it takes

AreaWhat change givesWhat change costs
Fit with new conditionsHabits adapt to new technology, new work and new risks.During the lag, old and new rules clash and people are unsure what is expected.
FreedomGroups once excluded gain rights and choices.Some people lose the status or certainty the old order gave them.
Diversity of ideasContact between cultures produces new food, music, science and art.Small cultures and languages can be absorbed by large ones and disappear.
Social trustOld prejudices fade as groups mix.Fast change can split a society into camps that no longer understand each other.
IdentityIndividuals can choose who to become.Choice can feel like loss of roots, belonging and meaning.

Language shows the cost most clearly. Linguists count roughly 7,000 languages in the world, and they class a large share of them, often estimated at around 40%, as endangered. Many have only a few elderly speakers. When a language dies, so do its songs, jokes and ways of describing the world. The same contact that spreads good ideas also flattens differences, and no one has found a way to have one without some of the other.

Chapter 12

Is change the same as progress?

It is tempting to tell the story of culture as a march from darkness to light, with your own society's current views as the destination. Historians call this Whig history, after British writers who told national history as an inevitable rise to their own political ideals. It is a trap. Some past changes now look like mistakes, and some old practices, such as extended families caring for their elderly, are things modern societies are trying to rebuild.

There are two honest positions, and thoughtful people hold some of each. The reformer says that many traditions protected the powerful and harmed the weak, so they deserve to be questioned. The conservative argues, in a line of thought associated with the eighteenth-century writer Edmund Burke, that traditions often contain practical wisdom that no single generation fully understands. The writer G.K. Chesterton made the point with an image now known as "Chesterton's fence": before you pull down a fence in a field, find out why it was put there. A fair judgement asks of each change what problem the old way solved, and whether the new way solves it better.

Chapter 14

One world culture, or a patchwork?

For decades people feared that globalisation meant Americanisation: the same burgers, films and brands everywhere. Some of that happened. English became the world's main second language, and a few global platforms carry much of the world's entertainment.

But the flow is no longer one way. South Korean music and television, Nigerian Afrobeats, Japanese anime, Indian cinema and Turkish television series have huge audiences far from home. The sociologist Roland Robertson used the word glocalisation for the way global products are adapted to local tastes, as when a fast-food chain sells a different menu in each country. The likely future is neither one culture nor a world of sealed ones. It is a world where everyone can draw on everything, and where local identity becomes something people choose and display rather than simply inherit. That makes culture richer. It also makes it more contested, because what you choose to keep becomes a statement.

The core idea

Culture is the shared, learned inheritance of a group: artefacts, practices, norms, values and institutions, each changing at its own speed. It changes when the conditions of life change (technology, economy, contact, demography, shocks). Deep values move mostly by generational replacement; norms can tip suddenly once a committed minority and visible public signals reveal that the old rule was weaker than it looked.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US approval of Black–white marriage: about 4% in 1958, about 94% in 2021 (Gallup).
  • A committed minority of roughly a quarter flipped group conventions in Centola's 2018 experiment.
  • About 7,000 languages exist; a large share are endangered.
The common mistake

Treating today's culture as either permanent or as the final destination of progress. Every culture is a snapshot of something still moving, and some changes are later reversed.

Can you explain why public opinion on an issue can stay stuck for decades and then flip in a few years? If you can use the words "pluralistic ignorance" and "tipping point" in your answer, you have the mechanism.
