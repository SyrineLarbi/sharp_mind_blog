Sharp Mind 04 · credibility

Presence and status. The same sentence, delivered two ways, gets two different answers.

Everything in the first three handbooks happens before you open your mouth. This one is about what happens after. Two people can make the identical point in the same meeting and have it received as insight or as noise, and the difference is almost never the content — it is a small set of legible signals that you are currently sending without choosing them. This handbook makes them choosable.

The uncomfortable premise

People do not evaluate your ideas and then form an impression of you. They form an impression of you first — within seconds, from signals that have nothing to do with the idea — and then evaluate the idea through it. This is not fair and it is not going away, and the only two responses available are to be annoyed by it or to learn the signals.

Nothing here asks you to be someone else. Every technique in this handbook is about removing interference between what you actually think and what people hear.

What this does not cover

The inner work — social anxiety, the spotlight effect, self-efficacy, graded exposure, rejection tolerance — is a separate and prior problem, taught in master-yourself/phase-11, files 01 and 03. If you cannot speak in the meeting at all, this handbook is premature; go there first.

Also: phase-9, file 01 already covers nonverbal communication and takes a firm line against "body language hacks". This handbook agrees with that line and explains why in section 02 — the hacks fail because they are performed while absent, and presence is the thing that makes any of it read as genuine.

Part I · How you are judged

The two axes and the two signals

Three sections on the machinery of impression. Section 01 is the model; 02 and 03 are what to do with it.

Presence 01

Warmth × competence

Social psychology has converged on a consistent finding: when people size each other up, they are running two judgements, not one. Susan Fiske and colleagues formalised this as the stereotype content model, and versions of the same two dimensions appear across decades of work under different names — warmth and competence, communion and agency, trustworthiness and ability.

THE TWO AXES HIGH COMPETENCE │ ENVIED │ ADMIRED "impressive, but │ "impressive and on I don't trust │ my side" — trusted their intent" │ AND listened to │ LOW WARMTH ─────────────┼───────────────── HIGH WARMTH │ DISMISSED │ LIKED, NOT HEARD "not able, not │ "lovely person, not on my side" │ someone I'd consult" │ LOW COMPETENCE ⭐ WARMTH IS JUDGED FIRST, AND FASTER. The evolutionary reading is straightforward: knowing whether someone intends you harm matters before knowing whether they are capable of it. Intent first, ability second.

The practical consequence is the part almost nobody acts on. Ambitious, capable people systematically over-invest in the competence axis and under-invest in the warmth axis, and land in the envied quadrant. They are respected. They are not trusted, not helped, not warned when something is coming, and not defended when something goes wrong.

The envied position also has a specific instability: people are not sorry when someone in it fails. That is a real cost, paid later, invisibly.

If you are read asThe symptomThe correction
Competent, cold
the common trap for capable people		 Your points are accepted but you are not brought in early. You hear about things after they are decided. People are careful around you. Add warmth signals, not niceness. Credit people by name. Ask about the thing they mentioned last week. Be visibly pleased when someone else does well. Say the thing you are thinking instead of the polished version.
Warm, not competent Everybody likes you. Nobody asks your opinion on the hard thing. You are in the room but not consulted. Add competence signals: one exact number (file 01, §04), a position stated without hedges (§04 here), and the answer before the reasoning (file 03).
Neither You are largely invisible in the room. Warmth first — it is faster to move and it makes the competence signals land rather than read as posturing.
Warmth signals that cost nothing and work
  • Use the person's name when you agree with them: "Yes — building on what Amine said..." Attribution is the cheapest warmth signal that exists.
  • Remember one non-work thing and ask about it once. Not as a technique — genuinely note it. The asking is what registers.
  • Be visibly glad about other people's wins. Neutrality reads as coldness here; there is no neutral.
  • Say the unpolished version sometimes. Constant polish reads as distance. A little visible ordinary humanity moves the warmth axis more than anything deliberate.
  • Give the benefit of the doubt out loud: "I assume there's a reason for that I'm not seeing — what is it?" This is warmth and a good question simultaneously.
A caveat on the model

The two-dimensional structure of social judgement is among the better-replicated findings in social psychology. What follows from it for your behaviour is inference, not experiment — it is a model of how perception is organised, not a validated set of instructions. Treat the quadrants as a diagnostic that is usually right about which direction to move, not as a law.

Presence 02

Charisma is behaviour, not a trait

Olivia Fox Cabane's useful contribution in The Charisma Myth — extracted fully in file 09 — is the decomposition. Charisma is not a substance some people have. It is the impression produced by three behaviours, each of which is separately observable and separately trainable.

The three components
① PRESENCE   · are you actually here, right now?
   The largest of the three and the most faked. Presence
   is not eye contact — it is the absence of anywhere else.
   People detect it with startling accuracy, because the
   tells are involuntary: the half-second late response,
   the slightly wrong follow-up, the eyes that are aimed
   at you while focused past you.

② POWER      · could you affect the world if you chose to?
   Read from posture, pace, stillness, and — much more
   than people think — from how little you seem to need
   from the interaction.

③ WARMTH     · do you mean well toward me?
   Section 01. Judged first, fastest to move.

⭐ THE DECOMPOSITION IS THE USEFUL PART:
  POWER WITHOUT WARMTH      → intimidating; the envied
                              quadrant
  WARMTH WITHOUT POWER      → pleasant; not influential
  EITHER WITHOUT PRESENCE   → performed, and everyone
                              can tell

⇒ SO PRESENCE IS NOT ONE OF THREE INGREDIENTS. IT IS THE
  THING THAT MAKES THE OTHER TWO COUNT. This is exactly
  why "body language hacks" fail — a confident posture
  held by someone who is mentally elsewhere reads as
  someone doing a confident posture.

Training presence

Presence is a skill of attention, which means the drills are attention drills. They are unglamorous and they work.

The physical anchor

When you notice you have drifted, put attention on a physical sensation — your feet on the floor, your hands. Two seconds. It interrupts the drift and returns you to the room without anyone noticing. Cabane's version of this is attending to your toes; the specific target does not matter, the return does.

Stop composing your reply

The single biggest presence leak: listening to the first half of a sentence, deciding what you will say, and going somewhere else for the second half. You will catch yourself doing it constantly once you look. Let the reply come after they stop — that is what the three-second pause in file 01, §06 is structurally for.

One full second before responding

Shorter than the three-second pause and for a different purpose: it proves to yourself that you listened to the end. Answering on the last syllable is the clearest possible signal that you were not listening, you were waiting.

Close the laptop

Nothing in this handbook beats it. Divided attention is visible, it is read as a status move whether or not you meant it that way, and no verbal technique compensates for it.

Presence 03

Status is a verb

Keith Johnstone, teaching improvisation, noticed something his actors could use immediately: status is not a thing people have, it is a thing people do, continuously, in tiny behaviours. Two people meeting for the first time negotiate it within seconds without either intending to. His book Impro is the most underrated title in this library — see file 09.

SignalReads as lower statusReads as higher status
PaceFast, rushing to finish before you are interruptedUnhurried. Implies you expect to be allowed to finish
PausesFilled — um, so, like, yeahEmpty. Silence you are not anxious about
InflectionRising at the end of statements, turning them into questionsFalling at the end. The sentence lands
EndingTrailing off — "...so, yeah" — or seeking the nodFull stop, then quiet. Eye contact held a beat after
MovementFrequent small adjustments, touching the face, nodding while speakingStillness. Movement that is deliberate when it happens
Reaction timeInstant — answering, laughing, agreeing on the last syllableA beat. Which reads as considering rather than complying
Self-referenceApologising for existing — "sorry, just quickly"Taking the time without narrating that you are taking it
The part most people get wrong about this

The list above is not a recommendation to play high status all the time. Johnstone's actual insight is about flexibility — the people who are genuinely good at this move up and down deliberately, and the ones who are stuck at either end are the ones who struggle.

Permanent high status reads as cold and defensive, and it puts you back in the envied quadrant of section 01. Deliberately lowering your status — self-deprecation, admitting you were lost, asking a basic question, laughing at yourself — is one of the strongest warmth moves available, provided it is chosen rather than leaked. The difference between confident self-deprecation and anxious self-deprecation is entirely in whether it was a decision.

Part II · The language

What your sentences are doing without you

The three highest-return sections in this handbook. Section 04 in particular will change how you sound within a week, because it is subtraction rather than addition.

Presence 04

The hedging audit

Most people apologise for their own sentences while saying them. The apologies are small, habitual, and almost invisible from the inside — and they are the largest single drag on how your ideas are received.

The audit · find these in your own speech, then cut them
THE PADDING — pure subtraction, nothing is lost
  "just"           "I just wanted to check —"
                   → "I wanted to check —"  or just "Quick check:"
  "sorry"          "Sorry, can I ask —"
     (when         → "Can I ask —"
   nothing is        ⚠ keep "sorry" for actual apologies. Spent
   wrong)             on nothing, it is worth nothing when needed.
  "quickly"        "Just quickly —"
                   → say the thing
  "a bit" /        "It's a bit of a problem"
  "kind of"        → "It's a problem"

THE PRE-EMPTIVE SURRENDER — these cost the most
  "I'm not an expert but —"
     → you have told them to discount what follows. Cut it.
       If the boundary is real, put it AFTER, as a
       qualifier: "— though I've only looked at October."
  "This might be stupid but —"
     → now it is in the room as possibly stupid.
  "I could be wrong but —"
     → true of everything anyone says.
  "You've probably already thought of this —"
     → cut. If they have, they will say so.

THE TAKE-BACK AT THE END — undoes the whole sentence
  "...does that make sense?"
     → asks whether YOU were coherent. Instead ask for
       their view: "What's your read?" / "Where does that
       land with you?"  Same pause, opposite status.
  "...if that makes sense"      → cut
  "...but I don't know"         → cut
  "...so, yeah"                 → cut. FULL STOP AND STOP.
  "...right?" / upward tone     → land it downward

⭐ WHAT TO KEEP — NOT ALL HEDGES ARE HEDGING
  ✓ "I'd say about 60% confident" — that is CALIBRATION
  ✓ "This is based on October only" — that is a BOUNDARY
  ✓ "I don't know" — that is the strongest sentence here (§05)
  ✓ "The three MAIN reasons" — bounding a claim (file 03 §03)

⇒ THE LINE: LANGUAGE THAT BOUNDS WHAT YOU KNOW IS PRECISION.
  LANGUAGE THAT APOLOGISES FOR SAYING IT IS COST.
  Cut the second. Keep — and use more of — the first.

How to actually find yours

You cannot hear your own hedges in real time. Everyone believes they use fewer than they do. There is one method that works, and it is the recording drill:

The audit, once
  1. Record two minutes of yourself answering a hard question. Phone, alone, no preparation.
  2. Transcribe it by hand. By hand — the point is to write every word, including the ones you would edit out unconsciously.
  3. Circle every item from the lists above. Count them.
  4. Rewrite the two minutes without them, and say the new version out loud once.

The count is usually somewhere between eight and twenty in two minutes, and seeing it in your own handwriting does more than any amount of reading about it. You only have to do this once — after that you start catching them live.

An honest note on who pays for hedging

Hedged speech is not read identically from everyone. There is a substantial research literature on this, and it is genuinely messy: studies have found that people — women particularly, but also anyone junior, anyone new, anyone with an accent the room is not used to — can be penalised for hedging and penalised for its absence, a bind that does not have a clean technical solution.

Two things are still worth saying. First, the padding category above costs everyone and helps no one — just, sorry for nothing, does that make sense are pure loss regardless of who is speaking. Cut those without hesitation. Second, warmth signals (section 01) and hedge-cutting are not substitutes; the combination of direct and warm is what actually travels well, and it is a different thing from simply becoming blunter.

Presence 05

"I don't know" — and the three recoveries

This is the most avoided sentence in professional life and one of the most valuable, and the reason for the gap is a straightforward misreading of what it signals.

WHAT PEOPLE THINK vs WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS "I don't know" "I don't know" │ │ ▼ ▼ reveals a gap reveals CALIBRATION │ │ ▼ ▼ they think less they now know your of me confidence carries information │ ▼ EVERYTHING ELSE YOU SAY IS WORTH MORE ⇒ IF YOU NEVER SAY "I DON'T KNOW", YOUR "YES" MEANS NOTHING — because it is what you would have said either way. Admitting the boundary is what makes the inside of it credible.
The formula, and the three recoveries when you cannot simply defer
⭐ THE FORMULA — three parts, never fewer
   ① "I don't know."     flat, no wince, no apology
   ② what you will do    "Let me check with the ops team."
   ③ WHEN                "I'll come back to you Thursday
                          morning."

   ⚠ PART ③ IS THE WHOLE THING. "I don't know" alone is a
     dead end. "I don't know, I'll find out" is a promise
     with no edge. A DATE converts an admission into a
     commitment, and commitments are what credibility is
     actually made of.

   ⚠ AND THEN HIT THE DATE. Once. That is the entire
     mechanism. Miss it and you have spent more than you
     saved.

THE THREE RECOVERIES — when "I don't know" is not enough

① REFRAME — answer the adjacent question, and say so
   "I can't tell you the landed cost. What I can tell you
    is the freight component, which is the part that moved
    — is that useful?"
   ⚠ SAY THAT YOU ARE DOING IT. An unannounced reframe is
     a dodge and reads like one. An announced one reads
     as precision about what you actually know.

② QUESTION BACK — legitimately, not as deflection
   "I don't have a view yet. What's driving the question —
    is this about the budget or the timing?"
   ⇒ their answer often narrows it to something you DO
     know. Genuinely useful, not a trick.

③ PARTIAL — draw the line out loud
   "Here's what I'm confident about: the volume numbers.
    Here's where my knowledge stops: whether the contract
    allows it. I'd want Legal on that second part."
   ⭐ THIS IS THE MOST SENIOR-SOUNDING MOVE IN THIS
     HANDBOOK. Knowing precisely where your knowledge ends
     is rarer than knowing things.

WHAT NOT TO DO — all three are transparent
  ✗ bluffing — you will be found out, and it is retroactive:
    everything you said before becomes suspect
  ✗ the word salad — talking at length around the gap.
    Everyone can hear it. It costs more than silence.
  ✗ "well, it depends" with no follow-up — depends on WHAT?
    Name the variable or do not use the phrase.
Presence 06

Being wrong in public

You will be wrong in front of people. The cost of that is almost entirely determined by what you do in the sixty seconds afterwards, and most of the damage people suffer is self-inflicted in those sixty seconds rather than caused by the error.

The correction, done properly
⭐ FLAT · EARLY · ONCE · THEN MOVE

  "I was wrong about the damage rate — it's 3.4%, not
   1.4%. It doesn't change the recommendation, but I
   wanted to correct it."

  FLAT   no wince, no laugh, no self-criticism. The tone
         says: this is information, here it is.
  EARLY  the moment you know. Delay is the expensive part —
         every minute you sit on a known error, you are
         choosing to let people act on it.
  ONCE   one acknowledgement. Not three. Repeated apology
         makes the room focus on the error instead of the
         correction, and it asks them to reassure you,
         which is a cost you are charging them.
  MOVE   say what it does and does not change, then carry
         on. The fastest way to make an error small is to
         treat it as small.

✗ WHAT DAMAGE ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE
  "Oh god, I'm so sorry, I completely messed that up, I
   should have checked, I don't know how I — sorry, let
   me start again..."
  ⇒ the error cost you nothing. THIS cost you something.
    You have converted a wrong number into a display of
    instability, and the room will remember the second one.

⭐ THE CREDIBILITY ARITHMETIC — the part that surprises people
  Someone who visibly updates is trusted MORE, not less.
  Why: it means their agreement is contingent on evidence
  rather than on position. If you never change your mind,
  your agreement is worthless — it was going to happen
  anyway.

  ⇒ SO THE STRONGEST VERSION IS PROACTIVE:
    "I've changed my mind on this since last week. I
     thought X; the October numbers say otherwise; I now
     think Y."
    Announced deliberately, this is a strength move. It
    is also rare enough to be remembered.

⚠ WHAT ACTUALLY DESTROYS CREDIBILITY
  Not being wrong. DEFENDING AFTER YOU KNOW.
  The room can tell the difference between someone who
  has not yet seen it and someone who has seen it and is
  still arguing. The second is the only unrecoverable one.
Separating the error from yourself

Notice the grammar in the good version: "the number was wrong", not "I'm an idiot". This is not a trick for the audience — it is how you stay usable to yourself for the rest of the meeting. An error attached to a fact can be corrected in one sentence. An error attached to your identity takes the rest of the hour, and you will spend that hour quiet.

Part III · The room

Floor and credit

Two situations that reliably cost people standing, and one drill set that makes the whole handbook stick.

Presence 07

Interruption, in both directions

When you are interrupted

The default response — stopping, letting them go, and never returning to your point — is by far the most common and by far the worst, because the cost is invisible. Nobody knows your point existed. Over a year this compounds into a reputation for having less to say than you have.

MoveWhat to doUse when
The continue Keep talking. Do not raise your pitch, do not speed up, do not look at them. A small volume increase, nothing else. Finish the clause. Early interruptions and habitual interrupters. Works far more often than people expect — most interrupters yield immediately to someone who simply does not stop.
The flag "Let me finish this one thought and then I want to hear that." Warm, direct, and it promises them the floor. When the interrupter is senior, or when the continue would read as a fight. The second clause is what makes it safe.
The reclaim "Coming back to what I was saying — there was a second part." Said calmly, after their point lands. Always, if you got cut off and it mattered. And note: if you announced "two things" (file 03, §05), the second one is structurally owed to you.
The yield Stop, let them have it, and genuinely drop your point. When their point is better, or the room has moved. Yielding by choice is a high-status move. Yielding by default is the problem.

When someone restates your point and gets the credit

This is common, it is often unintentional, and it is corrosive. There are two clean responses and one that makes things worse.

In the moment

"Yes — that's what I was getting at. And the part I'd add is —"

Claims it without accusing anyone. The "and" is doing the work: you are extending, which establishes ownership more convincingly than contesting would. Said warmly, it costs the other person nothing, which is why it works.

The ally system

The most effective fix is not yours to apply. Agree with one colleague to attribute each other: "That's Nadia's point from earlier — say more?" Third-party attribution is enormously more powerful than self-attribution, and it is free for both of you.

What not to do

"That's what I said." — technically true, reads as petty, and makes the room uncomfortable in a way that attaches to you rather than to them. You will win the point and lose more than it was worth.

When to let it go

If it happens once, let it go. If it is a pattern with the same person, handle it privately and directly outside the meeting — that conversation is file 05's subject.

Presence 08

Credit — claiming and giving

Under-claiming is not modesty. It is invisibility, and it is the single most common way competent people stay unpromoted. But over-claiming is worse and lasts longer. The calibration is narrower than people think, and it is mostly a grammar problem.

The pronoun rule, and the record
⭐ SAY "I" FOR WHAT YOU DID. SAY "WE" FOR WHAT THE TEAM DID.
  AND DO NOT BLUR THEM IN EITHER DIRECTION.

  ✗ UNDER-CLAIMING — "we got the returns rate down"
    when you personally found the cause and fixed it
    → your manager cannot promote a "we"

  ✗ OVER-CLAIMING — "I got the returns rate down"
    when four people did it
    → the four people are in the room, and this is the
      kind of thing that is remembered for years

  ✓ CALIBRATED — "The team got it from 6% to 3.4%. The
    part I owned was finding the packaging cause."
    → precise, generous, and completely clear about what
      you did. Both facts survive.

⭐ THE RECEIPT HABIT
  Once a week, four lines in a file: what you did, what
  changed, the number, who else was involved.
  ⇒ ten minutes a month, and at review time you have a
    record instead of a memory. Memory is systematically
    biased toward the last six weeks — yours and theirs.
  ⇒ this is the same file that feeds your answer bank
    (file 01 §11) and your canonical stories in
    supply-chain/phase-0.

GIVING CREDIT — the rules are simpler and the return is higher
  ① SPECIFIC, NOT VAGUE
     ✗ "great work everyone"          — worth nothing
     ✓ "Rim found the packaging link, which is the whole
        reason we caught this in October and not March"
     ⇒ vague praise is indistinguishable from politeness.
       Specific praise proves you were paying attention,
       which is the actual gift.
  ② PUBLIC, AND UPWARD
     credit given where their manager can hear it is worth
     several times credit given privately
  ③ CORRECT PRIVATELY, PRAISE PUBLICLY
     the inverse of what stressed people do by default
  ④ AND IT IS NOT ZERO-SUM
     the person who consistently names other people's
     contributions is read as senior, not as diminished.
     Attribution is a competence signal about YOU — it
     shows you can see who did what.
Presence 09

The drills

1 · The recording · weekly, 2 minutes ⭐

Answer one hard question to your phone. Watch it. Count the hedges, listen for rising inflection, watch where you trail off. This is the most uncomfortable drill in the library and the only one that shows you what other people actually experience. Everything else in this handbook is guesswork without it.

2 · One word, one week

Pick a single hedge — start with just — and cut only that one for a week. Do not try to cut everything at once; it makes you stilted. One word at a time, four words a month.

3 · The full stop · daily

End three sentences a day with a downward inflection, a real stop, and two seconds of silence. No "...so, yeah", no "does that make sense?". Just stop. The silence will feel enormous and last about one second in reality.

4 · "I don't know" on purpose · weekly

Use the full formula once a week — including a date — when you could have bluffed. Then hit the date. The point is the discovery that nothing bad happens, and that discovery is what makes the sentence available when you actually need it.

5 · One warmth signal per meeting

Credit someone by name, or ask about the thing they mentioned last week, or be visibly glad about someone's win. One per meeting. This moves the axis that capable people neglect, and it is the cheapest thing in this handbook.

6 · The receipt · Friday, four lines

What you did, what changed, the number, who else was involved. Ten minutes a month, and it feeds your answer bank, your review, and your CV simultaneously.

The minimum viable version

Drill 1 and drill 5. The recording is the only honest feedback loop you have, and one warmth signal per meeting corrects the specific imbalance that costs capable people the most. If you do nothing else from this handbook, those two will still move things.

You now have the delivery layer. What it does not tell you is who you are delivering to — and the same well-structured, hedge-free sentence lands differently on a manager who wants the headline than on a colleague who needs the detail, and differently again on someone who is currently worried about something you have not noticed. File 05 is reading that: the three unspoken questions everyone is asking you, managing up, stakeholder mapping, and disagreeing without damage.

On sourcing. The warmth–competence structure of social judgement (Fiske and colleagues' stereotype content model, and the broader agency/communion literature) is well replicated; the behavioural prescriptions drawn from it here are inference rather than tested intervention. Cabane's presence–power–warmth decomposition is a practitioner framework, not an experimental result — useful because it separates things worth separating. Johnstone's status work comes from theatre training and is observational. The hedging tables, the "I don't know" formula and the correction protocol are craft: field-tested, widely used, and to be judged by whether they work for you. Where research on how hedged speech is received is contested, section 04 says so rather than picking the convenient finding.
