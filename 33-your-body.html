Part I

Sleep

Chapter 01

What sleep is for

Sleep is not the absence of activity. Several things happen during it that do not happen otherwise, which is why you cannot simply do without.

Memories from the day are replayed and consolidated — moved from temporary into more durable storage, and connected to what you already knew. This is why studying then sleeping beats studying then staying up, and why a problem sometimes resolves itself overnight.

The brain also clears metabolic waste during sleep considerably more effectively than when awake. And various repair and regulatory processes run on a schedule tied to the sleep cycle rather than to the clock.

Sleep runs in cycles of roughly an hour and a half, moving through light sleep, deep sleep and the dreaming stage. Deep sleep dominates the early cycles and dreaming sleep the later ones — which has a direct practical consequence. Cutting your night short does not remove a uniform slice; it removes disproportionately the dreaming sleep at the end, which is the part most associated with emotional regulation and memory. A short night is worse than its length suggests.

Chapter 02

The things that actually help

Two systems govern when you sleep. One is a pressure that builds the longer you are awake. The other is a daily rhythm set largely by light. Most sleep problems are one of these being fought.

Light in the morning is the strongest signal for setting the rhythm, and it is under-used. Getting outside reasonably soon after waking does more for the following night than most bedtime routines.

A consistent wake time matters more than a consistent bedtime, because the wake time anchors the rhythm and the pressure system will handle the rest.

Caffeine works by blocking the molecule that signals accumulated tiredness. It does not remove the tiredness — it hides it, and it is still hiding it many hours later, because half of a dose is typically still present around five to six hours on. An afternoon coffee is affecting your night whether or not you feel it.

Alcohol makes you fall asleep faster and substantially damages the quality, particularly suppressing the dreaming sleep that the previous chapter said matters most. It is a sedative, not a sleep aid, and the difference is real.

If you cannot sleep, get up. Lying awake teaches your brain that the bed is a place for being awake and frustrated. Leave, do something dull in dim light, return when sleepy. This is the central move of the therapy that works best for insomnia, and it is more effective long-term than medication.

Part II

Food

Chapter 03

What is actually well established

Nutrition research is difficult — you cannot lock people in a room for thirty years — so most of it is observational and confounded. That is why advice changes and why people become cynical.

But a small core has survived decades of scrutiny and agreement across nearly all dietary traditions. Eat mostly plants, in a variety. Eat enough protein. Include fibre. Keep ultra-processed food limited. Do not drink your calories. That is close to the whole of it, and the arguments that fill bookshelves are about the margins.

Fibre deserves a note because it is the one most people are short of and the mechanism is interesting: you cannot digest it, so it feeds the gut bacteria from the life volume, adds bulk that slows sugar absorption, and produces fullness without calories.

The two macronutrient wars have largely resolved into the same place. Fat is not intrinsically fattening and the low-fat era was a mistake, because removing fat from food usually meant adding sugar. Equally, carbohydrate is not poison. What matters more than the ratio is the processing: whole foods in roughly their original structure behave very differently in the body from the same nutrients ground, refined and reassembled.

Chapter 04

Why processing matters more than ingredients

An apple and apple juice contain similar sugar. They do not behave similarly.

The apple's sugar is locked inside cell structures with fibre, so it is released slowly, and the chewing and bulk signal fullness. The juice arrives as a fast load with nothing to slow it and no fullness signal at all. The same molecules, arranged differently, produce a different outcome — which is the arrangement-versus-composition idea from the chemistry volume, applied to lunch.

This is also the most plausible explanation for why heavily processed food is associated with eating more. It is engineered to be easy — soft, calorie-dense, fast to eat, and combining fat, sugar and salt in proportions that rarely occur naturally. You can consume a great deal before any fullness signal catches up.

The practical version needs no calorie counting: favour food that still looks like what it came from, and treat a long ingredient list of things you would not have in your kitchen as a signal rather than a verdict.

Chapter 05

Supplements, honestly

For most people eating a varied diet, most supplements do nothing measurable, and the industry selling them is regulated far more lightly than medicines.

The genuine exceptions are specific and worth knowing. Vitamin D if you live somewhere with weak winter sun or spend little time outdoors, because it is made in skin from sunlight and food sources are limited. Folate before and during early pregnancy, where the evidence is very strong. Vitamin B12 if you eat no animal products at all, because there is no reliable plant source. And iron where a deficiency has actually been diagnosed — not speculatively, because excess iron is harmful.

Beyond those, the pattern in large trials has been consistently disappointing: nutrients that look protective in food repeatedly fail to help when extracted into a pill, and a few have done harm at high doses. The likely explanation is that the benefit came from the whole food or from whatever else characterises people who eat it.

The general rule: a supplement is worth taking when it corrects a deficiency you actually have. Otherwise you are buying expensive urine.

Part III

Movement

Chapter 06

The most reliable intervention there is

If exercise were a drug it would be the most prescribed one in existence. Its evidence base across heart disease, diabetes, several cancers, depression, anxiety, sleep and dementia risk is stronger and broader than almost any medicine.

The mechanisms are not mysterious. Muscles that contract regularly become better at pulling sugar out of the blood, which addresses the diabetes mechanism directly. The heart, being a muscle, gets stronger and does the same work with fewer beats. Loading bones signals them to maintain density. Blood flow to the brain increases and growth factors are released that support the connections from the life volume. And it reliably improves mood by several routes at once.

Two findings are worth holding onto because they contradict common assumptions.

The largest benefit is at the bottom of the scale. The step from nothing to a small amount is worth far more than the step from a lot to more. If you do nothing, almost anything is a large win.

Sitting is a separate risk from not exercising. A person who trains for an hour and sits for eleven is not in the same position as someone who moves throughout the day. Breaking up long sitting appears to matter independently.

Strength work deserves a mention because it is the one most often skipped and the one whose importance rises with age. From your thirties onward muscle is lost steadily unless it is used, and muscle is what keeps you able to get out of a chair, carry things, and survive a fall in later life.

Part IV

Medicines

Chapter 07

How to read a medicine

From the chemistry volume: a drug is a molecule shaped to fit a receptor, and side effects happen because similar receptors exist elsewhere. Several practical things follow.

The dose interval is not arbitrary. It is set by how fast your body removes the drug, to keep the amount in a useful band. Taking more sooner pushes you towards the toxic end rather than working faster.

Paracetamol is in a great many combination products — cold remedies, painkillers, flu powders — and overdose damages the liver in a way that can be delayed and irreversible. Taking a branded cold remedy alongside plain paracetamol is a genuinely common and genuinely dangerous mistake. Check the ingredients of everything you take at once.

Finish an antibiotic course as prescribed and never keep leftovers. Stopping early selects for exactly the bacteria that survived it. And they do nothing for a cold or flu, because those are viruses with no separate machinery to attack.

Grapefruit interacts with a surprising number of medicines by blocking the enzyme that clears them, which can raise the effective dose considerably. If a label mentions it, it is not being fussy.

And the general rule for anything new: tell whoever prescribes it everything else you take, including supplements and herbal products, which are pharmacologically active whatever the packaging implies.

Chapter 08

Why bad treatments feel like they work

This is the most useful chapter in the volume, because it protects you from everything not covered in the others.

Most conditions fluctuate, and most acute illnesses resolve by themselves. People tend to try a remedy when they feel worst — which is, statistically, the point from which they were most likely to improve anyway. Whatever was taken at that moment gets the credit. This single effect is enough to sustain an entire industry.

On top of it sits the placebo response, which is real and measurable, particularly for pain, nausea and anything with a large subjective component. Expecting relief genuinely produces some. That is a fact about people, not a trick, and it is exactly why a treatment must be compared against a convincing dummy rather than against nothing.

So the question to ask of any health claim is the one from the medicine volume: compared with what? Testimonials cannot answer it, because the people who did not improve do not write in. Before-and-after cannot answer it, because you have no idea what after would have looked like anyway.

Where this matters most

An ineffective remedy for a cold costs you money. An ineffective remedy taken instead of an effective one for a serious condition costs considerably more, and that is the real harm in this area — not the useless product itself but the delay and the substitution. Complementary alongside real treatment is a different proposition from alternative to it, and the distinction is worth being firm about.

Reference

What actually has the evidence

Sleep

Consolidates memory and clears waste — it is active, not idle. Cutting the night short removes mostly the dreaming sleep at the end. Morning light and a consistent wake time beat any bedtime ritual. Caffeine hides tiredness for longer than you think; alcohol is a sedative, not a sleep aid. If you cannot sleep, get up.

Food

Mostly plants, varied, enough protein, plenty of fibre, limited ultra-processed food, don't drink your calories. Processing matters more than the ingredient list — an apple and apple juice are not the same event. Supplements are worth it when they fix a deficiency you have.

Movement

The broadest evidence base of any intervention. The biggest gain is from nothing to something, sitting is a separate risk from not exercising, and strength work matters more the older you get.

The protective habit

Conditions fluctuate and illnesses resolve — so anything taken at the worst moment gets credit for the recovery that was coming anyway. Ask "compared with what?" And check what else contains paracetamol before you take two things at once.
