Aging and the brain. Slower, not emptier, and far more varied than the stereotype.

The popular picture of the ageing brain is a slow leak: neurons dying by the million, memory draining away, dementia waiting at the end. The real picture is different and more hopeful. Some abilities do decline, starting earlier than most people think. Others hold steady or improve into the sixties and beyond. The brain shrinks a little, rewires a lot, and compensates in clever ways. And people of the same age differ enormously, partly for reasons they can change. This file separates normal ageing from disease, and evidence from marketing.

Question 1 · What is it?

Normal ageing is not a mild form of dementia

Brain ageing is a set of gradual changes that happen to everyone. Dementia is a disease that happens to some. Confusing the two causes both needless fear and missed diagnoses.

Chapter 01

The sisters who donated their brains

In 1986 the epidemiologist David Snowdon began a study of elderly Catholic nuns of the School Sisters of Notre Dame in the United States. Several hundred sisters agreed to yearly tests of memory and thinking and to donate their brains after death. Nuns were ideal subjects: they had lived similar lives, with similar food, housing and health care, so differences between them were easier to interpret.

Two findings became famous. First, the brains did not always match the minds. Some sisters who had stayed sharp to the end turned out to have brains full of the protein clumps typical of Alzheimer's disease. Their brains had the damage, but their thinking had not shown it. Second, the convent kept short autobiographies the sisters had written when they joined, in their early twenties. Sisters whose youthful writing was richer in ideas were much less likely to develop Alzheimer's symptoms some sixty years later.

The Nun Study suggested two ideas that now shape the field. The amount of damage in a brain does not fully decide how well it works, and the brain's ability to cope with damage may be built over a whole lifetime.

Chapter 02

Ageing and disease: where the line is

Normal cognitive ageing means changes such as slower thinking, more trouble finding names, and finding it harder to do two things at once. Daily life continues independently. Dementia is an umbrella term for diseases, Alzheimer's being the most common, that damage the brain enough to interfere with daily life. Between the two lies mild cognitive impairment: changes that are noticeable and measurable but do not yet stop someone living independently. Some people with it go on to develop dementia; many do not.

General education

This file is not medical advice. If you or someone close to you notices changes in memory or behaviour that affect daily life, see a doctor; some causes, such as thyroid problems, vitamin deficiencies, depression or medication effects, are treatable.

Volume 42 gives the best everyday test: forgetting where you put your keys is ageing; forgetting what keys are for is a reason to get checked.

Chapter 03

What goes down, and what goes up

Psychologists distinguish two broad kinds of intelligence, an idea developed by Raymond Cattell and John Horn. Fluid intelligence is solving new problems quickly, holding information in mind and reasoning on the spot. Crystallised intelligence is accumulated knowledge, vocabulary and expertise. They age very differently.

A large online study published in 2015 by Joshua Hartshorne and Laura Germine found that different abilities peak at very different ages, some in the late teens, some in the thirties and forties, and vocabulary considerably later. There is no single age when the mind peaks.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Less wiring, slower signals, and clever compensation

The ageing brain loses fewer neurons than people think. The main changes are in connections, insulation, blood supply and chemistry, and the brain responds by reorganising.

Chapter 04

A brain that shrinks, but not the way you think

Repeated MRI scans of the same people show that the brain loses volume slowly from middle age, by a fraction of a percent a year, faster after about seventy. The loss is uneven. The prefrontal cortex, which supports planning and working memory, and the hippocampus, which forms new memories, shrink more than most areas. Areas for basic vision and movement change less.

For decades it was assumed that this meant mass death of neurons. More careful cell counts from the 1990s onward showed that in healthy ageing, most cortical neurons survive. What shrinks is mainly their branches and the connections between them, the synapses. Think of a tree that keeps its trunk but loses many small twigs. Fewer connections mean less efficient networks, which fits slower and less flexible thinking.

The white matter, the bundles of long fibres that connect distant brain areas, also changes. These fibres are wrapped in a fatty insulation called myelin, which speeds up signals. With age, myelin becomes thinner and patchier in places. Scans of older people often show small bright spots in the white matter, which usually reflect damage to tiny blood vessels. They are linked to high blood pressure, and they are one reason why what is good for the heart tends to be good for the brain.

Chapter 05

Chemistry, cleaning and inflammation

Several slower processes run alongside the structural changes. The brain's dopamine system gradually declines with age, which may contribute to slower movement and less flexible thinking, and connects to the reward and learning systems described in Decision-making. The brain's immune cells, the microglia, become more easily triggered, creating a low level of inflammation.

Cells also become worse at clearing waste. Proteins that should be broken down build up. Two of them, amyloid and tau, form the plaques and tangles found in Alzheimer's disease. A key and sometimes surprising fact is that many older people who think normally have some of these deposits. Whether and when they cause disease depends on how much there is, where it is, and how resilient the brain is, as the Nun Study suggested. The general causes of ageing, such as DNA damage and declining cell energy, are outlined in Volume 50 (Ageing, longevity).

Chapter 06

The brain recruits help

When older and younger adults do the same memory task in a scanner, a curious pattern often appears. Young adults tend to use one side of the prefrontal cortex. Many older adults use both sides. The psychologist Roberto Cabeza described this in 2002, and the older adults who performed best were often those who used both sides. One interpretation is compensation: the ageing brain recruits extra areas to make up for less efficient ones. Denise Park and Patricia Reuter-Lorenz called this building "scaffolding".

Not every extra activity is helpful. Some may reflect a brain that is less precise, with areas spreading their activity more widely. The balance is debated. But the main point stands: the ageing brain is not passive. It reorganises to keep working, which is the same capacity described in Brain plasticity.

A related question is whether adults grow new neurons. In many animals, new neurons form throughout life in the hippocampus. In humans the evidence is contested. In 2018 two respected groups published opposite conclusions in the same year, one finding new neurons almost absent after childhood, the other finding them into old age. The disagreement partly reflects differences in how brain tissue was preserved and stained, and it has not been fully settled.

Chapter 07

Cognitive reserve: the buffer built over a lifetime

The neuroscientist Yaakov Stern and others developed the idea of cognitive reserve to explain findings like the Nun Study. Reserve is the brain's ability to keep working despite damage, by using its networks more efficiently or by switching to alternative ones. It seems to be built by education, mentally demanding work, social engagement and an active life.

Reserve has a double edge. People with high reserve tend to show symptoms of dementia later. But when symptoms do appear, the underlying disease is often more advanced, so decline can then be faster. Reserve delays the visible effects of disease; it does not stop the disease. Some specific claims, for example that speaking two languages delays dementia by several years, have produced mixed results in later studies and remain debated.

Chapter 08

Superagers

Researchers at Northwestern University in Chicago, beginning with the work of Emily Rogalski, have studied people over 80 whose memory scores match those of people in their fifties or sixties. They call them superagers. Their brains show less thinning of the cortex than is typical for their age, especially in an area called the anterior cingulate cortex, which is involved in attention and motivation.

Superagers do not share one diet or one routine. Many describe strong social relationships and an active, engaged life, but the studies are small and cannot show cause and effect. Genes almost certainly play a part too. Their main lesson is that decline is common but not universal, and that the range of normal ageing is wide.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

An ageing world, and risk that can be changed

Populations are ageing almost everywhere, and a large share of dementia risk is linked to factors people and governments can do something about.

Chapter 09

The numbers behind the urgency

According to the World Health Organization, about 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases each year, and the number is expected to keep rising as populations age. The WHO also projects that by 2030 about one person in six in the world will be aged 60 or over. Dementia is one of the leading causes of disability and dependence in older people, and much of the care is given unpaid by family members, mostly women.

Because age is the strongest risk factor, an older population means more dementia even if the risk for each person stays the same. Encouragingly, several studies in high-income countries have found that the age-specific rate of dementia, the chance at any given age, has fallen in recent decades, probably because of better education and heart health. The social and health-system side of this change is in Aging populations.

Chapter 10

What protects the ageing brain

In 2024 the Lancet Commission on dementia, an international group of experts, concluded that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide are linked to fourteen risk factors that can in principle be changed. The figure is an estimate of potential prevention, not a promise for any individual, but it shifted the conversation.

Stage of lifeRisk factors named by the 2024 Lancet Commission
Early lifeLess education
MidlifeHearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, head injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, excessive alcohol
Later lifeSocial isolation, air pollution, untreated vision loss

Many of these act through blood vessels, inflammation and reserve. Physical activity has some of the best evidence; it improves blood flow and releases growth factors that support brain connections (see Vol 33 · The most reliable intervention). Hearing matters because a brain straining to decode sound has fewer resources for memory, and because hearing loss leads to isolation. A large 2023 US trial of hearing aids found no overall effect on cognitive decline over three years, but a benefit in the group at higher risk, so the question is not settled.

What about "brain training"? Practising a game makes you better at that game, and sometimes at similar tasks. Evidence that it protects against dementia is weak. In 2016 the maker of a popular brain-training app paid $2 million to settle US Federal Trade Commission charges that its advertising claims were not supported. A full, active, social life seems to do more than any app.

Chapter 11

The emotional advantage of age

One finding surprises younger people. On average, older adults report as much or more emotional well-being than younger ones, and fewer negative emotions in daily life. The psychologist Laura Carstensen explains this with socioemotional selectivity theory: as people sense that their time is limited, they focus on what is emotionally meaningful and spend less energy on unrewarding relationships and worries. In experiments, older adults often pay more attention to, and remember more of, positive information than negative, a pattern called the positivity effect.

This has practical meaning for workplaces and families. Older people are often slower but wiser in specific ways: better at managing conflict, reading social situations and using experience. Teams that combine the speed of the young with the judgement of the old tend to use both.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Losses, gains, and myths

The ageing brain trades speed for knowledge and emotional balance. Most of the frightening stories about it are exaggerated, and a few comforting ones are too.

Chapter 13

Myths and evidence

Myths

"You lose millions of brain cells a day." Healthy ageing loses mostly connections, not cells. "Dementia is a normal part of ageing." It is a disease, and many people reach very old age without it. "A supplement or puzzle will keep you sharp." Neither has strong evidence for preventing dementia.

Evidence

Exercise, controlled blood pressure, not smoking, treating hearing and vision loss, staying socially engaged and getting enough sleep are linked to better brain ageing. Much of the evidence is observational, which means it shows association more clearly than cause, but the direction is consistent.

One more balance is worth keeping. Risk-factor messages can make people with dementia feel blamed. Many people who do everything right still develop dementia, because age and genes matter most of all. The factors shift the odds; they do not decide individual outcomes.

The most dependable forecast is a social one. More people than ever will live into their eighties and nineties. How well their brains age will depend less on a breakthrough than on blood pressure clinics, hearing aids, safe streets for walking and ways to stay connected. Those are not exciting, and they are where the evidence points.

The core idea

Normal brain ageing means slower processing and weaker memory for new details, while knowledge and emotional regulation hold or improve. The brain loses connections more than cells, and it compensates by recruiting extra areas. Cognitive reserve, built over a lifetime, delays the effects of disease. Dementia is a disease, not normal ageing.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 57 million people lived with dementia worldwide in 2021 (WHO).
  • The 2024 Lancet Commission linked nearly half of cases to 14 changeable risk factors.
  • By 2030 about 1 in 6 people worldwide will be 60 or older.
The common mistake

Treating every forgotten name as early dementia, or treating real changes in daily functioning as "just age". Slower is normal; losing everyday abilities is not.

Can you explain why some of the nuns had Alzheimer's damage in their brains but no symptoms in life? If so, you understand cognitive reserve.

Gradual changes in thinking with age that do not stop independent daily life.
Mild cognitive impairment
Measurable decline beyond normal ageing that does not yet disrupt daily life.
Dementia
A group of diseases that damage thinking enough to interfere with daily life.

Solving new problems and reasoning quickly; it declines with age.
