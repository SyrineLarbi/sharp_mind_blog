Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 04 of 20

Brain plasticity. The brain you use is the brain you get.

For most of the twentieth century, students learned that the adult brain was finished. You were born with your neurons, the wiring settled in childhood, and after that the story was slow decline. That view was wrong. The brain changes physically every day, with every skill you practise and every habit you repeat. But the popular correction has overshot too. The brain is not endlessly flexible, and "rewiring" is neither quick nor free. The truth sits between the old dogma and the new hype, and it is more useful than either.

Question 1 · What is it?

The brain's ability to change its own wiring

Plasticity is not one process. It is a family of changes, from a single junction getting stronger to a whole region taking on a new job.

Chapter 01

The taxi drivers of London

To drive a black cab in London, you must pass a test called "the Knowledge". Trainees memorise the city's tangle of tens of thousands of streets and landmarks, and must plan routes in their heads without a map. It typically takes three to four years, and many people give up.

In 2000 the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire at University College London scanned the brains of qualified taxi drivers. The back part of the hippocampus, a region important for memory and navigation, was larger than in comparison volunteers. The longer someone had driven a taxi, the larger it tended to be.

A sceptic could say that people with a big hippocampus simply choose to become taxi drivers. So Maguire and Katherine Woollett followed trainees over time. In a study published in 2011, those who qualified showed growth in that region over the training years. Those who failed or quit, and a comparison group, did not. The training had changed the brain, not the other way round. There was a cost, too: the qualified drivers did slightly worse than before on some other memory tests.

Chapter 02

Five kinds of change

Neuroplasticity, or brain plasticity, means the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. "Plastic" here means shapeable, as in clay, not the material. The changes happen at very different scales.

KindWhat changesHow fastExample
Synaptic strengthHow strongly one neuron affects anotherMinutes to hoursRemembering a new name
New and lost connectionsTiny protrusions on dendrites, called spines, grow or disappearHours to daysPractising a new movement
InsulationMore myelin on frequently used fibresWeeks to monthsLearning an instrument
Map changesWhich part of the cortex handles which body part or taskWeeks to yearsA blind person's visual cortex used for reading Braille
New neuronsBirth of new cellsWeeksClear in rodents; contested in adult humans

The first two rows do most of the work. For most practical purposes, plasticity means changing connections, not growing new cells. Whether adult humans add new neurons at all is still argued about, as Neurons explains.

Chapter 03

The old dogma, and how it fell

The great Spanish anatomist Santiago Ramón y Cajal, who proved that the brain is made of separate cells, also wrote the line most often quoted for the old view.

“In adult centres the nerve paths are something fixed, ended, immutable. Everything may die, nothing may be regenerated.”

Degeneration and Regeneration of the Nervous System · Santiago Ramón y Cajal, 1913–14 (English translation 1928)

Cajal was talking about regrowth after injury, and on that he was largely right: lost brain cells mostly are not replaced. But the line was read more broadly, as if the adult brain could not change at all. Experiments from the 1960s onward took that idea apart. Rats raised in cages full of toys and companions grew a thicker cortex than rats in bare cages. Later, monkeys and then people showed that the brain's maps of the body redraw themselves in adulthood. By the 1990s the question had changed from "can the adult brain change?" to "how, how much, and how fast?"

Question 2 · How does it work?

Use strengthens, disuse prunes, and timing matters

A few simple rules explain most plasticity. The complications come from the fact that the rules change with age and depend on attention.

Chapter 04

Fire together, wire together

In 1949 the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed a rule. If one neuron repeatedly helps to make another fire, the connection between them grows stronger. The neuroscientist Carla Shatz later summarised it in a phrase now repeated everywhere: cells that fire together wire together. The reverse also holds. Connections that are rarely used weaken and may be removed.

The physical mechanism was found decades later and is the subject of the next topic, Memory formation. For now, the important point is the logic. The brain strengthens whatever it actually does, not whatever you intend to do. If you practise a tennis serve badly a thousand times, you build a strong bad serve. Repetition strengthens paths. It does not check them for quality.

A baby's brain makes connections at an astonishing rate. In the first few years of life many regions hold more synapses than they ever will again. Through childhood and the teenage years, the unused ones are cut back, a process called pruning. It is like a sculptor removing stone. The child ends up with fewer connections, but the remaining ones fit the world they actually grew up in.

Chapter 05

Critical periods: windows that close

In the 1960s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel studied how vision develops in kittens. If one eye was kept closed during a few weeks early in life, that eye was effectively blind afterwards, even though the eye itself was healthy. The brain had given its visual territory to the open eye. In adult cats, closing one eye for the same time had little lasting effect. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1981.

This kind of window is called a critical period or sensitive period: a stage when a brain system is especially open to shaping, after which change becomes much harder. It has direct medical consequences. Children with a "lazy eye", called amblyopia, are often treated by patching the good eye so the weaker one gets used. Treatment works best when started young, while the visual system is still flexible.

Language shows the same pattern. Children who learn a language early usually speak it without a foreign accent. Adults can become fluent, and often learn vocabulary and grammar faster than children at first, but most keep an accent. The windows rarely slam shut. They narrow, and change after them takes more effort.

Chapter 06

Maps that move

The cortex holds maps of the body, as Structure of the brain explained. Those maps are not fixed. In the 1980s Michael Merzenich and colleagues showed in adult monkeys that when the nerve from one finger was cut or the finger removed, the brain area that had served it was taken over by neighbouring fingers within weeks.

People show the same thing. In a 1995 study, string players had a larger brain representation for the fingers of their left hand, the hand that presses the strings, especially if they had started young. In people blind from early life, the visual cortex becomes active when they read Braille with their fingertips. Brain territory is not owned by a sense. It is lent to whatever uses it most.

Map changes also explain a strange experience. After losing an arm, many people still feel the missing limb, a phantom limb, and often feel pain in it. The neurologist V. S. Ramachandran found that touching some amputees on the face could produce sensations in the phantom hand. On the body map, the face sits next to the hand, and the face area had spread into the silent territory. His "mirror box", which lets a patient see a reflection of the healthy arm where the missing one should be, has helped some people with phantom pain, though results vary.

Chapter 07

Three months of juggling

In a study published in 2004, volunteers with no juggling experience spent three months learning a basic three-ball routine. Brain scans showed a small increase in grey matter in areas that track moving objects. After three more months without practice, the increase had partly faded.

The study was small, and scientists still debate exactly what such scan changes reflect: more connections, more support cells, changes in blood vessels, or several of these. But the pattern has been seen many times since. Adult brains change measurably within weeks of real practice, and change back when practice stops.

White matter changes too. Signals that need to arrive at the right moment depend on myelin, the insulation on nerve fibres. Animal studies show that active fibres gain more myelin. Learning a complex motor skill may partly be a matter of tuning the timing of signals by adjusting their insulation.

Chapter 08

What switches plasticity on, and off

Repetition alone is not enough. Experiments with monkeys and rats show that maps change much more when the animal pays attention and the task matters to it. Two chemical signals help open the door: acetylcholine, released when you are alert and focused, and dopamine, released when something is better than expected (see Neurotransmitters). Passive exposure changes little; focused, effortful practice with feedback changes a lot.

Helps plasticity

Attention and effort. Practice slightly beyond your current level. Feedback and reward. Sleep after learning, which helps stabilise new connections. Regular aerobic exercise, which raises growth factors in the brain in animal studies and is linked to better memory in people.

Hinders plasticity

Distraction and passive repetition. Severe or long-lasting stress, which in animal studies shrinks dendrites in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. Sleep loss. Age, which makes change slower and smaller, though it never stops it entirely.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Recovery, childhood, and the brain-training industry

Plasticity is the reason people recover after strokes, the reason early childhood matters so much, and the reason so many products claim to improve your brain.

Chapter 09

After a stroke: rewiring, not regrowing

A stroke kills neurons, and they are not replaced. Yet many survivors regain a great deal of movement and speech. That recovery comes from plasticity: surviving neurons near the damage, and sometimes in the other hemisphere, take over parts of the lost work.

Most natural recovery happens in the first few months, when the brain seems to be especially plastic. What happens in that window matters. The psychologist Edward Taub developed constraint-induced movement therapy, in which the stronger arm is restrained for much of the day so the patient must use the weaker one, intensively, for weeks. It has helped many patients. Without such pressure, people naturally use the good arm, and the weak arm's brain territory can shrink further. Taub called this "learned non-use".

Rehabilitation is plasticity on purpose: many repetitions, tasks that matter, and gradually rising difficulty. The amount of practice in typical rehabilitation sessions is often far below what animal studies suggest is needed, which is one reason researchers push for more intensive programmes.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Rehabilitation after stroke or injury should be planned with the medical team.

Chapter 10

Living well with half a brain

The most dramatic evidence for plasticity comes from an operation called hemispherectomy. For a small number of children with severe epilepsy confined to one hemisphere, surgeons remove or disconnect that whole half of the brain. Many of these children afterwards walk, talk, go to school and lead full lives. They usually have weakness on one side of the body and lose part of their field of vision, but far less is lost than you would expect.

Age is crucial. The younger the child, the better the remaining hemisphere takes over, including language when the left side is removed. In adults, the same operation would cause far more lasting loss. Childhood plasticity is enormous. Adult plasticity is real, but smaller and slower.

Chapter 11

Does brain training work?

Around 2010, apps and games promising to make users smarter or keep their brains young became a large industry. The idea seemed to follow from plasticity: train the brain like a muscle. The evidence has been disappointing.

People do get better at the games they practise. The problem is transfer: getting better at one task rarely makes you better at different tasks or at daily life. In 2014 a group of around seventy scientists signed a public statement warning that claims for brain games were not supported by evidence. In 2016 the company behind Lumosity paid two million dollars to settle charges by the US Federal Trade Commission that its advertising was deceptive.

This fits what plasticity actually is. The brain strengthens the specific circuits you use. A memory game strengthens the circuits for that game. If you want to be better at negotiating, a language or a sport, practise that, as Volume 01 argues (Chunking). Activities with the best evidence for general brain health are less exciting: physical exercise, good sleep, staying socially active, and lifelong learning of demanding real skills.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The same mechanism that teaches can also trap

Plasticity has no opinion about whether a change is good for you. It strengthens whatever is repeated.

Chapter 12

When plasticity helps, and when it harms

AreaPlasticity helpingPlasticity harming
SkillsPractice makes movements fluent and automaticBad technique, repeated, becomes just as automatic and hard to unlearn
PainLearning which movements caused injury protects youIn chronic pain, nerve pathways can become oversensitive, so pain continues after the tissue has healed
HearingThe brain sharpens sounds that matter, such as a familiar voiceAfter hearing loss, the brain's hearing areas may generate their own sound: one leading explanation of tinnitus
Fine motor controlMusicians develop extraordinary precisionIntense repetition can blur finger maps, causing focal dystonia ("musician's cramp")
RewardHabits free attention for other thingsAddiction is plasticity in reward circuits, strengthened by each use
FearOne frightening experience teaches lasting cautionIn PTSD, fear learning is too strong and too general

There is also a trade-off between flexibility and stability. A brain that changed too easily would lose old skills and memories each time it learned something new. The taxi drivers in Chapter 01 gained navigation and lost a little elsewhere. Some resistance to change is a feature: it protects what you already know. Part of the reason critical periods close is that the brain actively adds "brakes", such as protein nets around certain neurons, to lock in useful wiring.

Chapter 14

What the science honestly says to you

The word "neuroplasticity" now appears in self-help books, marketing and motivational talks, often stretched far beyond the evidence. It is worth keeping a simple test in mind. A real plasticity claim names a specific skill, needs many hours of practice, and expects gains mainly in what was practised. A doubtful one promises quick, general improvement from a small effort.

The honest message is still encouraging. You can learn demanding new skills at any age. It takes more effort as an adult, it works best with focus, feedback, sleep and spacing, and it stays specific to what you practise. The brain you have at sixty is partly the result of what you did at forty. That is the real promise of plasticity, and it is a big one.

The core idea

Plasticity is the brain's ability to change its connections with experience. Use strengthens circuits and disuse prunes them. It is strongest in childhood critical periods, continues for life, depends on attention and feedback, and is specific to what is practised. It can heal, as after stroke, or harm, as in chronic pain and addiction.

The numbers worth knowing
  • London taxi trainees take about 3–4 years to learn the city; their hippocampus changes measurably.
  • Most natural recovery after stroke happens in the first few months.
  • Three months of juggling practice produced measurable brain changes in adults (2004).
The common mistake

Believing that a brain game will make you generally sharper. Plasticity is specific: you get better at what you practise, and transfer to other tasks is usually small.

Test yourself

Why does a child recover from losing half the brain better than an adult would, and why does patching a lazy eye work better at four than at fourteen? If one idea answers both, you have understood critical periods.

The words, in plain words
Neuroplasticity
The brain's ability to change its structure and function with experience.
Hebbian learning
The rule that connections between neurons active together get stronger.
Synaptic pruning
The removal of little-used connections, especially in childhood and adolescence.
Dendritic spine
A tiny protrusion on a dendrite where a synapse forms; spines grow and vanish with learning.
Critical period
A window of development when a brain system is especially easy to shape.
Amblyopia
"Lazy eye": poor vision in one eye because the brain favoured the other during childhood.
Cortical map
An orderly layout in the cortex, such as the map of the body surface.
Phantom limb
The feeling, sometimes painful, that a lost limb is still there.
Transfer
Improvement on one task carrying over to a different task.
Constraint-induced therapy
Stroke rehabilitation that restrains the good limb to force use of the weak one.
Hemispherectomy
Surgery that removes or disconnects one half of the brain, used for severe epilepsy.
