Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 18 of 20

Artificial neural networks. Inspired by the brain, built very differently, and now teaching neuroscience back.

The software behind modern AI is called a neural network, and it is often described as "working like the brain". That phrase is about half true. The first artificial neurons, in the 1940s, were a deliberate cartoon of real ones, and some of the field's greatest ideas came from studying the visual cortex. But a real neuron is far more complicated than its artificial cousin, the brain learns in ways no current network does, and it runs on the power of a dim light bulb. This topic looks at the network from the neuroscientist's side: what was borrowed, what was dropped, and why the two fields are talking to each other again.

Question 1 · What is it?

A web of simple adding machines, loosely modelled on neurons

An artificial neural network is a large set of very simple units connected to each other, where each connection has a strength that can be adjusted. Learning means adjusting those strengths.

Chapter 01

A brain drawn as logic, 1943

In 1943 two Americans, the neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch and a young logician called Walter Pitts, published a paper with a long title and a bold idea. They asked what a neuron does if you strip away the biology. Their answer: it collects signals from other neurons, adds them up, and fires if the total crosses a threshold. They showed that networks of such idealised neurons could, in principle, compute any logical rule.

Real neurons were already known to be messier than this. McCulloch and Pitts did not claim otherwise. Their point was that thinking might be a kind of computation carried out by many simple parts, none of which thinks on its own. That idea, more than any single equation, is what artificial neural networks inherited from neuroscience.

Six years later the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb added the second ingredient. In his 1949 book The Organization of Behavior he proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them grows stronger. The slogan came later: "cells that fire together wire together". Learning, in this view, is a change in connection strengths. Every neural network since has been built on that assumption.

Chapter 02

One artificial neuron, step by step

Imagine a unit deciding whether to recommend an umbrella. It receives three inputs: how cloudy the sky is, the humidity, and the weather forecast, each as a number between 0 and 1. Each input has a weight, a number that says how much it matters. The forecast might have a weight of 3, cloudiness 2 and humidity 0.5.

The unit multiplies each input by its weight and adds the results. Then it passes the total through an activation function, a simple rule that decides the output. A common one today just says: if the total is negative, output zero; otherwise output the total. The output becomes an input for the next units in the network.

That is the whole of an artificial neuron. It is a weighted sum followed by a simple rule. It has no chemistry, no shape, no sense of time. All of a network's power comes from having very many of these units and finding good values for very many weights.

Chapter 03

Layers, depth and parameters

Units are arranged in layers. The first layer takes in raw data, such as the pixels of an image. The last layer produces the answer, such as "cat" or "dog". The layers in between are called hidden layers, because nobody tells them what to represent. They work it out during training. A network with many hidden layers is called deep, which is where the term "deep learning" comes from.

The adjustable numbers, the weights and a few related values, are called parameters. A small network might have thousands. Large language models have hundreds of billions or more. It is tempting to compare parameters to synapses, the connections between real neurons. The human brain has about 86 billion neurons and, by common estimates, on the order of a hundred trillion synapses. But the comparison is weak, for reasons the next part explains. A synapse is not a number, and a neuron is not a sum.

Question 2 · How does it work?

What was borrowed, what was simplified, and what was invented

The architecture of the most successful networks owes a real debt to the brain. Their learning method, and most of their details, do not.

Chapter 04

Learning by assigning blame

A network starts with random weights and gives random answers. Training shows it an example, compares its answer with the right one, and measures the error. Then comes the key question: which of the millions of weights were to blame, and by how much? The method that answers it is called backpropagation. It works backwards from the error, layer by layer, calculating how a small change in each weight would change the error. Each weight is then nudged in the helpful direction. Repeat this millions of times and the network improves.

Backpropagation was described by several people independently. It became widely known through a 1986 paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams. The details are in Machine learning. What matters here is that backpropagation was invented as mathematics, not copied from the brain. It may even be impossible for real neurons to do it exactly, as Chapter 07 explains.

Chapter 05

The cat, the lines, and the convolution

The clearest gift from neuroscience to AI came from two researchers and a slide projector. In the late 1950s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded from single neurons in the visual cortex of cats. For hours, nothing they showed produced much response. Then, as they slid a glass slide into the projector, its edge cast a faint line across the screen, and a neuron fired furiously.

They had found cells that respond to lines at a particular angle in one small part of the visual field. They called them simple cells. Other cells, complex cells, responded to the same angle anywhere in a slightly larger area. Their picture was a hierarchy: early cells detect edges, later ones combine edges into more complex shapes. They shared a Nobel Prize in 1981.

In 1980 the Japanese engineer Kunihiko Fukushima built a network, the Neocognitron, directly on this plan. Later in the 1980s Yann LeCun and colleagues combined the idea with backpropagation and built networks that read handwritten digits. By the late 1990s such systems were reading a significant share of cheques in the United States. These are convolutional networks: the same small pattern detector is slid across the whole image, as if one simple cell were copied to every position. Most of the computer vision of the 2010s was a mathematical version of Hubel and Wiesel's cat.

The dendrites are the biggest surprise. They are not passive wires. Their branches can generate their own small electrical events and combine inputs in non-linear ways before anything reaches the cell body. In 2021 a team in Israel trained an artificial network to copy the input-output behaviour of a single detailed model of a cortical neuron. It needed a network several layers deep to do it. One real neuron may be closer to a small network than to a single unit. The biology of the real thing is in Neurons.

Chapter 07

The learning-rule problem

Backpropagation needs two things the brain does not obviously have. First, it needs an exact error signal sent backwards to every connection, carried by the same weights used going forwards. Real synapses transmit in one direction only. Second, it needs a clean separation between a "forward" pass that computes an answer and a "backward" pass that learns. The brain does both at once, all the time.

The brain's known learning rules are mostly local. A synapse changes according to what the two neurons it connects are doing, plus broad signals such as a burst of dopamine that says "that went better than expected". Hebb's rule is local in this sense. Whether local rules can add up to something as powerful as backpropagation is one of the central open questions in theoretical neuroscience. Several proposals exist, and some researchers, including Hinton himself, have explored learning methods that avoid a backward pass. None is yet accepted as the brain's answer.

There is a deeper gap in efficiency. A child can learn a new animal from a picture book with one or two examples. A typical network needs many thousands of labelled images. Large language models are trained on far more text than a person could read in many lifetimes. Brains learn from little data. Networks make up for it with enormous amounts.

Chapter 08

Twenty watts, and the spiking alternative

The human brain runs on roughly 20 watts, less than a laptop charger. Training a large AI model uses the electricity of a small town for weeks, and running it for millions of users needs whole data centres. Volume 14 explains why (Data centres, and why AI became an electricity story).

Part of the brain's efficiency comes from spikes. Most neurons are silent most of the time and fire only when they have something to say. A typical artificial network, by contrast, computes every unit for every input. Spiking neural networks try to copy the brain's approach, and neuromorphic chips, such as IBM's TrueNorth and Intel's Loihi, are built to run them, with memory and computation mixed together as they are in neurons. They can be extremely energy-efficient for some tasks. So far they have not matched conventional networks on the big problems, partly because training spiking networks is harder.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A borrowed idea that changed the world, and returned home

Neural networks are now the core of most AI. They are also becoming one of neuroscience's best tools for testing theories of the brain.

Chapter 09

Seventy years of hope, disappointment and return

In 1958 the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt demonstrated the perceptron, a machine with a single layer of adjustable weights that learned to tell simple shapes apart. Newspaper coverage was ecstatic and predicted machines that would soon walk, talk and be conscious. In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book, Perceptrons, proving that single-layer networks could not learn some simple patterns. Funding and interest fell away for years. Historians still argue about how much of the blame belongs to the book.

The ideas returned in the 1980s with backpropagation and multi-layer networks, then faded again in the 1990s when other methods worked better. The lasting breakthrough came in 2012, when a deep network won a major image-recognition competition by a wide margin, helped by large data sets and graphics chips. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational work on neural networks. Hopfield had designed a network in 1982 that stored memories as stable patterns of activity, an idea taken directly from thinking about the brain.

The lesson of this history is that the brain-inspired idea was right long before it was useful. It needed fifty years of faster computers and larger data sets to show what it could do.

Chapter 10

Networks as theories of the brain

In 2014 a team led by James DiCarlo and Daniel Yamins at MIT trained networks to recognise objects in photographs. They then compared the networks' internal activity with recordings from the visual cortex of monkeys looking at the same pictures. The deeper layers of the best networks predicted the responses of real neurons in the monkey's higher visual areas better than any model scientists had designed by hand. Nobody had trained the network to imitate the brain. It had been trained only to recognise objects.

This has become a major research strategy. Train a network to do what a brain area does, then ask whether the solution it finds resembles the brain's. Similar comparisons have been made for hearing and for language, where the internal activity of language models partly predicts brain responses to the same sentences. The interpretation is debated. Matching activity does not prove the mechanism is the same, just as two clocks can show the same time with different gears.

Chapter 11

The dopamine surprise

Influence also runs from AI to neuroscience. In the 1980s the computer scientist Richard Sutton developed a learning method called temporal-difference learning. A system learns by comparing what it predicted with what actually happened, and updating on the difference, called the prediction error.

In the 1990s the neuroscientist Wolfram Schultz was recording dopamine neurons in monkeys. He found that they fired when a reward was better than expected, stayed quiet when it was exactly as expected, and dipped below normal when an expected reward failed to arrive. In 1997 he, Peter Dayan and Read Montague showed that this was almost exactly the prediction-error signal of Sutton's algorithm. An idea invented for machines turned out to describe a chemical signal in the brain. The story continues in Decision-making.

The word "neural" in AI also shapes public debate. Saying a system "has neurons" makes it sound like a mind. Saying it "multiplies large tables of numbers" makes it sound like a spreadsheet. Both are true, and neither settles questions about understanding or consciousness, which are discussed in Consciousness.

Chapter 13

Two famous failures, and what they reveal

In 2013 researchers showed that adding a carefully chosen pattern of noise to a photo, invisible to a human, could make a good image network confidently call a school bus an ostrich. These adversarial examples reveal that networks can rely on fine texture and statistical cues that people ignore. Human vision is also fooled, by optical illusions, but in different ways. The difference suggests the network has not found the same solution as the visual cortex, even when it gets the same answers.

The second failure is catastrophic forgetting. Train a network on one task, then on a second, and it often loses the first. The brain largely avoids this. One leading theory is that the hippocampus learns new things quickly and then, during sleep, replays them to the cortex gradually, mixed with older memories, so that new learning does not trample the old. Some AI methods now use a similar trick of replaying stored examples. Here the brain is still the better engineer, and its solution is being copied again. The biology is in Memory formation.

Chapter 15

Birds, aeroplanes, and the right lesson

A common analogy compares AI and the brain to aeroplanes and birds. Early flight pioneers studied birds closely. But aeroplanes do not flap their wings. Once engineers understood the principle, lift from a moving wing, they built it in a way that suited metal and engines. In the same way, neural networks took the principle of many simple units learning by changing connections, then built it in a way that suited digital chips.

The analogy has a limit. Engineers fully understand aerodynamics. Nobody yet fully understands how the brain computes, so there may still be principles left to borrow, as the replay idea suggests. The most likely future is not convergence, but continuing exchange: AI borrowing tricks from biology where they help, and neuroscience using AI as a microscope and a testing ground. The bigger questions about general intelligence are taken up in Future · Artificial General Intelligence.

The core idea

Artificial neural networks borrowed two ideas from the brain: computation by many simple connected units, and learning by changing connection strengths. Convolutional networks also borrowed the layered design of the visual cortex. Almost everything else, including backpropagation, is engineering, and a real neuron is far richer than an artificial one.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 86 billion neurons and, by common estimates, around 100 trillion synapses in a human brain.
  • The brain runs on about 20 watts.
  • Key dates: 1943 McCulloch–Pitts neuron, 1958 perceptron, 1986 backpropagation paper, 2012 deep learning breakthrough.
The common mistake

Treating "neural network" as proof that AI works like a brain, or as proof that it is nothing like one. It shares principles, not mechanisms.

Test yourself

Can you explain why backpropagation is hard for real neurons to do? If you can, you understand the difference between a local learning rule and a global one.

The words, in plain words
Artificial neuron
A unit that multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them, and applies a simple rule to produce an output.
Weight
A number for the strength of one connection, adjusted during learning. Loosely like a synapse's strength.
Activation function
The simple rule that turns a unit's total input into its output.
Hidden layer
A layer of units between input and output whose role the network works out for itself.
Parameter
Any adjustable number in a network, mostly weights.
Backpropagation
The method of working out how much each weight contributed to an error, by working backwards from the output.
Convolutional network
A network that slides the same small pattern detector across an image, inspired by the visual cortex.
Hebbian learning
Strengthening a connection when the two neurons it joins are active together.
Local learning rule
A rule where a connection changes using only information available at that connection.
Dendrite
The branching input part of a real neuron, which processes signals before they reach the cell body.
Spiking neural network
An artificial network whose units communicate with timed pulses, like real neurons.
Neuromorphic chip
Computer hardware designed to run brain-like, spiking computations efficiently.
Prediction error
The difference between what was expected and what happened, used as a learning signal by machines and by dopamine neurons.
