Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 07 of 20

Sleep and the brain. A third of your life spent offline, and the brain is busy the whole time.

Every night you lie still for hours, nearly blind and deaf to the world, unable to defend yourself. For an animal, that is an enormous risk. Yet every animal with a nervous system that scientists have studied closely sleeps in some form, from fruit flies to whales. Evolution would not keep something so dangerous unless it did essential work. Over the last seventy years, scientists have found much of that work. Sleep is not the brain switching off. It is the brain switching into several different modes, each with its own jobs, controlled by a precise switch and a clock.

Question 1 · What is it?

Not one state, but a cycle of very different ones

Sleep looks the same from the outside all night. Inside the brain, it changes every few minutes, through stages as different from each other as each is from waking.

Chapter 01

Eleven days awake

In the winter of 1963–64, a seventeen-year-old high school student in San Diego called Randy Gardner stayed awake for about 264 hours, eleven days, for a science fair project. Researchers came to observe. As the days passed, he became irritable, had trouble focusing and remembering, slurred his speech, and at one point imagined he was a famous football player. He then slept for about fourteen hours and appeared to recover without lasting harm.

The story is often told as proof that people can survive without sleep. The better lesson is the opposite. Even a healthy teenager, highly motivated and closely watched, fell apart mentally within days. Record attempts like his are no longer accepted by record-keepers because of the risks. In animal experiments, total sleep deprivation kept up for weeks has proved fatal.

Sleep is also universal. Fruit flies have periods of stillness when they are harder to wake, and they "catch up" after being kept awake. Dolphins sleep with one half of the brain at a time, so the other half can keep them swimming and surfacing to breathe. When even animals that must never stop moving find a way to sleep, sleep must be doing something that cannot be done awake.

Chapter 02

The stages of sleep

In 1924 the German psychiatrist Hans Berger first recorded the electrical rhythms of the human brain through the scalp, the technique called EEG (electroencephalography). It showed that the sleeping brain produces distinct wave patterns. Then in 1953 Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman in Chicago noticed periods when sleepers' eyes darted rapidly under their lids and their brain waves looked almost awake. People woken in these periods usually reported vivid dreams. They had found REM sleep, for "rapid eye movement".

Stages N1 to N3 are together called non-REM sleep. The "N" stands for non-REM. The slow waves of N3 are produced by millions of cortical neurons switching between active and silent together, about once a second. Deep sleep is not a quiet brain. It is a brain moving in unison.

Chapter 03

The shape of a night

The stages do not come in random order. You go down through N1 and N2 into deep sleep, come back up, and have a short period of REM. That is one cycle, lasting roughly 90 minutes. A full night has about four to six cycles. Their content changes as the night goes on.

Deep sleep dominates the first half of the night. REM grows longer towards morning. That is why, as Volume 33 notes, cutting a night short removes a disproportionate amount of REM. It is also why you are most likely to wake from a dream in the early morning.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A switch, a pressure gauge, a clock, and a night shift

Sleep is controlled by a few deep brain systems that work together. Understanding them explains insomnia, jet lag, narcolepsy and why teenagers cannot get up.

Chapter 04

The flip-flop switch

Waking is kept up by clusters of neurons in the brainstem and hypothalamus that release alerting chemicals such as noradrenaline, histamine, serotonin and acetylcholine across the brain. That is why older antihistamines, which block histamine, make you drowsy.

Sleep is turned on by a small group of neurons in the front of the hypothalamus. When they fire, they release GABA, the main "stop" signal, onto the waking centres and quiet them. The waking centres, in turn, can silence the sleep neurons. Each side holds the other down. In 2001 the American neurologist Clifford Saper and colleagues compared this to an electrical flip-flop switch: it is built to be firmly in one state or the other, not halfway. That is why you fall asleep fairly suddenly instead of fading slowly.

A third ingredient keeps the switch steady. In 1998 two research groups independently discovered a chemical messenger made by a few thousand neurons in the hypothalamus. One group called it orexin, the other hypocretin. Orexin holds the switch in the "awake" position. In 1999 researchers found that dogs with inherited narcolepsy had a faulty orexin receptor gene, and soon after it was shown that most people with the classic form of narcolepsy, the kind with sudden muscle weakness, have lost most of their orexin neurons. Without the stabiliser, the switch flickers. People fall asleep suddenly in the day and may collapse when they laugh, because bits of REM paralysis break into waking.

Chapter 05

Sleep pressure and the master clock

Two forces push the switch, an idea set out by the Swiss researcher Alexander Borbély in 1982. The first is sleep pressure. The longer you are awake, the more you need to sleep. Part of the signal is a chemical called adenosine, which builds up in the brain as neurons use energy. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine's receptors, as Neurotransmitters explains. Sleep clears the pressure, and deep sleep is most intense after long periods awake.

The second force is the circadian clock, from the Latin for "about a day". It sits in a pair of tiny clusters in the hypothalamus, about 20,000 neurons in total, just above where the optic nerves cross: the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Its neurons keep a roughly 24-hour rhythm even when removed and kept in a dish. The 2017 Nobel Prize went to Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young for discovering, in fruit flies, the genes that make this clock tick.

The clock is set each day by light. Special light-sensitive cells in the retina, not the ones used for seeing, report brightness directly to it. Morning light pulls the clock earlier. Bright light late in the evening pushes it later. As darkness falls, the clock signals the pineal gland to release melatonin, a hormone that tells the body it is night. Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sleeping pill, which is why it helps more with jet lag than with ordinary insomnia.

Why teenagers sleep late

During adolescence the circadian clock shifts later, and sleep pressure builds more slowly. A fifteen-year-old's brain often is not ready for sleep until late at night, so an early school start cuts into needed sleep. This is biology, not laziness, and it is why several school systems have experimented with later start times.

Chapter 06

REM: a waking brain in a paralysed body

During REM sleep, much of the cortex is as active as when you are awake, and the emotional regions, including the amygdala, are especially busy. At the same time, circuits in the brainstem block the signals to almost all your muscles, except those that move the eyes and run breathing. You are paralysed so that you do not act out your dreams.

When that block fails, people do. In REM sleep behaviour disorder, sleepers shout, punch and kick, sometimes injuring a bed partner, while dreaming of being attacked. It mostly affects older men. It has an important link discovered over long follow-up studies: many people with it go on, often years or decades later, to develop Parkinson's disease or a related condition. It is now one of the earliest known warning signs of those diseases.

The opposite failure is sleep paralysis. The mind wakes up while the REM paralysis is still on. For a few seconds or minutes, the person is awake but cannot move, sometimes with a frightening sense of a presence in the room. It is common, harmless, and probably behind many traditional stories of night-time visitors.

Chapter 07

Why we dream: the live debate

Dreams happen mostly in REM, though people woken from non-REM sleep also report dream-like thoughts. Nobody knows for certain what dreams are for, or whether they are for anything. The main views are set out here because this is genuinely unsettled.

A by-product

In 1977 Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley proposed that dreams are the cortex trying to make sense of random activity coming up from the brainstem during REM. The story is invented afterwards. Hobson himself later revised the idea.

Emotional processing

REM is rich in emotional activity and low in noradrenaline, a stress chemical. Some researchers argue that it helps take the sharp edge off painful memories while keeping their content.

Memory and creativity

Dreams often recombine fragments of recent and old memories in odd ways. One idea is that this helps connect new learning with older knowledge and find distant links.

Rehearsal

Dreams are often about threats and social conflict. A theory proposed by Antti Revonsuo holds that dreaming evolved to rehearse responses to danger in a safe setting.

Dreams can also be studied directly. In lucid dreams, the dreamer knows they are dreaming. In a 2021 study, research teams in several countries asked lucid dreamers simple maths questions while they were in REM sleep. Some dreamers answered correctly with agreed eye movements, while still asleep.

Chapter 08

The night shift: memory and clean-up

One job of sleep is now well established. During deep sleep, the hippocampus replays the day's experiences, in step with the slow waves and spindles of the cortex, and helps move them into longer storage. That mechanism is explained in Memory formation.

A second idea is more recent and more debated. In 2013 Maiken Nedergaard's laboratory reported in mice that the spaces between brain cells widened by about 60% during sleep, and that fluid flowing through the brain cleared waste, including the amyloid protein linked to Alzheimer's, much faster. They called this the glymphatic system. In 2019 a study in people showed slow waves of fluid washing through the brain in rhythm with deep-sleep brain waves.

But in 2024 another laboratory, using a different method in mice, reported that clearance of a dye from the brain was actually lower during sleep. The disagreement is not settled. That sleep matters for brain health is well supported; exactly how it clears waste is still being argued. Researchers do agree that sleep and Alzheimer's appear to affect each other: poor sleep is linked to more amyloid, and the disease itself damages sleep.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Safety, health and the many people sleeping badly

Sleep loss is so common that most people have forgotten what being fully rested feels like. Its effects are larger than they feel.

Chapter 09

Tired brains do not know how tired they are

In a 2003 study led by Hans Van Dongen, volunteers slept six hours a night for two weeks. By the end, their attention lapses were as bad as those of people kept awake for one to two nights in a row. Yet the six-hour group rated themselves only slightly sleepy. The brain adapts to feeling tired much faster than it adapts to being tired.

Being awake for long stretches also slows thinking in measurable ways. A well-known Australian study in 2000 found that after about 17 to 19 hours without sleep, people performed some tasks about as badly as people with a blood alcohol level of 0.05%, the legal driving limit in many countries. Exhausted brains also have microsleeps: lapses of a few seconds when the brain briefly falls asleep, often without the person noticing. At motorway speed, a few seconds is enough to cross a lane.

Chapter 10

Long-term costs

Large population studies link regular short sleep to higher risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease and depression. Some of this is cause and effect in both directions, since illness also disturbs sleep. But experiments show direct effects. A few nights of restricted sleep reduce the body's ability to handle sugar and raise appetite hormones. People who sleep poorly in the nights around a vaccination tend to produce fewer antibodies.

Working against the clock carries its own risk. In 2007 the World Health Organization's cancer agency classified night shift work as "probably carcinogenic to humans", a classification it confirmed in 2019, based mainly on animal evidence and limited human data. Sleeping at the wrong time of day is not the same as sleeping enough. The clock times hormones, digestion and repair processes throughout the body, and shift work puts them out of step.

Sleep apnoea deserves a special mention because it is so common and so often missed. A 2019 estimate put the number of adults affected worldwide at close to one billion. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing seen by a partner, and daytime sleepiness despite a full night in bed are the classic signs. Untreated, it raises the risk of high blood pressure, stroke and road accidents.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Long-lasting insomnia, loud snoring with breathing pauses, or falling asleep during the day are worth discussing with a doctor.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

An expensive habit that pays for itself

Sleep costs a third of life and leaves the body defenceless. Its benefits must outweigh those costs, and the trade-offs show up in the details.

Sleeping pills show the same trade-off in miniature. Older sedatives, such as benzodiazepines, press the brain's GABA brake broadly. They produce something that looks like sleep but changes its structure, and they bring tolerance and next-day grogginess. A sedated brain is not the same as a sleeping brain. Newer drugs work differently, by blocking orexin's "stay awake" signal, and appear to disturb sleep structure less, though they are not free of side effects.

Targeted drugs, sleep as early warning, and the dream of needing less

Chapter 14

The quiet direction: taking sleep seriously

The biggest change may be cultural, not technological. For much of the twentieth century, sleeping little was treated as a sign of toughness or ambition. The science has made that hard to defend. Medical training rules in several countries now limit doctors' shifts. Road safety agencies treat drowsy driving as a serious cause of crashes. Some schools start later for teenagers.

Most adults need about seven to nine hours, according to major sleep medicine bodies, and teenagers need more. A small minority genuinely need less, but far fewer people are true short sleepers than believe they are. The most reliable brain enhancer known is a full night of sleep at a regular time, and it is free.

The core idea

Sleep cycles roughly every 90 minutes through light, deep (slow-wave) and REM sleep. A flip-flop switch in the hypothalamus, stabilised by orexin, decides sleep or wake. It is pushed by sleep pressure (partly adenosine) and timed by a circadian clock set by light. Sleep consolidates memory, regulates emotion and supports brain health.

The numbers worth knowing
  • One sleep cycle lasts about 90 minutes; a night has 4–6.
  • After about 17–19 hours awake, some performance resembles a blood alcohol level of 0.05%.
  • Most adults need about 7–9 hours.
The common mistake

Believing you have adapted to short sleep because you no longer feel very tired. Feeling adapts; performance does not.

Test yourself

Why does a flight from Paris to New York leave you awake at 4 a.m. local time, even though you are exhausted? If you can explain it using sleep pressure and the circadian clock, you understand the two-process model.
