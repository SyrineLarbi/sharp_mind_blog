Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 09 of 20

Decision-making. Every choice is a value your cells compute, then correct by surprise.

From the inside, a decision feels like a single moment: you weigh things up and choose. From the outside, a brain is doing something more mechanical and more interesting. It gives each option a value, gathers evidence until one option crosses a line, and then compares what happened with what it expected. That last step, the surprise, is how it learns to choose better next time. This file follows the choice through the brain, from a railway accident in 1848 to the dopamine cells that inspired modern artificial intelligence.

Question 1 · What is it?

Choosing is computing value

For a neuroscientist, a decision is the moment the brain commits to one action out of several, based on what it expects each one to bring.

Chapter 01

The iron rod and the changed man

On 13 September 1848, near the town of Cavendish in Vermont, a railway construction foreman called Phineas Gage was packing explosive powder into a hole in the rock with an iron rod about a metre long. A spark set off the charge. The rod shot up through his left cheek, passed behind his eye, and left through the top of his skull. It landed many metres away. Gage was conscious within minutes and talked to the doctor who treated him, John Harlow.

He survived, but Harlow later reported that his character had changed. The efficient, well-liked foreman became impatient, crude and unable to stick to plans. His friends said he was "no longer Gage". The damage was mainly to the front of the brain, the prefrontal cortex, especially its lower middle part.

The story needs one honest correction. Historians, notably Malcolm Macmillan, found that later textbooks exaggerated the change. Gage went on to work for years, including as a stagecoach driver in Chile, a job that required planning and self-control, and he may have recovered a good deal before he died in 1860. Even so, Gage was the first famous clue that judgement and planning depend on a particular part of the brain, and that you can lose them while keeping memory, language and intelligence.

Chapter 02

Two kinds of decision

Researchers split decisions into two broad families, because they are studied differently.

Perceptual decisions

What is out there? Is that shape a face or a vase? Is the traffic light still amber? There is a right answer, and the brain must decide from noisy evidence. These are studied with simple visual tasks in monkeys and people.

Value-based decisions

What do I want? Coffee or tea, save or spend, this job or that one. There is no right answer, only a personal value. These are studied with choices between foods, money and risky gambles.

Both families share a problem. The options are often completely different things. To compare a holiday with a new car, the brain must convert them into a common currency, a single scale of "how good is this for me, now". Much of decision neuroscience is the search for where that scale is computed and how it is updated.

No single region "makes the decision". A choice emerges when these areas settle on one action, a little like a committee where some members bring information, some bring feelings, and one keeps an eye on the long-term plan.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Value, evidence, commitment, and the lesson afterwards

Four mechanisms are now well understood: a teaching signal, evidence that builds up, a value scale, and a system of brakes.

Chapter 04

Dopamine as the surprise signal

In the late 1980s and 1990s, the neuroscientist Wolfram Schultz recorded single dopamine neurons in monkeys while they received drops of fruit juice. At first, the cells fired a burst when juice arrived unexpectedly. Then a light was switched on a second or so before each drop. After some training, the cells stopped firing at the juice and fired at the light instead. And when the light came on but no juice followed, the cells went quiet, below their normal rate, at exactly the moment the juice should have arrived.

This is called a reward prediction error. Dopamine does not simply mean "pleasure". It means "things went better or worse than I predicted", and it tells the striatum and cortex to adjust their expectations. In 1996, Read Montague, Peter Dayan and Terrence Sejnowski pointed out that the signal closely matched a learning rule already used in computer science, temporal-difference learning, from the field of reinforcement learning. That match is one of the great meeting points of biology and AI. Programs that learned to play games at superhuman level use the same family of ideas.

It also explains an everyday puzzle. A pay rise you expected gives little lasting joy, while a small surprise gift can feel wonderful. The brain responds less to what you get than to the gap between it and your forecast.

Chapter 05

Evidence builds up until it crosses a line

For perceptual decisions, the clearest evidence came from experiments by Michael Shadlen, William Newsome and colleagues. Monkeys watched a screen of moving dots. Some dots moved together in one direction and the rest moved randomly. The monkey had to decide, left or right, and move its eyes to answer. Cells in a region of the parietal cortex, towards the back of the brain, slowly increased their firing as evidence for one direction piled up. When the firing reached a fixed level, the monkey moved its eyes.

Scientists describe this with a drift-diffusion model: imagine a ball rolling along a bumpy path between two walls. Evidence tilts the path one way, noise shakes the ball, and the decision is made when the ball hits a wall. The model explains a trade-off everyone knows. Set the walls far apart and you decide slowly but accurately. Set them close and you decide fast and make more mistakes. Rushed decisions are not made by a different system. They are the same system with a lower threshold.

Chapter 06

A value scale in the front of the brain

In 2006 Camillo Padoa-Schioppa and John Assad recorded from the orbitofrontal cortex of monkeys choosing between different amounts of different juices. Some neurons fired in proportion to the value of the offered option, in the monkey's own terms, whatever the juice. If a monkey valued one drop of juice A as much as three drops of juice B, the cells fired similarly for both offers. That is a common currency in physical form.

Human brain-imaging studies point the same way. Activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex tends to track how much a person values something: food, money, a piece of music, even a charitable donation. Value is also relative and changeable. The same snack is worth more when you are hungry, and a sum of money is worth less if you must wait a year for it. This preference for sooner rewards is called delay discounting, and people differ in how steep it is.

Chapter 07

Gut feelings: the somatic marker idea

In the 1980s and 1990s Antonio Damasio studied patients whose ventromedial prefrontal cortex had been damaged, like Gage's. One, whom he called Elliot, had had a brain tumour removed. His intelligence tests were normal, yet his life fell apart through poor choices in business and relationships. Asked to choose between two dates for his next appointment, he could list the advantages of each for a very long time without settling.

Damasio proposed the somatic marker hypothesis. Through experience, options become tagged with bodily feelings ("somatic" means of the body), such as a slight tension at the thought of a risky option. These markers quickly narrow the choices before slow reasoning begins. Without them, reasoning has no sense of what matters.

The main test was the Iowa Gambling Task, designed by Antoine Bechara with Damasio. Players draw cards from four decks. Two decks pay big rewards but bigger losses; two pay small rewards and smaller losses and win in the long run. Healthy players drift towards the safe decks. In the original studies, their skin began to sweat slightly before reaching for a bad deck, even before they could say why. Patients with ventromedial damage kept choosing the risky decks. Later researchers argued that healthy players knew more about the decks than first thought, so the claim that the body knows before the mind is contested. The broader point, that emotional value signals are needed for good choices, has held up.

Chapter 08

Brakes, habits and self-control

Choosing well often means overriding a tempting option. In a 2009 imaging study by Todd Hare, Colin Camerer and Antonio Rangel, dieters chose between foods. When they turned down tasty but unhealthy options, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was more active and seemed to change the value signal in the ventromedial area, giving health more weight. Self-control looks less like a muscle fighting temptation and more like adjusting the value of the options.

The brain also has two ways of choosing. A goal-directed system thinks about outcomes: "I want coffee, the kitchen has coffee, so go to the kitchen." A habit system simply repeats what worked before in that situation, with little thought. Habits depend heavily on the striatum and are fast and cheap, but slow to update. That is why you can drive to your old office on a day off. Volume 35 covers how habits form (How habits actually form).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Addiction, free will and the law

When choice has a mechanism, the mechanism can break, be exploited, or be put on trial.

Chapter 09

When the surprise signal is hijacked

Drugs such as cocaine and amphetamine raise dopamine directly, sending a "better than expected" signal every time, whatever actually happened. The learning system is fooled into rating the drug and everything linked to it as extremely valuable. Volume 37 explains why this is better understood as a learning problem than a moral one (Addiction, without the moralising).

A striking natural experiment comes from Parkinson's disease, in which dopamine cells die. Some patients take drugs that mimic dopamine. A minority of them develop compulsive gambling, shopping or eating, problems that usually fade when the dose is reduced. Medical leaflets for these drugs now warn about it. Gambling machines exploit the same system from outside: unpredictable rewards and "near misses" keep the prediction-error signal busy.

Chapter 10

Does your brain decide before you do?

In 1983 the physiologist Benjamin Libet asked people to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it and to note, using a fast clock, when they first felt the urge. Electrodes on the scalp showed a slow build-up of activity, the readiness potential, starting several hundred milliseconds before people reported deciding. Headlines concluded that free will is an illusion.

That reading is now widely doubted. In 2012 Aaron Schurger and colleagues argued that the build-up may be the brain's background noise drifting towards a threshold, as in Chapter 05, rather than a hidden decision already made. Later imaging studies could predict simple choices several seconds ahead, but only a little better than chance. The experiments show that decisions have a lead-up in the brain, not that "you" are a spectator. What free will means is a question for philosophy as much as for neuroscience.

Chapter 11

Teenagers, courts and responsibility

The prefrontal cortex keeps developing into the twenties, while reward circuits are very responsive in adolescence. Laboratory studies find that teenagers take more risks especially when friends are watching. This research entered American law. In Roper v. Simmons (2005) the US Supreme Court banned the death penalty for crimes committed under 18, drawing on research about adolescent development, and in Graham v. Florida (2010) it referred to developments in psychology and brain science when limiting life sentences without parole for juveniles.

Courts in several countries now hear brain evidence in criminal cases, such as scans showing frontal damage. Most legal scholars urge caution. A group-level brain difference cannot tell a judge what one person was able to do on one night, and plenty of people with similar scans never break the law.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A learning machine with exploitable habits

The brain's way of choosing is fast and adaptable, and its weaknesses are the reverse side of the same features.

Chapter 13

What the science gets right, and where it overreaches

Strong ground

Reward prediction error is one of the most solid findings in neuroscience, seen in monkeys, rodents and human imaging, and supported by computer models. Evidence accumulation to a threshold is also well supported for simple perceptual choices.

Weaker ground

Much of the rest comes from small imaging studies and from a handful of patients. Real decisions, such as choosing a career, take months and involve other people, which no scanner captures. "Neuromarketing" claims to read buyers' minds have mostly promised more than they delivered.

A fair summary is that neuroscience explains the building blocks of choice better than it explains any particular choice. It can say why you value surprise or why you rush under pressure. It cannot yet say why you married the person you married.

One caution runs across all three. As devices and apps learn to predict our choices from behaviour and, possibly, brain data, the same knowledge that helps treat compulsions can be used to create them. Several countries, starting with Chile in 2021, have begun writing protections for mental privacy into law. That debate is likely to grow.

The core idea

The brain decides by valuing options in a common currency (orbitofrontal and ventromedial cortex), accumulating evidence until a threshold is crossed, and learning from surprise: dopamine signals the gap between what happened and what was expected. Emotion and self-control adjust the values; habits bypass them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Phineas Gage's accident: 1848.
  • Dopamine signal = outcome minus expectation (Schultz, 1990s).
  • Libet's readiness potential starts several hundred milliseconds before the reported urge.
The common mistake

Calling dopamine "the pleasure chemical". It is a teaching signal about surprise, which is why expected rewards feel flat and unpredictable ones are so compelling.

The words, in plain words
Prefrontal cortex
The front part of the brain, involved in planning, valuing and self-control.
Orbitofrontal cortex
The part just above the eye sockets that represents how much options are worth.
Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex
The upper outer front, which holds goals in mind and supports self-control.
Striatum
A deep structure that selects actions and stores habits; a main target of dopamine.
Dopamine
A chemical messenger whose bursts and dips signal outcomes better or worse than expected.
Reward prediction error
The difference between the reward received and the reward expected.
Reinforcement learning
Learning by trial, error and reward, in brains and in computer programs.
Drift-diffusion model
A model in which noisy evidence builds up until it reaches a decision threshold.
Common currency
A single internal scale on which very different options can be compared.
Delay discounting
Valuing a reward less the longer you must wait for it.
Somatic marker
Damasio's term for a bodily feeling attached to an option that guides choice.
Readiness potential
A slow build-up of brain activity before a voluntary movement.
