Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 11 of 20

Motor control. The hardest thing your brain does is the thing you never notice.

Picking up a cup of coffee feels like nothing. In fact your brain must work out where the cup is, plan a path for an arm with dozens of muscles, judge the weight before you touch it, correct the movement while it happens, and do all of it with nerve signals that arrive late. Computers beat the world chess champion in 1997. In 2026 robots still struggle to fold laundry reliably in an ordinary home. That gap tells you how much hidden work goes into ordinary movement, and this file explains how the brain does it.

Question 1 · What is it?

The reason brains exist

Motor control is how the nervous system turns intentions into coordinated muscle action. Some scientists argue it is the main thing brains are for.

Chapter 01

The animal that gives up most of its brain

The young sea squirt is a small tadpole-like animal that swims through the sea with a simple nervous system. When it finds a good rock, it glues itself down and never moves again. Then it absorbs much of that nervous system as food. The neuroscientist Daniel Wolpert likes to tell this story to make a point: an animal that no longer needs to move no longer needs much of a brain. Plants, which do not move about, have none.

Seen this way, perception, memory and emotion are all in the service of one thing: choosing and carrying out the right movement. Every way you affect the world, including speaking, writing, a facial expression and a glance, is a movement of muscles. Even this sentence reaches you only because someone moved their fingers.

Computer science found the same thing by accident. In the 1980s the roboticist Hans Moravec noted that it was comparatively easy to make computers play chess at an adult level, and very hard to give them the perception and movement skills of a one-year-old. This is known as Moravec's paradox. Abilities that evolution has polished for hundreds of millions of years feel easy to us precisely because they are so well hidden.

Chapter 02

The problem the brain has to solve

The human body has more than 600 muscles. Your arm alone can reach the same cup in countless ways: elbow high or low, wrist bent or straight, fast or slow. In the 1930s and 1940s the Russian scientist Nikolai Bernstein called this the degrees of freedom problem: with so many possible combinations, how does the brain choose one?

Three more difficulties make it harder. Signals are slow. A message from the eye to the brain and back to a muscle takes around a tenth of a second or more, which is long enough for a ball to move a metre or two. Signals are noisy. Neurons and muscles do not fire exactly the same way twice, so every movement is slightly different. The body keeps changing. You grow, tire, carry bags and get injured, and the brain must keep recalibrating. Motor control is the set of solutions to these problems.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From reflex to skilled action

Movement is produced by circuits that get more flexible as you go up, and that all rely on prediction to beat the delays.

Chapter 04

The spinal cord is smarter than it looks

Every muscle is controlled by motor neurons, nerve cells whose bodies sit in the spinal cord or brainstem and whose long fibres reach the muscle. Each motor neuron controls a group of muscle fibres, called a motor unit. For the eye muscles a unit may be a handful of fibres, allowing precision; for the big thigh muscles it may be hundreds or more, giving power. The British physiologist Charles Sherrington, who won a Nobel Prize in 1932, called the motor neurons the "final common path": every command, from a reflex to a symphony, ends there.

When a doctor taps below your knee, a sensor in the stretched thigh muscle sends a signal to the spinal cord, which sends a command straight back to tighten the muscle. That stretch reflex needs no brain at all and keeps you steady when your knee buckles slightly. The spinal cord also contains central pattern generators, circuits that produce rhythmic movements like stepping without instructions for each step. Experiments since the early twentieth century have shown that animals with the spinal cord cut off from the brain can still produce walking-like leg movements on a treadmill. The brain turns walking on and adjusts it; it does not compose each step.

Chapter 05

The motor cortex and the little man on the brain

In 1870 two German doctors, Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig, discovered that weak electric current on a strip of a dog's cortex made its limbs twitch. In the 1930s to 1950s the Canadian neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield mapped the same strip in awake human patients during epilepsy surgery (the brain itself feels no pain). Stimulating one spot moved the thumb, another the lips, another the foot.

The result is the famous motor homunculus ("little man"): a map of the body laid along the primary motor cortex, a band running from the top of the head down each side. The map is distorted. The hands, lips and tongue have huge areas, while the back and legs have small ones. Brain space follows precision, not size. The parts you use for fine control, such as speech and handwriting, get the most cortex.

Later work complicated the neat picture. When the neuroscientist Michael Graziano stimulated the motor cortex of monkeys for longer, about half a second, they made complete actions, such as bringing a hand to the mouth and opening the mouth. The motor cortex seems to map useful actions as well as body parts. The left side controls the right of the body and vice versa, which is why a left-hemisphere stroke weakens the right arm and leg.

Chapter 06

The basal ganglia: releasing the brake

Deep in the brain sits a group of structures called the basal ganglia. A useful picture is a set of brakes. At rest, they hold back all possible movements. To act, the basal ganglia release the brake on one chosen movement while keeping the others held. That makes them central to choosing which action happens and when it starts. They depend heavily on dopamine from a small midbrain region, the substantia nigra ("black substance").

In Parkinson's disease, those dopamine cells gradually die. The result is slow movement, stiffness, often a tremor at rest, and great difficulty starting: a person may "freeze" in a doorway, unable to take the next step, yet be able to step over a line drawn on the floor or walk to the beat of music. External cues seem to bypass part of the faulty starting system. In the 1960s doctors found that levodopa, a chemical the brain turns into dopamine, could dramatically restore movement. It remains a main treatment, though its effect becomes less smooth over years. Huntington's disease shows the opposite failure: the brake fails and unwanted movements break through. More on both in Neurodegenerative diseases.

Chapter 07

The cerebellum: the quiet calibrator

At the back of the brain, below the main hemispheres, is the cerebellum ("little brain"). It makes up roughly a tenth of the brain's volume but contains more than half of its neurons, according to cell counts by Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues. Its job is less to start movements than to make them accurate, smooth and well timed.

Damage to the cerebellum causes ataxia: clumsy, jerky movement, overshooting a target, an unsteady wide-legged walk and slurred speech. Alcohol affects the cerebellum early, which is why police sobriety tests ask people to walk a straight line or touch their nose.

The cerebellum also drives adaptation. Put on glasses with prisms that shift your view sideways and throw a ball at a target. At first you miss to one side. Within a few dozen throws you are accurate again. Take the glasses off and you now miss to the other side, until you readapt. People with cerebellar damage adapt much less. The cerebellum learns from errors and quietly recalibrates every movement you make.

Chapter 08

Prediction: why you cannot tickle yourself

Because feedback is slow, the brain cannot just react. It predicts. Whenever it sends a movement command, it also sends a copy, called an efference copy, to circuits that predict what the movement will feel like. This prediction is called a forward model. The brain compares predicted with actual sensations and corrects only the difference.

This explains a famous puzzle. You cannot tickle yourself, because your brain predicts the touch precisely and turns down the sensation. In a 1998 study, Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith had people tickle their own palm through a robot. When the robot added a small delay or a change of direction, the touch felt more ticklish, because the prediction no longer matched. It is also why the world does not seem to jump when your eyes move: the brain expected the shift.

Prediction also explains Fitts's law, described by Paul Fitts in 1954: the smaller and farther a target, the longer it takes to reach accurately. Because muscle signals are noisy, faster movements are less precise, and the brain chooses speeds that trade time for accuracy. Designers use this rule when they size buttons on screens.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Injury, skill and machines

Movement problems are among the most common and disabling effects of brain disease, and the science of skill shapes rehabilitation, sport and robotics.

Chapter 09

When movement fails

General education

This is not medical advice. Sudden weakness on one side of the body is an emergency sign of stroke (see Vol 45 · Stroke).

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, and weakness of one side of the body, called hemiparesis, is its most common effect. Recovery shows the brain's capacity to reorganise (see Brain plasticity). An important finding came from the psychologist Edward Taub. Patients with a weak arm tend to stop using it and rely on the good one, and the weak arm then gets worse, a pattern he called "learned non-use". In constraint-induced movement therapy, the good arm is restrained for much of the day, forcing intensive practice with the weak one. Trials showed real gains for suitable patients.

Other conditions damage different links in the chain. Spinal cord injury cuts the path between brain and body. In ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, also called motor neurone disease), the motor neurons themselves die, while thinking usually stays largely intact. The principle across all of these: the location of the damage decides the kind of movement problem, which is why neurologists can often locate an injury just by watching someone move.

Chapter 10

How skills move into the background

When you first learn to drive, every gear change needs attention. Years later you do it while talking. In the brain, early learning relies heavily on the prefrontal cortex and attention. With practice, control shifts towards the basal ganglia, the cerebellum and well-tuned motor cortex circuits, and the movement becomes automatic. "Muscle memory" is a misleading name. The memory is in the brain; the muscles just get stronger.

Three findings help anyone learning a physical skill. Practice that varies conditions, such as different distances and angles, builds more flexible skill than repeating one perfect movement. Sleep after practice can improve some motor skills, as described in Sleep and the brain. And focusing attention on the effect of a movement, such as the flight of the ball, often works better than focusing on your own limbs.

Chapter 11

What robots are learning from brains

Industrial robots are fast and precise in fixed settings, but brittle when something changes. A human hand is soft, full of touch sensors, and controlled by circuits that predict and adapt. Robotics has borrowed several ideas from motor neuroscience: building in springiness instead of rigid stiffness, using prediction to handle delays, and learning from trial and error. Recent robots trained with large amounts of data are improving quickly at handling objects. Even so, as of 2026, reliable all-purpose dexterity remains one of the hardest problems in robotics. See Robotics and Humanoid robots.

Robust, adaptable, and slow to repair

The motor system's design gives astonishing flexibility, and specific weak points when it is damaged.

Chapter 13

Treatments: what works and what it costs

What works

Levodopa still helps most people with Parkinson's. Deep brain stimulation, electrodes placed in the basal ganglia and driven by a pacemaker-like device, has been used for decades to reduce tremor and stiffness in carefully chosen patients. Intensive, task-specific rehabilitation helps after stroke.

What it costs

None of these repairs the damage. Parkinson's treatments manage symptoms while the disease progresses. Surgery carries risks of bleeding and infection. Rehabilitation needs many hours that health systems often cannot provide, and gains vary widely between people.

The pattern is honest and slightly frustrating: we are better at working around motor damage than at reversing it. That is where the future research is aimed.

The core idea

Movement is controlled by a layered system: the spinal cord handles reflexes and rhythms, the motor cortex sends voluntary commands, the basal ganglia select and start actions, and the cerebellum times and calibrates them. Because nerves are slow, the brain predicts the results of its own commands and corrects the difference.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The body has more than 600 muscles.
  • The cerebellum holds more than half of the brain's neurons in about a tenth of its volume.
  • Visual feedback to a muscle takes around a tenth of a second or more.
The common mistake

Thinking movement is easy and thinking is hard. Moravec's paradox says the opposite for machines, and the brain spends a huge share of its neurons on movement.

The words, in plain words

A nerve cell that carries commands from the spinal cord or brainstem to a muscle.

Reflex
An automatic movement produced by a short circuit, often in the spinal cord.

A spinal or brainstem circuit that produces rhythms such as stepping.
Primary motor cortex

Motor homunculus

Substantia nigra

Cerebellum
The "little brain" at the back that times, smooths and recalibrates movement.
Ataxia

Efference copy
A copy of a movement command sent to prediction circuits.
Forward model
The brain's prediction of what a movement will feel like and do.
