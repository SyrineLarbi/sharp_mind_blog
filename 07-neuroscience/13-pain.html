Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 13 of 20

Pain. Not a message from the body, but the brain's verdict on it.

Common sense says pain is simple: damage happens, a nerve reports it, you feel it. The worse the damage, the worse the pain. Neuroscience has found that this picture fails in both directions. Soldiers with terrible wounds can feel almost nothing, and people whose injuries have fully healed can hurt for years. Pain turns out to be the brain's judgement about how much danger the body is in, and like any judgement it can be too high, too low, or stuck. That one idea explains a great deal, from rubbing a bumped elbow to the opioid crisis.

Question 1 · What is it?

An alarm, not a damage meter

Pain is an experience produced by the brain to protect the body. It usually tracks damage, but it is not a measurement of damage.

Chapter 01

The nail that missed, and the boy who felt nothing

In 1995 the British Medical Journal reported the case of a builder who jumped down onto a 15-centimetre nail. It went straight through his boot and came out of the top. He was in such agony that the smallest movement was unbearable, and doctors gave him strong painkillers before carefully pulling the nail out and removing the boot. The nail had passed cleanly between his toes. His foot was not injured at all. His pain was completely real, and it was caused by what his brain believed, not by damage.

The opposite case is just as instructive. A small number of people are born unable to feel pain. In 2006 researchers studying families in northern Pakistan traced one form of this condition to faulty copies of a gene called SCN9A, which builds a channel that pain nerves need to fire. The study began with a boy who performed street acts, walking on hot coals and pushing knives through his arms, without pain. He died young after jumping from a roof. People with this condition often bite their tongues badly, break bones without noticing and suffer damaged joints.

Together the two cases show what pain is for. It is a protective alarm. Without it, life is short and dangerous. But an alarm can go off when there is no fire.

Chapter 02

Pain and nociception are different things

Scientists separate two ideas that everyday language joins. Nociception (from the Latin for "harm") is the nervous system detecting potentially harmful events: heat, pressure, chemicals from injured tissue. It happens even under general anaesthesia, when you feel nothing. Pain is the conscious experience. In 2020 the International Association for the Study of Pain revised its official definition to "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage".

Notice two things in that wording. Pain is both sensory and emotional: it has a "where and how strong" part and a "how bad does this feel" part. And it can occur with damage that is only potential, or with none at all. Doctors also separate acute pain, which follows an injury and fades as it heals, from chronic pain, usually defined as pain lasting more than three months.

The last category was named officially only in 2017. Its patients were long told their pain was "all in their head", as if that made it imaginary. All pain is in the head, in the sense that the brain produces it, and nociplastic pain is as real as a broken leg.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sensors, a gate, a brain network and a volume knob

Pain signals pass through several stations, and each one can turn them up or down before you feel anything.

Chapter 04

The danger sensors

The skin, joints and organs contain nerve endings called nociceptors, which respond to intense heat, cold, pressure or chemicals. They come in two main kinds. Thin fibres with a fatty coating, called A-delta fibres, carry signals fast and give the sharp "first pain" when you stub a toe. Thinner uncoated C fibres carry signals slowly and give the dull, spreading "second pain" a moment later.

The molecular sensors on these nerves explain some everyday puzzles. In 1997 the scientist David Julius and his team found the receptor that responds to capsaicin, the chemical that makes chilli peppers hot. The same receptor responds to dangerous heat, which is why chilli feels burning even though no temperature has changed. A related receptor responds to cold and to menthol, which is why mint feels cool. Julius shared the 2021 Nobel Prize for this work.

Chapter 05

The gate in the spinal cord

When you bang your elbow, you rub it, and it helps. In 1965 the psychologist Ronald Melzack and the neuroscientist Patrick Wall explained why with the gate control theory. Pain fibres enter the spinal cord at the back, in a region called the dorsal horn. There, other inputs can open or close a "gate" before signals travel up to the brain.

The details of the original model have been revised, but its central claim has held up: pain signals are filtered and changed in the spinal cord before they reach the brain, and the brain itself controls part of that filtering. This was the first scientific theory to explain why attention, fear and distraction change pain. It also inspired TENS machines, which deliver mild electric tingles to the skin.

Chapter 06

No single pain centre

Brain imaging shows that pain activates a whole network. The somatosensory cortex works out where the pain is and how intense it is. The insula and the anterior cingulate cortex, the same areas involved in emotion (see Emotions and the brain), add how unpleasant it is and how urgently you want it to stop. The prefrontal cortex adds meaning and expectation.

A 1997 experiment by Pierre Rainville and colleagues separated these parts. People dipped a hand in hot water while under hypnosis. When the suggestion was that the heat would feel more or less unpleasant, without changing its intensity, activity changed in the anterior cingulate but not in the somatosensory cortex. The intensity and the suffering are processed partly separately, which is why some people on certain drugs or after certain brain surgeries report that the pain is "there" but no longer bothers them.

The strongest evidence that the brain makes pain is phantom limb pain. Most people who lose a limb continue to feel it, and many feel pain in it, sometimes severe, in a hand or foot that no longer exists. There are no nerves in the missing limb to send signals. The brain's map of the body is still there, and it can still produce pain. In the 1990s the neuroscientist V.S. Ramachandran used a simple box with a mirror to let patients "see" the missing hand move, and some reported relief. Larger trials of mirror therapy have given mixed results.

Chapter 07

The brain's own painkillers

In 1969 the researcher David Reynolds showed that electrically stimulating a region in the midbrain of rats, the periaqueductal grey, blocked pain so strongly that surgery could be done without anaesthetic. This region is the top of a descending pain control system that runs down to the spinal gate. In the 1970s scientists discovered that the brain makes its own morphine-like chemicals, the endorphins and related molecules, which act on the same opioid receptors as drugs like morphine.

This system explains why pain can disappear in emergencies. The American anaesthetist Henry Beecher noted in the Second World War that many badly wounded soldiers asked for little or no pain relief. A football player can finish a match on a broken bone. When survival or a goal matters more, the brain turns the volume down. It also explains part of placebo pain relief. In 1978 a study of dental patients found that a drug that blocks opioid receptors, naloxone, could reduce the pain-relieving effect of a placebo. Expectation releases the brain's own opioids, which is why placebo is real, measurable and strongest for pain.

Chapter 08

How pain gets stuck: sensitisation

After sunburn, a warm shower hurts and a friendly pat on the shoulder makes you wince. Normal touch has become painful, a state called allodynia. This is peripheral sensitisation: chemicals from damaged tissue make nerve endings easier to trigger. It is useful. It makes you protect the injured area while it heals.

The nervous system can also become sensitised further up. With repeated strong input, neurons in the spinal cord fire more easily and for longer, a process researchers such as Clifford Woolf described as central sensitisation. In the brain, the expectation of pain, fear of movement and attention to the body can all turn the volume up. Chronic pain often reflects an alarm system that has learned to be over-protective, a little like a smoke detector that now goes off at toast. The tissue may have healed long ago. This is why scans of painful backs often look similar to scans of pain-free backs, and why surgery to "fix" what the scan shows often fails.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A huge burden, a failed shortcut, and a new approach

Chronic pain is one of the largest causes of disability in the world, and treating it as damage alone has caused serious harm.

Chapter 09

The size of the problem

Surveys in many countries find that about one adult in five lives with chronic pain. Low back pain has for years been ranked by the Global Burden of Disease study as the single leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide. Chronic pain is linked to depression, poor sleep, lost work and isolation, and each of these in turn makes pain worse.

Pain is also shaped by the person and their situation, not only by their tissue. Stress, poor sleep, low mood and a sense of threat at work or home all tend to raise it, while feeling safe, supported and active tends to lower it. That is not blame. It is the gate and the volume knob at work.

Chapter 10

The opioid crisis: what happens when pain is treated as damage alone

Opioids such as morphine, oxycodone and fentanyl act on the same receptors as the brain's endorphins. For severe short-term pain, after surgery or a serious injury, and at the end of life, they are essential. In the United States from the late 1990s, manufacturers and parts of medicine promoted them heavily for long-term pain, with claims that the risk of addiction was low. Prescriptions rose steeply.

The result was a public health disaster that moved through prescription pills to heroin and then to illegal fentanyl. US overdose deaths passed 100,000 a year in the early 2020s, most involving opioids, before falling noticeably in 2024. Meanwhile research showed that long-term opioids often work poorly for chronic pain. A 2018 US trial in patients with chronic back, hip or knee pain found that opioids were not better than non-opioid medicines over a year, and caused more side effects. Long-term use can even make people more sensitive to pain, a condition called opioid-induced hyperalgesia. A drug that blocks the alarm does not retrain it.

Chapter 11

Treating the alarm, not just the tissue

General education

This is not medical advice. New, severe or unexplained pain should always be checked by a doctor, and nobody should stop a prescribed medicine without advice.

If chronic pain is partly a learned over-protection, treatment can aim to retrain it. Current guidelines for chronic back pain in several countries put activity, exercise and psychological approaches ahead of drugs and surgery. Pain education explains to patients how pain works, often using the ideas in this file, which reduces fear. Graded activity increases movement step by step so the nervous system learns that movement is safe. Cognitive behavioural therapy and related therapies help people reduce fear and avoidance.

Newer approaches go further. In a trial in Colorado, published in 2021, people with long-term back pain received "pain reprocessing therapy", which teaches them to reinterpret pain sensations as safe. A large share reported being pain-free or nearly pain-free afterwards, far more than in comparison groups. It was a single trial and needs repeating, but it fits the theory. For neuropathic pain, specific medicines that calm overactive nerves, including some antidepressants and anti-seizure drugs, help some patients.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A protector that can turn on its owner

The pain system's flexibility protects us, and the same flexibility lets it go wrong.

Chapter 13

The measurement problem

There is still no blood test or scan that shows how much pain someone is in. Doctors rely on what patients say, often on a scale from 0 to 10. That has costs.

What brain signatures can do

Researchers have built "pain signatures" from brain-imaging patterns that predict, on average, how much pain groups of people report under controlled heat. They help test new drugs and show that pain is a real brain state.

What they cannot do

They cannot yet tell whether one particular person is in pain, especially chronic pain, and experts warn against using them in courts or insurance claims. Meanwhile studies in the US and elsewhere have found that some groups of patients are more likely to have their pain underestimated or undertreated.

The honest conclusion is that for now, the best measure of pain is the person who has it, and that taking their account seriously is part of treatment.

The deepest change may be in how pain is explained. When people understand that pain is the brain's protection, not a direct readout of damage, they often fear it less, and pain that is feared less tends to hurt less. That is not a cure, but it is a change in medicine that costs almost nothing.

The core idea

Pain is the brain's protective judgement about danger to the body. Nociceptors detect harm, a gate in the spinal cord filters the signal, a brain network builds the experience, and the brain's own descending system can turn it down or up. Chronic pain often means the alarm has become over-protective.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 1 in 5 adults lives with chronic pain.
  • Chronic pain is usually defined as lasting more than 3 months.
  • Gate control theory: 1965; endorphins discovered in the 1970s.
The common mistake

Believing that more pain always means more damage, or that pain without damage is imaginary. Pain and damage are related, but they are not the same thing.
