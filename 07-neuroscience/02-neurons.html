Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 02 of 20

Neurons. A cell that turns salt water into a message.

Every thought, feeling and movement you have ever made was carried by one kind of cell. A neuron is a living cell like any other, with a nucleus and the usual machinery. What makes it special is that it keeps its inside electrically different from its outside, like a tiny battery. When it is stimulated enough, it lets that charge go in a sharp pulse that races along its length. Then it passes the news to the next cell with a squirt of chemicals. Everything else in neuroscience is built on these two tricks.

Question 1 · What is it?

A cell built to send messages over distance

Neurons come in many shapes, but they share one plan: a part that listens, a part that decides, and a long part that sends.

Chapter 01

Two rivals and a silver stain

Brain tissue under a microscope used to look like a grey tangle. In 1873 the Italian scientist Camillo Golgi found a way to stain it with silver salts. For reasons still not fully understood, the stain coats only a few cells in each slice, but it coats them completely. Suddenly single nerve cells stood out in black, with every branch visible.

Golgi believed the cells were fused into one continuous net. A Spanish doctor, Santiago Ramón y Cajal, used Golgi's own stain and drew a different conclusion. In hundreds of careful drawings he showed that each nerve cell is a separate unit, and that cells touch without merging. This became the neuron doctrine: the nervous system is made of separate cells that communicate across tiny gaps.

The two men shared the Nobel Prize in 1906, and used their prize lectures to disagree with each other. Cajal was right about the main point. Electron microscopes in the 1950s finally showed the gap between neurons directly.

Chapter 02

The parts of a neuron

A typical neuron has four parts, and each has one job.

PartWhat it looks likeIts job
DendritesBranching twigs around the cell bodyReceive signals from other neurons
Cell body (soma)The rounded centre, holding the nucleusKeeps the cell alive; adds up incoming signals
AxonOne long, thin fibre leaving the cell bodyCarries the outgoing pulse, sometimes over a metre
Axon terminalsSmall swellings at the ends of the axon's branchesRelease chemicals to pass the signal on

The proportions can be extreme. A motor neuron that moves your big toe has its cell body in the lower spinal cord and an axon that runs all the way down the leg, about a metre in a tall adult. If the cell body were the size of a tennis ball, the axon would be a garden hose roughly a kilometre long. Keeping that hose supplied and repaired is one of the hardest jobs any cell has.

Chapter 03

Three kinds of neuron, and the helpers around them

By job, there are three broad kinds. Sensory neurons carry information in from the skin, eyes, ears and organs. Motor neurons carry commands out to muscles and glands. Interneurons connect neurons to each other. Most of the brain's 86 billion neurons are interneurons in the broad sense: they talk mostly to other neurons.

Shapes vary hugely. Pyramidal cells in the cortex have a triangular body and a long tree of dendrites. Purkinje cells in the cerebellum have a flat, fan-shaped tree that can receive tens of thousands of inputs or more. Researchers now sort brain cells into thousands of types, using the genes each one switches on.

Neurons are surrounded by glial cells. Astrocytes, star-shaped, feed neurons, mop up spilled chemicals and help control blood flow. Oligodendrocytes in the brain, and Schwann cells in the body's nerves, wrap axons in insulation. Microglia are the brain's resident immune cells. They eat debris and prune unused connections.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Charge it, fire it, insulate it, pass it on

A neuron works in four steps. Each step has its own machinery, and each is a target for diseases, drugs and poisons.

Chapter 04

Step one: the battery

Body fluids are salty water. The salts are dissolved as ions, atoms carrying an electrical charge. The two that matter most here are sodium and potassium, both positively charged. A neuron pumps them in opposite directions. Tiny protein machines in its outer skin, the membrane, push sodium out and pull potassium in, using energy to do it.

The result is an imbalance. There is far more sodium outside than inside, and far more potassium inside than outside. Some potassium leaks back out, and that leak leaves the inside of the cell about 70 thousandths of a volt negative compared with the outside. This is the resting potential. It is small, but across a membrane only a few billionths of a metre thick it is a strong electrical pressure.

Keeping the battery charged is expensive. Pumping ions back is one of the biggest energy costs in the brain, and a large reason the brain uses so much of the body's fuel. When blood flow stops, the pumps fail within minutes, the battery drains, and neurons begin to die.

Chapter 05

Step two: the pulse

The membrane is studded with ion channels, tiny pores that open and close. Some sodium channels are voltage-sensitive. When the inside of the cell becomes a little less negative, they open. Sodium rushes in, which makes the inside even less negative, which opens more channels. It is a chain reaction.

Within about a thousandth of a second the inside flips to positive. Then the sodium channels shut themselves, potassium channels open, potassium flows out, and the voltage drops back. This sudden spike and recovery is the action potential, the nerve impulse. It does not fade as it travels, because each patch of membrane triggers the next, like a line of falling dominoes.

Two rules follow. First, the pulse is all-or-nothing. If the stimulus does not reach a threshold, nothing happens. If it does, the full spike fires, always the same size. Second, a neuron signals strength by rate, not size. A light touch might produce a few spikes per second, a firm press many more. Just after a spike, there is a short refractory period when the cell cannot fire again. That keeps pulses moving in one direction and sets a top speed of several hundred spikes a second.

This was worked out in the late 1940s and early 1950s by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley in Britain. They used the squid's giant axon, which is about half a millimetre to a millimetre wide, wide enough to push a wire inside. Their equations, published in 1952, still describe the spike with remarkable accuracy, and earned them a Nobel Prize in 1963.

Chapter 06

Step three: insulation for speed

In a thin bare axon, the pulse travels slowly, around a metre per second, walking pace. The squid got speed by making its axon fat. Vertebrates found a better solution: myelin.

Glial cells wrap the axon in many layers of fatty membrane, like insulating tape. Every millimetre or so there is a small bare gap, a node of Ranvier. The signal cannot leak out through the insulated stretches, so it effectively jumps from node to node. In the best-insulated human fibres, signals travel at roughly 100 metres per second or more. That is the difference between pulling your hand off a hot stove in time and not.

Myelin lets a thin axon be as fast as a thick bare one, so millions of fast fibres can fit in a small space. Without it, a human brain with the same speed would be far too big to carry.

Chapter 07

Step four: across the gap

When the pulse reaches an axon terminal, it cannot jump to the next neuron. Most neurons are separated by a gap called the synaptic cleft, tens of billionths of a metre wide. The junction as a whole is the synapse.

Here the signal changes form. The arriving pulse opens calcium channels. Calcium triggers small bubbles, called vesicles, to fuse with the membrane and spill their contents into the gap. These are chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. They drift across in a fraction of a millisecond and lock onto receptor proteins on the next cell, opening or closing its channels. Electrical inside the cell, chemical between cells: that is the basic pattern.

The chemical step was proven in 1921 by the German pharmacologist Otto Loewi. He stimulated the nerve to a frog's heart, which slowed it, then moved fluid from around that heart to a second heart. The second heart slowed too. Something chemical had been released. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1936 with Henry Dale. The chemicals themselves are the subject of the next topic, Neurotransmitters.

Some synapses are electrical instead: proteins form direct channels between two cells, and current flows straight through. These are faster but less flexible, and they are a minority in the adult human brain.

Chapter 08

The vote: how a neuron decides to fire

A single neuron in the cortex may receive inputs at thousands of synapses. Some inputs are excitatory: they nudge the cell towards its threshold. Others are inhibitory: they push it away. None of them alone is usually enough. The cell body adds them up, across space and over a few thousandths of a second. If the total at the base of the axon crosses the threshold, the neuron fires.

Think of a committee vote, where each member's vote has a different weight and some members vote against. Learning mostly means changing the weights: making some synapses stronger and others weaker. That is how experience is stored, as later topics explain (Brain plasticity and Memory formation).

Inhibition is just as important as excitation. Without it, activity would spread from cell to cell until the whole network fired at once. In fact, that is roughly what an epileptic seizure is.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Diseases, drugs and poisons all act on these steps

Once you know the four steps, a great deal of medicine makes sense. Most nervous system disorders, and most drugs that act on the brain, target one of them.

Chapter 09

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Symptoms such as new numbness, weakness, seizures or changes in vision need a doctor.

Chapter 10

The dentist, the pufferfish and the snake

When a dentist numbs your jaw, the drug, usually lidocaine or a relative, blocks the sodium channels in the nerves from your teeth. No sodium rush means no pulse. The pain signal starts but never arrives. Local anaesthetics work by stopping step two, and when the drug washes away, the nerve works normally again.

Nature found the same trick, more dangerously. The pufferfish, eaten as fugu in Japan, contains tetrodotoxin, which blocks the same channels throughout the body. A tiny dose can paralyse the breathing muscles. This is why chefs in Japan need a special licence to prepare it.

Other poisons attack the synapse. Some snake venoms block the receptors on muscle, so the signal from the motor nerve has nothing to land on. Botulinum toxin stops vesicles releasing their chemical, which in large doses causes the paralysing illness botulism, and in tiny doses, as Botox, relaxes wrinkle-causing muscles. Each poison is a natural experiment that proves one step of the chain.

Chapter 11

A cell that is meant to last a lifetime

Most neurons in your cortex were made before you were born. Unlike skin or gut cells, mature neurons do not divide. A neuron in your cortex today may be with you, still working, at ninety.

That is good for keeping long-term information in stable wiring. It is bad for repair. When neurons die after a stroke or injury, the brain mostly cannot replace them. Some recovery happens because surviving neurons form new connections, not because lost ones grow back. Nerves in the arms and legs can regrow slowly after a cut, because their Schwann cells guide the regrowth. Nerves in the brain and spinal cord mostly do not, which is why spinal cord injury is so often permanent.

Do adults make any new neurons? In rodents, clearly yes, in the hippocampus. In humans, it is genuinely contested. Studies published in 2018 reached opposite conclusions from human brain tissue, and the debate continues. If new neurons are made in adult humans, the number is small.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant signalling cell with built-in weaknesses

Every feature of the neuron is a compromise between speed, reliability, flexibility and cost.

Chapter 12

Compare it with a computer chip. A transistor switches millions of times faster than a neuron fires. Yet the brain does its work on about 20 watts. Neurons are slow, but they work in enormous parallel, and they rewire themselves as they go. No chip yet does both at that energy cost, which is why engineers study them.

Chapter 14

A neuron is not a simple switch

Artificial neural networks, the technology behind modern AI, are built from "neurons" that add up inputs and output a number. That idea came from the committee vote in Chapter 08. Real neurons do much more. Their dendrites are not passive wires. Branches can generate their own small spikes and combine inputs in non-linear ways before the signal ever reaches the cell body.

In 2020 a team studying human cortical tissue reported that single dendrites could perform a calculation that textbook artificial neurons need two layers to do. Other work has suggested that imitating one real cortical neuron accurately takes a small artificial network of several layers. One real neuron may be closer to a small computer than to a single switch.

That matters for the future in two ways. It means simulating a brain will be harder than counting its neurons suggests. And it means AI may still have things to learn from biology. The comparison is explored in Artificial neural networks.

The core idea

A neuron charges its membrane by pumping ions, fires an all-or-nothing spike when its inputs cross a threshold, sends it fast along an axon insulated by myelin, and passes it on chemically at the synapse. Electrical inside, chemical between. Learning changes the strength of synapses.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Resting voltage about −70 millivolts; a spike lasts about one millisecond.
  • Speed: about 1 m/s in thin bare fibres, roughly 100 m/s in the best insulated ones.
  • A cortical neuron receives inputs at thousands of synapses.
The common mistake

Thinking a stronger stimulus makes a bigger spike. Spikes are always the same size. Strength is coded by how often they fire and by how many neurons fire.

Test yourself

Why does a dental anaesthetic stop pain without stopping your thoughts? If you can explain it using sodium channels and where the drug is injected, you understand the pulse.

The words, in plain words
Neuron doctrine
The principle that the nervous system is made of separate cells, not one fused net.
Dendrite
A branch of a neuron that receives signals.
Axon
The long fibre that carries a neuron's outgoing pulse.
Ion
An atom with an electrical charge, such as sodium or potassium in body fluids.
Ion channel
A pore in the cell membrane that opens or closes to let particular ions through.
Resting potential
The small negative voltage inside a neuron at rest, about −70 millivolts.
Action potential
The brief all-or-nothing electrical spike that travels along an axon.
Threshold
The voltage a neuron must reach before it fires.
Myelin
The fatty insulation wrapped around many axons, which makes signals much faster.
Synapse
The junction where one neuron passes its signal to another.
Neurotransmitter
A chemical released at a synapse to carry the signal across the gap.
Glia
Support cells that feed, insulate, clean and defend neurons.
