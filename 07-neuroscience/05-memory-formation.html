Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 05 of 20

Memory formation. How an experience becomes a change in your wiring.

You will probably remember something from this page tomorrow. That means that, tonight, something physical will happen in your head. Particular connections between particular neurons will become stronger. New proteins will be built to keep them strong. And while you sleep, a structure deep in your brain will quietly replay parts of today, faster than real life, and help write them into longer-term storage. This is not a metaphor. Neuroscientists can now watch many of these steps happen, and in mice they can even switch individual memories on with light.

Question 1 · What is it?

A memory is a lasting change in connections

For a century scientists searched for the physical trace of a memory. They finally found it, not in one place, but in a pattern.

Chapter 01

The hunt for the engram

In 1904 the German biologist Richard Semon gave the physical trace of a memory a name: the engram. Nobody knew what it looked like or where it was. The American psychologist Karl Lashley spent much of his career, from the 1920s to the 1940s, trying to find it. He trained rats to run mazes, then removed different pieces of their cortex to see which piece held the maze.

No single piece did. The more cortex he removed, the worse the rats did, but it hardly mattered which part. In 1950 he summarised his frustration in a famous paper, "In Search of the Engram". He joked that the logical conclusion was that learning is simply impossible.

Lashley's failure turned out to be informative. A memory is not stored in one spot, like a file in a drawer. It is spread across many neurons, often in several regions. Lose some and the rest can still rebuild most of it. Lashley was also looking in the wrong place for the kind of memory a maze needs. A key structure sat deeper, in the temporal lobe.

Chapter 02

The hippocampus, and memories switched on with light

That deeper structure is the hippocampus. Its role became clear through the patient Henry Molaison, "H.M.", who could not form new memories of events after it was removed on both sides in 1953. His story is told in Psychology · Memory. The short lesson: the hippocampus is needed to form new memories of events and facts, but old memories and skills can survive without it.

Modern tools have made the engram visible. In 2012 a team in Susumu Tonegawa's laboratory at MIT genetically tagged the neurons in a mouse's hippocampus that were active while it learned to fear a particular box. Those cells were made sensitive to light. Later, in a completely different, safe box, the researchers shone light into the mouse's brain. The mouse froze in fear. Switching on only the tagged cells was enough to bring back the memory.

In 2013 the same group went further and created a false fear memory in mice, linking a box to a shock the animals had never received there. These experiments are in mice, with tools that cannot be used in people. But they settled an old question. An engram is a specific set of neurons whose connections were changed by an experience, and reactivating them can recall it.

Chapter 03

From seconds to decades: four timescales

Memory formation happens in stages, each with its own machinery. Psychology describes them as encoding, storage and retrieval. At the level of cells, the stages look like this.

StageTimescaleWhat physically happensWhat blocks it
Holding in mindSecondsNeurons keep firing in a sustained pattern, mainly in the prefrontal cortexDistraction
Short-term changeMinutes to hoursExisting proteins at the synapse are chemically modified; the synapse works more stronglyDrugs that block certain receptors
Long-term changeHours to days and longerGenes switch on; new proteins are built; synapses grow or new ones formDrugs that block protein production
Systems consolidationDays to yearsThe memory comes to depend less on the hippocampus and more on the cortexHippocampal damage before this is finished

The pattern is simple. Short-term memory uses what the synapse already has. Long-term memory requires building something new. That difference explains a great deal, from why a knock on the head can erase the last few minutes to why spaced practice works.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Strengthen, build, then move to long-term storage

The mechanisms were found in a sea slug, a rabbit, and a lot of rats and mice. They turn out to be remarkably similar across animals, including us.

Chapter 04

What a sea slug taught us

The human brain is too complicated to study memory one synapse at a time. So from the 1960s the Austrian-born American neuroscientist Eric Kandel chose a large sea slug, Aplysia. It has only about 20,000 neurons, many of them big enough to see and record from. When its siphon is touched, it pulls in its gill, a simple reflex served by a small circuit.

Kandel and colleagues trained the reflex. After a single unpleasant shock to the tail, the slug withdrew its gill more strongly for minutes. After several shocks spaced out over time, the effect lasted for days or weeks. They then traced what had changed. In short-term memory, the sensory neurons released more chemical messenger at existing synapses. In long-term memory, genes were switched on and the sensory neurons grew new synapses.

A key switch in this process is a protein called CREB, which turns on the genes needed for long-term change. It plays similar roles in flies, mice and, it appears, people. Kandel shared the Nobel Prize in 2000. The basic chemistry of memory is ancient and shared across the animal kingdom.

Chapter 05

Long-term potentiation: the synapse turns up

In 1973 Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo, working in Oslo, stimulated a pathway into the hippocampus of rabbits with a short, rapid burst of pulses. Afterwards, a single normal pulse on the same pathway produced a much bigger response than before. The connection had been strengthened, and the effect lasted for hours, in some animals for days. They had found long-term potentiation, or LTP: a lasting increase in the strength of a synapse after strong activity.

LTP has exactly the properties a memory mechanism needs. It is fast to create. It lasts. And it is specific: only the synapses that were active get stronger, not every synapse on the cell. The opposite process, long-term depression (LTD), weakens synapses after a different pattern of activity. Together they let the brain adjust each connection up or down.

Chapter 06

The coincidence detector

How does a synapse "know" it should strengthen? The answer is a receptor for glutamate, the brain's main excitatory messenger, called the NMDA receptor. It is a gate with two locks. The first lock opens when glutamate arrives from the sending cell. The second is a magnesium ion that plugs the channel, and it is pushed out only when the receiving cell is already strongly active.

So the NMDA gate opens only when both cells are active at the same moment. That is Donald Hebb's rule, "cells that fire together wire together", built into a single molecule. When the gate opens, calcium flows in. Calcium triggers enzymes that, among other things, add extra glutamate receptors of another type, called AMPA receptors, to the synapse. More receptors means a bigger response to the same signal. That is the strengthened connection.

Behaviour followed the chemistry. In 1986 Richard Morris and colleagues in Edinburgh gave rats a drug that blocks NMDA receptors and trained them to find a hidden platform in a pool of cloudy water. The drugged rats had great difficulty learning where it was. In 1999 Joe Tsien's group made mice with an extra boost to one part of the NMDA receptor. These mice, nicknamed "Doogie" after a television character, learned some tasks faster than normal mice.

Chapter 07

Making it last: proteins, genes and tags

The first hour of LTP uses proteins already present at the synapse. For the change to last longer, the cell must build new proteins and sometimes new structure, as in the sea slug. Experiments since the 1960s have shown that animals given a drug that blocks protein production soon after learning can remember for a few hours but then forget. No new proteins, no long-term memory.

This creates a puzzle. New proteins are made mainly near the cell's nucleus, but a neuron has thousands of synapses. How do the proteins reach only the right ones? In 1997 Uwe Frey and Richard Morris proposed an answer called synaptic tagging. Recently active synapses carry a temporary chemical "tag". Proteins made in the following hours are captured by tagged synapses only. A weak experience can therefore become a lasting memory if something important happens shortly before or after it, and the proteins it triggers are shared.

This also explains why spacing works, at the level of cells. In fruit flies and sea slugs, training spread out over time produces long-term memory that needs new proteins, while the same amount of training crammed together often does not. Each session restarts the chemistry.

Chapter 08

From index to library: systems consolidation

The hippocampus does not keep every memory forever. A useful picture is that it works as an index. The sights, sounds and meanings of an event are stored in the parts of the cortex that processed them. The hippocampus holds the links that tie those scattered pieces into one episode.

Over time, repeated reactivation strengthens direct links between the cortical pieces themselves, until they can be recalled with less help from the index. This slow shift is systems consolidation. It explains a pattern noticed in 1881 by the French psychologist Théodule Ribot: after brain injury, people often lose recent memories while keeping old ones. Their most recent memories had not yet been moved.

How completely memories leave the hippocampus is debated. One view holds that general facts become independent of it, while vivid personal memories, rich in detail, may always need it. Both views agree that the move happens slowly, and that much of it happens at night.

Chapter 09

The hippocampus overnight

In 1971 John O'Keefe in London found neurons in the rat hippocampus that fire only when the animal is in one particular place. He called them place cells. As a rat runs a track, different place cells fire in a sequence, like notes in a tune. Later, May-Britt and Edvard Moser found "grid cells" in a neighbouring region that help build the map. The three shared the Nobel Prize in 2014.

In 1994 Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton recorded place cells while rats slept after running. The cells that had fired together during the run fired together again in sleep. Later work showed the sequences being replayed, often many times faster than the original run, during brief bursts of hippocampal activity called sharp-wave ripples. In 2009 French researchers showed that interrupting these ripples during sleep made rats learn a maze more slowly.

In human deep sleep, these ripples tend to line up with slow brain waves and with short bursts of activity called sleep spindles, as if the hippocampus and cortex are taking turns in a conversation. In a 2007 study, people learned card locations while smelling a rose scent. When the same scent was released during their deep sleep, they remembered the locations better the next day. Sleep is not a pause in learning. It is one of its stages. More on sleep is in Sleep and the brain.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Emotion, disease and the way we learn

The cellular story is not only for scientists. It explains why some memories burn in, why Alzheimer's starts the way it does, and why some study habits fail.

Chapter 10

Why emotional events are written in bold

The amygdala, a small structure next to the hippocampus, reacts to emotionally charged events. Stress hormones released during such events, including adrenaline and cortisol, act through the amygdala to strengthen consolidation in the hippocampus and cortex. The American neuroscientist James McGaugh spent decades showing this in animals and people.

In a 1994 study by Larry Cahill, McGaugh and colleagues, volunteers heard a story with an emotional middle section, about a boy badly injured in an accident. People given a placebo remembered the emotional part especially well. People given propranolol, a common heart drug that blocks one of adrenaline's receptors, did not. Their memory for the neutral parts was unchanged. The emotional boost to memory runs partly on adrenaline-type chemistry, and can be blunted by a drug.

This is useful for survival: you remember the dog that bit you. It also has a dark side. In post-traumatic stress disorder, memories of trauma can be stored too strongly and return unbidden. Attempts to use drugs like propranolol to soften such memories are discussed in the Psychology · Memory file.

Chapter 11

Alzheimer's begins where memories are made

Alzheimer's disease usually begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, the gateway and the index of new memory. That is why its first sign is often trouble remembering recent events and conversations, while memories of decades ago remain clear for a long time. It follows Ribot's pattern.

In 1991 a study led by Robert Terry found that, among the brain changes in Alzheimer's, the loss of synapses matched the severity of memory loss more closely than the famous plaques and tangles did. Many researchers now see early Alzheimer's as a disease of failing synapses before it becomes a disease of dying neurons. The full story is in Neurodegenerative diseases.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Some forgetfulness is normal with age. Memory problems that are new, worsening or affecting daily life are worth discussing with a doctor.

Chapter 12

What the cells say about studying

The biology gives physical reasons for the study advice that psychology found by experiment.

Space it out

Long-term memory needs new proteins and tagged synapses. Several separate sessions restart that chemistry each time. One long session triggers it once. This is the cellular side of the spacing effect.

Sleep on it

Replay and the hippocampus-to-cortex conversation happen mostly in sleep, especially deep sleep early in the night. An all-night cram skips one of the stages of memory formation.

Make it matter

Attention, interest and mild emotion bring in dopamine, noradrenaline and the amygdala, which help tag and strengthen the right synapses.

Recall it

Pulling a memory out reactivates its engram, which makes it more stable in the long run. That is why testing yourself beats rereading.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A slow, distributed, chemical storage system

Compared with a hard drive, the brain stores memories slowly, expensively and imprecisely. Each weakness comes with a real strength.

Chapter 13

What the design buys, and what it costs

FeatureStrengthWeakness
Storage in many synapsesRobust: losing some neurons blurs a memory rather than deleting itOverlapping memories share synapses and can interfere or blend

Emotional boostDangers and important events are stored wellTraumatic memories can be stored too strongly
Recall reactivatesMemories can be updated with new informationMemories can drift or be distorted each time they are recalled

Artificial neural networks have a well-known problem called catastrophic forgetting: train them on a new task and they can lose an old one. The brain largely avoids this. A leading explanation, proposed in the 1990s, is exactly the two-stage design described here: a fast learner, the hippocampus, that teaches a slow learner, the cortex, a little at a time, interleaving new with old. The slowness that makes memory frustrating is also what keeps it from wiping itself.

Protecting, boosting and editing memories

The deepest open question is not technical. Neuroscientists know a great deal about how one synapse strengthens, and quite a lot about which regions are needed. The gap is in between: how millions of changed synapses add up to a memory of your tenth birthday, and how the brain finds that pattern again from a single smell. Closing that gap is one of the central problems of the field, discussed in The future of neuroscience.

The core idea

A memory is an engram: a set of neurons whose connections were changed by an experience. Synapses strengthen through long-term potentiation, triggered when the NMDA receptor detects two cells active together. Lasting memory needs new proteins. The hippocampus binds new memories and, largely during sleep, helps move them to the cortex.

The numbers worth knowing
  • LTP discovered in 1973 (Bliss and Lømo, rabbit hippocampus).
  • Aplysia, the sea slug that revealed memory's chemistry, has about 20,000 neurons; you have about 86 billion.
  • Short-term change lasts minutes to hours; long-term change needs new proteins built over hours.
The common mistake

Imagining that a memory sits in one neuron or one spot. Memories are spread across many synapses and regions, which is why brain damage usually blurs them rather than deleting them neatly.

Test yourself

Explain, at the level of cells, why three spaced study sessions followed by sleep beat one long night of cramming. If you can mention proteins, tags and replay, you have the whole chain.

Engram
The physical trace of a memory: a set of neurons whose connections changed together.
Hippocampus
A structure deep in each temporal lobe that binds new memories of events and places.
Long-term potentiation (LTP)
A lasting strengthening of a synapse after strong activity.
Long-term depression (LTD)
A lasting weakening of a synapse after a different pattern of activity.
NMDA receptor
A glutamate receptor that opens only when both cells are active, triggering LTP.
AMPA receptor
The glutamate receptor that carries most fast signals; adding more strengthens a synapse.
CREB
A protein that switches on the genes needed for long-term memory.
Synaptic tagging

The slow shift of a memory from depending on the hippocampus to depending on the cortex.

Replay
The re-firing of the day's activity sequences during sleep or rest.
Sharp-wave ripple
A brief, fast burst of hippocampal activity during which replay often happens.
