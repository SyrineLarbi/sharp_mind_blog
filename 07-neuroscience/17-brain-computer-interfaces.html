Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 17 of 20

Brain-computer interfaces. Not mind reading, but translating the brain's orders when the body can no longer carry them.

A brain-computer interface, or BCI, is a system that records activity from the brain and turns it into a command for a machine. It sounds like science fiction, and the headlines often treat it that way. The reality is narrower and, in some ways, more impressive. Dozens of people with paralysis have used implanted electrodes to move cursors, control robotic arms, write text and speak through a computer. None of them had their thoughts read. What the machines read is the brain's intention to move, and that turns out to be enough to give someone back a voice.

Question 1 · What is it?

A translator between neurons and machines

Every BCI does three things: it records brain activity, it decodes that activity into an intention, and it acts on the intention. The differences between systems lie in where they record and what they decode.

Chapter 01

A sip of coffee after fifteen years

In 2012 a team in the United States published a short video alongside a research paper. It showed a woman called Cathy Hutchinson, who had been paralysed by a brainstem stroke fifteen years earlier. She could not move her arms or speak. A small square of electrodes, about the size of a baby aspirin, had been implanted in the surface of her brain, in the area that plans arm movements. Next to her stood a robotic arm.

She looked at a bottle of coffee and imagined reaching for it. The arm reached. It closed its hand around the bottle, lifted it, and brought the straw to her mouth. She drank, and then she smiled. It was the first time in fifteen years that she had served herself a drink.

The moment captured what a BCI is. Hutchinson's arm nerves were cut off from her brain, but her motor cortex, the strip of brain that sends movement commands, still worked. It went on producing the orders for movements her body could no longer make. A BCI catches those orders and delivers them somewhere else.

Chapter 02

Reading, writing, and the oldest interface of all

Engineers distinguish two directions. An output BCI reads from the brain: it takes a signal out and uses it to control something. That is what moved Hutchinson's robotic arm. An input device writes to the nervous system: it puts information in, usually by stimulating nerves with small electric currents.

The most successful neural interface in history is an input device, and most people never think of it as one. The cochlear implant turns sound into electrical pulses delivered to the hearing nerve inside the ear. More than a million have been implanted around the world, and many deaf children who receive one early learn spoken language. The cochlear implant is a reminder that connecting electronics to nerves is not new. Connecting them to the thinking parts of the brain is.

The name itself dates from 1973, when the computer scientist Jacques Vidal at the University of California, Los Angeles, asked whether brain signals recorded from the scalp could control a computer. The scalp recording he used, the EEG or electroencephalogram, had been invented by the German psychiatrist Hans Berger in the 1920s.

Chapter 03

From the scalp to inside the cortex

The single biggest design choice is how close to the neurons the sensors sit. Closer means a clearer signal and more risk.

A useful analogy is a football stadium. EEG is a microphone outside the stadium. It hears the roar when a goal is scored, but not a single conversation. A surface sheet is a microphone above one section of the crowd. A penetrating array places microphones next to a few hundred individual fans. The more individual voices you can hear, the more detailed the message you can decode.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Listening to crowds of neurons, and learning their language

A BCI does not understand the brain. It learns a statistical translation between patterns of activity and the actions a person is trying to make. Both sides, machine and brain, keep adjusting.

Chapter 04

Neurons that vote for a direction

Neurons communicate with brief electrical pulses called spikes. A single neuron in the motor cortex fires more when you move your arm in some directions and less in others. Each has a preferred direction. In the 1980s the neuroscientist Apostolos Georgopoulos showed in monkeys that no single neuron specifies a movement. Instead, the direction emerges when you combine the "votes" of many neurons, each weighted by how strongly it fires. This is called population coding.

That finding is the foundation of the field. It means you do not need to record every neuron involved in a movement. A sample of a hundred or so, if they are the right kind, carries enough information to estimate where the person wants to go. The brain's own redundancy is what makes a small implant useful. More neurons give a smoother, faster estimate, which is why companies compete on electrode count.

The signals that matter are often produced even when no movement happens. When a paralysed person tries to move, or imagines moving vividly, the motor cortex still produces much of the pattern it would produce for the real movement. That is the raw material every motor BCI uses. The background on how the brain plans movement is in Motor control.

Chapter 05

Decoding: a translation learned from examples

The software that turns spikes into commands is called a decoder. It has to be trained for each person. A typical session goes like this. A cursor moves on a screen by itself, and the person is asked to imagine moving it. The computer records the neural activity alongside the cursor's known path. A statistical model then learns which patterns go with which movements. After a few minutes the computer hands control over, and the person's own activity starts moving the cursor.

The hard part is that the signal does not stay still. The electrodes shift slightly. Neurons near an electrode die or move away, and others come into range. Tiredness and attention change the patterns. Early systems had to be recalibrated every day. Keeping the decoder accurate as the signal drifts is one of the main practical problems in the field, and modern machine learning has helped a great deal with it.

Chapter 06

From cursors to handwriting to speech

For years the benchmark was typing by moving a cursor over a keyboard, which is slow. In 2021 a team at Stanford University tried a different idea. A man paralysed from the neck down imagined handwriting letters with a pen. The decoder learned the neural pattern of each letter. He reached about 90 characters a minute, close to the speed of people his age texting on a phone.

Then came speech. In 2023 two groups published in the same issue of the journal Nature. At Stanford, a woman with ALS who could no longer speak clearly had arrays placed in speech-related areas. The system decoded the movements she was trying to make with her lips, tongue and jaw, and reached about 62 words a minute. At the University of California, San Francisco, a team led by Edward Chang used a surface sheet on a woman paralysed by a stroke and reached a similar speed, with her words spoken by a digital face and a synthetic voice built from an old recording of her own.

Conversation runs at roughly 150 words a minute, so these systems were still slower than natural speech, and they made errors. But in 2024 a team at the University of California, Davis, reported a man with ALS whose implant decoded his attempted speech with about 97 percent word accuracy, and who used it daily to talk with his family. The key trick is the same in every case: decode the attempted movements of speaking, not abstract thoughts, and let a language model correct the likely errors, as a phone's predictive keyboard does.

Chapter 07

Closing the loop: putting touch back

Try picking up an egg with a numb hand. You will crush it or drop it, because grip depends on feeling. Early robotic arms controlled by BCIs had the same problem: the user could only watch.

Researchers at the University of Pittsburgh added a second set of electrodes, this time in the somatosensory cortex, the strip of brain that receives touch. When sensors on the robotic fingers registered pressure, small currents were sent to those electrodes. Their participant, Nathan Copeland, paralysed in a car accident, reported feeling pressure as if on his own fingers. In 2016 he used the arm to shake hands with President Barack Obama. In a 2021 study, adding this artificial touch roughly halved the time he needed for some grasping tasks.

A system that reads and writes at the same time is called bidirectional or closed-loop. It matters because the brain controls the body through constant feedback, not one-way commands. The sensations produced are still crude, more like buzzing or pressure than real texture. Writing detailed information into the brain is much harder than reading from it.

Chapter 08

The brain learns the machine, too

One surprise from monkey studies was that the brain adapts to the decoder. If the computer's translation is slightly wrong, the monkey's neurons gradually change their firing until the cursor goes where it should. The animal is learning a new skill, like learning to use a computer mouse, except the "hand" is a pattern of neural activity.

This two-way learning, called co-adaptation, is why users get better over weeks and months. Many describe a shift from consciously "trying" to just doing, the way a beginner driver eventually stops thinking about the pedals. It is a direct demonstration of the brain's plasticity, covered in Brain plasticity. A BCI is half engineering and half training.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Voice, movement, and the beginning of an industry

For a small group of people, BCIs are already life-changing. For everyone else, they raise the question of how much of the mind can be measured, and who will own the measurements.

Chapter 09

For people who are locked in

Imagine being fully aware, able to hear and see, but unable to move anything except perhaps your eyes. This is locked-in syndrome. It can follow a brainstem stroke, and it is where advanced ALS often leads. People in this state communicate, if at all, by blinking while someone else recites the alphabet. A sentence can take minutes.

For these people a BCI is not a gadget. It is the difference between being able to say "I am in pain, move my leg" and not being able to. It is the ability to tell a joke, to argue, to say goodbye. Many who have used research systems describe the return of communication as the return of their identity. The patients who need BCIs most are also the ones least able to complain when something goes wrong, which is one reason the ethics matter so much.

Chapter 10

Walking again through a digital bridge

In 2023 a team in Switzerland, led by the neuroscientist Grégoire Courtine and the neurosurgeon Jocelyne Bloch, reported a man called Gert-Jan Oskam. A cycling accident had injured his spinal cord and left his legs paralysed. The team placed recording devices over the movement areas of his brain and a stimulator on his spinal cord below the injury. A computer connected the two wirelessly.

When he intended to step, the stimulator activated the right muscles in the right order. He could walk with a walker, climb stairs and stand at a bar with friends. Some recovery of movement remained even when the system was switched off, which suggests the training helped damaged nerves regrow connections. The BCI did not move a robot. It bridged the gap in his own nervous system.

Chapter 11

From laboratories to companies

For twenty years almost all human BCI work was academic, and most of it used the Utah array, a square of 100 rigid needles about 4 millimetres across. The best-known research programme, called BrainGate, began implanting people in the United States in the mid-2000s. Since about 2020, private money has moved in.

Neuralink

Founded in 2016 by Elon Musk and others. Its implant uses about a thousand electrodes on 64 flexible threads, inserted by a surgical robot, with a wireless battery-powered unit sealed into the skull. The first human recipient, Noland Arbaugh, paralysed below the shoulders, received it in January 2024 and used it to play chess and video games. Some threads later pulled out of his brain, reducing performance until the software was adjusted. More people have been implanted since.

Synchron

Its Stentrode is threaded up a vein from the neck and opened like a stent against the vessel wall near the motor cortex. It needs no open brain surgery but records a much coarser signal. Users in Australia and the US have used it to text, email and shop online by thought, through simple "click" commands.

Other companies place thin films on the brain's surface or work on fully non-invasive systems. Investment brings speed, better engineering and the chance of devices that can be bought rather than borrowed from a lab. It also brings marketing. Company demonstrations are usually videos and press releases, not peer-reviewed studies, so they deserve the same caution as any claim made while raising money.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Remarkable gains, real risks, and a few uncomfortable questions

The benefits are concentrated in a small group of very disabled people. The risks include surgery, but also what happens to a person when a company or a research grant ends.

Chapter 13

The people left with a device nobody supports

The clearest warning comes from vision. A company called Second Sight made a retinal implant, the Argus II, that gave some blind people a crude sense of light and shapes. Several hundred people received it. Around 2020 the company ran into financial trouble and stopped supporting the device. Users found themselves with electronics inside their eyes that no one could repair or update. Some reportedly lost the use of it altogether.

That story raises a question every implant must answer. Who is responsible for a device in someone's body when the company or the study ends? Research participants usually consent to a study that lasts a few years, but the electrodes stay. Ethicists argue for long-term support commitments, open technical documentation and plans for removal.

A second question is about data. Brain recordings contain more than the signals a decoder uses. In 2021 Chile became the first country to write protection of "mental integrity" and brain data into its constitution. In 2024 the US states of Colorado and California added neural data to their privacy laws. Supporters call these neurorights. Critics argue that existing medical and privacy law already covers the risk, and that new rights may be premature. The debate is open, and it continues in Law · Privacy law.

Chapter 15

Why "mind reading" is the wrong picture

Every working BCI so far decodes something close to the body: the intention to move a hand, the attempted movements of the mouth. These signals are relatively easy because they are organised in known places with known maps. Abstract thought is different. A belief, a memory or a plan is not stored in one patch of cortex that an array could sample. It is spread across the brain in ways no one yet understands.

Non-invasive research has shown a little more. Studies using brain scanners combined with language models have produced rough paraphrases of stories a person was listening to or imagining. But these methods need hours of training in a scanner for each individual, and they fail if the person does not cooperate. The realistic near future is a medical tool, not a window into private thought.

The most useful habit is to ask of any BCI announcement: what exactly was decoded, in how many people, and was it published? That one question separates most real progress from most hype. The wider future of the field is the subject of The future of neuroscience.

The core idea

A BCI records brain activity, decodes it into an intention with a model trained for each person, and acts on it. It works because the motor cortex keeps producing movement commands after paralysis, and because a population of a few hundred neurons carries enough information to estimate them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A Utah array has 100 electrodes on a square about 4 mm across.
  • Handwriting BCI, 2021: about 90 characters a minute.
  • Speech BCIs, 2023: about 60–80 words a minute, against roughly 150 in normal conversation.
  • More than a million cochlear implants, the most successful neural interface, are in use worldwide.
The common mistake

Thinking BCIs read thoughts. They read trained, movement-related signals, and the person must actively try for them to work.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a speech BCI decodes attempted mouth movements rather than words in the head? If you can, you understand population coding and why motor signals are the easy ones.

The words, in plain words
Brain-computer interface (BCI)
A system that records brain activity and turns it into commands for a device, or delivers signals into the nervous system.
Motor cortex
The strip of brain across the top of the head that sends commands for voluntary movement.
Somatosensory cortex
The strip just behind it that receives touch and body sensation.
Spike
A brief electrical pulse by which a neuron sends a signal.
Population coding
Representing information, such as a movement direction, in the combined activity of many neurons.
Decoder
The software model that translates recorded brain activity into an intended action.
EEG
Electroencephalography: recording the brain's electrical activity from the scalp.
ECoG
Electrocorticography: recording from a sheet of electrodes on the brain's surface.
Utah array
A widely used implant of 100 tiny needles that records single neurons.
Closed-loop
A system that both reads from and writes to the nervous system, using feedback.
Co-adaptation
The process in which both the decoder and the user's brain adjust to each other over time.
Locked-in syndrome
Full awareness with almost complete paralysis, often with only eye movement left.
Neurorights
Proposed legal protections for brain data and mental privacy.
