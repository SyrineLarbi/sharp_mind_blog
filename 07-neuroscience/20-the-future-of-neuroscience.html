Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 20 of 20

The future of neuroscience. Drowning in data about the brain, and still waiting for the theory that explains it.

Neuroscience has never had better tools. Researchers can map every connection in a fly's brain, record thousands of neurons at once, read the genes active in a single cell and grow pieces of human brain tissue in a dish. Yet the deepest questions are still open. How does activity in neurons become a thought? Why does one brain develop depression and another not? This last topic in the category looks ahead. It asks where the field is going, what could change for patients and for society, and which promises deserve belief.

Question 1 · What is it?

A field that knows the parts better than the whole

"The future of neuroscience" means two things: the scientific questions the field is trying to answer next, and the effects those answers could have on medicine and society.

Chapter 01

Two big projects, two different bets

In 2013 two very large brain projects began within months of each other. In Europe, the Human Brain Project was launched with planned funding of around a billion euros over ten years. Its founder, the neuroscientist Henry Markram, had suggested that a detailed computer simulation of the human brain was possible within about a decade. In the United States, President Barack Obama announced the BRAIN Initiative, which set a more modest first goal: build better tools to record and map the brain.

The European project ran into trouble quickly. In 2014 hundreds of neuroscientists signed an open letter criticising its direction and management, and the project was restructured. It produced useful software platforms and brain atlases, and it ended in 2023 without a simulated human brain. The American project, by betting on tools, produced a wave of new techniques that labs around the world now use.

The contrast taught the field a lesson. You cannot simulate what you do not yet understand, and you cannot understand what you cannot measure. Most of neuroscience's near future is about measuring better. Its longer future depends on turning measurement into theory.

The pattern is clear. Knowledge is strongest at the bottom and the top, and weakest in the middle: the circuit level, where millions of neurons turn chemistry into computation. This is where most new tools are aimed.

Chapter 03

Why the brain is so hard

The first reason is size. About 86 billion neurons, each with thousands of connections, packed into a volume you could hold in two hands. The second is that the brain cannot be studied like a heart. You cannot easily take it apart in a living person, and animal brains differ from ours in important ways.

The third reason is subtler. In 2017 two researchers, Eric Jonas and Konrad Kording, took a classic microprocessor, the chip from early home computers, and applied standard neuroscience methods to it. They "lesioned" transistors, recorded their activity and looked for correlations with the games it ran. The methods produced plenty of results, but they did not reveal how the chip actually worked, even though engineers knew the answer exactly. The study was a gentle warning: collecting more data with the same methods is not enough. The field also needs better ideas about what to look for.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The tools that are changing the field

Five lines of work are likely to shape the next twenty years: wiring maps, cell atlases, recording at scale, lab-grown human tissue, and the use of AI to find patterns and build theories.

Chapter 04

Wiring maps: the connectome

A connectome is a complete map of every neuron and every connection in a nervous system. The first was published in 1986 for a tiny worm, C. elegans, which has 302 neurons. It took over a decade of tracing by hand through thin slices seen under an electron microscope. In 2024 an international project called FlyWire published the connectome of an entire adult fruit-fly brain: about 140,000 neurons and tens of millions of connections, traced with help from AI and thousands of hours of human checking.

Mammals are harder. In 2024 a team from Harvard and Google reconstructed a fragment of human cortex about one cubic millimetre in size, removed during epilepsy surgery. That tiny speck contained tens of thousands of cells and over a hundred million connections, and the data filled more than a million gigabytes. A similar map of a cubic millimetre of mouse visual cortex followed in 2025. A whole human brain is more than a million times larger.

The worm carries a warning. Its wiring has been known for forty years, and scientists still cannot fully predict its behaviour from it. Connections show who can talk to whom, but not how strongly, when, or under the influence of which chemicals. A wiring map is like a road map without traffic: essential, and not enough.

Chapter 05

Cell atlases: a parts list at last

For a century, neurons were classified mainly by shape, as Santiago Ramón y Cajal drew them in the 1890s. New methods now read which genes are switched on in each individual cell, cell by cell, for millions of cells. Cells with similar patterns are grouped into types.

In 2023 a large group of studies from the BRAIN Initiative described more than three thousand cell types in the human brain, and a separate atlas covered the whole mouse brain in similar detail. Many of these types had never been named. Some appear to exist mainly in humans and other primates. The brain has turned out to contain far more kinds of cells than textbooks suggested.

This matters for medicine. If a disease starts in one rare cell type, a parts list tells researchers where to look, and genetic tools may one day deliver treatment to that type alone. Many of the risk genes for schizophrenia and autism, for instance, are active in particular cell types at particular stages of development.

Chapter 06

Recording thousands of neurons at once

For most of the twentieth century, recording meant listening to one neuron, or a few, at a time. Today silicon probes can record hundreds of neurons simultaneously in a moving animal. Glowing indicator molecules let microscopes watch the activity of thousands of neurons across a surface of cortex. In recent years, a consortium of laboratories has recorded across almost the whole brain of mice performing the same decision task, pooling data in a way earlier generations of neuroscientists could not have imagined.

The result is a shift in thinking. Instead of asking what one neuron does, researchers increasingly study population activity, the pattern across a crowd of neurons over time. Often the interesting information is not in any single cell but in the shape of that collective pattern. This idea also powers brain-computer interfaces, described in Brain-computer interfaces.

Chapter 07

Growing human brain tissue in a dish

Many brain disorders are partly human. Mice do not get schizophrenia, and their brains develop differently. In 2013 Madeline Lancaster and Jürgen Knoblich in Vienna showed that human stem cells could be grown into small three-dimensional clusters of brain-like tissue, a few millimetres across. These are called brain organoids.

Organoids do not think. They lack blood vessels and the full structure of a brain. But they develop some of the same layers and cell types as a fetal brain. Researchers can grow them from the skin cells of a patient, turned back into stem cells, and watch how that person's genes affect development. In 2022 a team at Stanford transplanted human organoids into the brains of newborn rats, where they grew larger, connected to the rat's circuits and responded to touch on the rat's whiskers.

Such work raises questions that did not exist twenty years ago. At what point, if ever, could lab-grown human brain tissue have experiences? Most scientists think current organoids are very far from that. Ethicists argue that it is wise to agree on limits before the question becomes urgent, not after.

Chapter 08

AI as a microscope, and the need for theory

The new tools produce data at a scale no person can read. AI is already essential for tracing connectomes, sorting the signals of individual neurons and decoding brain recordings. Researchers are also training large models on brain data, much as language models are trained on text, in the hope that they will find general patterns. And trained networks are now used as theories of brain areas, as described in Artificial neural networks.

But AI can find patterns without explaining them. The field still needs theories: compact ideas that say why the brain is built the way it is. One example is predictive processing, the idea that the brain constantly predicts its incoming signals and mainly processes the errors. Another is the set of rival theories of consciousness. Since 2019 some of their supporters have agreed to adversarial collaborations: before running an experiment, both sides state what result would count against their own theory. The first big results, comparing two leading theories, challenged parts of both. The debate is covered in Consciousness.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The largest health burden, and some of the hardest questions about ourselves

Brain research matters because brain disorders are so common, because treatments have lagged, and because what we learn about the brain changes how we think about responsibility and self.

Chapter 09

The scale of the need

A major global analysis published in 2024 estimated that in 2021 more than 3 billion people, around 40 percent of humanity, were living with a neurological condition. These include stroke, migraine, dementia, epilepsy, nerve damage from diabetes and problems from premature birth. This made disorders of the nervous system the leading cause of ill health and disability in the world. Mental disorders such as depression and anxiety, counted separately, add hundreds of millions more.

Yet drug development for the brain has been unusually difficult. Many pharmaceutical companies cut back their psychiatry research in the 2010s after repeated failures. A major reason is that most psychiatric diagnoses are still made by describing symptoms, not by measuring a biological cause. Two people with the same diagnosis of depression may have quite different problems underneath. Better maps of cells and circuits are the field's best hope of changing this.

Chapter 10

New directions in treatment

Rethinking old drugs

Esketamine, a form of the anaesthetic ketamine, was approved as a nasal spray for treatment-resistant depression in the US in 2019. It works within hours rather than weeks, which suggested that depression involves faster-changing brain processes than older theories assumed. Psilocybin, the active compound in "magic mushrooms", is being tested in large trials, with some positive results. An application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD was rejected by US regulators in 2024, partly over trial design concerns.

Genetic medicine for the brain

Drugs that silence or replace faulty genes have begun to reach the nervous system. A gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy, a fatal inherited disease of motor neurons in babies, has changed its outlook since its US approval in 2019. Similar approaches are being tested for rarer forms of epilepsy, ALS and other genetic conditions. See Neurodegenerative diseases.

Devices are a third front, from adaptive deep brain stimulation to focused ultrasound, covered in Neurotechnology. The common thread is precision: treatments aimed at particular genes, cells or circuits rather than flooding the whole brain with a chemical.

Chapter 11

Beyond medicine: law, schools and the self

Brain science is already used outside hospitals. Defence lawyers in some countries have presented brain scans to argue for reduced responsibility. Courts have generally been cautious, and rightly so, because a scan can show a difference in a group but rarely proves what caused one person's act. As methods improve, the line between "my brain did it" and "I did it" will be argued more often, and the answers are philosophical as much as scientific.

Education is another area. Popular "neuromyths" spread widely in schools: that people are "left-brained" or "right-brained", or that we use only 10 percent of our brain. Neither is true. Real findings, such as the importance of sleep for memory and the value of spaced practice, are less exciting and more useful. And as neuroscience explains more of the mind in terms of cells, it pushes on old questions about free will and identity. Volume 52 gathers some of the classic arguments (Philosophy in the Original).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Great promise, real risks, and a hype problem

Here "advantages and disadvantages" means what the field's direction offers, set against what could go wrong, both scientifically and socially.

Chapter 13

The reliability problem

Neuroscience has its own version of the replication crisis that hit psychology. Many brain-imaging studies in the 2000s and 2010s used twenty or thirty participants. In 2022 a team led by Scott Marek and Nico Dosenbach analysed brain scans from tens of thousands of people. They found that links between individual brain differences and traits such as intelligence or mental health are usually small, and that reliably detecting them can require thousands of participants. Many earlier findings from small studies were probably exaggerated or wrong.

This is not a reason to distrust the whole field. It is a reason to prefer large, shared data sets, pre-registered studies, where methods are fixed before the data are seen, and replications. The habit for readers is simple. "Scientists find the brain region for X" is almost always an oversimplification. Ask how many people were studied, whether it has been repeated, and whether it shows cause or just association.

Chapter 15

How to follow the field from here

The safest forecast is that progress will be uneven. Tools and data will improve fast and steadily. Treatments will improve slowly, with long gaps between breakthroughs. Understanding of the mind's biggest puzzles may come in sudden jumps, or not for a long time. Expect more maps than explanations, and more explanations than cures.

Four questions help with any new announcement. Was it done in humans, animals, or a dish? How many subjects? Has anyone repeated it? Does it show what causes something, or only what goes with it? The general method is in Volume 50 (How to read the next announcement).

This category began with the structure of the brain, the parts and where they are. It ends here with the honest position of the field: we know a great deal about how the brain is built and far less about how it makes a mind. That gap is the most interesting open frontier in science, and it is likely to stay that way for a long time.

The core idea

Neuroscience's future is driven by new measurements: wiring maps, cell atlases, large-scale recording and human tissue models, all handled with AI. The weakest link is the circuit level and the lack of theory connecting cells to minds. Medical gains are likely to come through precision: earlier diagnosis and treatments aimed at specific genes, cells and circuits.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Worm connectome: 302 neurons (1986). Fly connectome: about 140,000 neurons (2024).
  • More than 3,000 cell types described in the human brain (2023).
  • More than 3 billion people lived with a neurological condition in 2021.
  • Reliable brain-trait links can need thousands of participants, not dozens.
The common mistake

Assuming that once we have a complete map of the brain, we will understand it. A map shows the roads, not the traffic, as forty years of the worm connectome show.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the Human Brain Project failed to simulate a brain while the BRAIN Initiative's tool-first approach paid off? If you can, you understand the gap between data and theory.

The words, in plain words
Connectome
A complete map of the neurons and connections in a nervous system.
Cell atlas
A catalogue of all the cell types in an organ, often defined by which genes they use.
Circuit
A group of connected neurons that together carry out a function.
Population activity
The combined pattern of firing across many neurons over time.
Brain organoid
A small cluster of brain-like tissue grown from human stem cells.
Stem cell
A cell that can develop into many other cell types.
Biomarker
A measurable sign of a disease, such as a protein in the blood.
Predictive processing
The theory that the brain constantly predicts incoming signals and learns from the errors.
Adversarial collaboration
A study designed jointly by rival camps, who agree in advance what would count against each theory.
Pre-registration
Publicly fixing a study's methods and analysis before collecting or seeing the data.
Neuromyth
A popular but false belief about the brain, such as "we use only 10 percent of it".
Precision medicine
Treatment aimed at the specific biological cause in a particular patient or group.
