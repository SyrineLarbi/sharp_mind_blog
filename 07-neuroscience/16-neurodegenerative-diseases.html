Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 16 of 20

Neurodegenerative diseases. Slow losses of brain cells that begin decades before anyone notices.

Most diseases attack tissue that can repair itself. The brain is different. Most of its neurons are with you from birth, and when they die they are mostly not replaced. A neurodegenerative disease is one in which particular groups of neurons die, slowly and in a predictable order, over years. Memory goes in one disease, movement in another, the ability to swallow in a third. They look like separate illnesses. Underneath, many of them share one strange mechanism: a protein that folds into the wrong shape and teaches its neighbours to do the same.

Question 1 · What is it?

A family of diseases defined by which cells die

Each disease is named after its symptoms, but it is better understood by asking two questions: which neurons are dying, and which protein is piling up inside or around them?

Chapter 01

A London doctor, a shaking hand, and a slow century of names

In 1817 a London surgeon called James Parkinson published a short book, An Essay on the Shaking Palsy. He described six people. Some were his patients. Others he had simply watched walking in the street. They had a trembling hand at rest, a bent posture, and small, hurried steps, as if they were always about to fall forward. Parkinson could not see inside their brains. He could only describe, carefully, a pattern that kept repeating. Some sixty years later the French neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot attached Parkinson's name to it.

Charcot also described, in 1869, a disease in which the nerves that drive the muscles slowly die: what we now call ALS. In 1872 the American doctor George Huntington described a hereditary movement disorder in families on Long Island. In 1906 the German psychiatrist Alois Alzheimer reported plaques and tangles in the brain of a woman with early dementia. Within one century, most of the family had been named.

Each was first recognised as a pattern of symptoms, then linked to damage seen in the tissue after death. Only in the last forty years has the third step arrived: finding the molecules behind the damage. That step revealed something none of these doctors could have guessed. The diseases they named separately are cousins.

Two words cause confusion. Dementia is not a disease. It is a description: a decline in thinking serious enough to interfere with daily life. Alzheimer's is the most common cause of dementia, responsible for most cases, but strokes, Lewy bodies and frontotemporal disease cause it too. ALS goes by different names: "motor neurone disease" in the UK, "Lou Gehrig's disease" in the US, after a baseball star who died of it in 1941.

Chapter 03

What makes a disease "neurodegenerative"

Three features define the group. First, the disease is progressive: it gets worse over time and, with today's treatments, does not reverse. Second, it involves the death of neurons, not just their malfunction. Third, it is usually selective. It hits particular cell populations first and spreads along connected pathways.

Compare a stroke. A stroke kills brain tissue in minutes when blood supply is cut, and then the damage stops. Volume 45 explains it (Stroke, the same disease in the brain). A neurodegenerative disease is the opposite: no single event, just a slow loss that keeps going. Multiple sclerosis sits on the border. It begins as an immune attack on the insulation around nerve fibres, but in its later stages it also causes steady nerve loss, so some researchers include it in the family.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Bad shapes, slow spread, and vulnerable cells

The details differ for every disease. But a common story has emerged over the past forty years, and it explains why these illnesses are so slow, so stubborn and so linked to age.

Chapter 04

Proteins that fold into the wrong shape

A protein is a long chain of building blocks that folds into a precise three-dimensional shape, like a length of wire bent into a paperclip. The shape is the function. A protein that folds wrongly cannot do its job, and worse, it tends to stick to other misfolded copies.

Cells have a quality-control system for this. Helper proteins refold mistakes, and disposal systems break down what cannot be saved. In a young, healthy cell this works well. Over decades it weakens. At some point misfolded proteins start to clump faster than the cell can clear them. They form small clusters, then fibres, then the large deposits that pathologists see under the microscope.

For years people assumed the large deposits were the killers. The current view is more subtle. The small, soluble clusters seem to be the most toxic form, and the large plaques and tangles may be partly a way of locking the poison away. That matters for treatment: clearing visible deposits does not automatically remove what is doing the damage.

Chapter 05

Spreading like a slow infection

The strangest part of the story began with sheep. A disease called scrapie, and a disease called kuru among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, spread from one individual to another, yet no virus or bacterium could be found. In the 1980s the American neurologist Stanley Prusiner argued that the infectious agent was a protein alone, which he called a prion. A prion is a misfolded protein that forces normal copies of the same protein to misfold. He was widely doubted, and he won the Nobel Prize in 1997.

Britain learned how real this was in the 1990s. Cattle had been fed meal made from other animals, and a prion disease, BSE or "mad cow disease", spread through the herds. People who ate infected beef developed a new human form, variant CJD. More than two hundred people worldwide are known to have died of it.

The key insight came next. Alzheimer's and Parkinson's are not infectious in daily life. But inside one brain, their proteins behave in a prion-like way. A misfolded tau or alpha-synuclein molecule can leave one neuron, enter a connected neuron, and seed misfolding there. In 2003 the German anatomist Heiko Braak described how Parkinson's damage seems to begin in the lower brainstem and the nerves of smell, and to climb upward over years. In a striking observation, some Parkinson's patients who had received transplants of healthy fetal neurons were found, many years later, to have Lewy bodies in the transplanted cells too. The disease had moved into the new tissue.

Chapter 06

Why some neurons die first

If misfolding happened everywhere equally, every disease would look the same. Instead each disease has a favourite target. Researchers call this selective vulnerability, and the explanation is partly about energy and size.

The dopamine neurons that die in Parkinson's are extraordinary cells. Each one sends out a hugely branched fibre with an enormous number of connection points, and it fires steadily all day. That costs a great deal of energy, which strains the cell's power stations, the mitochondria, and produces damaging by-products. Motor neurons, the cells lost in ALS, can have fibres about a metre long, running from the spinal cord to the foot. Cells that are large, busy and long-lived have the least room for error.

Other cells join in. The brain's immune cells, called microglia, normally clean up debris. In these diseases they can become chronically activated, and the inflammation they produce seems to speed the damage. Several genes that raise Alzheimer's risk are active mainly in microglia, which has made the brain's immune system one of the busiest areas of research.

Chapter 07

Genes: one certainty, many nudges

Huntington's disease is the clearest case in medicine of a single gene deciding a fate. The gene contains a short three-letter code, CAG, repeated over and over. Most people have fewer than about thirty-six repeats. People with around forty or more will develop the disease if they live long enough. Each child of an affected parent has a one in two chance of inheriting it. Much of what we know came from large families around Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela, studied over decades by a team led by Nancy Wexler, whose own mother had the disease. The gene was found in 1993.

Most neurodegenerative disease is not like that. A minority of cases run in families through a single strong gene. Most are called sporadic: they arise from many small genetic nudges combined with age and environment. The best-known nudge is a gene variant called APOE4. Carrying one copy raises Alzheimer's risk several-fold. Carrying two raises it much more. Yet many people with two copies never develop the disease, and many people with none do. A risk gene changes the odds, not the outcome.

Chapter 08

The silent decades

By the time someone notices a tremor from Parkinson's, a large share of their dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra have already gone. In Alzheimer's, amyloid starts to collect in the brain ten to twenty years before memory problems appear. The brain hides the damage for a long time by rerouting work through surviving circuits.

This hidden reserve explains a puzzling finding. People with more education, more demanding jobs and richer social lives can carry a lot of pathology in their brains with fewer symptoms. The idea is called cognitive reserve. It does not stop the disease, but it seems to delay the point at which it shows.

The silent period also leaves early clues. Many people with Parkinson's lost their sense of smell, or began acting out their dreams in their sleep, years before any tremor. Acting out dreams in this way has a name, REM sleep behaviour disorder, and a large proportion of people who have it go on to develop Parkinson's or a related disease. The disease starts long before the diagnosis.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The price of living longer

Age is the biggest single risk factor for almost all of these diseases. Humanity's great success, longer lives, has made them one of its great problems.

Chapter 09

The scale

The World Health Organization estimated that about 57 million people were living with dementia in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases a year. Alzheimer's accounts for roughly 60 to 70 percent of them. Parkinson's affected more than 8.5 million people in 2019, and it is one of the fastest-growing neurological conditions in the world. ALS and Huntington's are much rarer, but they are among the most feared diagnoses in medicine.

The growth is mostly about age. The risk of dementia roughly doubles every five years after the age of 65. As populations in Asia, Latin America and Africa grow older, most people with dementia now live in low- and middle-income countries, where diagnosis and care are hardest to get. The demographic side is explored in Society · Aging populations.

Chapter 10

The hidden workforce of carers

The WHO put the global cost of dementia at about US$1.3 trillion in 2019. The striking part is where that money goes. Around half of it is the value of unpaid care by family and friends, mostly women. A daughter who cuts her working hours, a husband who becomes a full-time nurse at eighty: that is the main way most societies currently cope.

These diseases are hard to care for because they are long. Someone with Alzheimer's may live with the disease for many years after diagnosis, needing more help each year. Someone with ALS faces a shorter course, typically two to five years from the first symptoms, but needs intense support with breathing, feeding and communication. The physicist Stephen Hawking, diagnosed at 21 and living to 76, was a famous exception, not the rule. The disease affects one brain, but it reorganises a whole family.

Chapter 11

What medicine can actually do today

Treatment falls into two kinds. Symptomatic treatments make up for what is lost without slowing the disease. Disease-modifying treatments try to slow the loss itself. Until recently, almost everything available was the first kind.

Parkinson's: the great symptomatic success

In the late 1950s the Swedish pharmacologist Arvid Carlsson showed that dopamine was a brain chemical linked to movement. By the late 1960s high doses of levodopa, which the brain turns into dopamine, were transforming patients' lives. It is still the main treatment. For some people, deep brain stimulation, thin electrodes implanted to calm faulty circuits, adds years of better movement. Neither stops the cells dying.

Alzheimer's: from modest to modestly more

Older drugs boost a memory chemical, acetylcholine, and help some people a little for a while. Since 2023, antibody drugs that clear amyloid, lecanemab and donanemab, have been approved in the US and several other countries for early disease. In trials they slowed decline by roughly a quarter to a third over about 18 months. They carry risks of brain swelling and small bleeds.

ALS has a small first: in 2023 US regulators approved tofersen, a drug designed to silence one faulty gene, SOD1, which causes about 2 percent of ALS cases. It helps only that small group. But it showed that a drug written against a specific gene can reach the nervous system and change the disease. That is a template for much of what comes next.

Chapter 13

Why so many trials have failed

For decades, drug trials in these diseases failed more often than in almost any other area of medicine. Researchers now point to four reasons, and each one teaches something about the biology.

Too late. Most trials enrolled people who already had clear symptoms. By then, as Chapter 08 showed, a large part of the vulnerable cells were gone. Treating at that point is like fitting a smoke alarm after the house has burned. Too mixed. Two people with the same label can have different processes underneath. Many people diagnosed with Alzheimer's also have Lewy bodies, TDP-43 or damaged blood vessels. A drug aimed at one process may help only some of them, and the benefit is diluted. Too hard to reach. The brain is protected by the blood-brain barrier, a tight wall of cells around its blood vessels. It keeps out most large molecules, including most antibodies, so only a tiny fraction of a dose arrives. Too hard to measure. These diseases change slowly, so trials must be long and large to show any difference.

The best-known argument in the field is about Alzheimer's and amyloid. Is amyloid the first domino that knocks over all the others, or one factor among several? The question is still contested. The modest success of the antibody drugs has been read as support by one side and as a warning by the other. The full debate, including a controversial approval in 2021 and a retracted paper in 2024, is in Medicine · Alzheimer's disease. The broader lesson holds across the family: most researchers now expect these diseases to need combinations of treatments, given early, as cancer does.

Chapter 15

The lever that already works: lowering risk

The most useful finding of the past decade is not a drug. In 2024 a commission of experts writing in the medical journal The Lancet estimated that around 45 percent of dementia cases could in principle be prevented or delayed by dealing with fourteen risk factors. They include high blood pressure, hearing loss, untreated vision loss, smoking, physical inactivity, high cholesterol, diabetes, air pollution, depression, social isolation, excess alcohol and head injury, plus low education in early life.

The figure is a model, not a promise, and it rests on observational studies. But its direction is supported by a quieter fact: in several rich countries, the age-specific risk of dementia has fallen over recent decades, probably thanks to better education and heart health. The total number of cases still rises, because there are many more old people.

The practical message overlaps with heart health almost entirely. What protects the blood vessels protects the brain. Volume 42 makes the same point from the patient's side, and the next big change may not be a cure but a world in which these diseases arrive later, so that more people die with early changes in their brain than of them.

The core idea

Neurodegenerative diseases are slow, progressive deaths of specific neurons. Many share one mechanism: a misfolded protein that clumps, seeds misfolding in neighbours and spreads along connected circuits. The damage begins years or decades before symptoms, which is why treatment started at diagnosis struggles.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 57 million people lived with dementia in 2021 (WHO), with around 10 million new cases a year.
  • Alzheimer's causes roughly 60–70% of dementia.
  • New antibody drugs slow early Alzheimer's by roughly a quarter to a third over about 18 months.
  • Around 45% of dementia may be preventable or delayable through 14 risk factors (Lancet Commission, 2024).
The common mistake

Thinking dementia is a disease, or a normal part of ageing. It is neither. It is a set of symptoms with several possible causes, and most old people never get it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a drug that clears plaques might still help patients only a little? If you can, you have understood soluble clumps, spread and the silent decades together.

APOE4
A common gene variant that raises the risk of Alzheimer's without guaranteeing it.
Sporadic
Occurring without a single inherited cause. Most cases of these diseases are sporadic.
Cognitive reserve
The brain's capacity to keep working well despite damage, built partly by education and activity.
Disease-modifying
