Language. The most human skill runs on a network, not on a single spot.

You are doing something extraordinary right now. Marks on a screen are turning into sounds you do not hear, words you did not look up and meanings you did not work out on purpose, at a few words a second. A child learns to do the spoken version without lessons. For 150 years the textbook answer to "where does language live?" was two small patches on the left side of the brain. That answer was a brilliant start and is now known to be incomplete. This file explains what the patches do, what the wider network does, and what happens when it breaks.

Question 1 · What is it?

Two famous patients and a system with many parts

The neuroscience of language began with people who lost it in different ways. Their injuries showed that language is not one ability but several.

Chapter 01

The man who could only say "tan"

In 1861 a French surgeon, Paul Broca, examined a patient at a Paris hospital called Louis Victor Leborgne. For years Leborgne had been able to say only one syllable, "tan", so the staff called him "Tan". He understood what people said to him and answered with gestures and tone of voice. His intelligence seemed largely intact. Only the ability to produce words was gone.

Leborgne died a few days later, and Broca examined his brain. He found damage in the lower part of the left frontal lobe, towards the side of the head just above the temple. After more cases, Broca announced in 1865 that "we speak with the left hemisphere". The area is still called Broca's area. It was one of the first convincing proofs that a specific mental ability depends on a specific part of the brain.

Leborgne's brain is still preserved in Paris. When researchers scanned it with MRI in 2007, they found the damage reached much deeper than Broca could see from the surface. That detail matters: the neat story of one small area was always a simplification.

Chapter 02

The opposite problem, and the first map

In 1874 a young German doctor, Carl Wernicke, described patients with the reverse pattern. They spoke fluently, with normal rhythm, but their sentences were full of wrong or invented words and made little sense. They also understood speech poorly, and often did not realise anything was wrong. The damage was further back, in the upper part of the left temporal lobe near the ear. This became Wernicke's area.

Wernicke then predicted a third kind of failure. If the bundle of fibres connecting the two areas were cut, a person should understand and speak, but be unable to repeat what they heard. Such patients were found. Language disorders after brain damage are called aphasias.

Chapter 03

Language is not the same as speech

A strong test of what these areas do comes from sign languages. British Sign Language and American Sign Language are full languages with their own grammar, not mimed versions of English. Deaf signers who have a stroke in the left hemisphere can develop sign aphasia, with signs that are halting or jumbled, even though they can still move their hands normally for other tasks. The left hemisphere handles language itself, not just sound or mouth movement.

Linguists break language into layers, and the brain seems to handle them partly separately. Phonology is the sound system: which sounds count as different in a language. Vocabulary is the stored set of words and their meanings. Syntax is grammar, the rules for combining words so that "dog bites man" differs from "man bites dog". Semantics is meaning. Pragmatics is use in context, such as knowing that "can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a question about your abilities.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Hearing, predicting, speaking and reading

Modern imaging replaced the two-area map with a network of streams and hubs, most of them on the left, working in parallel and always guessing ahead.

Chapter 04

From sound to meaning: two streams

Speech is a messy physical signal. There are no gaps between most spoken words; you hear them only because your brain knows the language. Listen to an unfamiliar language and it sounds like one long stream. The sound first reaches the auditory cortex on the upper surface of each temporal lobe. From there, according to an influential model proposed by Gregory Hickok and David Poeppel, it splits into two routes.

The dual-stream model of speech (simplified, left hemisphere)
                         FRONTAL LOBE                      PARIETAL
                     (Broca's area, motor)  <-----  dorsal stream  ----+
                         planning speech      "how do I say it?"       |
                                                                       |
  ear --> AUDITORY CORTEX (upper temporal lobe) -----------------------+
                   |
                   +---- ventral stream ----> middle and lower TEMPORAL LOBE
                         "what does it mean?"    word meanings, concepts
                         (partly on both sides)

The ventral stream (lower route) runs forward and down through the temporal lobe and turns sounds into meaning. The dorsal stream (upper route) runs back and up towards the parietal and frontal lobes and links sounds to the movements needed to say them. It is what you use to repeat a new word or a foreign name. The repetition failure that Wernicke predicted fits this route being cut.

Chapter 05

The brain is always guessing the next word

In 1980 Marta Kutas and Steven Hillyard recorded brain waves through the scalp while people read sentences one word at a time. Some sentences ended strangely, such as "He spread the warm bread with socks." About 400 milliseconds after the odd word, a clear negative wave appeared. It is called the N400, and its size tracks how unexpected a word is in its context. A word that is unusual but fits ("He spread the warm bread with honey") gives a smaller wave than one that makes no sense.

The lesson is that listening and reading are not passive. The brain predicts what is coming and spends its effort on the surprises. That is why you can follow a friend in a noisy café and why a missing word is often "heard" anyway. It is also why researchers became interested in large language models, which are trained to predict the next word. Several studies have found that the internal activity of these models predicts human brain responses to sentences surprisingly well. That does not mean the brain works like a chatbot, but prediction appears to be central to both.

Chapter 06

Speaking: from idea to muscle in a fraction of a second

Normal conversation runs at around two to three words a second. For each word, the brain must pick a concept, find the matching word among tens of thousands, retrieve its sounds in order, and send precise commands to dozens of muscles in the lips, tongue, jaw, throat and chest. Models of speech production, such as the one developed by Willem Levelt, describe these as overlapping stages.

The stages show up in mistakes. In a tip-of-the-tongue state you have the meaning and often the first sound or the number of syllables, but not the whole word: stage two worked, stage three stalled. In slips of the tongue, sounds swap between words, as in "a blushing crow" for "a crushing blow". The swaps follow the rules of the language, so the brain must be planning several words ahead and assembling sounds into slots.

Chapter 07

Left and right: a division of labour

In most people the left hemisphere is dominant for language. This is true for well over nine in ten right-handed people and for a clear majority of left-handed people too, though left-handers more often have language on the right or on both sides. Before brain surgery, doctors used to check this with the Wada test, which briefly puts one hemisphere to sleep with a drug so they can see whether the patient can still speak. Functional MRI is now often used instead.

The right hemisphere is far from silent. It contributes to prosody, the melody and stress of speech that tells you whether "really" is surprise or sarcasm. It helps with jokes, metaphors and following the thread of a story. People with right-hemisphere damage may speak in perfect sentences yet miss the point of an ironic remark. The left builds the sentence; the right helps you understand what the speaker means by it.

Chapter 08

Reading: a new skill in an old brain

Writing was invented only about 5,000 years ago, far too recently for evolution to have built a reading area. Yet in skilled readers of every writing system studied, a small patch on the underside of the left temporal lobe responds strongly to written words. The French neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene called it the visual word form area. His explanation is "neuronal recycling": learning to read takes over part of the brain's system for recognising objects and faces and retrains it for letters.

Reading also has to be connected to the spoken-language network. Dyslexia, a common and lifelong difficulty with reading, is mostly not a problem of seeing letters backwards, as people often think. In most cases it involves processing the sounds inside words. That is why the best-supported teaching methods train the link between letters and sounds directly.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Childhood, recovery, and what language is for

The brain science of language guides how children are taught, how stroke patients recover, and how we should think about machines that talk.

Chapter 09

Babies are citizens of the world, briefly

Young babies can tell apart speech sounds from any language, including contrasts their parents cannot hear. Studies by Janet Werker, Patricia Kuhl and others showed that this ability narrows in the second half of the first year. By around their first birthday, babies have become specialists in the sounds of the languages around them and lose sensitivity to others. That is why adult Japanese speakers often find English "r" and "l" hard to separate.

There appears to be a sensitive period for language: learning is easiest in childhood and harder later, especially for accent and some grammar. Adults can still learn new languages very well, and they learn vocabulary quickly. But children who are cut off from all language in their early years, such as some deaf children never exposed to sign language, may struggle with grammar for life. The practical message is that early exposure to rich language, spoken or signed, matters.

Chapter 10

Aphasia and the long road back

General education

This is not medical advice. Sudden difficulty speaking or understanding is a possible sign of stroke and an emergency (see Vol 45 · Stroke).

Aphasia is common after stroke, affecting a large share of survivors. It is often devastating because intelligence is usually intact: the person knows exactly what they want to say and cannot say it, which others can easily mistake for confusion. Recovery can continue for months and years. The brain reorganises around the damage, with surviving left-hemisphere areas and sometimes the right hemisphere taking over parts of the work. Intensive speech and language therapy helps, and more practice tends to help more.

One old clinical observation is moving. Some people with severe non-fluent aphasia can sing the words of familiar songs that they cannot speak. Singing seems to rely more on right-hemisphere networks and on stored sequences. Therapies built on rhythm and melody try to use this route.

Chapter 11

Language is not the same as thinking

The neuroscientist Evelina Fedorenko and colleagues mapped a "language network" in individual people with fMRI. It responds strongly to sentences in any language the person knows. But it is largely quiet during mental arithmetic, logic puzzles, music or understanding computer code. Patients with severe global aphasia have been shown to solve arithmetic and reasoning problems, and some play chess.

This has a modern consequence. Fluent language and good reasoning are separable in the human brain, so fluency alone is weak evidence of understanding. That applies to people who speak confidently and to AI systems that produce smooth text. Vol 49 explores what this means for chatbots (What a language model actually does).

Chapter 13

The long argument: instinct or learning?

The biggest debate about language is how much is built in. Two broad positions compete, and the truth may include both.

Built-in capacity

The linguist Noam Chomsky argued from the 1950s that children learn grammar too fast and from too little evidence to be learning it from scratch. Humans, on this view, have an inborn "universal grammar". Supporters point to how reliably every human community develops full languages, including new sign languages invented by deaf children.

Learned from use

Researchers such as Michael Tomasello argue that children build grammar from patterns in what they hear, helped by general learning and a strong drive to understand other people. Supporters point to the variety of world languages and to statistical learning in infants. AI models that learn grammar from text have added fuel to this side, though children learn from far less data.

Genetics adds a caution against simple answers. A family in London, known as the KE family, had an inherited speech and language disorder traced in 2001 to a gene called FOXP2. It was quickly called "the language gene". It is not. FOXP2 regulates many other genes, helps with learned movement sequences, and is found in other animals, including birds that learn songs. Language depends on many genes and many brain areas, none of which is language on its own.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Giving voices back, and reading words from the brain

Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 14

Three directions, three levels of confidence

The quiet change is in understanding. Large language models have given researchers a new tool for testing theories, because a model can be examined in ways a brain cannot. They have also sharpened an old question. If a system can produce language without a human brain's other abilities, what exactly was language doing for us? Neuroscience's answer so far is that language is how brains share thoughts, not where the thoughts are made.

The core idea

Language depends on a mostly left-hemisphere network, not two spots. The Broca's area region helps produce and structure speech, the Wernicke's region helps understanding, and two streams link sound to meaning and to movement. The brain predicts words as it goes, and language is separable from reasoning.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Broca described "Tan" in 1861; Wernicke his patients in 1874.
  • The N400 brain wave appears about 400 milliseconds after an unexpected word.
  • Writing is only about 5,000 years old, so reading recycles older circuits.
The common mistake

Treating fluent speech as proof of clear thinking, or aphasia as loss of intelligence. Language and thought use different networks, and either can fail alone.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a person with Wernicke's aphasia talks fluently but makes little sense, while a person with Broca's aphasia makes sense but struggles to talk?
