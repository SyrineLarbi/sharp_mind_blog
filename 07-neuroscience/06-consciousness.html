Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 06 of 20

Consciousness. The one thing you know for certain, and the one thing science cannot yet explain.

Right now there is something it is like to be you. Light hits your eyes and you see colour. A word on this line has a meaning that you feel as well as process. Every other fact about the world reaches you through that experience. Yet nobody can say how a wet, electrical organ produces it, or which other creatures, or machines, have it too. Neuroscience has made real progress on where and when consciousness happens in the brain. On why it happens at all, the experts still disagree, sometimes sharply. This file sets out what is known, what is argued, and why it matters in hospitals today.

Question 1 · What is it?

Being awake, being aware, and the problem underneath

The word "consciousness" covers several different things. Separating them is the first step to thinking clearly about any of them.

Chapter 01

Three meanings of one word

When a doctor says a patient is "conscious", they usually mean the patient is awake and responding. That is the level of consciousness. It runs from alert wakefulness, through drowsiness and sleep, down to anaesthesia and coma.

When a scientist studies what you are conscious of, they mean the content of consciousness. At this moment, you are aware of these words, but probably not of the pressure of your chair until this sentence mentioned it. The brain was processing that pressure all along. It just was not in your experience.

There is a third meaning: self-awareness, knowing that you are the one having the experience, and being able to think about your own thoughts. Many animals may have experiences without much self-awareness. Most confusion in debates about consciousness comes from people using different meanings without noticing. This file is mostly about the first two.

Chapter 02

"What it is like", and the hard problem

In 1974 the American philosopher Thomas Nagel published an essay called "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" Bats find their way by echolocation, sensing the echoes of their own calls. We can learn everything about how that works and still have no idea what it feels like from the inside. Nagel gave the field its most quoted definition.

“An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism — something it is like for the organism.”

What Is It Like to Be a Bat? · Thomas Nagel, 1974

In 1995 the Australian philosopher David Chalmers sharpened the point. He separated the "easy problems" of consciousness from the "hard problem". The easy problems are explaining how the brain tells sleep from waking, focuses attention, combines the senses and reports what it perceives. They are not easy at all, but they are the normal kind of scientific problem: explain a function by its mechanism. The hard problem is different. Why does any of that processing feel like something, instead of happening "in the dark"?

Philosophers call the felt qualities of experience, the redness of red or the hurt of pain, qualia. Whether the hard problem is a real problem, a temporary one, or a confusion is itself one of the field's central disagreements.

Most working neuroscientists are physicalists of some kind. They assume consciousness is something the brain does, and set out to find out how. That assumption guides the research, but it has not yet answered the hard problem. The rest of this file follows the neuroscience, while noting where the philosophy bites.

Question 2 · How does it work?

What science has found, and the theories that compete to explain it

Science cannot yet say why experience exists. It has learned a great deal about which brain systems keep it switched on, and what changes when something enters awareness.

Chapter 04

The switch: what keeps you awake and aware

The level of consciousness depends on a chain of structures. Clusters of neurons in the brainstem send arousal signals upwards. The thalamus, the relay at the brain's centre, keeps the cortex active and connected. And the cortex provides the content. Damage to a small area of the upper brainstem can cause coma, while much larger damage to the cortex may leave a person awake but changed. (The anatomy is in Structure of the brain.)

Locked-in syndrome shows why outward behaviour can mislead. The French magazine editor Jean-Dominique Bauby had a brainstem stroke in 1995 and could move only his left eyelid. He dictated a whole book, The Diving Bell and the Butterfly, one letter at a time by blinking. Being unable to respond is not the same as being unaware.

Chapter 05

Anaesthesia: switching it off every day

Every day, a huge number of operations around the world depend on drugs that reliably and reversibly remove consciousness. Many of them work by strengthening the brain's main "stop" signal, GABA (see Neurotransmitters), but others work quite differently, and how all of them end awareness is still not fully understood.

What researchers do see is a change in communication. Under anaesthesia, local brain areas may still respond to a sound or touch, but the signals stop spreading and being integrated across the cortex. The brain's regions stop talking to each other in a coordinated way. Consciousness seems to depend less on any single area being active than on large areas sharing information. That idea appears in almost every theory below.

Awareness during general anaesthesia is rare. A large UK audit published in 2014 found reported cases in roughly one in 19,000 general anaesthetics, with higher rates when muscle-relaxing drugs were used.

Chapter 06

Seeing without knowing it

In the 1970s the Oxford psychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz studied a patient known as D.B., who was blind in part of his visual field because of damage to his visual cortex. D.B. insisted he saw nothing there. Yet when asked to guess where a light was, or which way a line was tilted, he was correct far more often than chance. Weiskrantz called this blindsight.

Blindsight shows that the brain can use visual information that never reaches experience. This gives scientists a powerful method. If you can keep the stimulus the same while awareness changes, any brain activity that changes with awareness is a candidate for what consciousness requires.

Healthy people can be put in similar situations. A picture flashed for a few hundredths of a second, followed at once by a pattern, is often not seen at all, yet it can still affect choices. If each eye is shown a different image, people see one, then the other, alternating every few seconds, although the input never changes. In 1990 Francis Crick, co-discoverer of DNA's structure, and Christof Koch proposed searching for the neural correlates of consciousness: the minimal brain activity that goes with a particular experience. Much of the field still follows that plan.

Chapter 07

The decision you made before you knew it

In a famous 1983 experiment, Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it and to note, on a fast clock, the moment they felt the urge. Electrodes on the scalp showed a slow build-up of brain activity, the "readiness potential", starting several hundred milliseconds before the moment people reported deciding.

Many took this as proof that free will is an illusion. The interpretation is contested. In 2012 Aaron Schurger and colleagues argued that the build-up may reflect random background fluctuations that tip a choice only when people have no reason to pick one moment over another. A wrist flick at an arbitrary moment is also very different from a real decision. What the experiment does show is more modest and still striking: much of the brain's work, including the start of some actions, happens before or outside awareness.

Chapter 08

The leading theories

There are more than a dozen scientific theories of consciousness. Four are the most discussed.

Global workspace theory

Proposed by Bernard Baars in 1988 and developed by Stanislas Dehaene. Most processing is unconscious and local. Information becomes conscious when it wins a competition and is "broadcast" across a network linking the frontal and parietal cortex, so that many systems can use it at once. Brain scans show a sudden, widespread "ignition" when a stimulus is consciously seen.

Integrated information theory

Proposed by Giulio Tononi in 2004. Consciousness is integrated information: how much a system as a whole carries beyond what its parts carry separately, measured by a quantity called phi. It predicts that the back of the cortex matters most, and implies that some simple physical systems have a little consciousness.

Higher-order theories

A mental state is conscious when the brain represents it to itself: not just "red here", but a further state registering "I am seeing red". These theories give the prefrontal cortex a key role.

Predictive processing

The brain constantly predicts its own sensory input and corrects the predictions. Experience is the brain's best guess, a "controlled hallucination" in a phrase popularised by Anil Seth. This connects closely to how perception is built (Volume 27).

Each theory explains some findings well. None has yet bridged the gap from "this activity goes with experience" to "this is why it feels like something".

Chapter 09

A bet, a contest, and an angry letter

In 1998 Christof Koch bet David Chalmers a case of fine wine that within 25 years scientists would clearly identify the neural signature of consciousness. In June 2023, at a conference in New York, Koch conceded and paid up.

At the same meeting came the first results of an unusual project. Supporters of global workspace theory and of integrated information theory had agreed in advance on experiments whose results would count against each theory. Several laboratories ran them. The results, published in full in 2025, challenged key predictions of both. The expected "ignition" when an image disappeared from view was weak, against global workspace theory. The back of the cortex did not keep its activity synchronised in the way integrated information theory predicted.

Soon after, more than a hundred researchers signed an open letter calling integrated information theory "pseudoscience". Many others, including critics of the theory, said the label was unfair and harmful. The episode shows a field that has strong data and no agreement on what it means. Testing theories against each other in advance is likely to become standard.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Patients, animals and machines

Who is conscious is not an abstract question. It decides how we treat patients who cannot respond, which animals we protect, and how we will treat future machines.

Chapter 10

The woman who played tennis in her head

In 2006 the British neuroscientist Adrian Owen and colleagues published a study of a young woman who had been in an unresponsive state for five months after a road accident. Lying in a brain scanner, she was asked to imagine playing tennis, and then to imagine walking through her house. The first instruction activated a movement-planning area. The second activated navigation areas. Her pattern matched that of healthy volunteers. She was following instructions, inside her head, with no outward sign at all.

In 2010 the method was used to let one patient answer yes-or-no questions: imagine tennis for yes, the house for no. In 2024 a large international study found that roughly one in four patients who showed no outward response could follow commands in this hidden way, detected by brain scans or EEG recordings.

The consequences are serious. Decisions about pain relief, rehabilitation and withdrawing life support may depend on whether a patient is aware. Some medical guidelines now recommend these tests for patients with severe brain injury, though they are not available everywhere.

Chapter 11

Which animals?

No test can prove another being is conscious, not even another human. We infer it from behaviour, from similar brains, and from evolution. Using those clues, the scientific view has widened steadily. In 2012 a group of scientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, stating that mammals, birds and some other animals have the brain systems needed for conscious experience.

In 2022 the United Kingdom extended animal welfare law to octopuses, crabs and lobsters, after an expert review found strong evidence that they can feel pain. In 2024 a group of researchers signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, saying there is at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates and in many invertebrates, including insects. The burden of proof is shifting. Where there is a realistic chance of suffering, many now argue, it should be taken into account, even without certainty.

Chapter 12

Could a machine be conscious?

Large language models can talk fluently about their feelings. That proves little, because they were trained on huge amounts of human writing about feelings (see What a language model actually does). The question is whether anything is going on inside.

In 2023 a group of neuroscientists, philosophers and AI researchers published a report that took the main scientific theories and turned each into a checklist of features to look for in an AI system. They concluded that no current system was a strong candidate for consciousness, but that there seemed to be no obvious technical barrier to building one that met more of the indicators. Whether meeting them would mean real experience depends on which theory is right, which is the unresolved problem from Chapter 09. Some researchers argue that consciousness needs a living body that regulates itself. Others think the right computations are enough.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What consciousness buys, and what it costs

If most of the brain's work is unconscious, why have consciousness at all? Its strengths and weaknesses give some clues.

Chapter 13

Strengths and limits of being aware

Feature of conscious processingStrengthWeakness
IntegrationCombines senses, memory and goals into one picture you can act onThe picture is a construction and can be wrong, as illusions show
FlexibilityHandles new situations that habits cannotSlow: conscious reasoning takes seconds where reflexes take milliseconds
Narrow focusConcentrates resources on what matters nowTiny capacity: people miss large changes in plain view when attention is elsewhere

Felt experiencePleasure, meaning and value; arguably everything that makes life matterSuffering: pain, grief and fear exist only because experience does

The table suggests a working answer to "what is consciousness for?". It seems to be the brain's way of handling what is new, important or conflicting, by putting it where all systems can work on it together. Routine tasks are handed to unconscious processes as soon as they can be. You notice your driving only when something unexpected happens.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Better measures, sharper tests, and the question that may remain

Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Three directions, three levels of confidence

DirectionWhere it standsHonest outlook

The most practical pressure may come from outside neuroscience. As AI systems become more capable and more humanlike, societies will have to decide how to treat them before the science is settled. Questions about consciousness are moving from philosophy seminars into law, medicine and technology policy, which is why it is worth understanding the arguments now.

The core idea

Consciousness has a level (awake to coma) and a content (what you are aware of). The level depends on the brainstem, thalamus and cortex working together; content seems to need information shared widely across the cortex. Science can map these correlates, but the hard problem, why any of it feels like something, remains open.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 1 in 4 unresponsive patients showed hidden command-following in a 2024 study.
  • Reported awareness under general anaesthesia: about 1 in 19,000 (UK audit, 2014).
  • Libet's readiness potential began several hundred milliseconds before the reported urge to move.
The common mistake

Treating "no response" as "no awareness". Locked-in patients and some unresponsive patients are aware, and behaviour alone cannot tell you.

The words, in plain words
Level of consciousness
How awake and responsive a person is, from alert to coma.
Content of consciousness
What a person is aware of at a given moment.
Hard problem
The question of why brain processes are experienced at all, rather than happening without feeling.
Qualia
The felt qualities of experience, such as how red looks or how pain hurts.
Neural correlates of consciousness
The minimal brain activity that goes with a particular conscious experience.
Blindsight
Responding correctly to things in a blind area of vision without seeing them.
Unresponsive wakefulness
A state with open eyes and sleep cycles but no outward sign of awareness.
Locked-in syndrome
Full awareness with near-total paralysis, often after a brainstem stroke.
Global workspace
A theory that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely across the brain.
Integrated information
A theory that consciousness is the amount of information a system integrates as a whole.
Readiness potential
A slow build-up of brain activity recorded before a voluntary movement.
Panpsychism
The view that some simple form of experience is a basic feature of matter.
