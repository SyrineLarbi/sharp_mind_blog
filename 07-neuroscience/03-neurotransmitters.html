Neurotransmitters. The brain's chemical vocabulary, and every drug that speaks it.

Coffee, a glass of wine, a painkiller after surgery, an antidepressant, a cigarette, the anaesthetic before an operation. These look like unrelated substances. They all work in the same place: the tiny gap between two nerve cells, where one cell passes its message to the next with a chemical. The brain uses dozens of these chemicals, and each drug that changes the mind does it by copying, blocking, or adjusting one of them. Learning the handful that matter most explains a surprising amount of medicine, addiction and everyday life.

Question 1 · What is it?

A small set of chemicals that do most of the talking

More than a hundred substances can carry signals between neurons. A handful do most of the work, and they fall into a few families.

Chapter 01

A message in a bottle, sent across a gap

The previous topic ended at the synapse, the junction between two neurons. The electrical pulse arrives at the end of one cell and stops, because there is a gap. To cross it, the cell releases a chemical. That chemical is a neurotransmitter: a substance that one neuron releases to change the activity of another.

A useful picture is a message in a bottle. The sending cell keeps its messages sealed in tiny bubbles called vesicles. When a pulse arrives, some bubbles burst open into the gap. The message floats across and is read by receptors, proteins on the receiving cell shaped to fit that one chemical. The chemical carries no meaning on its own. The meaning depends on which receptor reads it. The same molecule can excite one cell and calm another.

That last point matters for everything that follows. Popular articles call dopamine "the pleasure chemical" or serotonin "the happiness chemical". Both labels are wrong, because each chemical does different jobs in different circuits, depending on the receptors there.

Two of these are by far the most common. Glutamate and GABA carry most of the fast, point-to-point traffic in the brain. The others are used by far fewer neurons, but those neurons send branches across huge areas, so they change the mood of whole regions at once.

Chapter 03

Fast messages and slow moods

Receptors come in two broad kinds, and the difference explains why some drugs work in seconds and others take weeks.

Fast: channel receptors

The receptor is itself a gate. When the chemical binds, the gate opens and charged particles flow straight into the cell. The effect starts within a thousandth of a second and ends almost as fast. Glutamate and GABA mostly work this way. It is like a doorbell: press and it rings.

Slow: signalling receptors

The receptor is not a gate. Binding sets off a chain of chemical reactions inside the cell that can change which channels open, how sensitive the cell is, and even which genes it switches on. Effects take longer and last longer. Dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline mostly work this way. It is more like a memo that changes office policy.

Chemicals that act mainly in the slow way are often called neuromodulators. They do not carry the content of a thought. They change how the circuits carrying it behave: more alert or less, more willing to act or less, more sensitive to reward or less. Glutamate and GABA are the words; the modulators are the tone of voice.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Make it, release it, read it, clear it away

Each step in a neurotransmitter's short life is a place where a drug can step in. Knowing the steps lets you read almost any drug's mechanism.

Chapter 04

         ^                                              (opens gates or
         |                                               sends signals inside)
  5 clean-up: pumped back in (reuptake) or broken down by enzymes

Step five is easy to overlook and very important. If the chemical stayed in the gap, the receiving cell would be stuck "on". So the message is cleared within milliseconds. Many neurotransmitters are pulled back into the sending cell by proteins called transporters, a process called reuptake, and recycled. Others are broken apart by enzymes.

Every step can be targeted. A drug can supply raw material (step 1), force extra release (step 3), copy the chemical or block its receptor (step 4), or slow its removal (step 5). Most mind-altering substances are one of these five moves.

Chapter 05

Copies and blockers

A drug that fits a receptor and switches it on, like a copied key, is an agonist. Morphine is an agonist at the receptors that the body's own endorphins use. A drug that fits the receptor but does not switch it on, like a key that jams the lock, is an antagonist. Naloxone, the emergency treatment for opioid overdose, is an antagonist. It competes for those same receptors, pushes heroin or fentanyl off them, and blocks them. Breathing can restart within minutes.

Each neurotransmitter also has several kinds of receptor, called subtypes. Dopamine has at least five; serotonin has more than a dozen. Different subtypes sit in different places. This is why one drug often has several effects, wanted and unwanted, and why chemists keep trying to design drugs that hit only one subtype. Volume 44 explains why side effects are almost unavoidable.

Chapter 06

Glutamate and GABA: the accelerator and the brake

Most of the brain's moment-to-moment activity is a balance between glutamate pushing neurons to fire and GABA holding them back. Tip the balance one way and you get seizures. Tip it the other way and you get sleep, sedation, and at the extreme, coma.

Many of the substances that calm people down push towards the brake. Alcohol strengthens GABA's effect and also weakens one of glutamate's receptors. Sedatives called benzodiazepines, such as diazepam, make GABA receptors respond more strongly. So do several general anaesthetics. This shared mechanism is why mixing alcohol with sedatives is dangerous: two brakes pressed together can stop breathing.

Glutamate has its own story. One of its receptors, the NMDA receptor, acts as a detector for when two neurons are active together, which makes it central to learning (see Memory formation). Ketamine blocks it. At high doses ketamine is an anaesthetic. At lower doses it produces a dreamlike detached state, and, more surprisingly, it can lift severe depression within hours. A nasal spray form, esketamine, was approved in the United States in 2019 for depression that has not responded to other treatment.

Chapter 07

Dopamine: wanting and learning, not pleasure

In the 1950s the Swedish pharmacologist Arvid Carlsson showed that dopamine was a neurotransmitter in its own right, and that depleting it in animals caused stiffness that a dopamine building block, L-DOPA, could reverse. That finding led to L-DOPA as a treatment for Parkinson's disease from the 1960s. Carlsson shared a Nobel Prize in 2000.

Dopamine's reputation as "the pleasure chemical" came later and does not hold up well. In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys. They fired when a reward such as juice arrived unexpectedly. Once the monkey learned that a light predicted the juice, the neurons fired at the light instead, and not at the juice. If the juice then failed to arrive, their activity dipped below normal.

So dopamine signals something more like "better than expected". It is a teaching signal, a prediction error, and it also drives the urge to go after rewards. The psychologist Kent Berridge showed that rats with almost no dopamine still enjoy sugar. They just stop working to get it. In short, dopamine is more about wanting than liking. The full story of reward and choice is in Decision-making.

Chapter 08

Serotonin: important, but not "the happiness chemical"

Serotonin is made by small clusters of cells in the brainstem whose branches reach almost the entire brain. It influences mood, impulse control, sleep, appetite and how strongly we respond to threats. Most of the body's serotonin is not in the brain at all. It is in the gut, where it helps control digestion.

The most widely prescribed antidepressants are SSRIs, short for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Fluoxetine, sold as Prozac, was approved in the United States in 1987. They block step five, the reuptake pump, so serotonin stays in the gap longer. That happens within hours. Yet the benefit for mood usually takes weeks. That delay is a clue that the simple story, "depression is low serotonin and the pill tops it up", cannot be the whole explanation. Slower changes in receptors, in gene activity and in the brain's ability to rewire are likely involved.

In 2022 a widely publicised review led by Joanna Moncrieff concluded that there is no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin. Many psychiatrists responded that few experts held the simple version anyway, and that the review did not test whether the drugs work. Both points can be true. Whether SSRIs help and how they help are separate questions. The evidence that they help many people with moderate to severe depression is solid, as Volume 43 explains. How exactly they do it is still being worked out.

Chapter 09

The body's own painkillers, and its own cannabis

In the early 1970s researchers found that the brain has specific receptors for opium-based drugs such as morphine. That raised an obvious question: why would the brain have a receptor for a poppy? In 1975 John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz in Scotland found the first part of the answer: small morphine-like molecules the brain makes itself. This family is now loosely called the endorphins, from "the morphine within". They damp pain during injury and stress.

The same pattern repeated with cannabis. Researchers found receptors for its active ingredient in the brain, and in 1992 a team including Raphael Mechoulam in Israel identified a natural molecule that uses them, named anandamide. The endocannabinoid system it belongs to helps regulate appetite, pain and how strongly synapses respond. The pattern is general: drugs that act on the brain usually hijack a system the brain already has.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicine, addiction and poisons all meet at the synapse

Almost every drug that changes how people think, feel or move acts on neurotransmitters. That makes this the most practical part of neuroscience.

One more category deserves a mention because it shows how precise this chemistry is. Nerve agents such as sarin block the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine. The chemical floods every synapse that uses it, including those controlling breathing. A failure of step five alone can kill within minutes.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Never start, stop or combine medicines that act on the brain without a doctor or pharmacist, and never mix sedatives with alcohol.

Chapter 11

Tolerance and withdrawal: the brain pushes back

The brain constantly adjusts to keep its activity in a normal range. If a drug keeps a neurotransmitter system turned up for weeks, receiving cells respond by removing some receptors, or making them less sensitive. The same dose now does less. That is tolerance.

Then the drug stops. The brain is still set for its presence, and the system swings the other way. That is withdrawal. Someone who has used alcohol heavily for years has turned up their accelerator and turned down their brake to compensate. Stop suddenly and the result can be tremors, anxiety and even seizures. That is why stopping heavy drinking or long-term sedatives should be done with medical help.

Addiction adds something more: drugs that release large amounts of dopamine hijack the prediction-error signal from Chapter 07. Each use teaches the brain "this was better than expected", louder than any natural reward. Over time, cues linked to the drug trigger strong wanting even after liking has faded. Volume 37 covers the human side (Addiction).

Chapter 12

Why "chemical imbalance" is too simple

For decades, patients were told that depression or anxiety was a "chemical imbalance", as if one substance was simply low. The phrase was meant kindly. It told people their illness was real and not a weakness. But it is not accurate.

There is no reliable blood or brain test showing a single chemical too high or too low in depression, anxiety or schizophrenia. These conditions involve circuits, genes, stress, sleep, inflammation and life events working together. Drugs act on chemicals because chemicals are where drugs can reach, not because the illness is purely chemical. A better description is that medicines nudge a disturbed system in a helpful direction, often while other treatments, such as therapy, change it in other ways.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Chemical signalling: flexible, tunable, and easy to hijack

Why did evolution put a chemical step in the middle of an electrical system? The benefits are large. So are the vulnerabilities.

The last row is the core problem of brain medicine. A pill spreads through the whole brain. The problem, however, may be in one circuit. Today's drugs adjust the volume of a chemical everywhere when what we want is to adjust one conversation. Much of the research described next is about getting more precise.

One more trend is worth watching. New sensors now let researchers watch dopamine, serotonin and other chemicals rise and fall in living animals, in real time and in chosen brain regions. For most of the field's history, scientists inferred the chemistry from drugs and dead tissue. Seeing it directly is likely to overturn some of the simple stories this file has warned about.

The core idea

Neurotransmitters carry messages across synapses. Glutamate excites and GABA inhibits; modulators such as dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline set the tone. The meaning of each chemical depends on the receptor that reads it. Nearly every mind-altering drug makes, releases, copies, blocks or slows the clearing of one of them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than 100 substances can act as messengers; a handful do most of the work.
  • Fast receptors act in about a thousandth of a second; antidepressants take weeks.
  • Most of the body's serotonin is in the gut, not the brain.
The common mistake

Calling dopamine "pleasure" and serotonin "happiness". Dopamine is closer to wanting and learning; serotonin touches mood, impulse, sleep and digestion, and neither works alone.

Test yourself

Why can naloxone reverse an opioid overdose but not an alcohol overdose? If you can answer using receptors, agonists and antagonists, you have the core of pharmacology.

The words, in plain words
Neurotransmitter
A chemical one neuron releases to change the activity of another.
Receptor
A protein on a cell shaped to fit and respond to one chemical.
Vesicle
A tiny bubble inside a neuron that stores neurotransmitter until release.
Reuptake
Pumping a neurotransmitter back into the cell that released it.
Neuromodulator
A chemical that changes how circuits behave rather than carrying a specific message.
Agonist
A drug that fits a receptor and switches it on.
Antagonist
A drug that fits a receptor and blocks it without switching it on.
Subtype
One of several versions of a receptor for the same chemical.
SSRI
An antidepressant that slows the reuptake of serotonin.
Prediction error
The difference between what was expected and what happened, signalled by dopamine.
Endorphins
The brain's own morphine-like painkillers.
Tolerance
Needing more of a drug for the same effect because the brain has adapted.
