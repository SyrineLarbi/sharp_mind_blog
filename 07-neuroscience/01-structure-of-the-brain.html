Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 01 of 20

Structure of the brain. A map of districts, and the roads between them matter more than the districts.

Hold a human brain and it feels like firm jelly. It weighs about as much as a bag of sugar and looks almost the same all over: grey, wrinkled, split down the middle. Nothing in that surface tells you that one fold controls your right hand, another recognises faces, and a small knot deep inside decides when you feel hungry. The brain is organised, and quite precisely. But it is organised less like a set of boxes with labels and more like a city: districts with a speciality, connected by roads, where most real work needs several districts at once.

Question 1 · What is it?

An expensive organ built in three layers

The brain is the control centre of the nervous system. Before looking at the parts, it helps to know what it is made of and what it costs to run.

Chapter 01

The most expensive kilo and a half you own

An adult human brain weighs roughly 1.3 to 1.4 kilograms. That is about 2% of the weight of an average adult. Yet at rest it uses about a fifth of the body's energy. It runs on roughly 20 watts, the power of a dim light bulb, and it cannot store much fuel. If its blood supply stops, you lose consciousness within seconds, and brain cells start to die within minutes.

For a long time, textbooks said the brain had about 100 billion nerve cells and ten times as many support cells. Both numbers were guesses that had been copied from book to book. In 2009 the Brazilian neuroscientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel and her colleagues actually counted. The answer was about 86 billion neurons, and roughly the same number of support cells, not ten times more.

The nerve cells are called neurons. They carry signals, and the next topic is about them (Neurons). The support cells are called glia, from the Greek for "glue". Glia feed neurons, insulate their wires, clean up waste, and help defend the brain against infection.

Chapter 02

The map: three big parts and a hidden core

Seen from the side, the brain has three large parts. At the bottom, the brainstem joins the brain to the spinal cord. Tucked behind it at the back is the cerebellum, which means "little brain". Above and around both is the cerebrum, the large wrinkled mass most people picture. Buried inside the cerebrum is a set of deeper structures that do some of the most important work of all.

Chapter 03

Grey matter, white matter, and why it is wrinkled

Cut a brain in half and you see two colours. Grey matter is where the bodies of neurons sit and where most of the computing happens. White matter is the wiring: long nerve fibres, called axons, wrapped in a fatty insulating layer called myelin. The fat is what makes it look pale. Think of grey matter as the offices and white matter as the cables running between buildings.

The cortex is a sheet of grey matter only about 2 to 4 millimetres thick, roughly the thickness of a coin or two. If you could unfold it and lay it flat, it would cover something like a quarter of a square metre. It fits inside the skull only because it is crumpled, the way a sheet of paper fits into a small ball when you scrunch it. The ridges are called gyri and the grooves sulci. The folding is a packing solution: more cortex in the same skull.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Specialised districts, wired into teams

Different parts of the brain do different jobs. But almost nothing you do uses only one of them. Understanding the brain means understanding both the specialities and the connections.

Chapter 04

The four lobes of the cortex

Anatomists divide each half of the cortex into four main lobes, named after the skull bones that cover them. The boundaries are partly a convention. The jobs are well established.

Frontal lobe

Behind the forehead. The largest lobe in humans. At its back edge is the strip that sends movement commands to the body. Further forward, the prefrontal cortex handles planning, holding goals in mind, self-control and judging consequences. On the left side in most people, an area helps produce speech.

Parietal lobe

On top, behind the frontal lobe. Its front edge receives touch, pressure and body position. The rest combines the senses into a sense of space: where things are, where your body is, how to reach for a cup without looking at your hand.

Temporal lobe

On each side, behind the temples. It handles hearing, understanding language (on the left in most people), recognising objects and faces, and, on its inner edge, forming new memories.

Occipital lobe

At the very back. Almost all of it is devoted to vision. Damage here can cause blindness even when the eyes are perfectly healthy, because the picture never gets processed.

A fifth region, the insula, is hidden inside a deep fold on each side. It reads signals from inside the body, such as heartbeat, breath, gut feelings and disgust. Its role in emotion is covered in Emotions and the brain.

Chapter 05

The map of the body on the brain

From the 1930s to the 1950s, the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield operated on people with severe epilepsy at the Montreal Neurological Institute. The brain itself has no pain sensors, so patients could stay awake under local anaesthetic. Before removing tissue, Penfield touched points on the exposed cortex with a weak electrical current and asked patients what they felt. Touching one spot made a thumb twitch. Another spot produced a tingle in the lip.

He found two strips running across the top of the brain from ear to ear, one for movement and one for touch. Each is laid out as a map of the body. The odd part is the proportions. The hands, lips and tongue take up huge areas. The back and legs get very little. Drawn as a figure, with each body part sized by its brain area, you get the homunculus ("little man"): enormous hands and lips on a small body. Brain space follows how finely you use a body part, not how big it is. The wiring is also crossed: the left half of the brain moves and feels the right side of the body, and the reverse. And the maps are not fixed. Years of practice, or the loss of a finger, redraw them (see Brain plasticity).

Chapter 06

The deep machinery: relay, thermostat, and gatekeeper

Below the cortex sit structures that rarely get public attention but that the cortex cannot work without.

The thalamus is the main relay station. Signals from the eyes, ears, skin and tongue stop here before going up to the cortex. Smell is the one sense that takes a more direct route. The thalamus is not a passive switchboard. It also filters, and it is part of the circuit that keeps the cortex awake. Damage to both sides of the thalamus can cause a coma.

The hypothalamus, about the size of an almond, keeps the body's settings steady. It is the thermostat that makes you shiver or sweat, the gauge that makes you hungry or thirsty, and the clock that times sleep (see Sleep and the brain). It also controls the pituitary gland hanging just beneath it, which releases hormones into the blood. Through that link, a small patch of brain tissue steers growth, stress responses, reproduction and metabolism across the whole body.

The basal ganglia are a group of clusters that act like a gatekeeper for action. At any moment you could do many things. The basal ganglia hold almost all of them back and release one. In Parkinson's disease, cells that supply the basal ganglia with the chemical messenger dopamine die off. The gate sticks. Patients find it hard to start moving, even though their muscles are fine. In Huntington's disease, a different part of the circuit fails, and unwanted movements leak through.

Chapter 07

The cerebellum: a small part with most of the neurons

The cerebellum makes up only about a tenth of the brain's volume. Yet it holds around 69 billion of the 86 billion neurons, about four in five. They are tiny and packed tightly in a sheet even more finely folded than the cortex.

Its best-known job is timing and correcting movement. When you reach for a glass, the cerebellum compares the movement you intended with the movement that is actually happening and adjusts it, many times a second. People with cerebellar damage are not paralysed. They overshoot, tremble as they approach a target, and walk as if drunk.

Chapter 08

Two halves, one bridge, and a famous experiment

The cerebrum is split into a left and a right hemisphere. They are joined by the corpus callosum, a thick band of roughly 200 million nerve fibres. It is the largest bridge in the brain.

In the 1960s, some patients with uncontrollable epilepsy had the corpus callosum cut to stop seizures spreading from one side to the other. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga studied these "split-brain" patients. In normal life they seemed almost unchanged. In the laboratory, careful tests showed something strange. If a picture was shown only to the right hemisphere, the patient could not name it, because in most people speech is controlled by the left. Yet the left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, could pick out the matching object. Each half knew things the other half did not. Sperry shared the Nobel Prize in 1981 for this work.

These experiments also fed a popular myth: that people are "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative). The evidence does not support it. Language does lean left in most people, but large studies of brain activity have not found people who are generally "left-dominant" or "right-dominant".

Chapter 09

Networks, not boxes

The table in Chapter 02 suggests that each job has one home. Real tasks do not work that way. Reading this sentence uses visual areas at the back, word-recognition areas on the underside of the left temporal lobe, language areas on the left side, attention areas in the parietal and frontal lobes, and memory structures that link it to what you already know. They are active together, in sequence and in loops.

Modern neuroscience describes the brain as a set of large networks: groups of distant areas that tend to switch on together. One example was spotted almost by accident. In brain scans, researchers noticed a set of areas that became more active when volunteers were resting between tasks. In 2001 the neurologist Marcus Raichle and colleagues named it the default mode network. It is active during daydreaming, remembering the past, imagining the future and thinking about other people. The brain at "rest" is busy.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Location is diagnosis, and maps guide the knife

Knowing which part does what is not just textbook knowledge. It is how doctors read symptoms, how surgeons avoid harm, and how the public can tell good brain science from bad.

Chapter 10

Monsieur Tan, and how damage drew the first maps

In 1861 the French physician Paul Broca examined a patient called Louis Leborgne. He could understand what was said to him, but for years the only syllable he could produce was "tan", so the hospital called him "Tan". After he died, Broca found damage in a specific part of the left frontal lobe. Over the following years Broca saw more patients like him and concluded that this area was needed to produce speech. It is still called Broca's area.

In 1874 the German neurologist Carl Wernicke described the opposite problem. His patients spoke fluently, but their words made little sense, and they struggled to understand others. The damage was further back, in the left temporal lobe, now called Wernicke's area. Together the two cases showed that language is not one ability but several, housed in different places. Today we know the picture is more complex and the areas are part of a wider language network, explored in Language.

Chapter 11

Stroke: the map in an emergency

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a vessel bursts. The symptoms tell you where. A blockage in the artery supplying the left side of the brain often causes weakness of the right face and arm, plus trouble speaking, because it hits the left movement strip and the language areas together. A stroke in the back of the brain may cause sudden loss of part of the visual field. A stroke in the cerebellum can show up mainly as dizziness and staggering.

In a stroke, minutes matter: treatments that clear a blocked artery work best when given early. Volume 45 covers this (Stroke).

Health note

This file is general education, not medical advice. Sudden weakness, numbness, confusion, trouble speaking or seeing, or a sudden severe headache needs emergency help immediately.

Chapter 12

Reading a brain scan without being fooled

Most brain news comes with a coloured scan. It helps to know what each tool really measures.

ToolWhat it measuresGood atWeak at

fMRIChanges in blood oxygen, which follow activityWhere activity rises, to within millimetresSlow: blood lags activity by seconds; indirect

PETWhere an injected, weakly radioactive tracer gathersChemistry: sugar use, receptors, Alzheimer's proteinsRadiation dose, cost, coarse detail

Functional MRI, developed in the early 1990s, changed the field. It also produced a lot of overconfident headlines. In 2009 a group of researchers led by Craig Bennett put a dead Atlantic salmon in a scanner, showed it photos of people, and ran a standard analysis. Without proper statistical correction, the analysis found "activity" in the fish's brain. The point was not about fish. It was that a scan tests millions of points at once, and some will light up by chance unless the statistics guard against it.

Also, a region "lighting up" only means it was busier than in a comparison task. Activity in the amygdala does not prove fear, because the same region joins in many tasks.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A design shaped by evolution, with the costs built in

The brain was not designed from a blank page. Each feature solved a problem and created another. Seeing both sides explains a surprising amount of human life.

Chapter 15

Why a map is not an explanation

There is a sobering lesson from a tiny worm. Caenorhabditis elegans, about a millimetre long, has exactly 302 neurons. Its complete wiring diagram was published in 1986. Yet nearly four decades later, scientists still cannot fully predict its behaviour from that diagram. The wiring says who can talk to whom. It does not say what they are saying, how strongly, or how that changes with hunger, chemistry and experience.

The same will be true of human maps. Structure tells you what is possible; activity and chemistry tell you what actually happens. That is why the rest of this category moves from anatomy to the cells (Neurons), to the chemicals they use (Neurotransmitters), and to how the whole system changes with use (Brain plasticity).

The core idea

The brain has a brainstem for survival basics, a cerebellum for precise movement, deep structures for relaying signals, regulating the body, choosing actions and forming memories, and a folded cortex for perception, language and planning. Regions specialise, but abilities come from networks of regions joined by white-matter cables.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 86 billion neurons, and roughly as many glial cells (counted in 2009).
  • About 2% of body weight, about 20% of resting energy.
  • The cerebellum: about a tenth of the volume, about four-fifths of the neurons.
The common mistake

Reading a lit-up region on a scan as "the place where X happens". Activity is not the same as being the cause, and most regions take part in many different tasks.

Test yourself

A patient suddenly cannot speak properly and has a weak right arm. Which side of the brain is affected, and why do those two symptoms come together? If you can answer, you understand crossed wiring and the left-side language areas.

The words, in plain words
Neuron
A nerve cell that carries electrical and chemical signals.
Glia
The brain's support cells, which feed, insulate, clean and protect neurons.
Brainstem
The stalk joining brain and spinal cord; controls breathing, heart rate and wakefulness.
Cerebellum
The "little brain" at the back that makes movement smooth, accurate and well-timed.
Cerebral cortex
The thin, folded outer sheet of the brain that handles perception, language and thought.
Thalamus
A deep relay station that passes sensory signals to the cortex.
Hypothalamus
A small deep structure that controls temperature, hunger, sleep timing and hormones.
Basal ganglia
Deep clusters that select which action to start and hold back the rest.
Myelin
The fatty insulation around nerve fibres that speeds up signals and makes white matter pale.
Corpus callosum
The thick band of fibres joining the left and right hemispheres.
Connectome
The complete wiring diagram of a nervous system.
Blood-brain barrier
The tight seal on brain blood vessels that keeps most substances out.
