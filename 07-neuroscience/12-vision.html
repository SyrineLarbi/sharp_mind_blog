Sharp Mind · Neuroscience · Topic 12 of 20

Vision. The eye collects light; the brain builds a world from it.

Open your eyes and a complete, sharp, coloured scene seems simply to be there. It is the most convincing illusion you will ever experience. What reaches your eye is a flat, upside-down, constantly jumping pattern of light, detailed only in a tiny central patch. From that, the brain works out depth, objects, faces, motion and colour in a fraction of a second, using a large share of the cortex. This file follows light from the retina to recognition, and shows what we learn when parts of the system fail.

Question 1 · What is it?

Solving a puzzle that has no single answer

Vision is the brain's way of working out what is in the world from the light that reaches the eye. The light alone is never enough, so the brain has to guess well.

Chapter 01

The cell that liked an edge

In the late 1950s two young researchers at Johns Hopkins University, David Hubel from Canada and Torsten Wiesel from Sweden, were recording from single neurons in the visual cortex of a cat. They projected spots of light onto a screen and waited for a cell to respond. For hours, nothing worked. Then, as they slid a glass slide into the projector, its faint edge swept a line across the screen and the cell burst into furious activity.

The cell did not care about spots. It responded to an edge at one particular angle, moving in one particular direction. Over the following years, Hubel and Wiesel showed that the primary visual cortex is full of such cells, each tuned to lines of a certain orientation in a certain small patch of the visual field, arranged in an orderly map. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1981.

The discovery changed how scientists thought about seeing. The brain does not look at a picture. It breaks the image into simple features and then rebuilds objects from them, step by step. That idea later inspired the design of the computer systems that learned to recognise images.

Chapter 02

Why seeing is a guess

The image on the back of your eye is flat. A small coin held close and a large plate far away can cast exactly the same shape. A white wall in shadow can send less light than a black shirt in sunshine. Infinitely many real scenes could have produced any given image. Scientists call this an inverse problem: working backwards from effects to causes when the effects do not pin down the causes.

In the nineteenth century the German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz described perception as unconscious inference. The brain combines the image with assumptions learned from experience and evolution: light usually comes from above, objects usually have stable colours, nearer things block farther ones. Most of the time the assumptions are right, and you see the world. When they are wrong, you see an illusion, with total conviction.

Chapter 03

The eye is a camera, until it isn't

The front of the eye works like a camera: the cornea and lens focus light into an upside-down image on the retina, a thin layer of nerve tissue lining the back of the eye. After that, the analogy fails.

Look at the numbers. More than 120 million light sensors report through about one million output fibres. The retina compresses the image more than a hundredfold before the brain sees it, keeping changes and edges and discarding much of the rest. And because only the fovea is sharp, your eyes jump about three times a second in quick movements called saccades, pointing the fovea at what matters. The brain stitches these glimpses into the stable scene you experience.

Colour comes from three kinds of cone, most sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths, often called blue, green and red. Your sense of colour is the brain comparing their outputs. If one type is missing or shifted, as in the commonest forms of colour blindness, some colours look alike. This affects about 1 in 12 men of northern European ancestry and far fewer women, because the relevant genes sit on the X chromosome.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From edges to objects to meaning

The visual system is a series of processing stages that run both forwards and backwards, splitting into routes for recognising things and for acting on them.

Chapter 04

The retina is part of the brain

The retina grows out of the brain in the embryo, and it behaves like a small piece of brain. Its output cells already do analysis. Many respond best to a small spot of light surrounded by darkness, or the reverse, so they signal contrast rather than brightness. That is why a grey square looks lighter on a black background than on a white one. Researchers have identified dozens of types of retinal output cell, each sending a different summary of the scene: some for edges, some for motion, some for overall brightness. One small type even reports daylight levels to the brain's body clock, which is part of why bright light in the evening affects sleep.

Signals pass through the thalamus to the primary visual cortex (V1) at the back of the head. Each side of the brain sees the opposite half of the world, which is why a stroke on one side can remove half of the visual field while leaving both eyes healthy. From V1, information flows to more than two dozen further visual areas, each emphasising different features.

Patients show how specialised they are. In 1983 the German neurologist Josef Zihl described a woman who, after damage to the motion area V5, could no longer see movement. Pouring tea, she saw the liquid as frozen, like a glacier, and the cup overflowed before she noticed it filling. People crossing the road seemed to jump from place to place. Damage near V4 can remove colour while leaving shape, a condition called cerebral achromatopsia. Motion and colour are not properties you simply receive. They are computed in particular places, and those places can fail alone.

Chapter 06

Two streams: seeing what, and seeing how

In 1982 Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin proposed that visual processing divides into two broad routes. The ventral stream runs from V1 down into the temporal lobe and identifies things: "that is a cup". The dorsal stream runs up into the parietal lobe and handles location and movement.

Later, David Milner and Melvyn Goodale refined the idea, thanks largely to a patient known as D.F. After carbon monoxide poisoning damaged her ventral stream, she could no longer recognise shapes or say which way a slot was tilted. Yet asked to post a card through the same slot, she turned her hand to exactly the right angle. Her brain could use what she could not consciously see. Milner and Goodale argued that the dorsal stream is "vision for action", working largely outside awareness, while the ventral stream is "vision for perception". The idea is influential, though most researchers now stress how much the two streams interact.

Chapter 07

Faces, objects and the famous-actress neuron

Along the underside of the temporal lobe, regions respond to particular categories. In 1997 Nancy Kanwisher and colleagues described the fusiform face area, which responds much more to faces than to other objects. Nearby areas prefer places or bodies, and in readers one patch prefers written words (see Language). Damage to face areas can cause prosopagnosia, or face blindness: a person may fail to recognise their own family until they hear a voice. Some people are born with a milder form and find out only as adults that others recognise faces easily.

In 2005 Rodrigo Quian Quiroga and colleagues recorded from single neurons deep in the temporal lobe of epilepsy patients who had electrodes implanted for treatment. One cell fired to many different photographs of the actress Jennifer Aniston, and to her written name, but not to other people. Other cells responded to other celebrities or landmarks. These cells sit at the meeting point of vision and memory, and they suggest that at the top of the visual system, cells represent concepts, not pictures.

Chapter 08

Seeing is predicting

The visual system is not a one-way conveyor belt. Higher areas send many connections back to lower ones; even the thalamic relay receives more input from the cortex than from the eyes. One influential theory, predictive coding, holds that higher areas constantly predict what lower areas should be seeing, and that mainly the errors, the surprises, are passed upwards. It is still debated how fully the brain works this way, but it fits many observations.

It explains why expectations shape what you see. A blurry shape becomes a face the moment you are told it is one, and you cannot then unsee it. It also explains change blindness: in experiments, large changes to a scene, such as a different person standing in it, often go unnoticed if they happen during a blink or a brief flicker. You do not store the whole scene. You store a summary and trust that the world is still there to check.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Consciousness, childhood and computers

Vision is the best-understood part of the brain, so it is where many of the big questions get tested first.

Chapter 09

Blindsight: seeing without knowing it

The British psychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz studied a man, known as D.B., who had lost part of his primary visual cortex and was blind in part of his visual field. When objects were shown there, D.B. said he saw nothing. But when asked to guess where an object was, or whether a line was vertical or horizontal, he was right far more often than chance. He was surprised to hear his score.

This condition, blindsight, shows that some visual information reaches other brain areas by routes that bypass V1, and that it can guide behaviour without any experience of seeing. It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the brain does a great deal of processing outside awareness, and it makes vision a key test case in the science of consciousness.

Chapter 10

Vision must be learned, and early

General education

This is not medical advice. Sudden loss of vision, flashes or a curtain over your sight need urgent medical attention.

Hubel and Wiesel also showed that if one eye of a young kitten was kept closed for a few weeks, the visual cortex rewired to favour the open eye, and the closed eye stayed weak even after it was opened. In adult cats the same closure had little effect. There is a sensitive period when visual circuits need normal input to develop.

The human equivalent is amblyopia, or "lazy eye", in which one eye sends a poorer image during childhood, for example because of a squint, and the brain learns to ignore it. Treatment, such as patching the stronger eye, works best when started early, which is why children's eye checks matter. The same principle explains why babies born with dense cataracts need them removed soon. A long-running project in India led by Pawan Sinha has removed cataracts in older children and young adults who were born blind. Many gained useful vision, although some fine skills stayed limited, showing that the brain keeps more flexibility than once thought, but not unlimited flexibility.

A modern visual problem is the rise of short-sightedness. In some East Asian cities, a large majority of young adults are now short-sighted. The strongest evidence points to time spent outdoors in childhood as protective, probably because of bright light. A widely cited 2016 projection suggested that about half the world's population could be short-sighted by 2050.

Chapter 11

How brains taught computers to see

In 1980 the Japanese engineer Kunihiko Fukushima built the "neocognitron", a layered network inspired directly by Hubel and Wiesel's simple and complex cells. Its descendants, called convolutional neural networks, began beating older methods at recognising images around 2012 and now power face unlocking, medical image reading and self-driving perception. In the 2010s neuroscientists found that the inner layers of such networks resemble responses along the ventral stream of monkeys surprisingly well.

The resemblance has limits. Tiny, carefully designed changes to an image, invisible to people, can make a network confidently mistake a panda for something else. Humans learn to recognise objects from far fewer examples. The networks capture part of how vision works, not all of it. See Artificial neural networks.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant guesser that can be fooled

Every strength of vision comes from its guessing, and so does every illusion.

Chapter 13

What is solved, and what is not

Well understood

The retina, the pathway to the cortex, the early feature detectors, the main specialist areas and the effects of many kinds of damage. Vision is the most thoroughly mapped system in the brain.

Still open

How separately processed colour, shape and motion are joined into one object, known as the binding problem. How the brain produces the experience of seeing, rather than just the processing. And how much of vision is truly predictive.

The useful attitude is the same as for memory: trust your eyes most of the time, and least when you are tired, rushed, expecting something, or looking at something designed to fool you, such as a magic trick or a manipulated image.

The core idea

Vision is inference. The retina compresses the image and signals contrast, the primary visual cortex breaks it into edges and features, specialist areas compute colour, motion and faces, and two streams handle what things are and how to act on them. Higher areas predict; the result is a best guess you experience as reality.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 120 million rods and 6 million cones report through about 1 million optic nerve fibres per eye.
  • Your eyes make about three saccades a second.
  • Hubel and Wiesel's Nobel Prize: 1981.
The common mistake

Thinking the eye is a camera and the brain watches the film. The brain constructs the scene; the eye supplies clues.

Test yourself

Can you explain how patient D.F. could post a card through a slot whose angle she could not describe? If so, you understand the two visual streams.

The words, in plain words
Retina
The layer of nerve tissue at the back of the eye that senses light and starts processing it.
Rods and cones
Light-sensing cells: rods for dim light, cones for colour and detail.
Fovea
The small central pit of the retina where vision is sharp.
Saccade
A quick jump of the eyes from one point to another.
Optic chiasm
The crossing point where half of each optic nerve's fibres switch sides.
Primary visual cortex (V1)
The first cortical stop for vision, at the back of the head.
Ventral stream
The "what" route into the temporal lobe, for recognising things.
Dorsal stream
The "where and how" route into the parietal lobe, for location and action.
Fusiform face area
A region on the underside of the temporal lobe that responds strongly to faces.
Prosopagnosia
Difficulty recognising faces, from injury or present from birth.
Blindsight
Responding accurately to things in a blind area of vision without consciously seeing them.
Amblyopia
"Lazy eye": reduced vision in one eye because the brain learned to ignore it in childhood.
Predictive coding
The theory that the brain predicts its input and passes on mainly the errors.
