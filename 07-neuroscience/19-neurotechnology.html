Neurotechnology. Tools that listen to the nervous system, and tools that talk back.

Neurotechnology is any device that measures or changes the activity of the nervous system. The phrase sounds futuristic, but much of it is already routine. More than a hundred thousand people have electrodes deep in their brains to control Parkinson's tremor. Clinics treat depression with magnetic pulses through the skull. A surgeon can now destroy a tiny cluster of faulty cells with focused sound waves, without a single cut. At the same time, cheap headbands promise better sleep and focus, and companies collect brain data with few rules. This topic maps the field: what works, what is unproven, and what needs watching.

Question 1 · What is it?

Measuring, changing, and replacing

Neurotechnology is a toolbox, not a single invention. Its tools do one of three jobs: they record what the nervous system is doing, they change what it is doing, or they stand in for a part that has stopped working.

Chapter 01

The tremor that stopped in Grenoble

In 1987 the French neurosurgeon Alim-Louis Benabid was operating on a patient with a severe tremor in Grenoble. The standard surgery at the time was to destroy a small part of the thalamus, a relay station deep in the brain. Before cutting, surgeons used an electrode to test the target with electric current.

Benabid noticed something. When he stimulated the spot at a high frequency, around a hundred pulses a second, the tremor stopped. When he switched the current off, it came back. The stimulation was doing what the destruction would have done, but reversibly. Instead of burning the tissue, why not leave an electrode there permanently, connected to a battery under the skin?

That idea became deep brain stimulation, or DBS. It was approved in the United States for tremor in 1997 and for Parkinson's disease in 2002. Its significance went beyond one disease. It showed that a brain circuit could be adjusted like a machine setting, turned up, turned down or off, rather than removed.

Two words help sort all this. Invasive means it requires surgery or enters the body. Non-invasive means it works from outside. The trade-off is constant across the field: the closer you get to the neurons, the more precise and powerful the tool, and the greater the risk.

Chapter 03

Two centuries of electricity and nerves

The field begins with frogs. In the 1780s the Italian physician Luigi Galvani found that touching a frog's leg nerve with metal made the dead leg twitch. He concluded that nerves carry electricity, which was broadly right. In 1924 Hans Berger recorded the first human EEG, the faint electrical rhythms of the brain picked up from the scalp. In the 1930s and 1940s the Canadian neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield stimulated the exposed brains of awake epilepsy patients and mapped which spots produced tingling in a hand, movement of a lip, or, occasionally, a vivid fragment of memory.

The last fifty years added imaging, implants and computers. The difference today is not a single breakthrough. It is that electronics have become small, cheap and smart enough to be left inside the body or worn all day, and software can now interpret the signals in real time.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Electric fields, magnetic pulses, sound and light

Neurons communicate with electrical changes across their membranes. Almost every neurotechnology either detects those changes or pushes them, using whatever form of energy can reach the right cells.

Chapter 04

Seeing the working brain: four ways, four compromises

No single scanner shows everything. Each measures something different, and each trades where against when.

EEG and MEG: fast but blurry

EEG picks up electrical activity through the scalp. MEG picks up the tiny magnetic fields the same currents create. Both track changes within thousandths of a second, which makes them ideal for sleep studies and epilepsy. But they struggle to say exactly where in the brain a signal comes from, especially deep structures.

fMRI and PET: precise but slow

Functional MRI detects changes in blood oxygen. Active areas draw more fresh blood a few seconds later. It locates activity to within millimetres, but it measures blood, not neurons, and it lags. PET uses a small radioactive tracer and can show specific chemicals, such as the amyloid of Alzheimer's.

Brain imaging is powerful and easy to misuse. In 2009 a group of researchers famously scanned a dead salmon while "showing" it photographs, and found apparent activity in its brain. The joke had a serious point. Without proper statistical correction, an fMRI study can find a signal in pure noise. Colourful brain pictures in the news deserve the same caution as any other single study.

Chapter 05

Deep brain stimulation: a pacemaker for circuits

A DBS system has three parts. Thin wires with electrodes at their tips are placed in a precise target deep in the brain, guided by scans and, often, by recording from neurons during surgery. The wires run under the skin to a battery-powered pulse generator in the chest, much like a heart pacemaker. A doctor adjusts the settings wirelessly.

In Parkinson's disease, the loss of dopamine makes certain circuits fire in abnormal, overly synchronised rhythms. These rhythms seem to jam the movement system. High-frequency stimulation appears to break up the abnormal pattern, a little like adding steady background noise to drown out an annoying hum. The honest truth is that more than thirty years after Benabid's discovery, exactly how DBS works is still debated. It works well enough to have been given to well over a hundred thousand people worldwide.

DBS does not slow Parkinson's. It reduces tremor, stiffness and slowness, and often lets patients reduce their medication. It carries the risks of brain surgery, including bleeding and infection, and the stimulation can sometimes change mood or impulse control, which settings can usually correct. The disease itself is covered in Neurodegenerative diseases.

Chapter 06

Through the skull without opening it

Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, was introduced in 1985 by Anthony Barker and colleagues in Sheffield, England. A coil held against the head produces a brief, strong magnetic pulse. The changing magnetic field passes harmlessly through the skull and creates a small electric current in the brain tissue beneath, enough to make neurons fire. Place the coil over the motor cortex and your thumb twitches.

Repeated sessions over weeks can change activity in a targeted circuit. US regulators cleared TMS for depression that had not responded to medication in 2008. The usual course involves daily sessions for several weeks. Newer, accelerated schedules give several sessions a day over a few days. TMS helps a meaningful minority of patients with hard-to-treat depression, and its main side effects are headache and scalp discomfort.

Focused ultrasound is newer and more striking. A helmet holding about a thousand small ultrasound sources aims them all at one point deep in the brain, while the patient lies in an MRI scanner. Each beam alone is harmless. Where they meet, the energy heats a spot a few millimetres across and destroys it. For some patients with severe tremor, the shaking stops on the operating table, with no incision. The destruction is permanent, which is both its strength and its risk.

Chapter 07

Working through the body's nerves

Not every neurotechnology targets the brain directly. The vagus nerve is a long nerve running from the brainstem down through the neck to the heart and gut. It carries a great deal of information back up to the brain. A small stimulator implanted under the collarbone, with a wire wrapped around the vagus nerve in the neck, can send regular pulses up into the brain.

Vagus nerve stimulation was approved for epilepsy in the US in 1997, and later for some cases of depression. In 2021 a system was approved that pairs brief vagus stimulation with arm exercises after stroke, on the theory that the pulses briefly boost the brain's plasticity while the movement is practised. Timing the stimulation to the training is the key idea, and it is likely to spread.

Spinal cord stimulators, implanted along the spine, are widely used for some kinds of chronic pain that do not respond to other treatment. And the cochlear implant, which turns sound into electrical pulses on the hearing nerve, remains the most successful neural device of all, with more than a million users.

Chapter 08

The research tools that changed neuroscience

Some of the most important neurotechnology is never used in clinics. In 2005 Edward Boyden, Karl Deisseroth and colleagues at Stanford reported a method now called optogenetics. They took a gene from algae that makes a light-sensitive protein and inserted it into specific neurons in animals. Shining light then switched those neurons on, with millisecond precision. Other proteins let light switch neurons off.

For the first time, researchers could ask "what happens if I turn on exactly these cells, now?" rather than only watching. That is the difference between correlation and cause, and it has transformed animal research on memory, fear, hunger and movement. A related approach has been tried in people: in 2021 a team reported that a man blinded by an inherited retinal disease regained partial vision, enough to locate and count some objects, after gene therapy made his remaining eye cells light-sensitive and he wore special goggles.

Recording has improved just as fast. Modern silicon probes, such as the Neuropixels design, can record from many hundreds of neurons at once in animals. Whole-brain imaging of small animals, like larval zebrafish, can capture activity across most of their neurons simultaneously. Neuroscience has moved from listening to one neuron at a time to listening to crowds.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

New treatments, new markets, and the most private data there is

Neurotechnology already helps patients whom drugs failed. It is also moving into everyday consumer products, carrying brain data into a world with few rules.

Chapter 09

When drugs are not enough

Brain medicines are blunt. A pill spreads through the whole brain and body, and its effects depend on where its target chemical happens to be. Many people with epilepsy, depression or Parkinson's get partial relief or unbearable side effects. Roughly a third of people with epilepsy, for example, continue to have seizures despite medication.

Devices offer something drugs cannot: precision in place and time. A responsive neurostimulator for epilepsy, approved in the US in 2013, sits in the skull, watches the brain's electrical activity, and delivers a brief pulse only when it detects the start of a seizure. It is an early example of a closed-loop system, one that senses and responds automatically. Many patients who have it see their seizures fall substantially over the years.

Electroconvulsive therapy, or ECT, is the older reminder that electricity can treat mind as well as movement. Introduced in Italy in 1938 and badly misused in some institutions in the mid-twentieth century, it is now given under anaesthesia and remains one of the most effective treatments for severe depression, though memory side effects are real.

Chapter 10

The consumer brain market

Walk into an electronics shop and you may find headbands that read your EEG while you meditate, earbuds with brain sensors, and kits that pass a weak current through your scalp, a technique called tDCS (transcranial direct current stimulation), claiming to boost focus or mood.

The evidence varies widely. Simple EEG devices can genuinely detect broad states such as drowsiness or eyes-closed relaxation, and they can make meditation feedback more engaging. Claims of improved intelligence, memory or productivity are mostly weakly supported or unsupported. Research on tDCS has produced mixed results, with many positive small studies and many failures to replicate.

Regulation has struggled to keep up. A medical device must prove it is safe and effective. A "wellness" device that only promises relaxation often faces lighter rules. The European Union has moved to bring non-medical brain-stimulation equipment under its medical-device rules, but in most places a gadget that reads or stimulates the brain can be sold with less scrutiny than a painkiller.

Chapter 11

Why brain data is different

Your heart-rate data reveals your fitness. Your brain data could, in principle, reveal far more: signs of fatigue, attention, emotional reactions, early disease. Today's consumer devices can infer only crude states. But data collected now could be reanalysed later with better methods, and people cannot easily foresee what it might reveal.

That is why neural data has become a legal category. Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to protect mental integrity. Two US states, Colorado and California, added neural data to their privacy laws in 2024. International bodies, including UNESCO, have worked on global ethical guidance. The debate over new "neurorights" is set out in Brain-computer interfaces and Law · Privacy law. The core argument is simple: the mind is the last private space, and the tools to measure it are arriving before the rules.

Chapter 13

A cautionary tale: deep brain stimulation for depression

In 2005 the neurologist Helen Mayberg and colleagues reported that DBS of a small region, sometimes called area 25, deep in the front of the brain, brought rapid relief to several patients with severe, treatment-resistant depression. Some described the moment the current switched on as a sudden lifting of a weight. The results were dramatic and widely reported.

Then came larger trials. At least one major company-sponsored trial was stopped because, at the pre-planned point, patients receiving real stimulation did not do clearly better than those receiving sham stimulation. Supporters argued that the trial design was flawed, that electrode placement needed better targeting, and that benefits took longer to appear. Some patients in long-term follow-up did very well. The question is still contested, and research continues, now with personalised targeting.

The lesson applies across neurotechnology. Early results in a few hand-picked patients are often more impressive than what later controlled trials show, especially for conditions such as depression, where expectation itself has strong effects. The general principles of judging a health claim are in Volume 44.

Chapter 15

From fixed settings to conversations with the brain

The most important trend is the shift from devices that do the same thing all the time to devices that sense and respond. A classic DBS system delivers constant pulses whether the patient is asleep or walking. An adaptive one can raise stimulation when tremor-related rhythms rise and lower it when they fall, potentially saving battery and reducing side effects. The epilepsy device that fires only at the start of a seizure follows the same logic.

This shift makes neurotechnology look more like a dialogue with the brain than a switch. It also depends on understanding which brain signals actually mean something, which is one of the big tasks discussed in The future of neuroscience. And it raises new questions. If a device decides when to change your brain activity, and your mood shifts, who made the decision? The more autonomous the device, the more the ethics of agency matter.

The core idea

Neurotechnology measures, changes or replaces nervous system function using electricity, magnetism, sound or light. The closer a tool gets to the neurons, the more precise and the more risky it is. The field's direction is from fixed stimulation to closed-loop devices that sense and respond.

The numbers worth knowing
  • DBS: discovered in 1987, approved in the US for Parkinson's in 2002, used by well over 100,000 people.
  • TMS: invented 1985, cleared in the US for depression in 2008.
  • Optogenetics: reported in 2005, now standard in animal research.
  • Roughly a third of people with epilepsy are not controlled by medication.
The common mistake

Treating all brain devices as equally proven. DBS for Parkinson's and a focus headband are both "neurotech", but their evidence is worlds apart.

The words, in plain words
Neurotechnology
Any device or method that measures or changes nervous system activity.
Invasive / non-invasive
Requiring surgery or entering the body, versus working from outside.
Deep brain stimulation (DBS)
Electrodes implanted deep in the brain, delivering adjustable electrical pulses.
TMS
Transcranial magnetic stimulation: magnetic pulses through the skull that make neurons below fire.
Focused ultrasound
Many sound beams aimed at one point to heat and destroy, or gently change, a small brain target.
Vagus nerve stimulation
Electrical pulses to a large nerve in the neck that carries signals into the brain.
tDCS
Transcranial direct current stimulation: a weak current passed through the scalp. Evidence is mixed.
fMRI
Functional MRI: a scan that tracks blood-oxygen changes as a sign of brain activity.
MEG
Magnetoencephalography: measuring the tiny magnetic fields produced by brain activity.
Optogenetics
Making chosen neurons respond to light, so researchers can switch them on and off.
Closed-loop
A device that senses the body's signals and adjusts its output automatically.
Neural data
Information recorded from the nervous system, now a protected category in some laws.
