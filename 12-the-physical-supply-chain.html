Sharp Mind 12 · how the world works

The physical supply chain. Eight things the modern world quietly runs on.

Six narrow places at sea that most of world trade squeezes through. A few hundred cables on the ocean floor that are the internet. Same eight parts per topic as file 11. Same short sentences.

One idea to carry through the whole file

You will see the same shape eight times, so it is worth naming now.

The choke point is almost never the raw material. It is the processing step.

Rare earths are not rare — separating them is hard. Lithium is not scarce — refining it is concentrated. Sand is everywhere — turning it into a chip needs one machine that one company makes. Once you are looking for the processing step, most supply chain news explains itself.

Part I · The machines

The hardest thing humans make

One topic, because it deserves the space. If you only read one entry in this file, read this one.

How it works 01

Computer chips, and the machine that prints them

In one sentence

A chip is a slice of silicon with billions of tiny switches printed onto it, in layers.

Think of it like…

Printing a photograph. But the photograph is a city map with billions of streets, the streets are a few dozen atoms wide, and you have to print sixty to a hundred of these maps on top of each other — each one lined up with the one below to a few atoms.

That is the whole business. It is printing, done to an accuracy that has no everyday comparison.

How it works · one layer, repeated a hundred times
① START WITH A WAFER
   A polished disc of extremely pure silicon, usually
   300 mm across. Silicon is sand, purified beyond
   anything else humans make.

② COAT IT WITH A LIGHT-SENSITIVE CHEMICAL
   Called photoresist. Light changes it.

③ SHINE LIGHT THROUGH A STENCIL
   The stencil (a "mask") carries the pattern for this
   layer. A lens shrinks the image down onto the wafer.

④ WASH THE RESIST AWAY
   Where the light hit, the chemical dissolves (or does
   not, depending on the type). The pattern is now
   physically on the wafer.

⑤ ETCH · ADD METAL · ADD IMPURITIES
   This builds the actual transistors and wires for
   this layer.

⑥ REPEAT 60 TO 100 TIMES
   Each new layer must line up with the layer below it
   to within a few nanometres. A nanometre is a
   millionth of a millimetre.

⑦ CUT, TEST, PACKAGE
   The wafer is sliced into individual chips, tested,
   and put in the black rectangle you recognise.

⭐ NOW THE PART THAT DECIDES EVERYTHING
  YOU CANNOT PRINT DETAIL SMALLER THAN YOUR LIGHT.
  Light has a wavelength. Try to print features much
  smaller than it and the image blurs.
  Older machines used ultraviolet light at 193
  nanometres. To print smaller features, the industry
  needed shorter light.

⭐ SO THEY BUILT EUV — EXTREME ULTRAVIOLET, 13.5 nm
  And here is how that light is made. This is not a
  simplification:

    · a droplet of molten tin falls
    · a laser hits it ONCE to flatten it into a pancake
    · a second laser pulse hits the pancake and blows
      it into plasma
    · the plasma glows at 13.5 nanometres
    · this happens FIFTY THOUSAND TIMES A SECOND

  ⚠ AND 13.5 nm LIGHT IS ABSORBED BY AIR. AND BY GLASS.
    So the whole machine is a vacuum, and there are no
    lenses — only mirrors. Those mirrors are the
    flattest objects ever manufactured: scaled up to
    the size of Germany, the biggest bump would be
    under a millimetre.
The numberWhat it means
One company. In the world.ASML, in the Netherlands, is the only maker of EUV machines. Not the leader — the only one. There is no second supplier and no substitute.
~$200 million each, and risingOne machine. It ships in many containers or several aircraft and takes months to install and calibrate.
~100,000 parts from thousands of suppliersIncluding the optics from Zeiss in Germany, which are their own near-monopoly. ASML is less a factory than the assembler of an enormous supply chain.
Tens of billions of transistors on one chipOn an advanced processor. Every one printed by the process above, and every one has to work.
What goes wrong
  • Everything is a single point of failure. One company for the machines. A handful for the chemicals, the wafers, the photoresist. And one company, TSMC in Taiwan, makes the large majority of the world's most advanced logic chips — on an island that China claims. There is no comfortable way to say this: it is the largest concentrated risk in the world economy.
  • Export controls are now the sharpest economic weapon in use. The US restricted sales of advanced chipmaking tools to China; the Netherlands and Japan followed, because the critical machines are theirs. You cannot build a modern fab without them, and you cannot buy them elsewhere. This is why "the chip war" is a real phrase and not a headline flourish.
  • Yield. When a new process starts, most chips coming off the line are defective. Over months and years the share that works climbs. That climb is the entire economics — the same factory can be losing money or printing money depending on it, and yield numbers are closely guarded.
  • A new fab costs $20 billion and takes years. So capacity always arrives late relative to demand, which is why chip shortages and chip gluts alternate.

Who controls it

Almost every step has one to three companies. ASML (machines, Netherlands), Zeiss (optics, Germany), TSMC (manufacturing, Taiwan), with Samsung and Intel behind, ARM (the designs in your phone, UK), Nvidia (AI chip design, US), Applied Materials / Lam / Tokyo Electron (the other tools), Shin-Etsu and SUMCO (the wafers, Japan).

Note how few countries that is.

Where you meet it

Everything with a plug or a battery. And more memorably: this is why you could not buy a car in 2021. Carmakers cancelled chip orders early in the pandemic, the capacity was reallocated to laptops and consoles, and when they came back the queue was two years long.

Say this

"There's exactly one company in the world that makes the machines that print advanced chips. It's Dutch."

"They make the light by firing a laser at a falling drop of molten tin, fifty thousand times a second. That's not a metaphor, that's the machine."

"The chip fight isn't really about chips. It's about who is allowed to buy the machines."

Part II · The rocks

Two materials everyone argues about

And in both cases, almost everyone argues about the wrong step.

How it works 02

Rare earths

In one sentence

Rare earths are seventeen metals used in tiny amounts to make magnets, screens and motors work.

Think of it like…

Salt in cooking. You use a very small amount. Without it, the dish does not work.

And one correction first, because it is the most common mistake about them: rare earths are not rare. Cerium is more common in the earth's crust than copper. The problem is that they are never found concentrated — they occur thinly, always mixed together, and the seventeen of them are chemically so similar that pulling them apart is genuinely difficult.

The word "rare" has misled two generations of headlines.

How it works · and why step three is the whole story
① MINE THE ORE
   It contains a mixture of several rare earths at low
   concentration, plus other things.

② CRUSH AND CONCENTRATE
   Standard mining. Nothing unusual.

③ ⭐ SEPARATE THEM — THE ACTUAL DIFFICULTY
   The seventeen elements are chemically almost
   identical. Ordinary chemistry cannot tell them
   apart. So you use SOLVENT EXTRACTION:
     · dissolve the mixture
     · shake it with an oily solvent that prefers ONE
       element very, very slightly over the others
     · take the tiny enrichment you got
     · do it again. And again. HUNDREDS OF TIMES, in
       long cascades of tanks
   ⇒ it is separating twins by shaking them, repeatedly,
     for a very long time. It is chemically hard, it is
     capital-heavy, and the practical know-how is not
     in a textbook.

④ TURN THE OXIDE INTO METAL, THEN INTO MAGNETS
   Neodymium alloyed with iron and boron makes the
   strongest permanent magnets there are: NdFeB.

WHERE THEY ACTUALLY MATTER
  NEODYMIUM + PRASEODYMIUM · the magnets in electric
    motors, wind turbine generators, hard drives,
    headphones, MRI machines
  DYSPROSIUM + TERBIUM · added so the magnet keeps
    working when it gets HOT. Critical for car motors,
    and the scarcest of the useful ones
  EUROPIUM, YTTRIUM · colour in screens and lighting
  LANTHANUM · catalysts in oil refining
The numberWhat it means
~1-2 kg of magnet in an EV motorSmall. But there is no substitute that performs as well, and there are tens of millions of motors.
Hundreds of kg to over a tonne in a big wind turbineDirect-drive offshore turbines use enormous permanent magnets, because they skip the gearbox. Cleaner energy, more rare earths.
China: most of the mining, far more of the separationRoughly 60-70% of mining, and something like 85-90%+ of separation and magnet manufacturing. The gap between those two figures is the entire point.
What goes wrong
  • The monopoly is in refining, not mining. A country can open a mine in a few years. Building the separation plants, and — harder — acquiring the operational know-how to run them at yield, takes much longer. Western mines have repeatedly opened and then shipped their ore to China to be separated, which does not solve the problem at all.
  • The environmental cost is real and it is why the West let it go. Rare earth ores usually contain thorium and uranium. The waste is mildly radioactive and the separation uses large volumes of acid. That is not a story China invented — it is a cost other countries chose not to carry.
  • It has been used as a lever, openly. China has restricted exports of gallium, germanium, graphite and rare-earth processing technology. Once demonstrated, a lever does not need to be pulled again to have an effect.
  • Substitution is possible and worse. Motors can be built without permanent magnets. They are heavier, less efficient, or both. Engineers can route around this — at a cost.
Say this

"Rare earths aren't rare. They're just never found concentrated, and separating them is brutally hard."

"The choke point isn't the mine, it's the refinery. That's true of lithium and cobalt too."

"The West didn't lose this industry. It decided not to want the waste."

How it works 03

Lithium

In one sentence

Lithium is the light metal that shuttles back and forth inside a rechargeable battery.

Think of it like…

The water in a water wheel. It is what moves to do the work — and it is not used up. It goes one way when you charge and back the other way when you discharge, thousands of times.

Which is also why battery recycling is genuinely possible: the lithium is still there at the end. It has not been burned.

How it works · two completely different ways to get it
ROUTE A · BRINE — South America
  Chile, Argentina, Bolivia — the "lithium triangle"
  ① pump salty water from under a salt flat into
    enormous shallow ponds
  ② let the sun evaporate it. FOR TWELVE TO EIGHTEEN
    MONTHS. You can see the ponds from space; they are
    turquoise and green
  ③ what is left is concentrated enough to process into
    lithium carbonate
  ⇒ cheap, very slow, and it consumes water in one of
    the driest places on earth

ROUTE B · HARD ROCK — Australia, and now Africa
  ① mine spodumene ore — ordinary open-pit mining
  ② crush it
  ③ ROAST IT at about 1,050°C. This changes the crystal
    structure so acid can get at the lithium
  ④ leach with acid, purify
  ⇒ faster, more energy-hungry, and MOST OF THE ROCK IS
    SHIPPED TO CHINA FOR THE CHEMICAL STEP

THEN, EITHER WAY
  Convert to battery-grade lithium carbonate or
  hydroxide. The purity required is extreme — parts per
  million of the wrong thing ruins a battery.
  ⭐ AND THIS LAST STEP IS WHERE THE CONCENTRATION IS.
    Australia digs the most. Chile evaporates a lot.
    China does most of the chemistry.
The numberWhat it means
~8-10 kg per electric carLithium content in a typical EV battery. Less than people imagine — the battery's weight is mostly other things.
Up roughly tenfold, then down againThe lithium price in 2021-22, then the collapse that followed. This is not a malfunction. It is what mined commodities do.
5-10+ years to open a new mineFrom discovery to production, including permits. Demand forecasts change in months. That mismatch is the price cycle, and it will happen again.
What goes wrong
  • Water in the Atacama. Brine extraction uses enormous volumes of water in one of the driest inhabited places on earth, next to communities who were not consulted early and whose objections are reasonable. The industry's answer is direct lithium extraction, which uses far less water, and which is mostly still being proven.
  • The price whipsaw destroys planning. When lithium is expensive everyone announces mines. By the time the mines open the price has collapsed and half of them are cancelled. Then supply is short again.
  • There is not much to recycle yet, because the first big wave of electric cars is still on the road. Recycling is real and growing and cannot meet demand until those cars retire — which is roughly a decade of lag built into the system.
Say this

"Australia digs it, Chile evaporates it, China refines it. The refining is the part that matters."

"Lithium went up tenfold and then collapsed. That's normal for mining — you order a mine ten years before you get it."

Part III · The moving

How things and information actually travel

Two systems. Both are far more physical, and far more concentrated, than almost anyone assumes.

How it works 04

Container shipping, and the six narrow places

In one sentence

Almost everything you own arrived in a steel box on a ship.

Think of it like…

A postal system where the envelope is the same size everywhere in the world — so nobody has to open it until the very end.

That is the entire invention. Before the container, loading a ship meant men carrying sacks and crates by hand, which was slow, expensive and easy to steal from. Malcolm McLean standardised the box in 1956 and loading costs fell by something like ninety-five percent.

Globalisation is not mainly a political event. It is a consequence of a steel box. Once moving goods became almost free, it stopped mattering where you made them.

How it works, and where it can be squeezed
THE SYSTEM
  ① the box is standard — TEU means "twenty-foot
    equivalent unit", the counting unit for everything
  ② the largest ships carry about 24,000 of them
  ③ hub and spoke: giant ships run between a few huge
    ports, smaller "feeder" ships serve everywhere else
  ④ a ship is ordered about two to three years before
    it is delivered
    ⇒ REMEMBER THAT LAG. It is why freight rates are
      always either far too high or far too low, and
      almost never right

⭐ THE CHOKEPOINTS — six narrow places, and the reason
  the shipping news is really geography news

  STRAIT OF HORMUZ
    a narrow gap between Iran and Oman. Roughly a fifth
    of the world's oil passes through it. There is no
    alternative route for most of it.

  SUEZ CANAL
    roughly 12-15% of global trade. When it was blocked
    by one grounded ship in 2021, the world noticed.
    Since 2023, attacks in the Red Sea have pushed much
    traffic around the bottom of Africa instead —
    adding about ten to fourteen days and a great deal
    of fuel.

  PANAMA CANAL
    ⭐ and this one is strange: IT IS LIMITED BY RAIN.
    The canal lifts ships over a hill using fresh water
    from Gatún Lake, and every ship that passes spends
    lake water. In a drought, the canal cuts the number
    of daily crossings. A drought in Panama is a global
    logistics event.

  STRAIT OF MALACCA
    the narrow way between Asia and the Indian Ocean. A
    very large share of China's imported oil goes
    through it, which is a fact Chinese strategy thinks
    about constantly.

  BAB EL-MANDEB · the entrance to the Red Sea
  THE BOSPORUS   · grain out of the Black Sea
The numberWhat it means
80-90% of world trade by volumeMoves by sea. Air freight is fast, expensive and tiny by weight.
~30-40 days Asia to EuropeNormally, via Suez. Around Africa instead, add about ten to fourteen days — and that extra time also removes ships from the market, which pushes rates up for everyone.
$1,500 → $14,000 → backRoughly what one container cost on the main Asia-Europe route through 2020-22. Tenfold, and back. If you have ever wondered why logistics people are fatalistic, this is why.
What goes wrong
  • Chokepoints are few and narrow, and the alternatives are longer rather than impossible. That is the important nuance: a closed strait usually means expensive and slow, not stopped. But expensive and slow, applied to everything at once, is enough to move inflation.
  • The three-year ordering lag guarantees overshoot. Rates spike, everyone orders ships, the ships arrive together three years later into a weak market, rates collapse. This cycle has run for a century and will run again.
  • Ports are a constraint too — depth for the biggest ships, cranes, and labour. A strike at a handful of ports backs up a continent.
  • Shipping is about 3% of global CO2 and is hard to clean up, because a battery big enough to cross the Pacific does not exist. Hence the interest in ammonia and methanol as fuels — which loops back to file 11.
Say this

"Eighty to ninety percent of world trade goes by sea, and it funnels through about six narrow places."

"The Panama Canal is limited by rainfall. It's a freshwater canal — every ship that crosses spends lake water."

"Freight rates went up tenfold and came back down. Shipping always overbuilds, because you order a ship three years before you get it."

How it works 05

Undersea cables

In one sentence

Nearly all international internet traffic travels through glass cables lying on the seabed.

Think of it like…

Not a cloud. A few hundred garden hoses on the bottom of the ocean.

This is the single most useful correction in this file. People picture the internet as satellites and air. It is not. Satellites carry a tiny fraction of international traffic. When you video-call another continent, your voice goes down a cable on the seabed and comes up on a beach somewhere.

How it works
① THE CABLE ITSELF
   Inside: a few pairs of glass fibres, each thinner
   than a human hair. Around them: steel wires for
   strength, a copper conductor, and plastic.
   In deep water it is about as thick as a garden hose.
   Near shore, where anchors and trawlers are, it is
   armoured and much thicker.

② LIGHT CARRIES THE DATA
   Pulses of laser light travel down the glass. The
   glass is so pure that the light can go 50 to 100 km
   before it fades too much.

③ ⭐ REPEATERS — and how they are powered
   Every 50-100 km sits an amplifier that boosts the
   light. It needs electricity, and it is at the bottom
   of the ocean.
   So: a few thousand volts of direct current is pushed
   along the copper conductor FROM THE SHORE, sometimes
   across an entire ocean, to feed them.

④ LANDING STATIONS
   The cable comes ashore into an unremarkable building.
   There are not many of these buildings, and they are
   listed on public maps.
The numberWhat it means
~550 cables, ~1.4 million kmThat is the entire physical internet between continents. It is a surprisingly short list.
100-200 faults a yearWorldwide. The overwhelming majority are fishing gear and ship anchors, not sabotage.
Hundreds of terabits per secondOn one modern cable. Capacity has grown enormously without laying many more cables, because the equipment at each end keeps improving.
What goes wrong
  • Anchors and trawlers. Most breaks are accidents. Repair means sending a ship, finding the fault, grappling the cable up from the seabed, cutting out the bad section, splicing in new cable, and lowering it back. Days to weeks.
  • The repair fleet is small and old. There are only a few dozen cable ships in the world and many are near the end of their lives. This is a genuine and underdiscussed fragility.
  • Some corridors carry far too much. The Red Sea route and the Luzon Strait each carry a large share of traffic between regions. Several cuts in one corridor is a different event from several cuts spread around.
  • Sabotage is now a live concern, after cable and pipeline incidents in the Baltic. The difficulty is that a dragged anchor and a deliberately dragged anchor look identical on the seabed.
  • Ownership has quietly changed. Cables used to be built by consortia of telecoms companies. Now Google, Meta, Amazon and Microsoft own or co-own a large share of new capacity. The physical layer of the internet is moving into the hands of a few content companies, and almost nobody is discussing it.
Say this

"The cloud is a few hundred cables on the seabed. Satellites carry almost none of the internet."

"Most cable breaks are anchors and fishing boats. The worrying part isn't sabotage — it's that the repair fleet is tiny and ageing."

Part IV · The invisible

Three systems nobody thinks about

One tells the world what time it is. One is rewriting who owns the sky. One feeds half the planet.

How it works 06

GPS — and why it is really a clock

In one sentence

GPS tells you where you are by telling you, extremely precisely, what time it is.

Think of it like…

Thunder and lightning. You see the flash, then you hear the bang. The gap tells you how far away the storm is, because you know how fast sound travels.

GPS does exactly that with radio instead of sound, and with clocks accurate to billionths of a second. That is the whole idea. Everything else is arithmetic.

How it works
① EACH SATELLITE CARRIES AN ATOMIC CLOCK
   And it broadcasts, continuously: "I am satellite
   number X, my position is Y, and the time right now
   is exactly Z."

② YOUR PHONE RECEIVES THAT MESSAGE, SLIGHTLY LATE
   The signal travelled at the speed of light, so it
   arrived a fraction of a second after it was sent.
   That delay × the speed of light = your distance from
   that satellite.

③ ONE SATELLITE PUTS YOU ON A SPHERE
   Two satellites: a circle. Three: two possible points,
   one of which is absurd. Four: your position, AND a
   correction for the fact that your phone's own clock
   is cheap and slightly wrong.
   ⇒ that fourth satellite is why your phone does not
     need an atomic clock in it.

④ ⭐ AND RELATIVITY IS NOT OPTIONAL HERE
   The satellite clocks run FAST compared to clocks on
   the ground — because gravity is weaker up there —
   and SLOW because they are moving quickly. The two
   effects do not cancel. Net result: about 38
   MICROSECONDS PER DAY fast.
   Uncorrected, your position would drift by roughly
   TEN KILOMETRES after a single day.
   ⇒ GPS is the most ordinary, everyday proof of
     Einstein's theories that exists. It is in your
     pocket and it would not work without them.

⭐ THE PART ALMOST NOBODY KNOWS
  GPS IS MAINLY A TIMING SYSTEM, NOT A MAP.
    · banks timestamp trades with it
    · power grids synchronise with it
    · mobile networks use it to hand your call from one
      tower to the next
    · data centres use it to keep logs in order
  ⇒ if GPS stopped, losing your way in the car would be
    nowhere near the biggest problem.
The numberWhat it means
~31 satellites, ~20,200 km upEnough that four are visible from almost anywhere, almost always.
A few metres, or a few centimetresOrdinary phone accuracy versus surveying accuracy with a correction signal. The difference is not better satellites — it is a second receiver at a known fixed point.
Four systems, not oneGPS (US), Galileo (EU), GLONASS (Russia), BeiDou (China), plus regional ones. Your phone quietly uses several at once, which is why it works better than it used to.
What goes wrong
  • The signal is extraordinarily weak. It has come 20,000 km from a transmitter the power of a light bulb. By the time it reaches you it is below the background noise, and your phone digs it out with clever maths. Which means it is easy to overpower.
  • Jamming means drowning it in noise so you get nothing. Spoofing means broadcasting a convincing fake so you confidently get the wrong answer, which is far worse. Both now happen routinely around conflict zones, and civil aircraft have been affected.
  • The timing dependence is a systemic risk nobody has priced. Many systems that quietly rely on GPS time have no backup, and the backups that exist — like ground-based timing networks — have been allowed to decay.
Say this

"GPS works by telling you the time. Your position is just arithmetic on top of that."

"It's mostly a timing system. Banks and power grids depend on it more than drivers do."

"The satellite clocks run about 38 microseconds a day fast because of relativity. Without that correction you'd be ten kilometres out by tomorrow."

How it works 07

Satellite constellations

In one sentence

Thousands of small satellites flying low and fast, so internet from space stops being slow.

Think of it like…

Old satellite internet was one enormous radio mast, very far away — 36,000 km up. It covered a third of the planet from a single point, which is wonderful, except that the signal had to travel 36,000 km up and 36,000 km back down. That round trip takes half a second, and half a second ruins a phone call.

The new approach: thousands of much shorter masts, much closer, all moving. One is always overhead. Your dish just has to keep handing you from one to the next.

How it works
① THE OLD WAY — GEOSTATIONARY, 35,786 km
   At exactly that height, one orbit takes 24 hours, so
   the satellite appears to hang still above one spot.
   Brilliant for television. Round trip of 500+
   milliseconds, which is fine for broadcasting and
   useless for a conversation.

② THE NEW WAY — LOW EARTH ORBIT, about 550 km
   Round trip: 25-50 milliseconds. Comparable to a
   normal broadband connection.
   ⚠ BUT at that height the satellite crosses the whole
     sky in a few minutes. So you need thousands of them
     for anyone to have continuous service. That is why
     these systems are all-or-nothing: a hundred
     satellites is useless, four thousand is a product.

③ YOUR DISH DOES NOT MOVE
   It is a "phased array": a flat panel of many small
   antennas. By changing the timing of each one very
   slightly, it steers its beam electronically, with no
   moving parts. Same trick as modern radar.

④ THE NEWER SATELLITES TALK TO EACH OTHER BY LASER
   So data can hop across the constellation without
   coming down to the ground. Which means service over
   oceans, and fewer ground stations.

⭐ AND THE THING THAT ACTUALLY MADE IT POSSIBLE
  Not the satellites — REUSABLE ROCKETS. Launch got
  cheap enough that putting thousands of objects in
  orbit stopped being absurd. The enabling invention is
  underneath, not above.
What goes wrong
  • Space is getting crowded. One operator now accounts for the majority of all active satellites. The worry has a name — Kessler syndrome — where one collision creates debris that causes more collisions, and a useful orbit becomes unusable for decades.
  • Astronomy. The satellites leave bright streaks across long-exposure images and interfere with radio telescopes. Operators have made real efforts to darken them; it helps and does not solve it.
  • They are designed to burn up on re-entry, which is good for debris. At these numbers, what all that vaporised aluminium does to the upper atmosphere is an open scientific question with no settled answer yet.
  • A new kind of dependence. When a single private company supplies connectivity to a country at war, and can switch it on or off, that is a form of power that has no precedent and no established rules.
Say this

"Old satellite internet was slow because the satellite was 36,000 km up. The fix wasn't better radio. It was flying lower, and using thousands of them."

"The thing that made it possible was cheap launch, not the satellites."

How it works 08

Fertiliser — the process that feeds half the world

In one sentence

Haber-Bosch pulls nitrogen out of the air and turns it into fertiliser — and roughly half the people alive are fed because of it.

Think of it like…

Being surrounded by food you cannot open.

The air is 78% nitrogen. Plants need nitrogen to grow. And plants cannot use the nitrogen in the air — because the two nitrogen atoms are held together by one of the strongest bonds in chemistry, and breaking it is very hard.

So for all of history, farming was limited by how much usable nitrogen you could find — manure, clover, bird droppings shipped from Peru. Haber-Bosch is the key that opens the air. It is probably the most consequential chemical process ever invented, and almost nobody has heard of it.

How it works
① THE PROBLEM IS ONE BOND
   Nitrogen in air is two atoms locked together by a
   TRIPLE bond. Very strong. Very stable. That stability
   is exactly why the air is not constantly reacting
   with everything.

② YOU NEED HYDROGEN
   And today, almost all of it comes from NATURAL GAS.
   ⇒ this is the "grey hydrogen" from file 11, and it is
     the single most important fact in this entry.

③ FORCE THEM TOGETHER
   Nitrogen + hydrogen, at about 400-500°C and 150-300
   times atmospheric pressure, over an iron catalyst.
   Out comes ammonia.
   The heat and pressure are why this needs an enormous
   plant and not a laboratory.

④ AMMONIA BECOMES FERTILISER
   Urea, ammonium nitrate, and the rest. It is also the
   feedstock for explosives, which is how the process
   was first funded.

⭐ WHY IT MATTERS SO MUCH
  Roughly HALF of the world's food production depends on
  synthetic fertiliser. Commonly cited estimates say
  about half the nitrogen atoms in your own body passed
  through a Haber-Bosch plant.
  ⇒ you are, in a real and literal sense, partly made of
    natural gas.
The numberWhat it means
1-2% of all the energy humans useGoes into this one process. For one product.
~1.5-2% of global CO2Ammonia production. Almost all of it from the hydrogen step, not the reaction itself.
Half the world's foodDepends on it. There is no gradual substitute — organic farming at global scale would not feed the current population.
What goes wrong
  • Fertiliser is made from natural gas, so fertiliser prices follow gas prices. When European gas prices spiked in 2022, ammonia plants across Europe simply shut down — it was cheaper to stop than to run. That is how an energy crisis in one region becomes a food price crisis everywhere. If you take one thing from this entry, take this chain.
  • Runoff. Excess nitrogen washes off fields into rivers, feeds algae, and the algae die and consume the oxygen — creating dead zones where nothing lives. The one at the mouth of the Mississippi is thousands of square kilometres.
  • Nitrous oxide released from fertilised soil is a greenhouse gas far more potent than CO2, molecule for molecule.
  • Concentration again. Russia and Belarus are among the largest exporters of nitrogen and potash fertilisers. Sanctions and export restrictions on them land, eventually, on food prices in countries with no connection to the conflict.
  • Green ammonia — made with green hydrogen instead of gas — is the decarbonisation route, and it is currently expensive. It is also one of the few genuinely strong cases for hydrogen.
Say this

"Haber-Bosch takes nitrogen out of the air and turns it into fertiliser. Roughly half the people alive are fed because of it."

"Fertiliser is made from natural gas. That's why a European gas crisis turns into a global food crisis."

The words, in plain words

When they say…They mean
TEUTwenty-foot equivalent unit. One standard shipping container. The counting unit for everything at sea.
ChokepointA narrow place that a lot of trade must pass through, with no cheap alternative.
The bullwhip effectA small change in customer demand causes a bigger swing at the retailer, a bigger one at the wholesaler, and an enormous one at the factory. Each layer over-reacts to protect itself. It is the central phenomenon of supply chains.
Lead timeHow long between ordering something and having it. Long lead times are what make the bullwhip worse.
FabA semiconductor factory. Costs tens of billions.
YieldThe share of chips off the line that actually work. The entire economics of a fab.
Node (3nm, 5nm)A marketing name for a generation of chip manufacturing. It has not literally meant a measured size for years.
LithographyThe printing step in chipmaking. EUV is the advanced kind.
FablessA company that designs chips and pays someone else to make them. Nvidia, Apple, Qualcomm.
FoundryA company that makes chips other people designed. TSMC.
Critical mineralsA political term, not a geological one. It means "materials we would struggle to get if a particular country stopped selling."
Onshoring / friend-shoringMoving production home, or to allies. Expensive, slow, and the current direction of policy in most of the West.
Just-in-timeHolding almost no stock and relying on deliveries arriving exactly when needed. Extremely efficient and extremely fragile, as 2020-22 demonstrated to everyone at once.

What next

The one pattern, stated once more
⭐ THE CHOKE POINT IS ALMOST NEVER THE RAW MATERIAL.
  IT IS THE PROCESSING STEP.

  sand            → the machine that prints it (one
                    company, Netherlands)
  rare earth ore  → the separation plants (China)
  lithium rock    → the chemical refining (China)
  the whole ocean → six narrow straits
  the internet    → a few hundred cables and a small,
                    ageing repair fleet
  the air itself  → a handful of ammonia plants running
                    on natural gas

⇒ SO WHEN YOU READ ABOUT A SHORTAGE, ASK:
  ① IS THE SHORTAGE IN THE GROUND, OR IN THE FACTORY?
    (almost always the factory)
  ② HOW LONG DOES IT TAKE TO BUILD ANOTHER ONE?
    (mines 5-10 years, fabs 3-5, ships 2-3, cable
     repair ships basically nobody is building)
  ③ WHO ELSE COULD MAKE IT, AND WHY DON'T THEY?
    (usually: they could, it would be expensive, and
     they decided not to want the waste or the risk)

⭐ AND THE HONEST SUMMARY OF THE LAST FIVE YEARS:
  the world optimised every one of these systems for
  cost, which meant concentrating them, which meant
  removing the slack. That worked extremely well until
  it did not, and "resilience" is the word for buying
  the slack back.
