The sky. Everything above your head, and how we know any of it.

Astronomy is the one science where you cannot touch anything, visit anything, or run an experiment. Everything we know about a star comes from light that left it a long time ago. What is remarkable is how much can be read out of that — what a star is made of, how fast it is moving, whether it has planets, and what the universe was doing before there were any stars at all.

Part I

How anyone knows

Chapter 01

Light carries a signature

Split sunlight with a prism and you get a rainbow — and if you look closely, the rainbow has thin dark lines missing from it, always in the same places.

Those gaps are the key to the entire subject. Each chemical element absorbs light at its own precise set of colours and nothing else's. Hydrogen removes one particular set, sodium another. So the pattern of missing lines in starlight is a fingerprint of what that star is made of — read from here, without going anywhere.

The same trick does more. If an object is moving away, all its light is stretched slightly towards red; moving closer, squeezed towards blue. It is the same effect as a siren dropping in pitch as it passes you. So the fingerprint tells you composition and its shift tells you speed and direction.

Almost everything in this volume was established by looking very carefully at the colours of faint smudges.

Chapter 02

Looking out is looking back

Light travels fast but not instantly, so everything you see is out of date by the time it takes the light to arrive.

The Sun you see is eight minutes old. If it went out you would have eight minutes of ordinary afternoon before noticing. The nearest star is four years old. Some galaxies you can photograph are showing you light that left before Earth existed.

Astronomers find this useful rather than frustrating. Looking further away is looking further back in time, so to study the early universe you simply look at very distant things — you cannot see the young universe nearby, because nearby things have had as long to age as we have.

It also means that when you look at the night sky you are not seeing a scene. You are seeing a composite of moments, each from a different time, arriving together.

Part II

Stars

Chapter 03

What a star actually is

A star is a balance between two things trying to win.

Gravity pulls all that material inward, and would collapse it entirely. Pressure from the heat inside pushes outward, and would blow it apart. A star is what you get when the two are exactly matched, and it stays that way for as long as it can keep generating heat.

The heat comes from the middle, where gravity has squeezed matter so hard that hydrogen nuclei are forced together to make helium — and the product weighs a fraction less than the ingredients, with that missing fraction released as energy. It is the same relationship as the fission in Volume 24, working in the opposite direction.

So a star is a controlled and extremely long-lived version of the reaction in a hydrogen bomb, held in check by its own weight. The Sun has been doing this for about four and a half billion years and has roughly the same again to go.

The counter-intuitive rule

Bigger stars live much shorter lives. More mass means more gravity, more compression, and a far hotter core burning through fuel at an enormous rate. A star ten times the Sun's mass does not last ten times as long; it lasts a tiny fraction as long — millions of years rather than billions. The biggest stars are the briefest.

Chapter 04

How stars die, and why it matters to you

When the fuel runs low, the balance breaks, and what happens next depends entirely on mass.

A star like the Sun swells enormously, sheds its outer layers gently into space, and leaves behind a dense hot cinder that cools over an extremely long time.

A much heavier star collapses suddenly and rebounds in an explosion bright enough to outshine its entire galaxy for weeks. What it leaves is either an object so dense that a teaspoon would weigh as much as a mountain, or — if heavy enough — a black hole.

Here is why this concerns you personally. The universe began with essentially only the lightest elements. Everything heavier was manufactured inside stars — carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, iron — and the heaviest elements in violent stellar deaths and collisions. Those explosions scattered the material into space, where it eventually gathered into new stars and planets.

The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were made inside a star that exploded before the Sun existed. This is not a metaphor. It is a sober description of where the atoms came from.

Chapter 05

Black holes

Squeeze enough mass into a small enough space and the gravity near it becomes so severe that nothing can get away — not even light, which is why it is black.

The boundary is not a surface. It is simply the distance at which escape becomes impossible, and crossing it would not feel like anything in particular. But from outside, that boundary is a genuine edge of the observable, because no information from inside can ever reach you.

Two misunderstandings are worth clearing. A black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner — its gravity at a distance is exactly the same as any object of the same mass. Replace the Sun with a black hole of identical mass and Earth would orbit undisturbed, and rather cold. And they are not rare oddities; there is an enormous one at the centre of our galaxy and of most others, and we now have images of two.

The oddest consequence is about time. Gravity slows time, so to a distant observer someone falling in appears to slow, redden and freeze at the boundary, never quite crossing. To the person falling, nothing unusual happens and they cross straight through. Both descriptions are correct, which takes some getting used to.

Part III

The whole thing

Chapter 06

Why we think it began

In the 1920s it was found that almost every distant galaxy shows the red stretch of something moving away, and the further away it is the faster it is going.

The natural reading is that everything is getting further apart. And if you run that backwards, everything was closer together, and further back still, extremely close and extremely hot.

Two things worth correcting. It was not an explosion in space with a centre you could point to — it is space itself expanding, everywhere at once, which is why every galaxy sees everything else receding from it and none is the centre. The usual image is dots on a balloon being inflated: every dot sees every other dot move away, and no dot is special.

And the strongest evidence is not the receding galaxies but something predicted before it was found. If the universe was once hot and dense, there should be leftover heat from that period, now stretched into a faint glow at a particular temperature, coming from every direction. In 1964 two engineers troubleshooting a persistent hiss in a radio antenna — they checked for pigeon droppings — found exactly that. A prediction made in advance, confirmed accidentally by people looking for a fault. That is about as strong as evidence gets.

Chapter 07

The two things we cannot see

Galaxies spin far too fast. At the observed speeds, the visible matter's gravity is nowhere near enough to hold them together — they should have flung themselves apart long ago.

Something is providing the extra gravity, and it emits no light at all. It is called dark matter, and that name is an admission rather than an explanation: we know roughly how much there is and where it is, and we do not know what it is. Every candidate so far has failed to be detected.

The second surprise came in the 1990s. The expansion was expected to be slowing under gravity. Measurements showed it is speeding up. Something is pushing space apart, and it is called dark energy, which is again a label for an unknown.

Put together, ordinary matter — everything in every other chapter of this volume, every star and planet and person — is something like five per cent of the universe. The rest is two things we have named and cannot identify.

This is worth knowing precisely because it is humbling. Modern cosmology is spectacularly successful at predicting what it can predict, and it is also a subject in which ninety-five per cent of the contents are unaccounted for.

Chapter 08

Is anyone else out there

We now know that planets are ordinary. Thousands have been found around other stars, mostly by watching for the tiny regular dip in brightness as a planet passes in front, and the statistics suggest planets outnumber stars.

Many are in the range of distance from their star where liquid water could exist. That is not proof of anything — it is a statement about temperature, not about life — but it removes the objection that suitable places might be rare.

Against which sits the awkward observation: if life is common, and some of it becomes capable of spreading or signalling, and the galaxy is old, we should probably have noticed something. We have not.

The proposed resolutions are all unsettling in one way or another. Perhaps life is far harder to start than we assume. Perhaps simple life is common and complex life is not. Perhaps civilisations reliably destroy themselves. Perhaps they exist and communicate in ways we are not equipped to detect, or have no interest in announcing themselves.

Nobody knows, and the honest position is that this is a genuinely open question where confident answers in either direction are unearned.
