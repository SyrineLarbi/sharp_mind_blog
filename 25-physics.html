Physics. How the universe behaves, without a single number.

Physics has a reputation for being the subject where the equations live, and that reputation hides how few ideas are actually doing the work. There are perhaps a dozen, and every one of them can be understood in plain language by anybody. This volume explains what is actually happening — in a falling object, a magnet, a hot cup of tea, a beam of light — and contains no mathematics whatsoever.

Part I

Things that move

Chapter 01

The idea that took two thousand years

Watch a rolling ball and it slows down and stops. Watch a pushed cart and it stops when you stop pushing. The obvious conclusion — and Aristotle drew it, and it went unchallenged for two millennia — is that motion needs a cause. Things naturally come to rest.

It is wrong, and it is wrong in an interesting way. The ball stops because of friction and air, and if you remove those it does not stop. But you cannot remove them on Earth, so the truth is hidden behind the very thing that makes it look false.

What is actually true is the opposite. Things keep doing what they are doing. Something moving keeps moving, in a straight line, at the same speed, forever, unless something pushes it. Something still stays still until something pushes it. Motion does not need a cause. Change in motion needs a cause.

That is why you lurch forward when a bus brakes. You were moving; the bus stopped; nothing stopped you. You are not thrown forward — you are simply continuing, and the bus has stopped cooperating.

Chapter 02

Why heavy things do not fall faster

Drop a hammer and a feather and the hammer lands first, which seems to settle the matter. It does not. Take them to the Moon, where there is no air, and they land together — an astronaut did this on camera precisely to show it.

The feather loses because air resistance affects it enormously relative to its weight, not because it is lighter. Strip the air away and everything falls at exactly the same rate.

Why? Because a heavier object is pulled harder, and is also harder to get moving, and those two effects cancel exactly. Double the mass and you double the pull, but you also double the reluctance to accelerate. The result is the same fall for everything.

Why they cancel so precisely was an unexplained coincidence for two hundred years, and the man who explained it is in the next chapter.

Chapter 03

Gravity is not a force pulling you down

Newton described what gravity does superbly and admitted he had no idea what it was. Action at a distance across empty space, instantly, with no visible connection, bothered him considerably.

Einstein's answer was that there is no pull at all. Instead, mass bends space and time around it, and things moving through bent space follow curved paths — not because something is tugging them, but because that is now what "straight ahead" means there.

The usual picture is a heavy ball on a stretched rubber sheet, making a dip that a rolling marble curves into. It is a decent picture with one serious flaw: it explains gravity by using gravity to pull the ball into the sheet. Take it as a hint rather than an explanation.

The better way in is this. You cannot tell the difference between standing on Earth and standing in a sealed room being accelerated upwards in deep space. Everything falls the same way in both. If no experiment can distinguish them, perhaps they are the same thing — and gravity is not a force but what you feel when the floor pushes you off the path you would otherwise take.

This sounds like philosophy and it is testable. Bent space bends light, and starlight passing the Sun during an eclipse is deflected by exactly the predicted amount. Clocks run measurably slower lower down in a gravitational field, which is not a quirk — your phone's satellite navigation corrects for it constantly, and would drift by kilometres a day if it did not.

Chapter 04

Why nothing goes faster than light

The strange fact underneath relativity is this: however fast you are moving, light always passes you at the same speed. Chase a light beam at almost light speed and it still recedes from you at full speed.

That should be impossible. If I run after a train at half its speed, it pulls away at half speed. Light does not do this.

Einstein took the observation seriously and asked what would have to be true for it to work. The answer is that space and time must adjust instead. If everyone must measure the same speed for light, and speed is distance divided by time, then people moving differently must disagree about distances and about durations.

So a fast-moving clock genuinely runs slow, as seen from here. This is not an illusion and it is not theoretical: particles created in the upper atmosphere that should decay long before reaching the ground arrive anyway, because at their speed their internal clock is running slow enough to survive the trip.

And the speed limit follows. The faster something goes, the harder it becomes to speed it up further — the resistance grows without limit as you approach light speed, so reaching it would take infinite effort. Light manages it only because it has no mass at all.

Part II

Heat, light and electricity

Chapter 05

Heat is just jiggling

Temperature is not a substance. Everything is made of particles, and those particles are always moving — vibrating in a solid, sliding past each other in a liquid, flying about in a gas. Temperature is how vigorously they are moving. That is all it is.

So heating something means making its particles move faster, and cooling means slowing them. Touch something hot and its fast particles knock into yours and speed them up, which you experience as heat flowing in.

Several everyday puzzles dissolve at once. A metal spoon feels colder than a wooden one at the same temperature because metal conducts — it takes heat out of your hand quickly — while wood does not, so it is a statement about your hand, not the object. Ice cools your drink not mainly by being cold but by melting, because breaking the solid structure apart absorbs a surprisingly large amount of energy. And there is a coldest possible temperature, where the jiggling stops, and nothing can be colder because there is no slower than stopped.

Chapter 06

Why your tea never spontaneously reheats

Heat always flows from hot to cold and never the other way. Nothing forbids the reverse — the particles could all happen to knock energy back into the cup — it is simply overwhelmingly unlikely.

Here is the reason, and it is one of the deepest ideas in science. There are very few arrangements of particles we would call ordered and an unimaginably larger number we would call disordered. Shuffle at random and you land in the huge group essentially every time. Things run down not because of a force pushing them, but because there are vastly more ways to be messy than tidy.

That is why a broken cup never reassembles, why smoke never gathers back into the cigarette, and why you can tell a film is running backwards. It is also the only law of physics that distinguishes past from future — everything else works equally well in either direction.

Life appears to violate it, building order out of disorder. It does not: a living thing maintains its own tidiness by making a larger mess outside itself, which is what eating and breathing are for.

Chapter 07

Electricity and magnetism are the same thing

Electric current is charged particles flowing, normally electrons drifting through metal. Voltage is the push behind them. Resistance is how much the material obstructs them, and obstruction makes heat, which is how a kettle and a light bulb work.

The surprising discovery — made when a lecturer noticed a compass needle twitch near a wire — is that a moving charge creates magnetism, and a moving magnet creates current. They are two faces of one phenomenon.

Almost all electrical technology is those two sentences. A motor puts current into a magnetic field to make something turn. A generator turns something in a magnetic field to make current. Spin a magnet near a coil and you have a power station, whether the thing spinning it is steam, falling water or wind.

And when a charge wobbles, the disturbance travels outward as a wave of electricity and magnetism taking turns — which turns out to be light. Radio waves, microwaves, visible colours, X-rays: all the same phenomenon, differing only in how rapidly they wobble. Your eyes detect one narrow band of it, and your radio another.

Chapter 08

Why the sky is blue and sunsets are red

Sunlight contains all colours together. As it passes through the air it strikes molecules and scatters off them — and the shorter, bluer wavelengths scatter far more than the longer, redder ones.

So blue light gets bounced around the sky in all directions, and when you look up at any part of the sky away from the Sun, the light reaching you is that scattered blue. The sky is blue because you are seeing sunlight that has been knocked sideways.

At sunset the light comes in at a shallow angle through far more air. By the time it reaches you almost all the blue has been scattered out of it, leaving the reds and oranges that survive the journey. Sunsets are red for exactly the same reason the sky is blue — you are seeing what is left after the blue has been taken away.

Part III

The very small

Chapter 09

Why the quantum world is strange

At everyday scales, things have a definite position and speed. Very small things do not behave this way, and the difference is not a limitation of our instruments.

Two ideas carry most of it.

The first is lumpiness. Energy does not come in a smooth continuum but in indivisible steps, like coins rather than liquid. An electron in an atom cannot sit between two allowed levels any more than you can have half a penny. This is where the word quantum comes from, and it explains why atoms emit only specific colours — each colour corresponds to a particular jump.

The second is spread-outness. Before it is measured, a small particle does not have a single location. It has a range of possibilities, all genuinely there at once, and measuring forces one of them.

The classic demonstration fires particles one at a time at a barrier with two slits. Each arrives as a single dot. But over thousands of arrivals a striped pattern builds up that can only happen if each particle somehow went through both slits and interfered with itself. Put a detector on the slits to catch it doing this, and the stripes vanish and it behaves like an ordinary particle. Looking changes the outcome.

Nobody has an agreed explanation of why this is so. The mathematics predicts experiments to extraordinary precision and physicists disagree fundamentally about what it means. That is an honest state of affairs and worth knowing: this is not a topic where you are failing to understand something everyone else has grasped.

Chapter 10

What everything is made of

Matter is made of atoms. Atoms have a tiny dense nucleus with a cloud of electrons around it, and they are overwhelmingly empty — if the nucleus were a marble on a football pitch, the electrons would be somewhere around the stands.

Which raises a fair question: if atoms are mostly nothing, why is a table solid? Not because the particles are packed tight. It is because electrons repel other electrons, and because they refuse to occupy the same state as each other. You are not resting on matter. You are hovering on electrical repulsion, a tiny distance above the chair. Nothing you touch have you ever actually touched.

The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, and those are made of quarks, and so far as anyone can tell quarks and electrons are not made of anything further. There are a handful of such fundamental particles and four fundamental forces — gravity, electromagnetism, and two that act only inside the nucleus, one holding it together and one responsible for certain kinds of radioactive decay.

Three of those four are described by one theory and gravity by another, and the two do not fit together. Making them fit is the largest unsolved problem in physics, and the candidate answers — strings, loops, and others — currently have no experimental evidence deciding between them. Another honest gap, and one to be suspicious of anyone claiming to have closed.

Reference

What to carry forward

Motion and gravity

Things keep doing what they are doing; change needs a cause, not motion. Heavy and light objects fall together once air is removed. And gravity is not a pull — it is mass bending space and time, with things following what counts as straight in bent space.

Heat and the arrow of time

Temperature is how hard particles are jiggling. Things run down because there are vastly more disordered arrangements than ordered ones — which is the only law that tells past from future, and the reason a film running backwards looks wrong.

Light and electricity

Moving charge makes magnetism; moving magnets make current. That is the motor, the generator and the power station. A wobbling charge sends out a wave — light, radio and X-rays are the same thing at different speeds of wobble. Blue sky and red sunset are one phenomenon.

The small and the honest gaps

Energy comes in steps, and unmeasured particles are genuinely spread out until looked at — nobody agrees what that means. Solidity is electrical repulsion, not contact. And gravity still does not fit with the other three forces. Knowing which questions are open is part of understanding the subject.
