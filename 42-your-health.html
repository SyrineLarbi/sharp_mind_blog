Part I

Tests and what they mean

Chapter 01

A result is a probability, not a verdict

The chance volume already did the hard work here, and this is where it pays. Every test has two ways of being wrong: it can miss something that is there, and it can find something that is not. Both happen routinely, in good tests, run correctly.

Which is why a positive result on a test for something rare usually means you probably do not have it — the false positives outnumber the true ones, because there are so many more people without the condition. This is not a flaw in the test; it is the arithmetic of rarity, and it explains why an abnormal result is so often followed by a second, more specific test rather than by treatment.

Two more things worth knowing about ordinary blood results. The "normal range" printed beside your number is defined so that a small percentage of perfectly healthy people fall outside it — so on a panel of twenty measurements, having one outside the range is the expected outcome, not a finding. And a single value matters far less than a trend; one reading is a snapshot of a day that included what you ate, how you slept, and whether you were anxious in the waiting room.

Blood pressure deserves a specific mention because it is the most commonly misread number in medicine. It varies through the day, it rises in clinical settings for many people, and a single high reading is not a diagnosis. Repeated measurement, often at home, is how it is properly assessed.

Chapter 02

Screening, and why more is not always better

Screening means testing people who have no symptoms, in the hope of catching something early. It saves lives. It also causes harm, and the harm is invisible in a way the benefit is not, which is why the debates about it are so strange.

Three effects make screening harder to judge than it looks.

Overdiagnosis. Some conditions found by screening would never have caused a problem in that person's lifetime. They are real, they are correctly identified, and treating them delivers no benefit while carrying the full cost of treatment. Nobody can tell which ones at the time.

The lead-time illusion. Finding a disease earlier makes survival time from diagnosis longer even if the person dies on exactly the same day they would have anyway. Survival statistics after screening therefore look better automatically, whether or not anything was gained.

The cascade. An abnormal result leads to further tests, each with its own risks, its own false alarms, and a considerable amount of fear along the way.

None of this means screening is bad — for several conditions the evidence of benefit is strong and the programmes are unambiguously worth joining. It means the honest question is specific: for this condition, at my age, with my history, does this programme prevent more harm than it causes? That is a question with a real answer, and it differs by programme. It is also exactly the question to ask a doctor rather than a search engine.

Chapter 03

Scans, and what each one sees

The imaging alphabet confuses people unnecessarily. Each method sees a different property, which is why a doctor chooses between them rather than ordering the "best" one.

X-rays pass through soft tissue and are stopped by dense material, so they show bone and lungs well and soft tissue poorly. Fast, cheap, small radiation dose.

CT is many X-rays from many angles, assembled into slices. Excellent for injury, bleeding and detail — and a substantially larger radiation dose than a single X-ray, which is why it is not ordered casually.

Ultrasound bounces sound off tissue and listens to the echo. No radiation at all, which is why it is used in pregnancy. Good for soft tissue, fluid, and anything moving; useless through bone or air.

MRI uses a powerful magnet and radio waves to map how water sits in different tissues. No radiation, superb soft-tissue detail — and slow, loud, expensive, and impossible for people with certain implants.

The practical point: radiation dose is a real consideration for the X-ray family and a non-issue for ultrasound and MRI. A scan that will not change what anyone does is a scan worth questioning, which is the single most useful thing a patient can ask — not "can I have a scan" but "what would this change?"

Part II

The parts people neglect

Chapter 04

Teeth and gums

Dentistry is treated as cosmetic and separate, and it is neither. Two processes are worth understanding because both are preventable and neither hurts until late.

Decay happens because bacteria on the tooth turn sugar into acid, and the acid dissolves mineral out of the enamel. The tooth can repair this in the early stages, drawing mineral back in from saliva — which means the critical variable is not how much sugar but how often. Sipping a sweet drink through an afternoon keeps the surface under acid attack continuously and does far more damage than the same sugar eaten at once. Between attacks, saliva repairs; without gaps, it cannot.

Gum disease is inflammation from bacteria at the gum line. It begins as bleeding when brushing — which is a sign of disease rather than of brushing too hard, and the usual response of brushing that spot less gently is precisely backwards. Left alone it progresses to destroy the bone holding the teeth, and that part is not reversible. It is the leading cause of tooth loss in adults, and it is painless until it is severe.

What actually works is dull: clean between the teeth as well as on them, since a brush cannot reach where decay and gum disease mostly start; use fluoride toothpaste and spit rather than rinsing, so it stays on the teeth; wait after acidic food or vomiting before brushing, because the enamel is temporarily softened; and keep the gaps between sugary moments long. Also worth knowing: gum inflammation is associated with problems well beyond the mouth, and mouth ulcers or patches lasting more than a few weeks should be looked at rather than waited out.

Chapter 05

Skin, sun and moles

Sunlight damages skin in two ways that behave differently. One kind of ultraviolet penetrates deeper and drives ageing — the leathering, the loss of elasticity, the pigmentation — and it passes through cloud and window glass, so it is working on ordinary overcast days and while driving. The other kind causes burning, and burning is the part most clearly linked to the dangerous cancers.

Which gives one of the clearest cost-benefit calculations in personal health: the great majority of visible skin ageing is sun exposure rather than time, and protection is cheap. The usual failure is quantity — most people apply a fraction of what the product was tested with, which converts high protection into modest protection.

On moles, the worth-knowing pattern is about change and asymmetry. A spot that is uneven in shape, ragged at the edge, mixed in colour, larger than a pencil eraser, or — most importantly — changing deserves a look. Change over months is the signal that matters most, and the skin cancer with the worst reputation is also among the most curable when caught early, which is what makes the check worth doing.

Two everyday additions. A tan is damage, by definition — it is the skin's response to injury, not a sign of health. And the common advice to worry only in summer or only in hot countries mismatches the mechanism: altitude, water, snow and reflection all increase exposure regardless of temperature.

Chapter 06

Allergy, intolerance and the difference that matters

These two words are used interchangeably and describe completely different things, with completely different stakes.

An allergy is the immune system reacting to something harmless as though it were dangerous. It can be triggered by a trace amount, it tends to involve hives, swelling, breathing difficulty, and it can escalate. The severe form — swelling of the airway, a sudden drop in blood pressure, collapse — is a medical emergency, it can occur within minutes, and the treatment is adrenaline given promptly followed by an ambulance regardless of improvement, because a second wave can follow hours later.

An intolerance is a digestive problem. The classic is lactose, where the enzyme that breaks down milk sugar declines after childhood in most of the world's adults — which makes it the normal human condition rather than a disorder. It is dose-dependent, unpleasant, and not dangerous. A small amount is usually tolerated; the immune system is not involved.

Coeliac disease sits between the two and belongs in neither box: an immune reaction to gluten that damages the gut lining over time. It requires strict avoidance, and — importantly — it must be diagnosed before removing gluten, because the tests depend on the reaction still being present.

Two practical notes. Most food reactions people self-diagnose are not allergies, and cutting foods out without testing can both mask a real diagnosis and cause nutritional gaps. And allergies change across a life — children often outgrow some, and adults can develop new ones, which surprises people who assume the pattern is fixed.

Part III

Time, and the medical system

Chapter 07

What actually changes as you age

Ageing is not one process and not uniform, and separating what is inevitable from what is modifiable is the point of this chapter.

Muscle declines steadily from middle age unless it is used, and the decline is a leading cause of the falls that end independence — which makes strength, not cardio, the exercise most worth protecting in later life. Bone thins, more sharply in women after menopause, and responds to loading and to adequate nutrition. Balance degrades quietly and trains easily. Eyes lose their close focus for mechanical reasons, essentially universally. Hearing fades at the high frequencies first, which is why consonants blur before volume becomes a problem, and hearing loss is strongly associated with later cognitive decline — making it one of the more worthwhile things to address early rather than tolerate.

On the mind: some slowing is normal, and forgetting a name is not dementia. What warrants attention is a change in function rather than in speed — getting lost somewhere familiar, difficulty with a task once routine, personality change, or repetition within a single conversation. The distinction is between forgetting where you put the keys and forgetting what the keys are for.

And the honest headline from the evidence: the things that most reliably protect the ageing brain and body are not supplements or puzzles. They are physical activity, sleep, hearing and vision correction, blood pressure control, not smoking, and sustained social connection — with social isolation carrying a risk comparable to well-known physical risk factors. That list is unglamorous and it is what the evidence keeps returning.

Chapter 08

Anaesthesia, surgery and consent

General anaesthesia is not sleep and not unconsciousness in the ordinary sense. It is a controlled state combining loss of awareness, loss of memory formation, loss of pain response and often muscle relaxation — each produced separately, adjusted continuously, with breathing and circulation supported throughout. That continuous management is why an anaesthetist stays with you the whole time, and it is why the field became one of the great safety successes of modern medicine.

Local and regional anaesthesia block nerves instead, leaving you awake. For many procedures this is safer, and being offered it is not a lesser option.

Around any procedure, consent is the part patients under-use. Real consent means understanding what is proposed, what the alternatives are, what happens if you do nothing, and what could go wrong. You may ask for time, ask for it in writing, ask for another opinion, and change your mind — and none of these are difficult or unusual requests.

Which leads to the four questions that improve almost any medical conversation, and they are the closing practical gift of this library. What else could this be? What happens if we do nothing or wait? What would this test or treatment change about the plan? And what are the risks and the alternatives? Asked calmly, they are welcomed by good clinicians, because they are the questions a good clinician is already asking.

Scope, stated plainly

This volume explains concepts so you can follow a conversation and ask better questions. It is not medical advice and cannot know your situation. Nothing here should delay seeing someone about symptoms — and the specific things that always warrant urgent attention regardless of anything in this book: sudden severe chest pain, sudden weakness or drooping on one side or difficulty speaking, difficulty breathing, sudden severe headache unlike any before, and any bleeding that will not stop.

Reference

What to carry forward

Results and screening

A test result is a probability. On a twenty-item blood panel, one value outside the normal range is expected, and a trend beats a single reading. Screening genuinely saves lives and genuinely overdiagnoses — the question is always about a specific programme at your age, not about screening in general.

Teeth and skin

Decay depends on how often sugar arrives, not how much — the tooth repairs between attacks. Bleeding gums mean disease, not over-brushing. And most visible skin ageing is sun exposure; watch moles for change, which is the signal that matters most.

Allergy vs intolerance

An allergy is the immune system — triggered by traces, can escalate, adrenaline then ambulance. An intolerance is digestion — dose-dependent and not dangerous. Coeliac must be tested before gluten is removed.

The four questions

What else could this be? What if we wait? What would this change? What are the risks and alternatives? — and for ageing well, the evidence keeps returning to strength training, sleep, hearing and vision, blood pressure, not smoking, and staying connected.
