Part I

What money is

Chapter 01

A promise that everyone accepts

Start with what money does rather than what it is made of. It does three jobs. It lets you exchange without needing the other person to want exactly what you have. It lets you compare unlike things on one scale. And it lets you move value across time — earn now, spend later.

The third job is the one that explains everything else in this volume, and it is the one that can fail.

The story you were probably told is that money replaced barter. Historians and anthropologists have looked hard for the barter economy that supposedly preceded it and have not found it. What they find instead is credit — societies keeping track of who owes what, informally or in writing, long before coins existed. Money did not replace swapping goods; it made obligations countable and transferable.

For most of history money was valuable because it was made of something scarce. Then it was paper redeemable for something scarce. Now it is neither. A banknote is worth something because everyone believes it is, and because the state that issues it demands taxes in it and will enforce contracts written in it. This sounds fragile and mostly is not — but when the belief goes, it goes quickly, which is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter 02

Where new money comes from

Here is the part that surprises most people. The great majority of money in a modern economy was not printed by anyone. It was created by commercial banks, in the act of making loans.

The intuitive picture is that a bank gathers deposits and lends them out. That is not how it works. When a bank grants a loan it writes the amount into the borrower's account — and that deposit did not exist a moment earlier. New money, created by a keystroke, matched by a debt of the same size. When the loan is repaid, that money is extinguished.

This is not a conspiracy; it is the documented mechanism, described in these terms by central banks themselves. What limits it is not a vault of cash but regulation, capital requirements, the bank's own appetite for risk, and whether anyone wants to borrow.

Which means the money supply expands when lending expands and contracts when it stops — and that is why credit conditions drive booms and busts so directly. A central bank influences all of this mainly by setting the rate at which it lends to banks, which ripples outward into every other rate in the economy.

Part II

The two forces

Chapter 03

Inflation, properly understood

Inflation is not prices going up. It is the value of money going down — the same event described from the other side, and the other side is the one that tells you what to do about it.

When it is mild it is barely noticed, and central banks deliberately aim for a small positive rate rather than zero, because a little inflation keeps money moving and because falling prices are worse. When it is severe it destroys the third job of money entirely: nobody will hold something that loses value while they hold it, so everyone spends immediately, which pushes prices up further, which makes holding money worse still. That feedback loop is how hyperinflation happens, and it is always ended by a credible break with the old money rather than by patience.

Three consequences are worth carrying.

Inflation is a transfer. It quietly moves value from lenders to borrowers, from savers to spenders, from people on fixed incomes to people whose income adjusts. Nobody votes for this transfer and it happens anyway.

Your headline salary is not your salary. A raise below the inflation rate is a pay cut, and one that feels like a reward. The number that matters is always what your money buys, not what it says.

Cash is not safe. It is safe against losing its number and guaranteed to lose its value, slowly and invisibly. There is no option that carries no risk; there is only a choice about which risk you take.

Chapter 04

Interest, and the astonishing effect of compounding

Interest is the price of time. If you hand over money now and get it back later, you are giving up the use of it, taking the risk it does not come back, and absorbing whatever inflation does in between. The rate is what you charge for that.

Simple interest earns on the original amount. Compound interest earns on the amount plus everything it has already earned, so the base keeps growing — and that difference, trivial over a year, becomes enormous over a working life.

The clearest way to feel it without arithmetic is the doubling rule: divide seventy-two by the yearly rate and you get roughly how many years it takes to double. At seven per cent, about ten years. Which means money left alone for forty years at that rate doubles four times — not four times bigger, sixteen times bigger. This is why time in the market is worth more than the rate, and why starting a decade earlier beats saving twice as much later.

It runs in reverse with equal force. Debt at twenty per cent doubles in under four years, and this is the entire mechanism by which people fall into credit-card and payday debt they cannot escape — the balance grows faster than any reasonable repayment can shrink it. Paying off a debt at twenty per cent is a guaranteed twenty per cent return, which is better than almost any investment on offer and carries no risk. That single sentence is the most valuable piece of financial advice most people will ever receive.

The one comparison that always applies

Before any investment decision, compare it against paying down your most expensive debt. The return is certain, immediate, tax-free and risk-free. Very little else is.

Part III

Markets

Chapter 05

What a price actually is

A share price is not a measure of how good a company is. It is a collective bet on its future, already reflecting everything the crowd currently knows and expects.

That is why good news sometimes drops a price: the news was already expected and the actual result was slightly less good than the bet. You are never trading against the facts; you are trading against everyone else's opinion of the facts, which is already in the number.

The practical implication is humbling and well-evidenced. Most people who pick individual investments do worse than simply owning a broad slice of the whole market, and so do most professionals, over long periods, after fees. The fees are the quiet killer: a percentage charged every year compounds against you exactly as returns compound for you.

Which leaves a short list of things that genuinely help, none of which is exciting. Spread your risk rather than concentrating it. Keep costs low. Leave it alone — the largest single cause of poor returns in ordinary investors is selling when frightened and buying when confident, which is buying high and selling low with extra steps. And match the timeframe to the money: anything you need within a few years should not be exposed to a market that can halve.

Chapter 06

Bubbles, and why they keep happening

The pattern is old enough that the Dutch were doing it with tulip bulbs, and it repeats with a regularity that suggests something structural rather than accidental.

It usually begins with something genuinely new and genuinely valuable — railways, radio, the internet. Prices rise for good reasons. Then the reason for buying changes: people are no longer buying because the thing is valuable, but because the price is going up. Which is a self-confirming belief, for a while.

Then the social proof from the persuasion volume takes over. Friends are making money doing nothing. Sitting out feels like stupidity, and a story circulates explaining why the old rules no longer apply — that sentence, in one form or another, has appeared in every bubble ever documented. Credit makes it bigger, because people borrow to join in.

And the end is always the same, because it has to be: the price depends on new buyers arriving, and there is a finite number of people. When they stop arriving the mechanism reverses, and the fall is faster than the rise, because fear moves quicker than greed and the borrowed money must be repaid.

You will not reliably time this, and nobody else does either. What you can do is recognise the two signals that are always present — you are buying because it is rising, and someone is explaining why this time is different — and size your position so that being wrong is survivable.

Part IV

Your own money

Chapter 07

The short list that actually matters

Personal finance is an industry built on complexity, and the genuinely important part is short enough to state in a few paragraphs. Nearly everything else is either detail or someone's commission.

Spend less than you earn, and know the gap. Not a budget in the punitive sense — one honest number. Most people do not know theirs, and everything downstream depends on it.

Hold a buffer. A few months of expenses in ordinary accessible savings. Its purpose is not returns; its purpose is that a broken car does not become high-interest debt, and that you can refuse a bad situation. The buffer buys options, and options are the actual product.

Clear expensive debt before anything else. See the doubling rule. This is the only guaranteed return you will be offered.

Insure the catastrophic, self-insure the annoying. Insurance is worth buying for things that would ruin you and never worth buying for things you could absorb — which is why extended warranties on small appliances are reliably a bad deal and income protection frequently is not.

Automate the saving. Money that moves before you see it is saved; money that depends on a monthly act of virtue is not. This is the habit chapter applied to finance: change the environment rather than trusting resolve.

Start early and be boring. The doubling rule does the work, provided you give it time and do not interrupt it.

Chapter 08

Why money decisions go wrong

Every bias from the psychology volumes has a financial form, and this is where they cost you directly.

You treat money differently depending on where it came from — a windfall is spent more freely than earned money, though it buys exactly the same things. You keep a losing investment to avoid making the loss real, and sell a winner to make a gain real, which is precisely backwards. You value what you already own above what you would pay for it, which is why it is so hard to sell anything.

Price anchors work on you constantly: a recommended price makes a discount feel like an achievement, and a deliberately expensive option makes the middle one feel sensible. Small recurring amounts feel free and are not — a monthly subscription is a yearly cost, and the yearly number is the honest one.

And the deepest one: the future feels less real than the present, which is why a reward now beats a larger reward later even when the trade is plainly bad. Every lending product ever designed exploits this. The defence is not willpower but structure — decide in advance, automate it, and remove the moment of choice.

Scope

This volume explains mechanisms; it is not financial advice and does not know your situation, your country's tax rules or your obligations. Anything that promises high returns with no risk is either misunderstood or a fraud, and anything urgent, secret, or guaranteed is a fraud — the urgency is there to stop you taking the twenty-four hours from the persuasion volume.

What to carry forward

What money is

A transferable promise, not a thing — and most of it is created by commercial banks when they make loans, then destroyed when the loans are repaid. Money supply and credit are the same story.

The two forces

Inflation is money losing value, and it is a silent transfer from savers to borrowers; a raise below it is a pay cut. Interest compounds — divide seventy-two by the rate for the doubling time, and remember it runs in reverse on debt.

Markets

A price already contains what everyone knows. Most pickers, professionals included, underperform a broad slice of the market after fees. Spread, keep costs low, leave it alone, match the timeframe.
