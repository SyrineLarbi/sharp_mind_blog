Part I

What the machine is doing

Chapter 01

Learning from examples rather than rules

The older approach to making computers do clever things was to write down the rules. If the temperature is above this and the pressure below that, do the following. It works beautifully where the rules can be stated and fails completely where they cannot — and nobody can write the rules for recognising a cat, because you do not know them either.

The modern approach gives up on rules entirely. You show the system enormous numbers of examples and let it find the patterns itself, by adjusting millions of internal values until its outputs match the examples better. Nobody writes the rules; nobody can read them afterwards either.

That second point matters more than it sounds. The knowledge ends up distributed across a vast number of numerical settings, not stored as statements anywhere. You cannot open it up and find the part that knows about Paris. This is why these systems are hard to debug, hard to explain, and hard to correct precisely — and it is a property of the approach, not a temporary limitation.

The word "learning" is doing some rhetorical work here, and it is worth deflating. What happens is closer to fitting an extremely flexible shape to a great many examples than to what a person does when they learn something. The system is not understanding; it is finding structure that reproduces the examples well.

Chapter 02

What a language model actually does

Here is the whole thing, and it is simpler and stranger than most people assume.

A language model is trained to do one task: given some text, predict what comes next. That is all. It was shown a colossal quantity of written material and adjusted until its predictions got good. Then, to produce an answer, it predicts the next fragment, adds it, predicts the next one from the result, and continues.

The genuinely surprising empirical fact — and it surprised the people building them — is how much capability falls out of that. To predict text well across everything humans have written, a system ends up having to represent an enormous amount: grammar, facts, styles, the structure of arguments, something like the ability to follow a chain of reasoning. Capabilities nobody designed emerged from making the prediction better, and this remains only partly understood.

But the objective is still prediction. The model is producing text that is likely given everything it has seen — and likely is not the same as true. That single gap is the source of nearly every failure mode in the next chapter.

Two further things worth knowing. There is a second training stage where humans rate outputs and the model is adjusted toward what they prefer — which is what makes it helpful and conversational rather than merely a text continuation engine, and which also bakes in the preferences of the people doing the rating. And it does not, by default, remember you between conversations; what looks like memory is either the visible conversation being fed back in each time, or a deliberate feature storing things separately.

Chapter 03

Why it invents things, and why it sounds so sure

The polite word is hallucination and it is somewhat misleading, because it suggests a malfunction. It is the system working exactly as designed.

Asked for a citation, the model produces text that looks like a citation — plausible author, plausible title, plausible year — because that is what the pattern calls for. It has no separate store of facts to check against and no mechanism that distinguishes remembering from generating. The confident invented answer and the confident correct answer are produced by the same process, in the same way, with the same fluency.

Which explains the property that makes this genuinely hazardous: there is no confidence signal in the writing. A human expert who is unsure sounds unsure. A model that is fabricating sounds precisely like a model that is right. The usual cue you use to calibrate trust in a text — how hedged and careful it is — has been severed from accuracy.

Where it is worst is predictable and worth memorising: specific facts, numbers, dates, citations, quotations, legal and medical specifics, anything recent, and anything obscure. Obscurity is the aggravating factor — on well-covered topics the likely answer and the true answer mostly coincide; at the edges they diverge, and that is exactly where you were consulting it because you did not know.

Two structural biases are worth adding. These systems are trained to be agreeable, which makes them prone to going along with you — push back on a correct answer and it may well fold, which means agreement is weak evidence. And a model has a training cutoff, so unless it is actually searching, its picture of the world stops at a date it may not state.

Part II

Using it

Chapter 04

What it is genuinely good and bad at

The pattern is consistent once you see it. These systems are strong where the answer is a transformation of material in front of them and weak where it requires retrieval of specific truth or genuine reasoning about consequences.

So they do well at: rewriting, summarising and restructuring text you supply; changing tone or register; explaining a concept at a chosen level; drafting a first version of something you will edit; translating; generating many options to react to; and acting as a tireless thing to think out loud at. They are excellent at getting you off a blank page.

They do badly at: anything requiring a fact you cannot verify; arithmetic and precise counting, unless using a tool; keeping track of long chains of constraints; knowing what they do not know; and — the most important one — judging what matters. They will produce a competent, average, plausible answer, and average is often exactly what you did not want.

The practical rule that follows: use them where you can check the output, and not where you cannot. If you could verify or would recognise a wrong answer, the leverage is real. If you are asking precisely because you have no way to evaluate the response, you are trusting a system with no mechanism for distinguishing truth from plausibility — and doing it in the one situation where you are least able to notice.

Chapter 05

How to actually work with one

A short set of habits that make the difference between usefulness and nonsense.

Give it the material. Its weakest mode is recalling facts from training; its strongest is working with text you provide. Pasting the document and asking about that is a different and far more reliable activity than asking what it remembers.

Say who it is for and what it is for. Audience, length, format and purpose. Without them you get the average of everything ever written on the topic, which is what makes the output feel generic.

Ask for the reasoning, then check the reasoning rather than the answer. A visible chain of steps is inspectable in a way that a conclusion is not. Be aware the stated reasoning is not necessarily what produced the answer — but it usually exposes a bad assumption.

Iterate rather than perfecting the request. React to a draft; that is where the information is.

Ask it to argue against itself, or to state what would make its answer wrong. This counteracts the agreeableness and is the Volume 40 technique applied to a machine.

Verify anything load-bearing, independently. Every name, number, date, quotation, citation and legal or medical specific. Not by asking it whether it is sure — it will confirm either way — but by going to a source.

Two things not to do

Do not paste confidential material — personal data about other people, medical records, client information, unreleased work, credentials — into a service without knowing its terms. Assume anything you send may be retained and may be used for training unless you have specifically established otherwise.

Do not treat it as a professional. It has no licence, no duty of care, no liability, and no knowledge of your circumstances. It is genuinely useful for understanding your situation well enough to ask better questions — which is what much of this library is for — and it is not a doctor, a lawyer or a financial adviser.

Part III

The arguments

Chapter 06

Bias, and where it comes from

A system trained on human output reproduces human patterns, including the ones nobody wanted. This is not a matter of bad intent anywhere; it is a mechanical consequence of learning from examples.

It arrives by several routes. The training material carries historical patterns — who appears in which roles, whose writing was published, which languages and places are well represented. The data is not a neutral sample of humanity; it over-represents the online, the English-speaking and the recent. Feedback bakes in preferences of the particular people who rated outputs. And where a system is trained on past decisions — who was hired, who was approved, who was policed — it learns to reproduce those decisions, including the discriminatory ones, while wearing the appearance of objectivity.

That last point is the one with teeth, and it connects to Volume 37: discrimination does not require anyone to hold a prejudice. A system can produce systematically worse outcomes for a group while every person involved sincerely intended fairness, and the aggregate handling makes it invisible.

Removing the obvious attribute does not fix it, because other variables stand in as proxies — postcode for ethnicity, employment gaps for parenthood. And the failures are not evenly distributed: facial recognition and speech recognition have both performed measurably worse on groups under-represented in their training data, which is not a philosophical concern when the system decides who gets stopped or understood.

The reasonable posture: a system that seems objective because it is a machine is the most dangerous kind, because the appearance of neutrality removes the scrutiny a human decision would face. The right question about any automated decision is what it was trained on, whether anyone measured how it performs across groups, and whether a person can be appealed to.

Chapter 07

Work, and the honest uncertainty

Every previous wave of automation produced the same two confident predictions — mass permanent unemployment, and no real change — and both were wrong in the same way. What actually happened: specific tasks were automated, jobs were restructured around what remained, some occupations shrank badly, new ones appeared that nobody had predicted, and the transition was genuinely painful for the people in the middle of it even where the long-run aggregate was fine.

What is different this time is the direction. Previous automation hit physical and routine work; this targets cognitive and creative tasks, including in professions that assumed themselves safe. Whether that difference is fundamental or merely another instance of the pattern is genuinely unsettled, and anyone stating it confidently in either direction is going beyond the evidence.

The more robust framing is about tasks rather than jobs. Few roles are entirely automatable; most contain some tasks that are. Which means the realistic effect is jobs changing shape, with the value moving toward what the systems are bad at — judgement about what matters, responsibility for outcomes, physical and interpersonal work, and knowing enough to tell when the output is wrong.

And a caution that applies to this whole field, drawn from Volume 39: the loudest predictions come from people with a stake in them. Companies selling these systems benefit from both extremes — from "this changes everything" and from "this is so powerful it is dangerous", which are both claims of enormous capability. Discount accordingly, in both directions.

Chapter 08

What to actually worry about

The public conversation is dominated by the most speculative risks, which is unfortunate, because several concrete ones are already here and better worth your attention.

Convincing fabrication at scale. Text, voice and video are now cheap to fake well, which is the mechanism behind the scams in Volume 47 and behind a corrosion of evidence generally. The second-order effect is the more serious one: when anything can be faked, genuine recordings become deniable.

Automated decisions without recourse. Systems deciding on loans, jobs, benefits, insurance and policing, with the reasoning unavailable and no person to appeal to. This is happening now, and the absence of an appeal route is a policy choice rather than a technical necessity.

Concentration. Training these systems requires resources only a few organisations possess, which places considerable influence over a general-purpose technology in very few hands.

Degraded information. Enormous volumes of plausible generated text, including subtly wrong text, entering the same channels you use to check things — which makes the verification habits in this volume harder rather than easier over time.

And the quiet one: your own skills. Capabilities you stop exercising decline. Using these systems to avoid thinking rather than to think faster is a real trade, and it is the one entirely within your control.

On the long-range existential arguments: they are made seriously by serious people and disputed seriously by other serious people, the disagreement is about things nobody can currently resolve, and the honest description is genuine uncertainty rather than consensus in either direction. Treating that as settled — in either direction — is the confident-explanation failure from Volume 35.

Reference

What to carry forward

The mechanism

A language model predicts what text comes next — that is the whole objective, and the capabilities emerged from doing it well. But likely is not true, and the knowledge is spread across millions of numerical settings rather than stored as statements anywhere.

Why it invents

Fabrication is the system working as designed, not malfunctioning. The confident wrong answer and the confident right one are produced identically — so there is no confidence signal in the writing. Worst on specifics, citations, numbers, recent and obscure things.

Using it well

Use it where you can check the output, not where you cannot. Give it the material rather than asking what it remembers; say who and what it is for; ask it to argue against itself; verify anything load-bearing at a source, not by asking if it is sure.

The arguments

Bias arrives mechanically from the data and the feedback — and a system that seems objective because it is a machine is the most dangerous kind. Think in tasks rather than jobs. And discount the loudest predictions in both directions: hype and doom are both claims of enormous capability, sold by people with a stake.
