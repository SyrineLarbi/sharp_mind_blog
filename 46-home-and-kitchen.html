Part I

Fire, and the gas you cannot smell

Chapter 01

What fire needs, and how to take it away

Fire needs three things at once: fuel, oxygen, and enough heat to keep the reaction going. Remove any one and it stops. Every extinguishing method is one of those three removals, and knowing which one you are attempting is what stops you making things worse.

Water removes heat. It works well on wood, paper and fabric — and it is dangerous on two things, for two different reasons.

On an oil or fat fire, water is catastrophic. Burning oil is far hotter than water's boiling point, so water poured in sinks beneath the oil, flashes instantly into steam, and expands enormously as it does — throwing burning oil across the room in a fireball. This is the single most common way an ordinary kitchen fire becomes a house fire and a hospital admission. Never put water on a pan fire. Turn off the heat if you can reach it safely, and smother the pan — a lid, a metal tray, a fire blanket — cutting off the oxygen. Then leave it alone; moving a burning pan outside is how people are seriously burned.

On an electrical fire, water conducts. Cut the power if you can do so safely, and use an extinguisher rated for electrical use.

Smothering removes oxygen — lids, blankets, foam. Starving removes fuel — turning off the gas supply, which is why a gas fire is usually best left burning until the supply is off rather than blown out into a room slowly filling with gas.

Two more things worth having decided in advance. Smoke kills far more people than flames do, and it does so quickly and often in sleep — which is the entire argument for working smoke alarms, tested occasionally, one on each floor, and for closing doors at night, since a closed internal door buys a remarkable amount of time. And a fire doubles in size very fast: the honest rule is that if it is bigger than a waste bin, do not fight it. Get everyone out, close doors behind you, and call. Possessions are replaceable and re-entering a burning building is how people die.

Chapter 02

Carbon monoxide

This chapter is short because the content is simple and because it is, per word, the most likely thing in this library to save a life.

When fuel — gas, oil, coal, wood, charcoal, petrol — burns with enough oxygen, it produces carbon dioxide. When it burns with too little, it produces carbon monoxide instead. Any blocked flue, any poorly serviced boiler, any indoor use of something meant for outdoors will do it.

Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and tasteless. There is no smell. You cannot detect it with any sense you have.

It kills because it binds to the molecule in your blood that carries oxygen, and it binds far more tightly than oxygen does — so the blood fills with something that cannot be released to the tissues. You suffocate with your lungs working perfectly.

The symptoms are the reason people die in their homes without acting: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, drowsiness. It is indistinguishable from flu or a hangover, and confusion is itself a symptom — so the poisoning removes the judgement needed to leave.

The four things to know

Fit an alarm. An audible carbon monoxide alarm is inexpensive and is the only way to detect it. It is a different device from a smoke alarm. Every home with any fuel-burning appliance needs one.

Never bring outdoor burning indoors. A barbecue, a camping stove, a patio heater or a generator in a house, a garage, a tent or a caravan — including a barbecue brought in "just to cool down", which kills people every year. A running engine in an attached garage does it too.

Suspect it when several people or the pets feel ill at once, when symptoms improve on leaving the house and return on coming back, or when a gas flame burns yellow or orange rather than crisp blue, or there is sooting around an appliance.

If you suspect it: get out, open doors on your way, do not stop to investigate, and call emergency services from outside. Do not go back in.

On a gas leak — the smell added to gas is deliberately unpleasant for exactly this reason — the rules are similar with one addition: do not create a spark. No light switches, no appliances, no doorbell, no phone used inside. Open windows and doors, turn the supply off at the meter if you can reach it safely, leave, and call the emergency gas number from outside.

Chapter 03

Damp, mould, and the quiet hazards in the structure

Damp and mould are the most common household health problem and the most commonly misdiagnosed. Mould grows where there is moisture, and moisture accumulates where warm humid air meets a cold surface — behind furniture against an external wall, in corners, around windows. Ordinary living produces a surprising amount: cooking, showering, drying clothes indoors and simply breathing.

The health effects are real — respiratory irritation, worsened asthma and allergy, and more serious consequences for infants, the elderly and anyone immunocompromised. Treating it as cosmetic is the mistake. And cleaning it off without fixing the moisture guarantees its return, because the visible growth is the symptom. Ventilation is the actual remedy: extractor fans used and maintained, windows opened briefly even in winter, furniture pulled slightly off cold walls, and clothes dried with ventilation rather than in a sealed room. Heating a home unevenly and intermittently produces more condensation than heating it modestly and steadily.

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps up from certain rocks, accumulates in ground floors and basements in poorly ventilated buildings, and is — after smoking — a leading cause of lung cancer. It is colourless and odourless, it varies enormously by geology, and whether it concerns you depends entirely on where you live. Many countries publish maps and inexpensive test kits. It is worth ten minutes to find out whether your area is affected, and if so, testing is cheap and remediation is usually straightforward.

Asbestos and lead matter mainly in older buildings and mainly during work on them. Asbestos was used widely in insulation, ceiling tiles, roofing and flooring, and it is harmless while undisturbed and hazardous when drilled, sanded or broken — which is the crucial point, because the danger is created by renovation. Lead in old paint behaves the same way: stable under later layers, dangerous when sanded or burned off, and particularly harmful to children. In a building of any age, find out before you start work rather than after, and use a licensed professional for asbestos rather than attempting removal.

Chapter 04

Batteries, electricity and the rest of the cupboard

Lithium batteries are in everything now and they fail in a specific and alarming way. Damage, overheating, overcharging or a manufacturing defect can start a self-sustaining reaction inside the cell that produces its own oxygen — which means it cannot be smothered, burns extremely hot, and can reignite after appearing to go out. Practically: do not charge devices on beds, sofas or under pillows; do not use damaged, swollen or dented batteries or chargers; use the manufacturer's charger; and treat a device that is hot, swollen, hissing or smelling odd as something to move outside away from anything flammable rather than something to investigate. E-bike and scooter batteries are the most common cause of serious fires of this kind, and charging them in a hallway blocks the exit you would need.

Button batteries deserve their own sentence because the mechanism surprises people. Swallowed by a small child, a button battery lodged in the throat generates a current in the surrounding tissue that burns through it chemically — causing severe internal injury within a couple of hours, not days. There may be no obvious symptoms at first. This is an immediate emergency, not a wait-and-see, and it is a good reason to keep them and the devices containing them genuinely out of reach.

Electrical basics. Water and electricity is the obvious one and the reason bathrooms have specific rules. Beyond that: daisy-chained extension leads and overloaded adaptors are a real fire cause, especially with high-power appliances like heaters and kettles; a plug or socket that is warm, scorched or buzzing is a fault rather than a quirk; and cables run under rugs get damaged where you cannot see.

And the cupboard itself, briefly: keep products in their original labelled containers, never decant anything into a drinks bottle, keep the cleaning chemicals from Volume 41 separated from each other, and keep medicines and chemicals genuinely out of children's reach rather than merely high up.

Part II

The kitchen

Chapter 05

Heat, and the reaction that makes food taste good

The brown crust on a seared steak, the crust of bread, roasted coffee, fried onions, toast — all of these are the same reaction, and it is not caramelisation. It is a reaction between proteins and sugars that begins at high temperature and produces hundreds of new aromatic compounds. It is where most of the flavour in cooked food comes from.

Two conditions govern it, and understanding them fixes most home-cooking disappointments.

It needs heat above the boiling point of water — which means that as long as the surface of your food is wet, it cannot happen. The water must boil away first, and while it does, the surface stays at boiling temperature and steams rather than browns. This is why you dry meat before searing it, why a crowded pan produces grey food instead of brown, and why adding too much at once drops the pan temperature and floods it with released moisture.

Caramelisation is different — that is sugar alone breaking down at high heat, and it is what happens to onions cooked slowly for a long time, and to sugar in a pan.

The familiar claim that searing "seals in the juices" is false and was tested long ago. Searing does something better: it creates flavour. The juices escape either way.

And resting meat after cooking is not a ritual. Heat drives moisture toward the centre and tightens the muscle fibres; a few minutes off the heat lets the temperature even out and the fibres relax, so the liquid stays in the meat instead of on the board.

Chapter 06

Oils, and why smoke point matters

Every fat has a temperature above which it begins to break down, smoke, and produce acrid compounds and an unpleasant taste. That is its smoke point, and it is the main practical difference between cooking oils.

Broadly: refined oils have higher smoke points than unrefined ones, because the things that burn have been removed. So a refined oil suits high-heat frying and searing; unrefined and strongly flavoured oils — extra virgin olive oil, sesame, nut oils — are better used at moderate heat or added at the end, where their flavour survives rather than being destroyed.

Two corrections. Extra virgin olive oil is not as fragile as commonly claimed and is perfectly suitable for ordinary pan cooking; it is genuinely wasted on deep frying, for cost and flavour reasons more than safety. And reusing oil repeatedly is the real problem — each heating degrades it further, lowering the smoke point and accumulating breakdown products. Oil that smells acrid, foams, or has darkened considerably should go.

Storage follows directly from Volume 41: oxygen, light and heat cause rancidity, so oil belongs in a closed container in a cupboard, not in a clear bottle beside the cooker. And never pour used oil down the drain — it solidifies and blocks, both in your pipes and in the sewer.

Chapter 07

Salt, acid, and the things that make a dish work

Salt does far more than taste salty. It suppresses bitterness, which is why a pinch improves coffee and chocolate. It draws moisture out of food and then, given time, back in carrying flavour with it — which is the whole principle of brining and of salting meat in advance rather than at the table. It firms vegetables by affecting cell walls, and it strengthens dough. When you salt therefore changes the result as much as how much: early salting seasons through, late salting sits on the surface.

Acid — lemon, vinegar, wine, tomato — is what most home cooking is missing when it tastes flat despite being adequately salted. It provides contrast, cuts richness and fat, and brightens everything around it. The habit of tasting a finished dish and asking whether it needs acid rather than more salt is one of the more useful things a cook can learn.

Baking soda and baking powder are not interchangeable. Baking soda is an alkali that produces gas only when it meets an acid — buttermilk, yoghurt, honey, cocoa — so a recipe using it must contain one. Baking powder is baking soda with a powdered acid already included, so it works on its own when wet and again when heated. Substituting one for the other without adjusting produces either a flat result or a soapy, metallic one.

Baking soda has a second use worth knowing: being alkaline, it speeds browning. A pinch in onions makes them caramelise faster, and in the water when boiling makes vegetable skins soften.

Finally, heat management beats technique for most home cooks. A pan that is not hot enough steams; a pan that is too hot burns the outside before the inside cooks. And most domestic ovens run meaningfully hotter or cooler than the dial says — an inexpensive oven thermometer resolves more baking problems than any recipe change.

Chapter 08

Caffeine and alcohol, chemically

Two substances almost everyone uses and almost nobody understands mechanically.

Caffeine does not give you energy. A molecule called adenosine accumulates in the brain while you are awake and produces the sensation of sleepiness. Caffeine is shaped enough like it to occupy the same receptors without activating them — so it blocks the signal rather than supplying anything. The adenosine continues accumulating behind the blockade, which is why the tiredness arrives all at once when it wears off.

It takes several hours for half of a dose to clear, and considerably longer for the rest — which means an afternoon coffee is still meaningfully present at bedtime. It reliably degrades sleep quality even in people who fall asleep fine, and the resulting tiredness is then treated with more caffeine. Tolerance builds, so the familiar morning coffee is often restoring normal function rather than improving on it.

Alcohol is processed by the liver in two steps, and the intermediate compound produced in the first step is considerably more toxic than the alcohol itself — it is responsible for much of what a hangover is. The liver works at a roughly fixed rate that nothing accelerates: not coffee, not cold showers, not food, not exercise. Food before drinking slows absorption, which is genuinely useful, but once it is in you the clock is the only remedy.

Two mechanisms worth knowing. Alcohol is a sedative, not a sleep aid — it shortens the time to fall asleep and then suppresses the restorative stages and fragments the second half of the night, which is why alcohol-assisted sleep is unrefreshing. And it is a diuretic, which is where a good part of the headache comes from. People also vary substantially in how efficiently they perform that first processing step, which is why the flushing reaction is common in some populations and why tolerance is partly inherited rather than earned.

Reference

What to carry forward

Fire

Never water on a pan fire — it flashes to steam under the oil and throws it across the room. Turn off the heat, smother, leave it. Smoke kills more than flames, closed doors buy time, and if it is bigger than a bin: out, doors closed, call.

Carbon monoxide — the one to remember

No colour, no smell, no taste. Symptoms are flu-like and confusion is one of them. Fit an alarm; never bring a barbecue, stove or generator indoors; suspect it when several people feel ill at once or symptoms improve on leaving. Get out and call from outside.

The structure and the cupboard

Mould is a moisture problem — ventilation is the remedy, cleaning is not. Radon depends on your geology and is cheap to test. Asbestos and lead are dangerous when disturbed, so check before renovating. Lithium batteries make their own oxygen and cannot be smothered; button batteries swallowed are an immediate emergency.

The kitchen

Browning needs a surface hotter than boiling water — dry the food, do not crowd the pan. Searing creates flavour, it does not seal. Rest meat. Match oil to heat and never reuse it repeatedly. Salt early to season through; add acid when a dish tastes flat. And caffeine blocks the tiredness signal rather than supplying anything.
