Two thousand years of moral philosophy comes down to a small number of genuinely different answers to one question, and knowing them changes how you argue — because most disagreements are not about the facts but about which of these frameworks each side is standing in without saying so. Part I

Three answers

Chapter 01

Judge by the outcome

The first answer is the one most people reach for when pressed: an action is right if it produces the best results. Count up the good and the harm, and choose the option with the better total.

Its appeal is obvious. It is impartial — nobody's suffering counts for more because they are closer to you. It is practical. And it takes seriously the thing that surely must matter: whether people are actually better or worse off.

Its problems are equally serious and they cluster in one place. If only the total matters, then anything at all is permitted provided the total comes out right. Punishing an innocent person to prevent a riot. Harvesting one person's organs to save five. Most people recoil, and the recoil is itself evidence about morality that the framework has trouble accommodating.

There is also the practical difficulty that you cannot actually calculate it. Consequences ripple outward indefinitely, you do not know them in advance, and the comparison of one person's suffering with another's happiness has no honest unit.

Where it is at its strongest is exactly where intuition is weakest: policy, and large numbers. When you are deciding how to spend a health budget across a population, refusing to count is not a moral stance, it is an abdication — someone is affected either way.

Chapter 02

Judge by the duty

The second answer says some things are simply wrong regardless of outcome. There are duties, and you do not get to breach them because the sums looked favourable.

Two tests give it teeth. Could everyone do this? — not "what if everyone did", loosely, but whether the rule you are acting on would still make sense if universal. Lying fails immediately: a world where everyone lies when convenient is one where lying no longer works, so the practice destroys its own conditions. And: are you treating this person as a person, or only as a means to your end? Using people is permitted — you use the shopkeeper — provided you are not treating them merely as an instrument, with their own purposes discounted.

The strength is that it protects individuals. Rights make sense here in a way they never quite do under pure outcome-counting; a person is not an input to a calculation.

The weakness appears at the edges. Duties conflict, and the framework is not good at telling you which wins. The classic case is blunt: someone is hiding in your house, a murderer asks where they are, and the rule against lying appears to require you to tell the truth. Refusing to lie in that situation strikes almost everyone as monstrous, and rescuing the framework from that conclusion takes considerable work.

Chapter 03

Judge by the person

The third answer is the oldest and asks a different question entirely. Not what should I do but what kind of person should I be — and it holds that acting well flows from being a certain sort of person rather than from consulting a rule.

The virtues are the traits that make a life go well: courage, honesty, generosity, justice, temperance, practical wisdom. And each one sits between two failures rather than at an extreme. Courage lies between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity between meanness and profligacy. There is no formula for where the middle is, and finding it is exactly the skill being described.

This has the great advantage of matching how moral development actually happens. Nobody becomes trustworthy by deriving trustworthiness; they become trustworthy by practising, until it stops requiring a decision — which is the habit chapter from the psychology volume, arriving at the same conclusion from the other direction.

Its weakness is that it gives poor guidance in a hard case. Told to do what a person of good character would do, you are back where you started when the honest and the kind response point different ways.

Chapter 04

What to do with three frameworks

The temptation is to pick one and apply it consistently. Almost nobody manages this, including professional philosophers, and the failure is informative.

The more honest use is as three lenses on the same decision. What are the consequences, all of them, including the ones on people you will never meet? What duties are in play, and would I accept this rule if I were on the other end of it? And what does this choice make me into if I keep making it?

When all three agree, the case is easy. When they diverge, you have located the actual difficulty instead of arguing past it — and most public moral arguments are exactly this: one side counting outcomes, the other invoking a right, neither noticing that they are answering different questions.

One further test is worth more than its simplicity suggests. Would you be comfortable if this were published, with your name on it? It catches most of the self-justification from the dark side volume, because the self-justifying machinery works best in private.

Part II

The hard cases

Chapter 05

The dilemmas, and what they are for

The famous runaway-trolley problems are often dismissed as artificial, and they are — deliberately. Their purpose is not realism; it is to hold everything constant except one variable, so you can find out what you actually think.

Diverting a runaway vehicle so it kills one person instead of five: most people say yes. Pushing one person into its path to stop it, saving the same five: most people say no. The outcome is identical. So whatever is producing the difference, it is not the outcome — it is something about using a person as the instrument, and about doing versus allowing.

That gap between the two answers is the real finding, and it is stable across cultures. It tells you that people's actual moral psychology is not outcome-counting, whatever they say when asked abstractly.

Two other distinctions do real work in practice. Doing versus allowing: most systems of morality treat causing harm as worse than failing to prevent it, though the consequences may be the same — and this is why a failure to donate does not feel like a killing, which is either a deep insight or a convenient one depending on who you ask. And intending versus foreseeing: a harm you aim at is judged more harshly than an equal harm you knew would follow from something else you aimed at. That distinction underpins a great deal of medical and military ethics, and it is doing enormous work in arguments where nobody names it.

Part III

How to argue

Chapter 06

What an argument is

An argument is a set of claims offered in support of another claim. That is all. It is not a disagreement, and being good at it is not the same as being persuasive.

Two things must both be true for an argument to work, and separating them is most of the skill.

Does the conclusion follow? — if the supporting claims were true, would the conclusion have to be true, or at least be well supported? This can be assessed without knowing whether anything is true, and it is where formal reasoning lives.

Are the supporting claims actually true? — a perfectly constructed argument from false premises is worthless, and this is the more common failure by a wide margin.

Most real disagreements are about an unstated premise rather than about logic. Which gives the single most productive move available in an argument: state the other side's position back to them, better than they stated it, until they agree that is what they think. Half the time the disagreement dissolves; the other half, you are now arguing about the real thing.

Chapter 07

The fallacies worth knowing by name

A fallacy is a move that looks like reasoning and is not. Learning them is not a debating trick — it is a way of noticing when you have been moved by something that should not have moved you.

Attacking the person instead of the claim. Their motives, their character, their hypocrisy. Worth noting that a person's unreliability is legitimate grounds for discounting their testimony — but it is never grounds for rejecting their argument, which stands or falls on its own.

Rebuilding their position as something weaker and defeating that. Extremely common, usually unconscious, and the direct opposite of the restate-it-better move above.

Offering only two options when more exist. Almost all political framing works this way.

Claiming one step leads inevitably to a distant one, without showing the mechanism. Sometimes there really is a slope; the question is always whether the intermediate steps were argued or asserted.

Appealing to an authority outside their field, or to popularity, or to tradition, or to novelty. Expert consensus within a field is genuine evidence; a famous person's opinion outside theirs is not, and neither is "everyone knows" or "we have always done it this way".

Assuming what you are trying to prove, usually hidden inside the phrasing of the question. And loading the question itself, so any answer concedes the point.

Treating a correlation as a cause, or treating one event following another as the second having been caused by the first. The chance volume is the long version of why this fails.

Appealing to consequences — arguing that a claim must be false because it would be terrible if true. Reality is not obliged to be convenient.

Demanding certainty selectively, or shifting the burden so that the other side must disprove something never established. And the moving target: a claim that quietly weakens each time it is challenged, while the speaker behaves as though nothing has changed.

The point of all this

Naming fallacies at other people is an unpleasant habit and rarely persuades anyone. The value is internal: use them on your own arguments first. The question that matters is not "did they commit a fallacy" but "would I accept this reasoning if it pointed the other way" — which is the confirmation-bias test from Volume 35, wearing a philosopher's coat.

Chapter 08

Disagreeing well

Everything in this library about persuasion says that argument rarely changes minds in the moment. That is true, and it is not a reason to give up — it is a reason to change what you are trying to do.

The things that measurably help are few. Ask how something works rather than asserting that it does not. People asked to explain a policy in mechanical detail reliably become less confident about it, because the explanation reveals the gap; people simply told they are wrong become more confident. Concede the strongest point on the other side out loud, which costs nothing and changes the temperature. Separate the claim from the person holding it, in both directions. And leave people a way to change their mind without losing face — the humiliation finding from the dark side volume applies here exactly.

And be honest about which disagreements are which. Some are factual and resolvable. Some are about values, where both sides may be reasoning perfectly from different starting points, and the achievable goal is understanding rather than agreement. Mistaking the second kind for the first produces most of the bitterness in public life, because each side concludes the other must be stupid or lying, when in fact they are counting different things.

Reference

What to carry forward

Three frameworks

Outcomes — best total result, strongest for policy, weakest when it permits anything. Duties — some things are simply wrong, protects individuals, struggles when duties conflict. Character — what kind of person this makes me, matches how morality is actually learned, vague in hard cases.

Use them as lenses

Run a decision through all three. When they agree it is easy; when they diverge you have found the real difficulty instead of arguing past it. And ask whether you would be comfortable if it were published under your name.

Arguments

Two separate questions: does the conclusion follow, and are the premises true? Most disagreements are about an unstated premise. Restate the other side's view better than they did before responding to it.

Disagreeing well

Ask how it works rather than asserting it does not — explaining a position in detail reduces overconfidence; being contradicted increases it. And distinguish factual disagreements from disagreements about values; confusing the two is where the bitterness comes from.
