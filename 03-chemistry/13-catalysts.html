Sharp Mind · Chemistry · Topic 13 of 20

Catalysts. The matchmakers that make reactions happen and walk away unchanged.

Under your car there is a box holding a few grams of platinum, palladium and rhodium. Hot exhaust passes over it and leaves with most of its poisonous gases turned into harmless ones. The metals are not consumed. They just make reactions happen faster. Inside every cell of your body, thousands of proteins do the same job for the reactions of life. A catalyst is one of the oldest ideas in chemistry and one of the most valuable. Most of the modern economy, and all of biology, runs on it.

Question 1 · What is it?

A substance that speeds a reaction and comes out the other side intact

The definition is short. The consequences take the rest of this file.

Chapter 01

A lighter with no flint

In 1823 the German chemist Johann Döbereiner built a strange lighter. A jet of hydrogen gas was aimed at a small piece of spongy platinum. The platinum began to glow, and the hydrogen caught fire. Thousands of these "Döbereiner lamps" were sold before matches became cheap. And after each use, the platinum was exactly as it had been before.

Hydrogen and oxygen "want" to react. As Topic 11 explained, their product, water, is far more stable. But at room temperature they sit together harmlessly, because the reaction must first climb an energy hill. The platinum gave them an easier route. In 1835 the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius gave the effect a name: catalysis, from a Greek word meaning "loosening".

The modern definition: a catalyst is a substance that makes a reaction go faster without being used up by it. It takes part in the reaction, often grabbing the molecules and changing them along the way, but it is given back at the end, ready to do it again. A single catalyst site can repeat the cycle millions of times.

Chapter 02

A lower pass through the mountains

Picture two valleys separated by a high mountain. The starting materials are in one valley, the products in a lower one. Very few travellers have enough energy to climb over the peak. A catalyst does not lower the peak. It opens a different route, a lower pass through the mountains, so far more travellers get across in the same time.

The energy path with and without a catalyst
 energy
   |            /\         without catalyst: one tall hill
   |           /  \
   |          /    \
   |   start /  _   \      with catalyst: smaller hills by a different route
   |  ------/  / \_  \
   |          /    \  \
   |                \__\________  finish (same depth either way)
   +------------------------------------------- progress of reaction

Two consequences follow, and both are commonly got wrong:

  • A catalyst speeds the reverse reaction exactly as much as the forward one. The pass works in both directions. So it does not change where a reaction's balance point lies, as the previous topic explained. It only gets you there sooner.
  • A catalyst cannot make an impossible reaction possible. It cannot make the far valley deeper. If thermochemistry says a reaction will not run on its own, no catalyst will change that.

Because reaction rates depend steeply on the height of the hill, lowering it even modestly has a huge effect. A reduction that sounds small can make a reaction millions of times faster at the same temperature, or let it run at hundreds of degrees cooler. In industry, a better catalyst usually means the same product with far less heat and pressure.

Chapter 03

Three families of catalyst

FamilyWhat it isTypical example
HeterogeneousA solid catalyst working on gases or liquids that flow over it. "Hetero" means different: the catalyst is in a different state from the reactants.Platinum in a car exhaust; iron in ammonia plants; minerals called zeolites in oil refineries
HomogeneousA catalyst dissolved in the same liquid as the reactants. Often a metal atom wrapped in carefully designed organic molecules.Many steps in making medicines and fine chemicals
Enzymes (biocatalysts)Proteins made by living things, each shaped for one reaction or a few.Digestive enzymes; the enzymes in biological washing powder

Each family has a typical trade-off. Solids are easy to separate from the product and survive heat, which is why heavy industry prefers them. Dissolved catalysts can be tuned very precisely but are harder to recover. Enzymes are the most selective and work at body temperature, but many are fragile outside a cell.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Holding molecules in the right way, for just long enough

Every catalyst does a version of the same trick. It grabs the reacting molecules, holds them in a position where the reaction is easier, and then lets the product go.

Chapter 04

On a surface: stick, react, leave

A solid catalyst works only at its surface. Gas molecules land on it and stick, a process called adsorption (with a d: sticking to a surface, not soaking in). Sticking weakens their internal bonds. A hydrogen molecule, for example, can split into two separate hydrogen atoms on a platinum or nickel surface. Separate atoms react far more easily than a tightly bound pair. Once the new molecule forms, it lets go and leaves, freeing the spot for the next one.

That last step reveals a deep rule, often called the Sabatier principle after the French chemist Paul Sabatier, who shared the 1912 Nobel Prize for using finely divided nickel to add hydrogen to organic molecules. The rule: a good catalyst holds molecules neither too weakly nor too strongly. Too weak, and nothing sticks long enough to react. Too strong, and the product never leaves, so the surface clogs. The best catalysts sit in the middle, and much of catalyst research is the search for that "just right" grip.

Because only the surface works, catalysts are made with as much surface as possible. Industrial catalysts are often tiny metal particles spread on a porous support, a material riddled with microscopic channels. A teaspoon of such a support can have an internal surface the size of a tennis court or more. This is one reason why catalysis and nanochemistry are close relatives (Chemistry · Nanochemistry).

Chapter 05

The catalytic converter, step by step

A car engine's exhaust contains three main pollutants: carbon monoxide, which is poisonous; unburnt fuel, which helps form smog; and nitrogen oxides, which form smog and acid rain. A "three-way" catalytic converter attacks all three at once.

Converters became standard on new cars in the United States from 1975, and later in Europe and elsewhere. They forced a second change: leaded petrol had to go, because lead coats the precious metals and kills them. That had a large health benefit of its own, covered in Chemistry · Toxicology.

The converter's weakness is the first few minutes of a journey. Until the honeycomb reaches a few hundred degrees, the reactions are too slow. A large share of a modern car's total pollution comes from cold starts, which is why short trips are dirtier per kilometre than long ones.

Chapter 06

Enzymes: catalysts shaped by evolution

An enzyme is a protein folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. Somewhere on it is a small pocket called the active site, where the reacting molecule fits. The old picture was "lock and key": only the right molecule fits the pocket. The modern picture, called induced fit, is more like a hand closing around an object. The enzyme changes shape slightly as the molecule binds, squeezing and positioning it so that the bonds to be broken are strained and the atoms that must meet are held side by side.

The speedups are almost unbelievable. Carbonic anhydrase, the enzyme that handles carbon dioxide in your blood, can process up to around a million molecules per second. Some enzymes accelerate reactions that would otherwise take millions of years, so that they finish in a fraction of a second. Without enzymes, the chemistry of life would be far too slow to keep anything alive.

Enzymes are also exquisitely selective. Many molecules come in two mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. An enzyme usually works on only one hand. Your body's chemistry is built from one hand of amino acids and one hand of sugars, and its enzymes reflect that. Volume 26 shows the same fit-the-pocket logic in medicines (How a medicine knows where to go), and the wider chemistry of enzymes is in Chemistry · Biochemistry.

Chapter 07

How catalysts die

"Not used up" is true in principle, but real catalysts wear out. Chemists recognise a few main causes:

Poisoning

Some substances bind to the active sites so strongly that they never leave, blocking them for good. Lead and sulfur are classic poisons for metal catalysts. Many drugs work as enzyme "poisons" on purpose: aspirin, for example, permanently blocks an enzyme involved in pain and inflammation.

Sintering

At high temperature, tiny metal particles slowly merge into bigger ones, like droplets joining on a window. Bigger particles have less surface per gram, so activity falls.

Fouling

Carbon deposits, often called coke, build up on the surface and smother it. Oil refineries burn the coke off their catalysts continuously and reuse them.

Unfolding

Enzymes lose their shape when heated or exposed to the wrong acidity, just as egg white turns solid when cooked. A shape-dependent catalyst without its shape does nothing.

Chapter 08

Selectivity: making the right product, not just any product

Speed is only half the value. The other half is selectivity, a catalyst's ability to favour one product over others. The same starting materials can often react in several ways. Change the catalyst and you change which way wins.

Ethylene, a simple gas, shows this well. With one family of catalysts, discovered by Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta in the 1950s (Nobel Prize 1963), it links into long, regular chains: the polyethylene of bottles and bags, with precisely controlled properties. Other catalysts turn the same gas into other chemicals. Mirror-image selectivity matters enormously for medicines, where one "hand" of a molecule may treat a disease and the other do nothing or cause harm. The 2001 Nobel Prize went to William Knowles, Ryoji Noyori and Barry Sharpless for catalysts that make one hand selectively. In 2021 Benjamin List and David MacMillan were honoured for showing that small organic molecules, with no metal at all, can do it too.

A selective catalyst saves twice: once by not making waste, and again by not having to separate the wanted product from the unwanted ones. This idea is central to Chemistry · Green chemistry.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The hidden machinery of the economy, and of the atmosphere

Catalysts rarely appear in the news. Remove them and fuel, food, plastics and medicines would all become scarcer and dearer, and some would disappear.

Chapter 09

Almost everything made in a chemical plant

A figure commonly cited in the chemical industry is that around 90% of chemical manufacturing processes use a catalyst at some stage. The exact share is hard to verify, but nobody in the field disputes the order of magnitude. A few of the big ones:

Fluid catalytic crackingZeolites, porous minerals with molecule-sized channelsBreaking heavy crude oil into petrol and lighter fuels

There is a historical footnote in the hydrogenation row. Partly hydrogenated oils contained trans fats, a by-product of incomplete reaction. They were later found to raise the risk of heart disease, and many countries have since restricted or banned them. A catalyst that does the job is not automatically one that does it safely.

Chapter 10

The catalyst that tore a hole in the sky

Catalysis also explains one of the great environmental scares of the twentieth century. High in the atmosphere, a thin layer of ozone absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light. In 1974 Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland showed that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), then used in fridges and aerosol cans, would drift up there, break apart in sunlight and release chlorine atoms.

The danger was that chlorine is a catalyst for destroying ozone. A chlorine atom breaks an ozone molecule, is released again by a second step, and goes on to break another. One chlorine atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before it is finally locked away. In 1985 British scientists reported a deep seasonal "hole" in the ozone over Antarctica. The 1987 Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs, and Molina, Rowland and Paul Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The ozone layer is slowly recovering, with projections suggesting the Antarctic hole could return to 1980 levels around the 2060s.

The lesson is that a catalyst's power cuts both ways. Because a catalyst is not used up, a tiny amount can do enormous work, good or bad.

Chapter 11

Enzymes in your kitchen and your laundry

Industrial enzymes are a quiet success story. Biological washing powders contain enzymes that break down protein stains, fats and starch at low temperatures, which saves energy because clothes can be washed cooler. Cheesemakers have used rennet, an enzyme mixture from calves' stomachs, for thousands of years, and much cheese is now made with the same enzyme produced by microbes. Lactose-free milk is ordinary milk treated with the enzyme lactase. Much of the sugar syrup in American soft drinks is made by an enzyme that converts glucose into the sweeter fructose.

Medicine and biology lean on enzymes too. The PCR test, used to copy DNA for diagnosis and forensics, depends on a heat-tolerant enzyme from a microbe found in hot springs. Producing proteins such as insulin, or dairy proteins without cows, uses microbes as living enzyme factories (Precision fermentation). An enzyme is a catalyst you can grow, and that makes it cheap once you have the right organism.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous leverage, with expensive ingredients

Catalysts save energy, waste and money. Their costs come from rare materials, fragility, and the difficulty of finding new ones.

One subtle disadvantage is that a cheap catalysed process can make a product so abundant that its downstream problems grow. Ziegler-Natta catalysts made plastics cheap enough to use once and throw away, and Volume 26 traces where that led (Plastics, and the problem with a good idea). A catalyst speeds up whatever we choose to make, including our mistakes.

Chapter 14

Enzymes that eat plastic

In 2016 Japanese researchers described a bacterium, found near a bottle recycling site, that could live on PET, the plastic of drinks bottles. It used an enzyme, since named PETase, to break the plastic back into its building blocks. Scientists have since engineered faster, more heat-tolerant versions, and at least one company has announced plans for commercial-scale enzymatic recycling plants, with timelines that have slipped.

The attraction is that the building blocks come out pure enough to make new, food-grade plastic, which ordinary recycling cannot reliably do. The limits are real too: the process works best on clean PET, it needs the plastic shredded and treated first, and it does nothing for the many other kinds of plastic (Plastics, and why most recycling is not). Enzymatic recycling is a promising tool for one plastic, not a cure for plastic waste.

The bigger picture is that catalysis is where chemistry meets the energy transition. Making hydrogen from water, turning carbon dioxide into fuels, and making fertiliser without fossil gas all depend on catalysts that are cheaper, longer-lived or more selective than today's.

The core idea

A catalyst speeds a reaction by offering a route with a lower energy hill, and is given back unchanged at the end. It speeds both directions equally, so it never changes the equilibrium and cannot make an impossible reaction happen. Solid catalysts work at surfaces by holding molecules neither too weakly nor too strongly. Enzymes do the same in a shaped pocket, with extraordinary speed and selectivity.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 90% of chemical manufacturing processes use catalysts (a widely cited industry estimate).
  • Carbonic anhydrase handles up to about a million molecules per second.
  • One chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules.
  • Catalytic converters became standard on new US cars from 1975.
The common mistake

Thinking a catalyst supplies energy or increases yield. It supplies a route, not energy, and it reaches the same end point faster.
