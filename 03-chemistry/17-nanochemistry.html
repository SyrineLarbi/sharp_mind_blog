Nanochemistry. Make a material small enough and it stops behaving like itself.

A gold ring is yellow, shiny and stubbornly unreactive. Cut that gold into particles a few billionths of a metre across and it can turn red, purple or blue in water, and it can speed up chemical reactions that solid gold ignores. Nothing about the gold atoms has changed. Only the size has. Nanochemistry is the chemistry of that strange in-between world, larger than a molecule and far smaller than anything you can see. It asks how to make such tiny pieces of matter, how to control their shape, and why they behave so differently.

Question 1 · What is it?

Chemistry at the scale where size becomes a property

At ordinary sizes, a material's colour, melting point and reactivity are fixed. At the nanoscale they start to depend on how big the piece is.

Chapter 01

A Roman cup that changes colour

The British Museum holds a glass cup made in the Roman Empire, probably in the fourth century, known as the Lycurgus Cup. Lit from the front, it looks a dull jade green. Lit from inside, it glows ruby red. For centuries nobody could explain it. Modern analysis found the answer: the glass contains tiny particles of gold and silver, tens of billionths of a metre across, spread through it. The Roman glassmakers almost certainly had no idea what they had done. They had made a nanomaterial by accident.

Medieval glassmakers did something similar on purpose, if without understanding. The deep red of many cathedral windows comes from gold particles of that size. In 1857 the English scientist Michael Faraday made ruby-coloured liquids by reducing gold salts in water and argued that the colour came from gold in extremely finely divided form. Some of his samples survive at the Royal Institution in London. The same metal, divided small enough, takes on a completely different colour.

Chapter 02

How small is "nano"?

A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. It is hard to picture, so some comparisons help:

ObjectApproximate size
Width of a human hairTens of thousands of nanometres (roughly 50,000–100,000)
A red blood cellAbout 7,000 nanometres
A typical virusAbout 20–300 nanometres
Width of a DNA double helixAbout 2 nanometres
A single gold atomAbout 0.3 nanometres across

By the most common definition, a nanomaterial has at least one dimension between about 1 and 100 nanometres. That is the range where a particle contains anywhere from a few dozen to a few million atoms. Nanochemistry is the branch of chemistry that makes and studies such materials. Its sister field, nanotechnology, is about using them in devices and products.

Chapter 03

Two reasons small is different

Almost everything strange about the nanoscale comes from two effects:

1 · Almost everything is surface

In a lump of metal you can hold, almost all atoms are buried inside, surrounded by neighbours. In a particle a few nanometres wide, a large share of the atoms sit on the surface, with fewer neighbours and loose "unused" bonding capacity. Surface atoms are more reactive, so the whole particle becomes more reactive.

2 · Quantum rules start to show

Electrons behave like waves. In a big crystal they have plenty of room, and the material's colour and electrical behaviour are fixed. In a tiny particle the electrons are squeezed, and the energies they can have depend on the particle's size. Change the size and you change how it absorbs and emits light.

Every nanomaterial in this file is an example of one or both. At the nanoscale, size and shape become chemical properties, as important as which atoms you started with.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Surfaces, quantum colours, and building from the bottom up

First what happens to matter when it gets small, then how chemists actually make and see things that small.

Chapter 04

The surface explosion

Take a sugar cube one centimetre on each side. Its surface is six square centimetres. Cut it in half along each direction and you have eight smaller cubes with twice the total surface. Keep cutting until each piece is one nanometre across, and the total surface of the same amount of material reaches about 6,000 square metres, roughly the area of a football pitch. Nothing was added. The material was just divided.

Reactions happen at surfaces, so this matters enormously. Flour dust in a mill and fine coal dust in a mine can explode, while a loaf or a lump burns slowly. And materials that are inert in bulk can become active. Bulk gold is famous for not reacting with anything. In the 1980s the Japanese chemist Masatake Haruta showed that gold particles a few nanometres wide, on a suitable support, are excellent catalysts for turning carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide, even below room temperature. This is why catalysis and nanochemistry are so closely linked (Catalysts).

Size changes physical properties too. Because surface atoms are held less tightly, small particles melt at lower temperatures than the bulk material: gold particles a couple of nanometres across melt hundreds of degrees below the familiar melting point of solid gold. Below about a hundred nanometres, the textbook values of a material stop being fixed numbers.

Chapter 05

Colours from size: quantum dots and plasmons

Quantum dots are crystals of a semiconductor (a material that conducts electricity partly, like the silicon in chips) only a few nanometres across. Shine ultraviolet light on a tray of them and they glow in vivid colours. The striking thing is that a single substance can glow in any colour, depending only on size. Smaller dots squeeze their electrons more, so they emit higher-energy light, toward blue. Larger dots emit toward red.

Alexei Ekimov and Louis Brus discovered the effect independently in the early 1980s, and Moungi Bawendi developed a reliable way to make dots of precise sizes in 1993. The three shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Quantum dots are now used in some television and monitor screens, sold as "QLED", to produce purer colours.

Gold and silver particles get their colours differently. Their electrons can slosh back and forth together across the particle, like water in a bowl, when light of the right colour hits them. This collective wave is called a plasmon. The particle strongly absorbs and scatters that colour, and the rest passes through. That is the Lycurgus Cup's secret, and it is also how many rapid tests work: the red line on a typical lateral-flow test, such as many home pregnancy tests and COVID-19 tests, is usually made of gold nanoparticles.

Chapter 06

Two ways to build small: carving down and growing up

There are two basic strategies for making nanostructures.

Top-down

Start with a large piece and carve it. Grinding and milling break solids into fine powders. The computer-chip industry uses lithography, printing patterns with light onto silicon and etching away the rest, to make features only nanometres across. Top-down is precise in position, but expensive and limited by the tools.

Bottom-up

Start with atoms or molecules and let them assemble into particles. This is the chemist's natural method. It can make billions of identical particles in a flask at once, cheaply, but placing each one exactly where you want it is hard.

A classic bottom-up recipe makes gold nanoparticles. Dissolve a gold salt in boiling water and add sodium citrate, a substance related to the acid in lemons. The citrate pulls electrons onto the gold ions, turning them into metal atoms, which cluster into particles. The citrate then coats the particles and stops them sticking together. Within minutes the pale yellow liquid turns deep red. Versions of this method, described by John Turkevich in 1951, are still taught and used.

The key ideas are controlling nucleation, the moment tiny seeds first form, and growth, how they get bigger. If all the seeds form at once and then grow together, the particles come out almost the same size. Bawendi's quantum-dot method did exactly this, by injecting the ingredients quickly into a very hot liquid. Getting particles of one size, rather than a mix, is most of the craft, because at this scale size decides properties.

Chapter 07

Self-assembly: letting molecules build themselves

The most elegant bottom-up method is self-assembly: designing molecules that arrange themselves into larger structures on their own, guided by the forces between them. Soap is the everyday example. Each soap molecule has a water-loving end and an oil-loving end, and in water they gather spontaneously into tiny balls called micelles, with the oily tails hidden inside (Why soap works). The fat bubbles of mRNA vaccines are a more elaborate version of the same trick.

Chemists now push this much further. In 2006 Paul Rothemund at Caltech showed that a long strand of DNA could be folded into almost any two-dimensional shape, including a smiley face, by adding hundreds of short "staple" strands that each bind to specific places. The technique, called DNA origami, uses DNA purely as a building material. Life itself is the master of self-assembly: cell membranes, protein machines and viruses all assemble from their parts without outside help.

Chapter 08

The carbon family: footballs, tubes and sheets

Carbon atoms can link into nanostructures with remarkable properties.

FormWhat it isDiscovery

Carbon nanotubesSheets of carbon rolled into tubes a few nanometres wide. Very strong and, depending on structure, metal-like or semiconducting.Widely credited to Sumio Iijima's 1991 report, though earlier observations exist
GrapheneA single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb pattern, one atom thick. Very strong, conducts electricity and heat superbly.Isolated in 2004 by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov, famously using sticky tape on graphite (Nobel Prize in Physics 2010)

Graphite in a pencil is simply graphene layers stacked loosely. A pencil line may well contain a few flakes thin enough to count as graphene. What was hard was isolating a single layer and proving what it could do. Turning these materials into cheap, reliable products at scale has proved slower than the early excitement suggested.

Chapter 09

Seeing the invisible

Visible light cannot show anything much smaller than a few hundred nanometres, so nanochemistry needed new eyes. In 1981 Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM invented the scanning tunnelling microscope, which moves an extremely sharp tip across a surface and measures a tiny electric current that depends on the distance to each atom. It produced the first images of individual atoms on a surface and earned a Nobel Prize in 1986. The related atomic force microscope "feels" the surface with a tip on a tiny spring and works on non-conducting materials too.

In 1989 another IBM researcher, Don Eigler, used such a microscope to push 35 xenon atoms into the letters "IBM". It was a stunt, but a meaningful one: for the first time, people could both see and move individual atoms. Electron microscopes, which use beams of electrons instead of light, are the everyday workhorses for checking the size and shape of nanoparticles.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

In your arm, on your skin, and under the microscope of regulators

Nanomaterials are already in medicines, sunscreens, screens and catalysts. The same properties that make them useful raise new safety questions.

Chapter 10

Nanomaterials you have already met

The most widely used nanomedicine in history arrived in 2020 and 2021. The mRNA COVID-19 vaccines work only because their fragile genetic message is packed inside lipid nanoparticles, fat bubbles roughly a hundred nanometres across that protect it and help it enter cells. Hundreds of millions of people received them. Long before that, a cancer drug wrapped in tiny fat bubbles (liposomes), approved in the United States in 1995, showed that packaging a drug at this scale could change where it goes in the body and reduce some side effects.

Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. As coarse powders they leave a thick white layer on the skin. Ground to nanoparticles, they still block ultraviolet light but scatter far less visible light, so they look nearly transparent. Silver nanoparticles, which release silver ions that kill bacteria, appear in some wound dressings and fabrics. Catalysts in cars and refineries rely on metal nanoparticles. Nanochemistry is less a future technology than a present one that usually goes unlabelled.

Chapter 11

Same substance, new hazard: nanotoxicology

The rule from Toxicology is that the dose makes the poison. At the nanoscale, a complication appears: the size and shape can change the hazard, not only the amount. A substance known to be safe as a coarse powder may behave differently as nanoparticles, because they are more reactive, can reach places in the body that larger particles cannot, and may cross some biological barriers.

Two cases show how regulators have responded. Some studies in animals found that long, rigid carbon nanotubes, when inhaled, can cause lung reactions resembling those caused by asbestos fibres, which share their needle-like shape. This has led to cautious workplace handling rules. Shorter or tangled tubes appear less concerning, and the risk depends on the type. And in 2022 the European Union banned titanium dioxide as a food colouring (E171), after its food safety authority concluded in 2021 that it could not rule out harm to DNA, partly because of the nano-sized fraction of the particles. Other regulators, including in the United States, have so far reached different conclusions. The evidence is still contested.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

New properties come with new unknowns

The case for nanochemistry is that it unlocks properties no bulk material has. The case for caution is the same sentence.

Chapter 12

Weighing it up

FeatureAdvantageDisadvantage
Huge surface areaBetter catalysts and batteries, more efficient use of scarce metals.More reactive, so some powders are flammable or explosive, and particles tend to clump, losing their benefit.
Size-tuned propertiesColours, conductivity and reactivity can be chosen by adjusting size.Every batch must be tightly controlled. A slightly different size gives a different product.
Bottom-up synthesisBillions of particles cheaply, in a flask.Scaling up while keeping sizes uniform is difficult and often expensive.
Reach inside the bodyDelivers fragile drugs and vaccines to cells.The same ability raises questions about where particles end up and how long they stay.
Novel materialsGraphene, nanotubes and quantum dots have properties no older material matches.Some quantum dots contain toxic metals such as cadmium, and long-term environmental fate is poorly known.
RegulationAgencies increasingly assess nano forms separately.Definitions and rules differ between countries, and testing methods are still being standardised.

A fair overall judgment is that nanochemistry has delivered real, widespread benefits, while the grander early promises, of tiny machines building anything atom by atom, have not materialised. The useful nanomaterials so far are mostly clever particles and coatings, not miniature robots.

Chapter 14

Where the chemistry hands over to the technology

Much of nanochemistry's future is quiet improvement. Battery electrodes made of nanostructured materials charge faster because ions have less distance to travel. Catalysts that place single metal atoms on a support, the logical end point of the surface story, are an active research field and could stretch scarce platinum and iridium much further. Safer quantum dots, free of cadmium, are already replacing older ones in displays.

The bigger visions, from nanoelectronics beyond silicon to sensors woven into fabrics, belong to nanotechnology and are weighed in Future · Nanotechnology. What chemistry contributes is the foundation: whoever controls size, shape and surface controls the properties. The Roman glassmakers did it by luck. Modern chemists do it by design, and the difference between those two is the whole history of the field.

The core idea

Between about 1 and 100 nanometres, materials behave differently because a large share of their atoms are on the surface and because quantum effects make their properties depend on size. Chemists build nanomaterials top-down (carving) or bottom-up (growing from atoms and self-assembly), and see them with tip-based and electron microscopes. Size and shape can change hazard as well as usefulness.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. A hair is tens of thousands of nanometres wide.
  • One cubic centimetre cut into 1 nm cubes has about 6,000 m² of surface.
  • Quantum dots: Nobel Prize 2023. Graphene: 2004, Nobel 2010.
The common mistake

Assuming a substance known to be safe in bulk is equally safe as nanoparticles, or the reverse. At this scale, size is part of the chemistry, so each form needs its own assessment.

Test yourself

Can you explain why gold can be red, and why gold nanoparticles can be catalysts when a gold ring is inert? If you can, you understand the two reasons small is different.

The words, in plain words
Nanometre
One billionth of a metre.
Nanomaterial
A material with at least one dimension between about 1 and 100 nanometres.
Nanoparticle
A particle in that size range in all three dimensions.
Surface-to-volume ratio
How much surface a material has compared with its bulk. It rises sharply as things get smaller.
Quantum dot
A tiny semiconductor crystal whose glow colour depends on its size.
Plasmon
A collective sloshing of electrons in a metal particle, which gives gold and silver nanoparticles their colours.
Top-down
Making small structures by carving or grinding larger ones.
Bottom-up
Making small structures by growing or assembling them from atoms and molecules.
Nucleation
The first formation of tiny seed particles, which then grow.
Self-assembly
Molecules arranging themselves into larger structures without outside guidance.
Graphene
A single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb pattern.
Lipid nanoparticle
A tiny fat-based bubble used to carry fragile medicines, such as mRNA, into cells.
Nanotoxicology
The study of how nanomaterials can harm living things, beyond what their chemistry alone predicts.
