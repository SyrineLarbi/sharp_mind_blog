Chemical equilibrium. The balance point where a reaction looks finished but never stops.

Open a bottle of fizzy water and it hisses. Leave it open and it goes flat. Close a fresh bottle and it stays fizzy for months. Nothing in that bottle is still. Gas is dissolving and escaping all the time, in equal amounts, so nothing seems to change. That state is chemical equilibrium, and it is everywhere: in your blood, in the oceans, in the factories that make the fertiliser behind half the world's food. Understanding it means understanding why reactions stop short, and how to make them go further.

Question 1 · What is it?

A reaction going forward and backward at the same speed

Most people picture a reaction as a one-way street: ingredients in, products out, done. Many reactions are two-way streets, and they stop changing when the traffic in each direction is equal.

Chapter 01

The fizzy bottle

Carbon dioxide gas can dissolve in water, and dissolved carbon dioxide can escape back into gas. Both happen at once. In a sealed bottle, the space above the liquid fills with gas until molecules are escaping and re-dissolving at the same rate. From outside, nothing changes. Inside, there is constant two-way traffic.

Open the cap and the gas above the liquid rushes out. Now dissolving is slower than escaping, because there is so little gas left to dissolve. Bubbles form, the drink fizzes, and if you leave it open it eventually goes flat, settling into a new balance with the open air. Put the cap back on while it is still fizzy and it stops getting flatter, because a fresh balance forms inside.

Chemists write such reversible changes with a double arrow, ⇌, instead of a single one. And they call the state where the two directions run at equal speed chemical equilibrium. Equilibrium is not the absence of reaction. It is two opposite reactions cancelling out.

Chapter 02

Dynamic, not dead

An analogy helps. Picture a shop with two doors, one in and one out. In the morning, people pour in and few leave, so the crowd grows. As the shop fills, more people leave each minute. Eventually the number leaving per minute equals the number arriving. The crowd stays the same size all afternoon, but the people inside keep changing.

Chemists proved this is literal, not just a metaphor. Using atoms of a slightly heavier variety (isotopes, the same element with a different mass), they can "tag" some molecules in a mixture that has stopped changing. Over time, the tags spread into both sides of the reaction. The products are still turning back into starting materials, and the starting materials still turning into products. A mixture at equilibrium is busy, just balanced.

One point matters a lot later. The same equilibrium is reached whether you start from the left side or the right side. Mix hydrogen and iodine gas in a hot sealed flask, or start with pure hydrogen iodide, and at the same temperature both flasks end with the same proportions. The equilibrium is a property of the reaction and the conditions, not of the history.

Chapter 03

Where the balance sits: the equilibrium constant

Every reversible reaction has a characteristic balance point at a given temperature. Chemists capture it in a single number, the equilibrium constant, written K. Roughly, K is the amount of products divided by the amount of starting materials once the mixture has settled.

Size of KWhat the settled mixture looks likeExample
Huge (far above 1)Almost entirely products. The reaction effectively "goes to completion".Strong acids splitting up in water, a fire burning out
Around 1A real mixture of both sidesMany reactions in living cells, which can be pushed either way
Tiny (far below 1)Almost entirely starting materials. Little seems to happen.Water splitting into its ions: only about one molecule in 550 million at any moment

Two warnings. First, K says nothing about speed. A reaction with an enormous K can take a million years, as Topic 11 explained with the energy hill. Second, K connects directly to the free energy of Topic 11: a reaction with a strongly negative free-energy change has a large K. Equilibrium is where the downhill push runs out.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Push it, and it pushes back

The most useful rule in this topic fits in one sentence, and it lets you predict what happens when you change almost anything about a reacting mixture.

Chapter 04

Le Chatelier's principle

In 1884 the French chemist Henri Le Chatelier stated the rule now named after him. In modern words: if you disturb a system at equilibrium, it shifts in the direction that partly undoes the disturbance. It does not fully undo it, but it leans against the push.

Three kinds of push matter:

Add or remove a substance

Add more of a starting material and the reaction runs forward to use some of it up. Remove a product as it forms and the reaction keeps running forward to replace it. This is the cheapest trick in industry.

Change the pressure (gases)

Squeeze a gas mixture and it shifts toward the side with fewer gas molecules, because fewer molecules push less hard. If both sides have the same number, pressure makes no difference.

Change the temperature

Heat favours the direction that absorbs heat. Cooling favours the direction that releases it. Unlike the other two, this changes K itself (see the next chapter).

The fizzy bottle is the pressure rule in action. Sealing gas under pressure pushes more carbon dioxide into the water. Releasing the pressure lets it come back out. Divers meet the same rule with nitrogen in their blood: breathing compressed air at depth dissolves extra nitrogen in the body, and rising too quickly lets it come out as bubbles, which is the main cause of decompression sickness, "the bends".

Chapter 05

Why temperature is the special one

Adding a substance or squeezing a gas moves the mixture to a new position, but the balance point, K, stays the same. Temperature is different. Changing the temperature changes K itself.

The logic follows from Topic 11. Think of heat as if it were one of the ingredients. For a reaction that releases heat, heat is on the product side. Add heat, and by Le Chatelier the reaction shifts back toward the starting materials to absorb it. So for heat-releasing reactions, hotter means a smaller share of products. For heat-absorbing reactions, hotter means more.

Here is the cruel twist that runs through industrial chemistry. Hotter almost always means faster. So for a heat-releasing reaction, the conditions that give you the best yield (cool) are the conditions that give you the slowest reaction. Chemists must pick a compromise, and the most famous compromise in history is the one in the next chapter.

Chapter 06

Haber-Bosch: the compromise that feeds billions

Ammonia, the starting point for most nitrogen fertiliser, is made from nitrogen and hydrogen:

The ammonia equilibrium, and how each condition is chosen
   N2   +   3 H2      ⇌      2 NH3        releases heat (about 92 kJ)
   1 molecule + 3 molecules  ->  2 molecules     (4 gas molecules become 2)

   PRESSURE     fewer molecules on the right  ->  high pressure helps
                chosen: very high, roughly 150-300 atmospheres

   TEMPERATURE  heat is released             ->  cool helps the yield...
                ...but cool is far too slow
                chosen: about 400-500 °C, the compromise

   CATALYST     iron-based                    ->  speeds both directions,
                                                  does not move the balance

   RECYCLING    unreacted gas is cooled, ammonia condensed out as liquid,
                and the leftover N2 and H2 sent round again

The German chemist Fritz Haber showed in 1909 that the reaction could be made to work at high pressure with a suitable catalyst. Carl Bosch, at the company BASF, turned it into an industrial process by 1913, which meant building steel vessels that could survive the pressures and temperatures. Both received Nobel Prizes, Haber in 1918 and Bosch in 1931. Haber's legacy is deeply mixed: he also led Germany's chlorine gas programme in the First World War.

Each condition is a Le Chatelier decision. High pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules, which is ammonia. The temperature is a deliberate trade: lower would give more ammonia at equilibrium but would take too long. Even so, only a fraction of the gas turns into ammonia on each pass through the reactor. The plant gets around this with the cheapest trick from Chapter 04: it removes the product by cooling the mixture until ammonia liquefies, and sends the rest round again. Over many loops, nearly all of the feed gas ends up as ammonia.

The catalyst deserves a precise sentence because it is so often misdescribed. A catalyst does not change where the equilibrium lies. It only helps the mixture get there faster. It speeds the forward and backward reactions equally (Chemistry · Catalysts).

Chapter 07

Which way will it go? Comparing now with the balance point

At any moment, before a mixture has settled, chemists can calculate the same ratio as K using the current amounts. This snapshot is called the reaction quotient, Q. Comparing the two answers the question "which way will this reaction run from here?"

  • If Q is smaller than K, there are too few products. The reaction runs forward.
  • If Q is larger than K, there are too many products. It runs backward.
  • If Q equals K, the mixture is at equilibrium and nothing changes overall.

This is how a cell can run the same reaction in opposite directions in different organs. The reaction and its K are fixed. What changes is the local mix of ingredients, so Q sits on one side of K in the liver and on the other in a muscle. Direction depends on where you are relative to the balance point, not just on the reaction.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Your blood, your breath and the sea

Some of the most important equilibria on Earth are not in factories. They run inside your body every second, and across the whole ocean.

Chapter 08

How your blood holds its acidity steady

Human blood is kept within a narrow acidity range, a pH of about 7.35 to 7.45. pH is the scale from Volume 26: lower is more acidic, and each step is tenfold. Outside that range, enzymes and nerves stop working properly, and large departures are life-threatening.

The main control is an equilibrium linking carbon dioxide, water and bicarbonate:

The bicarbonate buffer
   CO2 + H2O   ⇌   H2CO3   ⇌   H+   +   HCO3-
   (breathed out)  (carbonic acid)  (acidity)  (bicarbonate)

   too much acid (H+) in the blood  ->  equilibrium shifts LEFT,
                                        makes more CO2, which the lungs breathe out
   too little acid                  ->  shifts RIGHT, releasing H+

A mixture that resists changes in acidity like this is called a buffer. The clever part is that one ingredient, carbon dioxide, is a gas your lungs can remove in seconds, and the other, bicarbonate, is managed by your kidneys over hours to days. Breathing is partly a way of steering a chemical equilibrium. When someone hyperventilates during panic, they blow off too much carbon dioxide, the equilibrium shifts, and their blood becomes slightly too alkaline. The tingling fingers and light-headedness that follow are partly chemistry.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Breathing problems, confusion or chest pain need real medical assessment.

Chapter 09

Oxygen, haemoglobin and the silent poison

Haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells, binds oxygen reversibly. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, the equilibrium shifts toward bound oxygen. In working muscles, where oxygen is scarce and carbon dioxide and acidity are high, it shifts the other way and the oxygen is released. Your circulation is an equilibrium being pushed one way in the lungs and the other way in the tissues, about once a minute.

Carbon monoxide hijacks this. It binds to the same site on haemoglobin about two hundred times more strongly than oxygen. Even a small amount in the air ties up a large share of the blood's carrying capacity (Carbon monoxide). The treatment is pure Le Chatelier: give the patient a high concentration of oxygen, sometimes in a pressure chamber, so the flood of oxygen pushes carbon monoxide off the haemoglobin faster.

Chapter 10

The ocean as a giant buffer under strain

The same carbon dioxide equilibria run in seawater. The ocean has absorbed roughly a quarter to a third of the carbon dioxide humans have emitted, which has slowed climate change. But the extra carbon dioxide shifts the equilibria toward more acidity. Average surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 before the industrial era to about 8.1 today. Because the scale is logarithmic, that small-looking drop means roughly a 30% increase in acidity.

The seawater is still alkaline, not acidic, so "ocean acidification" means "becoming less alkaline". The harm comes through a linked equilibrium. Corals, oysters, mussels and many plankton build shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate, and more acidity shifts the balance away from the carbonate they need. Building shells becomes more costly, and in some waters existing shells can start to dissolve. The details vary by species and region, and research is active (Environment · Greenhouse gases).

The same chemistry carves caves. Rainwater carrying dissolved carbon dioxide slowly dissolves limestone. When that water drips into an open cave and loses carbon dioxide to the air, the equilibrium shifts back, and calcium carbonate comes out of solution again, one thin layer at a time, as stalactites. A cave is Le Chatelier's principle running for tens of thousands of years. The limescale in a kettle is the same reaction, driven by heat.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful map with one big blank area

Equilibrium thinking is one of the best predictive tools in chemistry. Its weak point is that much of the real world is never at equilibrium.

Chapter 11

What equilibrium thinking gets right, and where it fails

FeatureStrengthWeakness
Predicts the end pointTells you the maximum yield possible under given conditions, before you build anything.Says nothing about how long it takes to get there.
Le Chatelier's principleSimple, qualitative and almost always right about direction.Tells you which way, not how far. For numbers you need K and calculation.
Removing productsLets industry beat a poor balance point by recycling, as in ammonia plants.Recycling gas loops and separations cost energy and equipment.
Temperature trade-offClear logic for choosing operating conditions.For heat-releasing reactions, yield and speed pull in opposite directions. There is no free lunch.

Chapter 12

Equilibrium is the one place life must never reach

A living cell is crowded with reactions that are held deliberately away from equilibrium. It keeps far more potassium inside than outside, far less sodium, and a steady store of energy-carrying molecules that would break down if left alone. All of that costs a constant supply of food and oxygen. When the supply stops, the reactions slide toward their balance points. For a cell, reaching chemical equilibrium is another word for death.

What a cell keeps instead is a steady state: amounts that stay constant because matter flows in and out at matching rates, like a bath with the tap running and the plug out. It looks like equilibrium from outside, and it is the opposite inside. Confusing the two is a common error, even in textbooks. The same distinction applies to a river, a flame, or Earth's atmosphere, whose high oxygen content is maintained by photosynthesis and would slowly fall without it.

Chapter 14

Steering the ocean's equilibrium on purpose

Because ocean chemistry is an equilibrium, some researchers propose pushing it deliberately. Ocean alkalinity enhancement means adding finely ground minerals or other alkaline substances to seawater. By Le Chatelier, that shifts the carbonate equilibria so the water can hold more carbon dioxide as dissolved bicarbonate, drawing more from the air and counteracting acidification locally.

The chemistry is sound. The open questions are scale, measurement and side effects. Verifying how much extra carbon dioxide a patch of ocean actually absorbed is hard, and the effects on marine life of adding minerals at large scale are not well understood. As of 2026 it remains at the stage of small field trials, and it is contested: supporters see a necessary tool, critics see a distraction from cutting emissions. Both sides agree that it could only ever add to, not replace, emission cuts.

The wider lesson carries beyond chemistry. Systems that sit at a balance push back when you push them, and they often settle somewhere you did not intend. Le Chatelier's principle was written about flasks. It is a useful habit of mind about any system with feedback.

The core idea

Many reactions are reversible. At equilibrium the forward and backward reactions run at equal speed, so the amounts stop changing while molecules keep reacting. The equilibrium constant K describes where the balance sits. Le Chatelier's principle predicts how it shifts when you change amounts, pressure or temperature. Only temperature changes K itself, and a catalyst changes only the speed.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Haber-Bosch runs at about 400–500 °C and 150–300 atmospheres, a compromise between yield and speed.
  • Blood pH is held at about 7.35–7.45.
  • Surface ocean pH has dropped from about 8.2 to 8.1, roughly 30% more acidic.
  • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin about 200 times more strongly than oxygen.
The common mistake

Thinking a catalyst increases the yield. A catalyst gets you to the same balance point faster. It does not move the balance point.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Haber-Bosch plants run hot even though heat lowers the yield of ammonia? If you can, you have understood Le Chatelier, temperature and speed together.

The words, in plain words
Reversible reaction
A reaction that can run in both directions, written with ⇌.
Chemical equilibrium
The state where forward and backward reactions run at the same speed, so amounts stop changing.
Dynamic
Still moving underneath. Equilibrium is dynamic: molecules keep reacting both ways.
Equilibrium constant (K)
A number describing the balance of products to starting materials once a reaction has settled.
Reaction quotient (Q)

Yield
How much product you actually get, compared with the most that is possible.

Constant amounts kept up by a continuous flow in and out. It looks like equilibrium but is not.
