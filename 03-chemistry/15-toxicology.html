Toxicology. Everything is a poison. The question is always how much, how often, and to whom.

Drink too much water too fast and it can kill you. Swallow a tiny amount of the toxin made by one bacterium and it can kill you too, yet doctors inject that same toxin, highly diluted, to smooth wrinkles and treat muscle spasms. Toxicology is the science that makes sense of this. It asks how substances harm living things, at what dose, by what route, and over what time. Its answers decide what goes into food, medicines and drinking water.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of harm, and of the amounts that cause it

Toxicology starts from an uncomfortable fact. There are no safe chemicals and no dangerous ones in the abstract. There are only safe and dangerous exposures.

Chapter 01

The doctor who said it first

In the early sixteenth century, a Swiss-German physician who called himself Paracelsus attacked the medicine of his day. He used mercury, arsenic and other dangerous substances as treatments, and critics accused him of poisoning his patients. His defence, written in the 1530s, became the founding idea of toxicology:

"All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; only the dose makes a thing not a poison."

Paracelsus · 1530s · usually shortened to "the dose makes the poison"

Water, drunk in huge amounts in a short time, dilutes the salt in the blood and can cause fatal brain swelling; this has killed people in drinking contests and some marathon runners. Oxygen at high pressure damages the lungs and nervous system. And the most poisonous substance known, botulinum toxin, made by the bacterium that causes botulism, is used safely as a medicine in doses measured in billionths of a gram. Toxicity is never a property of a substance alone. It is a property of a substance at a dose.

Chapter 02

Hazard is not risk

The single most useful distinction in the field is between two words people treat as the same.

Hazard

What a substance could do to you, in principle, at a high enough exposure. Petrol is a hazard: it is flammable and harmful to breathe. A shark is a hazard.

Risk

The likelihood that it actually will harm you, given how much you are exposed to, and how. Petrol sealed in a car's tank is a low risk. A shark in an aquarium on the other side of the glass is no risk at all.

Risk is hazard multiplied by exposure. A highly hazardous substance you never meet carries no risk. A mildly hazardous one you meet every day can matter a lot. Many scares are hazard stories told as risk stories: a substance harmful to rats at enormous doses is reported as if a trace on a label were the same.

Chapter 03

Fast poisons and slow ones

Toxicologists separate harm by time. Acute toxicity is harm from a single large exposure, appearing within hours or days: a child swallowing tablets, a worker breathing a gas leak. Chronic toxicity is harm from small exposures repeated over months or years: lead in old paint, asbestos fibres, decades of smoking. The two can be completely different.

For acute toxicity, the classic measure was introduced by the British pharmacologist J. W. Trevan in 1927: the LD50, the dose that kills half of a group of test animals, usually given per kilogram of body weight. A lower LD50 means a more poisonous substance. It is now used much less: it kills many animals and says nothing about chronic harm. Modern tests use far fewer animals, and increasingly none.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Getting in, getting around, doing damage, getting out

Every poisoning is a journey with four stages, and a target at the end.

Chapter 04

The journey through the body

Toxicologists and pharmacologists use the same four-step map, often shortened to ADME:

StageWhat happensWhy it matters
AbsorptionThe substance gets in: through the gut, the lungs, the skin or an injection.Mercury in a thermometer is poorly absorbed if swallowed, but its vapour is readily absorbed through the lungs. The route can matter more than the substance.
DistributionBlood carries it around. Some substances stay in the blood, others settle in fat, bone or the brain.Lead is stored in bone for years. Fat-loving chemicals accumulate in body fat.
MetabolismEnzymes, mostly in the liver, chemically change it, usually to make it easier to excrete.Usually this detoxifies. Sometimes it creates something worse (next chapter).
ExcretionIt leaves, mainly through urine, faeces or breath.Substances that leave slowly build up with repeated exposure.

A useful measure is the half-life: the time the body takes to clear half of a substance. Caffeine's half-life in a healthy adult is a few hours, which is why an afternoon coffee can still affect your sleep. Some industrial chemicals have half-lives in the body of several years. A short half-life forgives repeated small exposures. A long one adds them up.

Chapter 05

When the liver makes things worse: paracetamol

Paracetamol (called acetaminophen in North America) is one of the safest painkillers at normal doses, and one of the most common causes of serious liver poisoning in overdose. The reason is a lesson in metabolism.

Most of each dose is converted by the liver into harmless forms and excreted. A small fraction is turned into a highly reactive by-product that damages liver cells. Normally the liver neutralises it straight away using a protective molecule called glutathione. In an overdose, the glutathione runs out, the by-product builds up, and liver cells die. The damage often shows only a day or more later, when the person may feel well and the danger is easy to miss.

The antidote, acetylcysteine, works by helping the liver replenish its glutathione. It is most effective when given early, which is why medical services treat a suspected paracetamol overdose as urgent even when the patient seems fine. Volume 44 has the practical side (The painkillers, and the one that is genuinely different). The body's own chemistry can turn a safe substance into a dangerous one when its defences are overwhelmed.

Chapter 06

How poisons actually kill: four classic targets

Most poisons work by interfering with one specific piece of the body's machinery. Four famous examples show the range:

Carbon monoxide: blocking delivery

It binds to haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in blood, about two hundred times more strongly than oxygen. The blood keeps flowing but carries less and less oxygen. See Chemical equilibrium for why high-dose oxygen is the treatment.

Cyanide: blocking use

It shuts down a key enzyme that cells need to use oxygen to make energy. Blood stays full of oxygen, but the cells cannot use it, so the heart and brain fail quickly.

Nerve agents and some insecticides: jamming the signal

Nerves pass messages using a chemical messenger, and an enzyme clears it away after each message. Nerve agents such as sarin, and the related organophosphate insecticides, block that enzyme. Signals never switch off, and muscles, including those for breathing, go into spasm and then fail.

Lead: the impostor

Lead atoms are similar enough to calcium to slip into processes that use calcium, including signalling between nerve cells. In children's developing brains, this harms learning and behaviour, often without obvious symptoms.

This "one target" logic is also how most medicines work, which is why the line between a drug and a poison is so often just dose.

Chapter 07

The dose-response curve, and the argument about thresholds

If you give increasing doses of a substance and measure the effect, you get a dose-response curve. For most kinds of harm, the curve has a threshold: below a certain dose, the body's defences cope and there is no measurable effect. Above it, harm rises with dose.

For substances that cause cancer by damaging DNA, and for radiation, regulators often assume there is no safe threshold. Every dose is treated as carrying a small risk in proportion to its size. This "linear no-threshold" assumption is deliberately cautious. It is also contested: some scientists argue the body repairs low-level damage well enough that small doses are harmless, and a minority argue that very small doses can even be beneficial, an idea called hormesis. The honest summary is that risks at very low doses are too small to measure directly, so the shape of the curve there is a choice of assumption, not an observation.

Chapter 08

Climbing the food chain: Minamata and DDT

Some substances are not excreted easily. Each meal adds a little more than the body can remove, so the amount builds up over a lifetime: bioaccumulation. And when a predator eats many contaminated prey, it collects all of their loads at once. Up each step of the food chain, the concentration rises: biomagnification.

The town of Minamata in Japan is the textbook case. From the 1930s to the 1960s, a chemical factory released mercury waste into the bay. Bacteria and chemistry turned some of it into methylmercury, a form that is readily absorbed and travels to the brain. It concentrated in fish, and the fishing families who ate them most suffered severe nerve damage, with cats in the town affected first. Babies were born damaged by mercury that had crossed the placenta. "Minamata disease" was officially recognised in 1956, but the factory continued discharging for years afterward.

The insecticide DDT showed the same effect in wildlife. It was cheap and highly effective against malaria mosquitoes. But it built up in birds of prey, thinned their eggshells and caused populations of species such as the bald eagle and peregrine falcon to crash. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring brought the issue to the public. Most agricultural use of DDT was banned in the following decades, while limited use against malaria continues in some countries. A substance can be harmless at the dose one creature receives and devastating at the dose the top of the food chain receives.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lead, limits and a drug that changed the rules

Toxicology turns lab findings into rules for billions of people. Its successes are invisible; its failures are remembered.

Chapter 09

Leaded petrol: the largest poisoning ever reversed

In the 1920s, the American engineer Thomas Midgley found that adding a lead compound, tetraethyl lead, to petrol stopped engines "knocking". (The same man later developed the CFCs that damaged the ozone layer, described in Catalysts.) Leaded petrol spread across the world. For decades, cars emitted lead into city air, and it settled into the dust where children played.

Researchers showed that even modest lead levels in children's blood were linked to lower test scores and attention problems. Health authorities now say there is no known safe level of lead exposure for children. Countries began phasing out leaded petrol in the 1970s and 1980s, helped by the arrival of catalytic converters, which lead would ruin. In 2021 the United Nations Environment Programme announced that leaded petrol for cars was no longer sold anywhere, the last country being Algeria.

The result was a huge drop in human lead exposure. In the United States, average blood lead levels in children fell by more than 90% between the late 1970s and the 2010s. Lead is still a serious problem from old paint, old water pipes, battery recycling and some cosmetics and spices, especially in lower-income countries.

Chapter 10

How a "safe" limit is actually set

For a substance with a threshold, regulators follow a recipe that is simple in outline:

  1. Find, from animal or human studies, the highest dose that caused no observed harmful effect. Toxicologists call it the NOAEL ("no observed adverse effect level").
  2. Divide it by a safety factor, typically 10 to allow for differences between animals and humans, and another 10 for differences between people, such as children, the elderly and the sick. That gives a factor of 100, more if the data are weak.
  3. The result is an acceptable daily intake: an amount that can be consumed every day for a lifetime without appreciable risk.

So the limit on a food additive or pesticide residue is usually around a hundred times below the dose that showed no harm in studies. Crossing it once does not mean harm. It means the safety margin has shrunk. A regulatory limit is a cautious line drawn far inside the danger zone, not the edge of the cliff.

The European Union's REACH law, in force since 2007, puts the burden on companies to provide safety data before substances can be sold in large amounts: broadly, "no data, no market". The United States regulates chemicals mainly under the Toxic Substances Control Act, strengthened in 2016, where the regulator has historically had to do more of the work of showing harm. The EU also applies the precautionary principle more readily: when the evidence of harm is uncertain but serious, it leans toward restriction.

Chapter 11

Thalidomide and the birth of modern drug testing

In the late 1950s, a sedative called thalidomide was sold in dozens of countries, and was given to pregnant women for morning sickness. It had seemed remarkably safe in standard tests. But it caused severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs. More than ten thousand children were affected worldwide before it was withdrawn in 1961 and 1962.

The United States largely escaped because Frances Kelsey, a reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration, refused to approve it while she waited for better safety data. The disaster transformed drug regulation. Laws in many countries began to require proof of safety and effectiveness, and testing for effects on unborn children. It also gave rise to developmental toxicology. Its central lesson: the same dose can be harmless to an adult and devastating to a foetus at a particular stage of development, so who is exposed, and when, matters as much as how much.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A strong science working with incomplete evidence

Toxicology's methods have prevented enormous harm. They also have blind spots that its practitioners openly discuss.

Chapter 12

What the methods do well, and where they struggle

Method or principleStrengthWeakness
Animal testingReveals effects on a whole living body that cell tests miss.Ethically costly, slow, and animals do not always respond like humans. Thalidomide's effects did not show in the standard rodent tests of its time.
Safety factors of 100Simple, transparent and conservative.Somewhat arbitrary. They may be too cautious for some substances and not cautious enough for others.
One substance at a timeClear, measurable results.Real life is mixtures. Combined effects of many low-level chemicals are much less studied.
Short and medium-term studiesPractical and repeatable.Effects that appear after decades, or only in the next generation, are hard to catch.
Epidemiology (studying human populations)Shows effects in real people at real exposures.Hard to separate a chemical from other causes. Associations are not always causes.
Precautionary restrictionProtects people before harm is proven.Can ban useful substances on weak evidence, or push industry to substitutes that are less studied and no safer.

The last row points to a recurring problem called regrettable substitution. When bisphenol A (BPA), a chemical in some plastics, came under pressure as a possible hormone disruptor, some manufacturers replaced it with close chemical cousins that had been studied far less. Some of those cousins appear to have similar effects. "BPA-free" did not necessarily mean safer. The debate about hormone-disrupting chemicals at low doses is real and still contested, with regulators in different regions reaching different conclusions from the same evidence.

Chapter 14

From one chemical to a whole life of exposures

The biggest conceptual shift in the field is from single substances to the exposome: the total of everything a person is exposed to from conception onward, including chemicals, diet, air pollution, stress and infections. Most chronic disease, on this view, comes from genes meeting that whole history, not from one villain. New techniques can now measure thousands of chemical traces in a single blood sample, which makes the idea testable for the first time.

The risk is drowning in data: measure a thousand chemicals and some will correlate with some disease by chance.

For ordinary readers, the old principles still do most of the work. Ask about the dose, the route and the duration. Ask whether a finding is about hazard or risk. Ask who is most exposed and most vulnerable. Most chemical fears dissolve under those questions, and the few that survive them are the ones worth taking seriously.

The core idea

The dose makes the poison. Harm depends on how much, by what route, for how long, and to whom. Risk is hazard times exposure. Substances travel through the body by absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion, and most poisons hit one specific target. Safe limits are set by finding the dose with no observed harm and dividing by large safety factors.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Typical safety factor: 100 (10 for animal to human, 10 for human variation).
  • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin about 200 times more strongly than oxygen.
  • US children's average blood lead fell by more than 90% after leaded petrol was phased out.
  • Thalidomide affected more than 10,000 children before its withdrawal in 1961–62.
The common mistake

Treating "linked to cancer" or "contains chemicals" as proof of danger. Everything is a chemical, and every hazard needs an exposure before it becomes a risk.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a pesticide limit sits far below the dose that harmed test animals, and why exceeding it once is not automatically dangerous? If you can, you understand thresholds and safety factors.
