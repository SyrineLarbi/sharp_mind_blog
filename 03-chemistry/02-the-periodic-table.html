The periodic table. A map on which position is a prediction.

Most people remember the periodic table as a poster on a classroom wall, a grid of letters to be memorised for an exam. That is the least interesting way to see it. The table is a compressed theory of matter. Where an element sits tells you how big its atoms are, whether it conducts electricity, what it will bond with, and even whether your body will mistake it for something else. It was built before anyone knew why it worked, and it predicted elements nobody had seen. A century and a half later, it still tells chemists where to look.

Question 1 · What is it?

A list of every element, folded so that families line up

An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. There are 118 known. The table puts them in order and then breaks the line at exactly the points where behaviour starts to repeat.

Chapter 01

The density that was measured wrong

In 1875 a French chemist, Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, found a new metal in a zinc ore and named it gallium. He measured its density and reported a value of about 4.7 grams per cubic centimetre. Soon after, he received a letter from Saint Petersburg. The Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev told him that his number was wrong. The true value, Mendeleev wrote, should be close to 5.9.

Mendeleev had never seen gallium. Six years earlier, in 1869, he had published a table of the roughly 63 elements then known. Where the pattern demanded an element that nobody had found, he left a gap and described what would fill it. One gap sat below aluminium. He called the missing element "eka-aluminium" and listed its expected properties. Lecoq purified his sample further and measured again. The density came out at about 5.9.

Two more of Mendeleev's gaps were filled within fifteen years: scandium in 1879 and germanium in 1886. Germanium matched his predicted atomic weight, density and the kind of compounds it would form remarkably closely. That is why the table won: it did not just organise what was known, it made risky predictions about what was not, and they came true.

Chapter 02

How to read the grid

Each square holds one element. The big number on the tile is the atomic number: how many protons sit in the nucleus of that atom. Protons are the positively charged particles at the centre of the atom, and their count is what makes an element what it is. One proton is hydrogen, six is carbon, 79 is gold. Change the count and you have a different element.

About three quarters of the elements are metals. Since 2016, when the last four names (nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson) were approved, the seventh row has been complete. The table you see today has no holes. Any new element would have to start an eighth row.

Chapter 03

The people who nearly got there first

Mendeleev did not start from nothing. In 1829 the German chemist Johann Döbereiner noticed "triads": groups of three similar elements, such as chlorine, bromine and iodine, where the middle one's atomic weight was roughly the average of the other two. In 1865 the English chemist John Newlands arranged elements by weight and saw that properties repeated every eighth element. He called it the "law of octaves", after music. Colleagues at the Chemical Society mocked him, and one asked whether he had tried listing the elements alphabetically.

The German chemist Lothar Meyer built a similar table at almost the same time. Mendeleev gets most of the credit because he was bolder about the gaps, and because he broke his own rule when the chemistry demanded it. Tellurium is heavier than iodine, but he put it first, because iodine clearly belonged with chlorine and bromine.

Nobody could explain why that swap was right until 1913. The young English physicist Henry Moseley fired electrons at different metals and measured the X-rays that came out. The X-ray frequency rose in neat steps from one element to the next. Each step was one more proton. Moseley showed that the true ordering principle is the proton count, not the weight, and with it the swaps made sense. He was killed at Gallipoli two years later, aged 27.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Electrons fill layers, and behaviour repeats when a layer starts again

The table's shape is not a design choice. It is a picture of how electrons arrange themselves around a nucleus. Once you see that, the strange widths of the rows stop being strange.

Chapter 04

Why the rows are 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 long

Electrons are the light, negatively charged particles that surround the nucleus. They cannot sit just anywhere. Quantum physics allows them only certain energy levels, usually called shells. Each shell is divided into subshells, labelled s, p, d and f. An s subshell holds 2 electrons, a p holds 6, a d holds 10 and an f holds 14.

Now walk along the table, adding one proton and one electron at each step. Row 1 fills only one small s subshell, so it has 2 elements: hydrogen and helium. Rows 2 and 3 fill an s and a p: 2 + 6 = 8 elements each. From row 4 a d subshell joins in, giving 2 + 6 + 10 = 18. From row 6 an f subshell joins too, giving 32. The four blocks of the table are simply the four kinds of subshell being filled. The tall columns at the left are the s block. The wide middle is the d block. The right-hand side is the p block. The two rows printed underneath are the f block.

The chemistry lives in the outer electrons, the valence electrons, which touch other atoms. Every element in a column has the same outer arrangement, which is why sodium and potassium behave like cousins although one is nearly twice as heavy.

Chapter 05

The trends: four arrows that explain most of chemistry

Two forces compete in every atom. The positive nucleus pulls the electrons in. The inner electrons partly hide the nucleus from the outer ones, an effect called shielding. Moving across a row, you add protons but no new layer, so the pull wins and atoms actually get smaller. Moving down a column, you add a whole new layer each time, so the outer electrons sit further out and are held more loosely.

Chemists name these trends. Atomic radius is the size of the atom. Ionisation energy is the energy needed to pull one electron off. Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself in a bond. The American chemist Linus Pauling built the usual scale for it in the 1930s. Fluorine tops it at about 4.0, and caesium sits near the bottom at about 0.8.

These numbers predict bonds. When two atoms differ a lot in electronegativity, one takes the other's electron: an ionic bond, as in table salt. When they are similar, they share: a covalent bond, as in water. Most of what a chemist "just knows" about two elements comes from where they sit on the grid.

Chapter 06

Families with personalities

Some columns are so consistent that they have names.

Alkali metals (group 1)

Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium. One outer electron, easily lost. Soft enough to cut with a knife, and they react with water more violently the further down you go. Potassium bursts into a lilac flame; caesium can shatter the container.

Halogens (group 17)

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine. One electron short of a full layer, so they grab electrons from almost anything. The name means "salt-maker", because they form salts with metals. Fluoride in toothpaste is fluorine that has already taken its electron and calmed down.

Noble gases (group 18)

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Full outer layers, so they barely react. Mendeleev had no column for them, because none were known. When Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay found argon in 1894, and the others followed within a few years, the table simply gained a new column. It absorbed a surprise without breaking.

Transition metals (groups 3 to 12)

Iron, copper, nickel, gold, platinum and the rest of the wide middle. Their d electrons let them lose different numbers of electrons in different situations. That gives them vivid colours in compounds and makes many of them excellent catalysts, substances that speed up reactions without being used up.

The f block deserves a word. The lanthanides, the first of its two rows, are nearly identical to each other chemically, because the electrons being added are tucked deep inside the atom where they barely affect bonding. This is why the "rare earth" metals used in magnets are so hard to separate, a problem Volume 12 describes (Rare earths). The actinides, the second row, include uranium and plutonium. All of them are radioactive.

Chapter 07

Where the simple picture bends

The rules above are very good, and they are not perfect. Three exceptions are worth knowing, because each one teaches something.

Hydrogen has no proper home. It has one electron, like the alkali metals, so it usually sits above lithium. But it is a gas, not a metal, and it can also gain an electron like a halogen. Some tables float it above the middle on its own. Helium has the same problem in reverse: its outer arrangement is like group 2, but its behaviour is noble gas.

Gold is yellow because of relativity. In very heavy atoms the innermost electrons move at a large fraction of the speed of light, and Einstein's relativity shifts their energy levels. As a result, gold absorbs blue light and reflects yellow. Silver, one row up, has the same outer arrangement but much weaker relativistic effects, so it looks white. Calculations suggest that the same effect is a large part of why mercury is a liquid at room temperature.

Diagonal neighbours can be more alike than column neighbours. Lithium resembles magnesium, one step down and to the right, because size and charge balance out in similar proportions. The underlying law is the electron structure. The table is a picture of it, drawn flat.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Chips, poisons, and the stars inside you

The table matters outside the laboratory because nature and industry both follow its logic. Elements in the same column can stand in for each other, for better and for worse.

Chapter 08

Designing materials by moving one square

Silicon sits in group 14 with four outer electrons. In a silicon crystal, every atom shares those four with four neighbours, and almost no electrons are free to carry current. Now replace one atom in a million with phosphorus, from group 15 next door. Phosphorus brings a fifth electron that has no partner and can wander. Replace it instead with boron, from group 13, and you create a missing electron, a "hole", that also moves. This deliberate contamination is called doping, and it is how every computer chip is made. The whole design logic is a step left or right on the table.

The same reasoning builds new materials. Gallium (group 13) and arsenic (group 15) average four outer electrons, so gallium arsenide behaves like a faster silicon and is used in radio chips and lasers. Chemists searching for a new battery, magnet or catalyst still begin by asking which neighbours on the table could replace an expensive or toxic element.

Chapter 09

The body reads the table too, and gets fooled

Living cells handle elements by their size and charge. An element that resembles a useful one can slip through the same doors. That makes position on the table a guide to toxicity.

ImpostorMistaken forWhat happens
Strontium-90Calcium, directly above itBuilt into bones and teeth, where it keeps emitting radiation. In the 1950s and 1960s a survey of children's baby teeth in St. Louis tracked it from nuclear weapons tests, and helped build support for the 1963 test ban treaty.
Caesium-137Potassium, two rows upSpreads through soft tissue like potassium. It was a major contaminant after Chernobyl and Fukushima.
ThalliumPotassium (similar size and charge)Enters cells through the pathways meant for potassium and poisons them. Tasteless and slow-acting, it has a long history as a murderer's poison.
CadmiumZinc, directly above itDisplaces zinc in enzymes and damages kidneys and bones. Cadmium pollution in Toyama, Japan, caused the painful bone disease called itai-itai ("it hurts, it hurts").
ArsenicPhosphorus, directly above itIts compounds can imitate phosphate, which cells use to store energy and build DNA, and so jam that machinery.

The same logic can help. Lithium, above sodium and potassium, has treated bipolar disorder since the Australian psychiatrist John Cade reported its effect in 1949, although exactly how it steadies mood is still not fully understood. The table hints at why an element is dangerous or useful in the body long before anyone works out the details.

Chapter 10

Made in stars, mined on Earth

The table is also a history of the universe. Hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium came out of the Big Bang. Everything heavier was made later, inside stars. Stars fuse light nuclei into heavier ones and release energy, but only up to iron. Fusing beyond iron costs energy instead of releasing it. So the elements heavier than iron were made in violent events: the explosions of massive stars and the collisions of neutron stars. In 2017 telescopes watched the afterglow of such a collision, first detected as gravitational waves, and saw the signature of freshly made heavy elements. Volume 29 tells the story of how stars die (How stars die, and why it matters to you).

On Earth, those elements are unevenly spread and some are scarce. Helium is a striking example. It is the second most common element in the universe, but on Earth it escapes into space once released. Almost all we use comes from radioactive decay deep underground, trapped in a few natural gas fields. It cools the magnets in hospital MRI scanners, and supplies have been squeezed several times in recent years. Cobalt, lithium, the rare earths and the platinum metals are concentrated in a handful of countries. The table is now a supply-chain map too.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant summary that hides some of what it summarises

The table is one of the most successful diagrams in science. Like any compression, it throws information away, and the places where it does so are worth knowing.

Chapter 12

The arguments that are still going on

Chemists still argue about a few placements. The best-known is group 3. Older tables put lanthanum and actinium under scandium and yttrium. Many newer ones put lutetium and lawrencium there instead, because their electron structures fit the column better. A working group of IUPAC, the international body that sets chemistry's naming rules, has studied the question and leaned towards lutetium and lawrencium, but plenty of textbooks still show the older version.

Others have redrawn the whole thing, as spirals, as three-dimensional shapes, or as a "left-step" table proposed by the French engineer Charles Janet in the 1920s. None has replaced the standard layout, mainly because it is good enough and everyone already knows it.

At the far bottom of the table, the rules may actually fail. The heaviest elements exist only as a few atoms that last fractions of a second. Calculations suggest relativistic effects may stop some of them behaving like their column. Experiments on copernicium and flerovium, done with a handful of atoms at a time, hint that they are unusually volatile. The results are hard to obtain and still debated. The table works superbly where we live, and becomes a hypothesis at its edge.

Chapter 14

The table as a search engine

The quieter change is in how the table is used. For most of its history, chemists used it in their heads. Now computer programs run quantum calculations for thousands of possible combinations of elements, and machine-learning models trained on known materials suggest which untried combinations might make a good battery electrode or a superconductor. In 2023 Google DeepMind announced a very large list of predicted stable crystals; other chemists soon disputed how many were useful or truly new.

These tools speed up the old method rather than replace it. They still ask what the table has always asked: which elements, in which proportions, will fill which electron slots? Mendeleev asked it with cards for 63 elements. The same question now runs across the whole grid.

The core idea

Elements are ordered by proton count. Rows break where a new electron shell begins, so every column shares the same outer electrons and behaves alike. Size, grip on electrons and metallic character change in steady trends across rows and down columns, and those trends predict how elements bond.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 118 confirmed elements; row 7 complete since 2016.
  • Rows hold 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 elements, because s, p, d and f subshells hold 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons.
  • Mendeleev published in 1869 with about 63 elements known; Moseley fixed the ordering by proton count in 1913.
The common mistake

Thinking the table is ordered by weight, or that its shape is a convention. The order is the proton count, and the shape is the electron structure, drawn flat.

Test yourself

Can you explain why strontium-90 ends up in bones, using only its position on the table? If you can, you have understood columns, outer electrons and why cells mistake one element for another.

The words, in plain words
Element
A substance made of only one kind of atom, defined by its number of protons.
Atomic number
The number of protons in an atom's nucleus. It decides which element the atom is.
Period
A row of the table. All its elements have the same number of electron shells.
Group
A column of the table. Its elements share the same outer electron arrangement.
Shell
One of the allowed energy levels that electrons occupy around a nucleus.
Subshell (s, p, d, f)
A division of a shell. They hold 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons and give the table its four blocks.
Valence electrons
The outermost electrons, the ones that take part in bonding.
Electronegativity
How strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself in a bond.
Isotope
A version of an element with a different number of neutrons. Same chemistry, different mass, sometimes radioactive.
Doping
Adding a tiny amount of a neighbouring element to a semiconductor to control how it conducts.
