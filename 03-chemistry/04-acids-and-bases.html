Acids and bases. The whole story is one tiny particle changing hands.

Lemon juice, stomach acid, vinegar, blood, bleach, soap, seawater and drain cleaner sit on a single scale. What decides where each one sits is the behaviour of one particle: a hydrogen atom stripped of its electron, which is simply a bare proton. Acids give it away. Bases take it. That single exchange decides whether a cake rises, whether a lake can hold fish, whether your tooth enamel survives a soft drink, and whether a coral reef can keep building. Your body spends a lot of energy every minute keeping it in balance.

Question 1 · What is it?

Proton givers and proton takers

An acid is a substance that hands out protons. A base is one that accepts them. Almost everything else in this file follows from that sentence.

Chapter 01

The stomach that does not digest itself

Your stomach contains hydrochloric acid, the same acid used industrially to clean rust off steel. Its acidity usually sits between about pH 1.5 and 3.5, strong enough to break down meat and kill most bacteria that arrive with food. So why does the stomach not digest its own wall? Because the wall covers itself in a thick layer of mucus and keeps that layer laced with bicarbonate, a mild base. Right against the cells, the acid is constantly being neutralised. The defence is chemistry, not toughness.

For most of the twentieth century, doctors believed stomach ulcers were caused by stress and too much acid, and treated them with diet and antacids. In the early 1980s two Australians, the pathologist Robin Warren and the physician Barry Marshall, argued that a bacterium living in the stomach, Helicobacter pylori, was the real cause in most cases. Few believed them, since bacteria were not supposed to survive that acid. In 1984 Marshall drank a culture of the bacterium and developed stomach inflammation within days. The two shared the Nobel Prize in 2005. The bacterium survives partly by making a base, ammonia, around itself. It brings its own neutraliser.

Chapter 02

Three definitions, each wider than the last

Chemists have defined acids three times, and each definition contains the one before.

DefinitionAn acid is…A base is…Why it was needed
Arrhenius (Svante Arrhenius, 1880s)Something that releases hydrogen ions (H+) in waterSomething that releases hydroxide ions (OH−) in waterThe first explanation in terms of charged particles. It only works in water.
Brønsted–Lowry (Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry, 1923)A proton donorA proton acceptorExplains bases like ammonia, which contain no hydroxide, and reactions outside water.
Lewis (Gilbert Lewis, 1923)Something that accepts a pair of electronsSomething that donates a pair of electronsCovers reactions with no protons at all, which matters for many industrial catalysts.

For everyday purposes the middle definition is the one to keep. An ion is an atom or molecule with an electric charge, because it has lost or gained electrons. A hydrogen atom that loses its one electron is just a proton, H+. In water it never floats around alone. It attaches to a water molecule to form H3O+, called hydronium. When chemists talk about "hydrogen ions" in water, they mean hydronium.

The Brønsted view brings one idea that makes many puzzles easy. When an acid gives away its proton, what is left behind can take a proton back, so it is a base. Chemists call it the acid's conjugate base. Acids and bases come in pairs, and every acid-base reaction is a proton moving from one pair to another.

Chapter 03

Strong is not the same as concentrated

This is the most common confusion in the subject. Strength is about how readily an acid lets go of its proton. A strong acid, such as hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, gives up essentially all of its protons in water. A weak acid, such as the acetic acid in vinegar or the citric acid in lemons, gives up only a small fraction; most of its molecules keep their protons. Concentration is simply how much acid is dissolved in how much water.

So you can have a dilute strong acid that is fairly mild, and a concentrated weak acid that is nasty. Strength also says nothing simple about danger. Hydrofluoric acid is chemically a weak acid, yet it is one of the most feared chemicals in any laboratory. It passes through skin without much pain at first, and the fluoride then binds the calcium in tissues and bone. Small skin exposures can be dangerous to the heart. "Weak" in chemistry is a technical word, not a safety rating.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A scale that counts in powers of ten, and the chemistry that resists change

pH is a way of writing very small numbers so that humans can use them. Buffers are how living things keep those numbers almost still.

Chapter 04

pH, invented in a brewery

In 1909 the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen was working at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen, studying proteins involved in brewing. He needed a convenient way to describe acidity. The amount of hydronium in a solution is usually a tiny number, such as 0.0000001 moles per litre. (A mole is chemistry's counting unit, a fixed and very large number of particles.) Sørensen proposed using the power of ten instead. That number is 10−7, so its pH is 7.

Two consequences follow. First, a lower pH means more acid, because the exponent is negative. Second, each step is a factor of ten. Lemon juice at pH 2 has about a hundred thousand times more hydronium than pure water at pH 7. A change from 8.2 to 8.1 sounds tiny, but it means about 26 percent more hydronium.

Where does pH 7 come from? Pure water is not completely inert. A tiny fraction of its molecules pass protons to each other, making equal amounts of hydronium and hydroxide. At 25 °C that balance point is pH 7, which is why 7 is called neutral. Acids push the balance one way and bases the other.

Chapter 05

Neutralisation, and how to see it happen

When an acid meets a base, the proton moves to the base. If the base is hydroxide, the proton and the hydroxide together make water. What remains, the metal from the base and the leftover part of the acid, forms a salt. Table salt is only one example; chemists use "salt" for any compound made this way. Hydrochloric acid plus sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride and water.

You cannot see protons, so chemists use indicators: dyes that change colour as pH changes. Litmus, the classic one, comes from lichens and turns red in acid and blue in base. Red cabbage works too. Boil it and the purple water turns pink with vinegar and green-yellow with washing soda, because its colour molecules change shape when they gain or lose protons. Hydrangea flowers do a version of this in the garden: in acidic soil they can absorb aluminium and turn blue, and in alkaline soil they stay pink.

In laboratories, the classic technique is titration: adding a base drop by drop to an acid until the indicator flips colour. Knowing how much base was needed tells you exactly how much acid was there. It is still how many food, water and medicine quality checks are done, now often with an electronic pH meter instead of a dye.

Chapter 06

Buffers: the chemistry that refuses to change

A buffer is a solution that keeps its pH nearly constant when small amounts of acid or base are added. It contains a weak acid together with its conjugate base, in similar amounts. Add acid, and the base half soaks up the extra protons. Add base, and the acid half releases protons to neutralise it. The pH barely moves until one half runs out.

Your blood is a buffer, and a very tightly controlled one. Its pH normally stays between about 7.35 and 7.45. The main system is carbonic acid and bicarbonate. Carbon dioxide from your cells dissolves in blood to form carbonic acid, and that acid partly splits into bicarbonate and a proton. Two organs adjust the balance. The lungs change how much carbon dioxide you breathe out, within seconds to minutes. The kidneys change how much bicarbonate and acid leave in urine, over hours to days.

This explains some everyday experiences. If you breathe too fast in a panic, you blow off too much carbon dioxide, your blood becomes slightly too basic, and you can feel tingling and dizziness. Breathing slowly corrects it. In the other direction, serious conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes can flood the blood with acids, a dangerous state called acidosis. Doctors read blood acidity as a basic vital sign.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. "Alkaline diets" and "alkaline water" cannot change your blood's pH in any meaningful way, because the lungs and kidneys hold it steady. If you have symptoms such as persistent heartburn, see a doctor.

Chapter 07

Acids that cook

Kitchens run on acid-base chemistry. Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate, a base. On its own it needs an acid in the recipe, such as buttermilk, yogurt or lemon, to release carbon dioxide bubbles. Baking powder is baking soda that comes with its own dry acid already mixed in. Many are "double-acting": one acid reacts when the batter gets wet, and a second only when it gets hot in the oven, which gives a second rise.

Acid also changes proteins. In ceviche, raw fish is "cooked" in lime juice: the acid makes the protein chains unfold and tangle, the same change heat produces, so the flesh turns firm and opaque. Milk curdles with acid for the same reason, which is how paneer and ricotta are made. And acidity preserves food, because most dangerous bacteria cannot grow below about pH 4.6. That figure is the line food-safety rules use for pickles and canned foods. Volume 46 covers how cooks use acid for flavour (Salt, acid, and the things that make a dish work).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Rain, oceans and soil all run on the same scale

At the scale of the planet, small pH changes mean large changes in which living things can survive. One of these stories was a success. Another is still under way.

Chapter 08

Acid rain: a problem that was actually solved

Even clean rain is slightly acidic, around pH 5.6, because carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it. In the twentieth century, coal-burning power stations and factories released large amounts of sulfur dioxide, and engines and furnaces released nitrogen oxides. In the air these turn into sulfuric and nitric acids. By the 1970s rain in parts of Scandinavia, central Europe and the north-eastern United States often fell well below pH 5.

The damage was visible. Lakes in Sweden and Norway lost their fish, because acid water releases aluminium from soil, and aluminium damages fish gills. In Germany people spoke of Waldsterben, "forest death". Stone buildings and statues dissolved faster. The pollution crossed borders, so British and German smoke fell on Scandinavia.

Then governments acted. A 1979 European agreement on cross-border air pollution set the framework. Power stations installed scrubbers that spray the exhaust with lime, a base, to capture the sulfur. In 1990 the United States added a cap-and-trade scheme for sulfur dioxide to its Clean Air Act. Sulfur emissions in Europe and North America fell steeply, and many lakes have slowly recovered. Acid rain is one of the clearest examples of an environmental problem being diagnosed, regulated and largely fixed. It is now a bigger issue in parts of Asia where coal use grew later.

Chapter 09

Ocean acidification: the other carbon dioxide problem

The ocean has absorbed roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide humans have emitted. That has slowed global warming. It has also changed the sea's chemistry. Dissolved carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, and the extra protons lower the pH. Average surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 before the industrial era to about 8.1 today. On the logarithmic scale, that is an increase in hydronium of about 30 percent.

The ocean is still basic, not acidic. The word "acidification" describes the direction of travel. The harm comes from a side effect. The extra protons combine with carbonate ions in seawater, and carbonate is exactly what corals, oysters, mussels and many plankton need to build their shells and skeletons. With less of it, building costs more energy, and in extreme cases shells start to dissolve. In the late 2000s oyster hatcheries on the US Pacific coast suffered mass die-offs of larvae when corrosive, carbon-rich deep water welled up. Some now monitor and adjust the water they pump in.

The broader effects on marine ecosystems are still being studied, and they vary a lot by species. Some algae and seagrasses may even benefit. But the chemistry itself is not in dispute. As long as carbon dioxide in the air rises, the ocean's pH will keep falling. The carbon side of the story is in Environment · Carbon cycle.

Chapter 10

Soil, fertiliser, and the most-made chemical on Earth

Farmers think about pH constantly, because soil pH controls which nutrients plants can actually absorb. In very acidic soils, phosphorus gets locked up and aluminium becomes soluble and toxic to roots. In very alkaline soils, iron and manganese become hard to absorb and leaves turn yellow. Most crops prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil. Farmers raise the pH by spreading crushed limestone and lower it with sulfur.

Industry's favourite acid is sulfuric acid, made in larger quantities than any other chemical, in the order of hundreds of millions of tonnes a year. About half goes into fertiliser: it is used to dissolve phosphate rock so that plants can use the phosphorus. It also refines metals, makes car batteries, and goes into countless other processes. For much of the twentieth century, economists used a country's sulfuric acid output as a rough gauge of its industrial strength.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Indispensable, and dangerous in specific ways

Acids and bases are among the most useful substances humans handle. Their harms are predictable, which means they are also preventable.

Chapter 12

The dangerous side, honestly

Most people fear acids more than bases. For eyes and skin, that is often backwards. Strong bases, such as drain cleaner and oven cleaner, can be more damaging than many acids. An acid burn tends to form a layer of damaged tissue that slows its own spread. A strong base breaks down fats and proteins in a way that lets it keep penetrating deeper. That is why an alkali splash in the eye is treated as a top emergency: rinse with large amounts of water, for a long time, and get medical help.

The other major risk is mixing. Volume 41 explains why bleach must never meet acid or ammonia (Cleaning, and the mixtures that are genuinely dangerous). And concentrated acids and bases release a lot of heat when diluted. Laboratory staff learn a rule early: add acid to water, never water to acid. A little water poured into concentrated sulfuric acid can boil instantly and spit acid out of the container.

Finally, there is deliberate harm. Acid attacks, in which corrosive liquid is thrown at a person's face, cause lifelong injuries and have been reported in many countries. Some governments have responded by restricting sales of the strongest corrosives. Immediate, prolonged rinsing with water is the most important first aid.

Chapter 14

pH as a switch in medicine

One future direction already has a long past. Many tablets carry an enteric coating, a polymer that stays intact in the acid of the stomach and dissolves only in the less acidic small intestine. It protects drugs that stomach acid would destroy, and protects the stomach from drugs that irritate it. The coating works as a pH-triggered switch.

Researchers are trying to push this idea further. The tissue around many tumours tends to be more acidic than healthy tissue, partly because of how cancer cells use sugar. Scientists are designing drug carriers and imaging agents that release their cargo or light up only below a certain pH. Some have reached clinical trials, but most are still experimental. The idea is simple: if you know where in the body the pH is different, you can use acidity as an address label.

The core idea

An acid gives away a proton and a base accepts one. pH measures how many protons (as hydronium) are present on a scale of powers of ten. Buffers, made of a weak acid and its partner base, hold pH steady. That is how blood stays near 7.4 and how the sea absorbs carbon dioxide.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Each pH step is a factor of ten. Neutral water is pH 7 at 25 °C.
  • Blood stays between about 7.35 and 7.45; the stomach runs at about 1.5 to 3.5.
  • Surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1 since pre-industrial times: about 30% more acidity.
The common mistake

Confusing strong with concentrated, and both with dangerous. Strength is how readily an acid gives up its proton. A "weak" acid like hydrofluoric acid can be deadly, and strong bases are often worse for eyes than acids.

Test yourself

Can you explain why breathing too fast makes your blood slightly basic? If you can, you have linked carbon dioxide, carbonic acid, buffers and pH in one chain.

The words, in plain words
Acid
A substance that gives away protons (hydrogen ions) in a reaction.
Base
A substance that accepts protons. A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali.
Ion
An atom or molecule with an electric charge, from losing or gaining electrons.
Hydronium
H3O+, a water molecule carrying an extra proton. It is what "hydrogen ions" really are in water.
pH
A number describing acidity: the negative power of ten of the hydronium concentration. Lower is more acidic.
Strong acid
An acid that gives up essentially all of its protons in water.
Weak acid
An acid that gives up only a small fraction of its protons in water.
Conjugate base
What remains of an acid after it gives away a proton. It can take one back.
Neutralisation
An acid and a base reacting to form a salt and, usually, water.
Salt
Any compound formed from the metal of a base and the leftover part of an acid.
Indicator
A dye whose colour changes with pH, such as litmus or red cabbage juice.
Buffer
A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base that resists changes in pH.
Ocean acidification
The fall in seawater pH caused by the ocean absorbing carbon dioxide.
