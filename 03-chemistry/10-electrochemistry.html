Electrochemistry. A reaction with a wire in the middle.

Every battery, every aluminium can, every litre of chlorine bleach, every glucose meter and every nerve impulse depends on one trick. Take a redox reaction, in which electrons move from one substance to another, and split it in two. Put the electron giver on one side and the electron taker on the other, so that the electrons can only travel between them through a wire. Now the reaction makes electricity. Run it backwards, pushing electrons in with a power supply, and electricity makes chemicals. That two-way door between chemistry and electricity is electrochemistry, and the energy transition is being built on it.

Question 1 · What is it?

Chemistry and electricity, converted into each other

Electrochemistry studies reactions that produce an electric current, and electric currents that force reactions. It began with an argument about a frog.

Chapter 01

The frog, the professor and the pile

In the 1780s the Italian physician Luigi Galvani, in Bologna, noticed that the legs of a dissected frog twitched when touched with two different metals. In 1791 he published his explanation: the animal's tissue contained its own "animal electricity", and the metals merely released it.

Alessandro Volta, a physicist at the University of Pavia, disagreed. He thought the electricity came from the two different metals touching through a moist substance, and the frog was only a sensitive detector. To prove it, he built a stack of alternating zinc and copper discs, each pair separated by cardboard or cloth soaked in salt water. In 1800 he reported that the stack, soon called a voltaic pile, produced a steady electric current, with no animal anywhere. It was the first battery.

Both men were partly right. Volta was right that two different metals and a salty liquid make electricity; that is every battery since. Galvani was right that living tissue is electrical; that is how your nerves work, as Chapter 11 explains. The unit of electrical push, the volt, is named after Volta. And within weeks of his report, other scientists were using his pile to split water into hydrogen and oxygen: the two directions of electrochemistry were discovered in the same year.

Chapter 02

Two directions, one set of parts

An electrochemical cell has the same basic parts whichever way it runs. There are two electrodes, solid conductors where reactions happen. Between them is an electrolyte, a substance in which charged particles called ions can move, such as salt water. An external wire connects the electrodes.

Galvanic cell: chemistry makes electricity

The reaction wants to happen on its own. Electrons leave the electron giver, flow through the wire and do useful work on the way, before reaching the electron taker. A battery being used is a galvanic cell. So is a fuel cell.

Electrolytic cell: electricity makes chemistry

The reaction would not happen on its own. A power supply pushes electrons in the "wrong" direction and forces it. Charging a phone, making aluminium, plating a tap with chrome and splitting water are all electrolysis.

Two names apply in both cases. The anode is the electrode where oxidation happens, where electrons are taken away from a substance. The cathode is where reduction happens, where electrons are delivered. The English scientist Michael Faraday introduced these words in 1834, with help from the scholar William Whewell. A rechargeable battery swaps the roles of its electrodes each time it changes from discharging to charging, which is why the labels on battery diagrams can be confusing.

Chapter 03

Voltage is a measure of electron greed

Why does one pair of metals give more voltage than another? Because each substance has a measurable tendency to gain or lose electrons. Chemists measure it against a standard reference, a hydrogen electrode, which is set at zero. The result is called the standard electrode potential. The more negative the value, the more eagerly the substance gives up electrons. The more positive, the more strongly it grabs them.

The voltage of a cell is roughly the gap between its two electrodes on this list. Zinc against copper gives about 1.1 volts, which is what the classic Daniell cell of 1836 produced. Lithium sits at the very top of the list, which is the main reason lithium batteries give so much voltage and store so much energy for their weight. The table is the reactivity series of Oxidation and reduction, turned into numbers.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Electrons through the wire, ions through the liquid

A cell needs two circuits that meet: electrons travelling outside, and ions travelling inside. Block either one and everything stops.

At the zinc electrode, zinc atoms give up electrons and dissolve into the liquid as zinc ions. The electrons travel through the wire, lighting the bulb, to the copper electrode. There, copper ions from the solution pick them up and become copper metal. The zinc electrode slowly wastes away and the copper one slowly grows.

The barrier in the middle is essential and easy to overlook. Without a path for ions, positive charge would pile up on the zinc side and negative charge on the copper side within a fraction of a second, and the current would stop. A battery needs an inside route for ions as well as an outside route for electrons. In a modern battery, that inside route is the electrolyte soaked into a thin porous plastic sheet called the separator.

Chapter 05

Batteries, from lead to lithium

Batteries that are used once and thrown away are primary cells, like the alkaline batteries in a remote control. Batteries that can be recharged are secondary cells. The first practical rechargeable battery was the lead-acid battery, invented by the French physicist Gaston Planté in 1859. Its descendants still start most petrol cars.

The lithium-ion battery changed everything after Sony began selling it in 1991. Its inventors, Stanley Whittingham, John Goodenough and Akira Yoshino, shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Its clever idea is that the electrodes are not consumed. Lithium ions shuttle back and forth between two layered materials, usually graphite on one side and a metal oxide on the other, slotting in between the layers like books on a shelf. This is why it is sometimes called a "rocking-chair" battery.

There is a hidden constraint here. Water breaks down into hydrogen and oxygen at about 1.23 volts, so a battery with a water-based electrolyte cannot easily go much above about 2 volts. A lithium-ion cell runs at about 3.6 to 3.7 volts. To reach that, it must use an organic, carbon-based liquid electrolyte instead of water. That liquid is flammable, and it is the root of most lithium battery fires. Much of the research on solid-state batteries is an attempt to replace it.

Chapter 06

Electrolysis: Faraday's bookkeeping

In the 1830s Faraday measured how much substance electrolysis produced for a given amount of electricity. His result, now called Faraday's laws of electrolysis, is simple: the amount of chemical produced is proportional to the electric charge passed. Each atom of a metal that needs two electrons takes exactly twice the charge of one that needs one. Electricity can be counted in atoms.

That rule makes electrolysis a predictable industrial tool. Three examples carry huge weight.

ProcessWhat goes inWhat comes out
Aluminium smeltingAluminium oxide dissolved in a molten salt, and large amounts of electricityAluminium metal. The process is described in Topic 06.
Chlor-alkali processSalt water (brine)Chlorine (for PVC, disinfectants), sodium hydroxide (for soap, paper, aluminium refining) and hydrogen

Because the product is tied to the charge, the cost of these products is tied to the price of electricity. Aluminium is sometimes described as "solid electricity", and smelters are built where power is cheap, such as Iceland, Norway, Canada and the Gulf states.

Chapter 07

Fuel cells: a battery that never runs out

A battery carries its chemicals inside. A fuel cell is fed them continuously. In the most common type, hydrogen is supplied to one electrode and oxygen from the air to the other. Hydrogen gives up electrons at the anode, the electrons flow through the external circuit, and at the cathode they combine with oxygen and hydrogen ions to form water. The only direct product is water, plus heat.

The Welsh scientist William Grove demonstrated the principle around 1839 to 1842, but fuel cells stayed a curiosity for more than a century. Their first big job was in space. The Apollo spacecraft used fuel cells for electricity, and the water they made was used by the crew. Today fuel cells power some buses, trucks, forklifts and backup generators. Their advantage over batteries is fast refuelling and long range. Their disadvantage is that clean hydrogen is still expensive and losing energy at each step, as Volume 11 explains.

Chapter 08

Corrosion is an accidental battery

Rust, described in Oxidation and reduction, is electrochemistry nobody asked for. One patch of iron acts as the anode and dissolves. Another acts as the cathode, where oxygen takes up the electrons. The metal itself is the wire and the water on it is the electrolyte. Salt makes the electrolyte better, so the accidental cell runs faster.

Knowing this lets engineers turn the cell against itself. Joining two different metals in a wet place, such as a steel bolt in an aluminium boat, creates a battery in which the more eager metal corrodes fast. This is called galvanic corrosion, and designers avoid such pairings. The opposite trick is cathodic protection: make the structure you care about into the cathode, either by attaching a more eager metal that sacrifices itself or by connecting a power supply that pushes electrons onto the steel. Pipelines, ship hulls and harbour piles are protected this way.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

From copper wire to blood sugar to the thoughts in your head

Beyond batteries, electrochemistry purifies the metals in our wiring, measures the chemistry of our blood, and carries every signal in our nervous system.

Chapter 09

Plating and purifying

The copper in electrical wiring must be extremely pure, because small impurities raise its resistance. It is purified by electrolysis. Slabs of impure copper are made the anode and dissolve. Copper ions travel through the solution and are deposited on thin sheets of pure copper at the cathode, reaching purities of about 99.99 percent. Impurities either stay in solution or fall to the bottom as a sludge that is valuable in its own right, because it can contain silver, gold and other precious metals.

Electroplating uses the same idea to coat one metal with a thin layer of another. In the 1840s the Elkington company in Birmingham patented and commercialised electroplating with silver, which made "silver-plated" cutlery affordable for the middle classes. Today electroplating puts chrome on taps, gold on electrical contacts, zinc on screws and copper into the tiny wiring channels of computer chips.

Chapter 10

Sensors: a drop of blood, a number in seconds

Electrochemistry is also a way of measuring. If a reaction produces electrons in proportion to how much of a substance is present, the current tells you the amount. The finger-prick glucose meter used by millions of people with diabetes works like this. The test strip carries an enzyme that reacts only with glucose. The reaction moves electrons onto the electrode, and the meter converts the tiny current into a blood sugar reading. The American scientist Leland Clark described the first such enzyme electrode in 1962. Continuous glucose monitors worn on the skin use the same principle.

Other examples are everywhere. A pH meter measures the voltage across a thin glass membrane, which depends on acidity. Many police breath-alcohol devices now use a small fuel cell that oxidises alcohol and produces a current proportional to the amount. Carbon monoxide alarms in homes often use an electrochemical sensor too. When a small device gives a fast, cheap chemical measurement, there is a good chance an electrode is doing the work.

Chapter 11

Your nerves are electrochemical cells

Galvani's "animal electricity" is real, but it is not carried by electrons in a wire. It is carried by ions crossing membranes. Your cells use pumps, powered by ATP, to keep more potassium inside and more sodium outside. That imbalance gives the inside of a resting nerve cell a voltage of about −70 millivolts compared with the outside: each cell is a tiny battery.

A nerve impulse happens when channels in the membrane briefly open and sodium ions rush in, flipping the voltage. The flip triggers neighbouring channels, so a wave of changing voltage travels along the nerve. Afterwards the pumps restore the balance. The details are in Neuroscience · Neurons.

Some animals turned this into a weapon. The electric eel of South America has thousands of modified muscle cells stacked in series, each adding a small voltage, and can deliver shocks of several hundred volts. Volta knew about electric fish and described his pile as an artificial version of their electric organ. The battery was, from the start, an imitation of something life had already invented.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Clean at the point of use, costly upstream

Electrochemical technologies are efficient, quiet and emission-free where they are used. Their costs show up in mining, electricity supply and safety.

Chapter 13

Fires, mines and the hidden cost of stored energy

A charged battery is a store of chemical energy held back only by a thin separator. If that separator is damaged, by a manufacturing flaw, a crush or overheating, the two electrodes can connect inside the cell. The current flows internally, heats the cell, and the heat can set off further reactions in the electrolyte: thermal runaway. In 2016 Samsung recalled its Galaxy Note 7 phone and then stopped making it after batteries caught fire; investigations blamed defects in the cells. Such failures are rare per battery, but there are billions of batteries.

Safety note

Do not charge swollen, damaged or very hot batteries, and avoid cheap uncertified chargers. Volume 46 covers household battery safety (Batteries, electricity and the rest of the cupboard).

The other cost is upstream. Lithium is mined from hard rock or evaporated from salty underground water in very dry regions. A large share of the world's cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where some is dug by hand in small informal mines, with documented cases of dangerous conditions and child labour. Volume 12 covers the lithium supply chain (Lithium). Battery makers have responded partly by chemistry: lithium iron phosphate cells, which use no cobalt or nickel, have taken a large share of the car and grid markets in the 2020s.

The common thread is a change in where the energy for chemistry comes from. For centuries, industry drove reactions by burning things. Electrochemistry drives them with electrons from a wire, and it can store electricity as chemistry and give it back. As electricity gets cleaner and cheaper, more of the world's chemistry is likely to move from the furnace to the electrode.

The core idea

An electrochemical cell splits a redox reaction so that electrons travel through a wire and ions through an electrolyte. In a galvanic cell (a battery or fuel cell) the reaction makes electricity. In an electrolytic cell electricity forces the reaction. Voltage reflects the gap in electron greed between the two electrodes.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Lithium's standard potential is about −3.04 V, the most negative of common electrode materials.
  • Water splits at about 1.23 V, so lithium-ion cells (about 3.7 V) need a non-water, flammable electrolyte.
  • A resting nerve cell sits at about −70 mV.
The common mistake

Thinking a battery stores electricity. It stores chemicals that want to react, arranged so the reaction can only proceed by sending electrons through your device.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a cell stops working if the barrier between its two halves is sealed? If you can, you have understood that a cell needs two circuits: electrons outside and ions inside.

The words, in plain words
Electrochemical cell
A device with two electrodes and an electrolyte in which a reaction and an electric current are linked.
Electrode
A solid conductor where electrons enter or leave the reaction.
Electrolyte
A substance in which ions can move, carrying charge inside the cell.
Anode
The electrode where oxidation (electron loss) happens.
Cathode
The electrode where reduction (electron gain) happens.
Galvanic cell
A cell in which a spontaneous reaction produces electricity, such as a battery.
Electrolysis
Using electricity to drive a reaction that would not happen by itself.
Standard electrode potential
A measure, in volts, of how strongly a substance gains or loses electrons compared with hydrogen.
Separator
The thin porous layer in a battery that keeps the electrodes apart but lets ions through.
Fuel cell
A cell that makes electricity from a continuously supplied fuel, usually hydrogen, and oxygen.
Electroplating
Using electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of metal.
Cathodic protection
Preventing corrosion by making the metal structure the cathode of a cell.
