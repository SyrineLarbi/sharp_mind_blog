Sharp Mind · Chemistry · Topic 05 of 20

Chemical reactions. Nothing is created or destroyed. Everything is rearranged.

A log burns to ash, bread dough rises, iron rusts, and the food you ate this morning is right now being turned into the energy that lets you read this sentence. All of these are chemical reactions: atoms letting go of one set of partners and taking up another. The atoms themselves never change. What changes is how they are joined, and with that, everything about the substance: its colour, its smell, whether it is a gas or a solid, whether it feeds you or poisons you. Learning how reactions work is learning why the material world behaves as it does.

Question 1 · What is it?

Atoms changing partners

A chemical reaction breaks some chemical bonds and makes new ones. The atoms are conserved. The substances are not.

Chapter 01

The man who weighed everything

In the eighteenth century, most chemists explained burning with a substance called phlogiston. Things that burned well were supposed to be full of it, and burning released it into the air. Ash was what was left once the phlogiston had gone. The theory sounded reasonable. It had one awkward problem: when some metals were heated in air, the "ash" they formed weighed more than the metal had.

In Paris, the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier took that problem seriously. Working with his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, who kept his records and drew his apparatus, he heated substances in sealed vessels and weighed everything before and after, including the air. The total never changed. Burning was not something leaving. It was something from the air joining in. He named that something oxygen.

The principle Lavoisier set out, in his 1789 textbook among other places, is now called the conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, the total mass of what you end with equals the total mass of what you started with. A burning log seems to vanish, but if you could collect the smoke, the gases and the ash, they would weigh more than the log, by exactly the mass of the oxygen that joined it. Lavoisier was executed by guillotine in 1794, during the French Revolution, because of his earlier job as a tax collector.

Chapter 02

What changes, and what does not

Atoms are joined to each other by chemical bonds: shared or transferred electrons that hold atoms together. In a reaction, some bonds break and new ones form. The starting substances are called reactants, and the new ones are products. The nuclei of the atoms are untouched. A carbon atom that goes into a fire comes out as a carbon atom, now joined to oxygen in carbon dioxide. Changing one element into another is a nuclear reaction, a different subject with energies millions of times larger.

Chemists separate chemical change from physical change. Melting ice, boiling water and dissolving sugar change the form of a substance but not its molecules. The water in steam is still water. Burning sugar is different: it becomes carbon dioxide and water, and you cannot get the sugar back by cooling the smoke. The signs that a chemical change has happened are usually one of five: a gas appears, the colour changes, heat or light is given off or taken in, a solid appears in a liquid, or the smell changes. None is proof on its own, but together they are a good guide.

Chapter 03

Reading a chemical equation

Chemists write reactions as equations. Here is natural gas, which is mostly methane, burning in a cooker:

Methane burning, balanced
   CH4      +     2 O2      -->     CO2       +     2 H2O
 methane         oxygen          carbon dioxide       water

 atoms:  1 C, 4 H, 4 O    on the left
         1 C, 4 H, 4 O    on the right      nothing lost, nothing gained

 masses: 16 g  +  64 g    -->   44 g   +   36 g      80 g in, 80 g out

The numbers in front are what make the equation balanced: every atom on the left must appear on the right. That is Lavoisier's law written as bookkeeping. The equation also works as a recipe. Chemists count particles in moles; one mole is about 602 thousand billion billion particles (6.02 × 1023). The point of this huge number is that one mole of an element weighs, in grams, the same as its atomic mass. So the equation says that 16 grams of methane needs 64 grams of oxygen and makes 44 grams of carbon dioxide.

This calculation, called stoichiometry, sounds dry and is enormously practical. It is why we can say exactly how much carbon dioxide a tonne of fuel will produce, how much fertiliser a factory can make from its natural gas, or how much of a drug a batch will yield. Every climate figure that converts fuel burned into emissions rests on it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Energy decides whether, a barrier decides when

Two separate questions govern every reaction. Does it release energy overall? And how hard is it to get started? Confusing the two is the root of most misunderstandings.

Chapter 04

There are millions of known reactions, but most fall into a few families.

Chapter 05

Energy: breaking costs, making pays

Here is a fact that surprises many people: breaking a chemical bond always takes energy, and forming one always releases it. There is no bond that gives you energy by breaking. So where does the heat of a fire come from? From the difference. Burning methane breaks the bonds in methane and oxygen, which costs energy, and forms the bonds in carbon dioxide and water, which releases more. The surplus comes out as heat and light.

A reaction that releases energy overall is exothermic. One that absorbs energy overall is endothermic. You can hold both in your hand. A disposable hand warmer contains iron powder, salt and water; when you open it, the iron rusts quickly and gives off heat. An instant cold pack contains a salt such as ammonium nitrate and a pouch of water; squeeze it, the salt dissolves, and that process absorbs heat from its surroundings, so the pack turns cold.

Energy alone does not decide whether a reaction goes. A second quantity, entropy, roughly the number of ways the energy and particles can be arranged, also matters. That is why some endothermic processes, like the cold pack, still happen on their own. The full accounting is the subject of Thermochemistry.

Chapter 06

Why a reaction needs a push

Paper and the oxygen around it should, by the energy accounting, react and release heat. Yet a book can sit on a shelf for centuries without catching fire. The reason is the activation energy: the energy needed to start breaking bonds before the new ones can form. Think of a boulder in a hollow at the top of a hill. Rolling it down would release a lot of energy, but first you have to push it over the rim of the hollow.

The energy hill of an exothermic reaction
 energy
   |              .-''-.        <- the barrier: activation energy
   |             /      \          (a match, a spark, heat)
   |   ---------/        \
   |  reactants           \
   |  (paper + oxygen)     \
   |                        \_____________
   |                         products (ash, CO2, water)
   |                  energy released = the drop from left to right
   +-------------------------------------------------- progress of reaction

A match supplies the push for the first few molecules. The heat their reaction releases pushes their neighbours over the barrier, and the fire sustains itself. This is why Volume 46 describes fire as needing fuel, oxygen and heat (What fire needs, and how to take it away). Remove the heat and the reaction can no longer climb its own barrier.

The barrier explains why so much of the world is stable but not at rest. Diamonds are slowly turning into graphite in principle, and your own body would release energy by reacting with the oxygen in the air. Both are held back only by high barriers. Life exists in that gap.

Chapter 07

What sets the speed

For two molecules to react, they must collide, hard enough and facing the right way. This simple picture, called collision theory, explains the four things that change reaction speed.

Temperature

Hotter molecules move faster and more of them clear the barrier. A rough rule for many reactions near room temperature is that ten degrees warmer roughly doubles the speed. It is why a fridge slows food spoiling and why batteries are sluggish in frost.

Concentration

More molecules in the same space means more collisions. A fire burns far more fiercely in pure oxygen than in air, which is only about a fifth oxygen. This is why oxygen cylinders and smoking are a dangerous mix.

Surface area

Only the surface of a solid can react. Break it into powder and the surface grows enormously. A sack of flour will not burn easily, but flour dust in air can explode. Grain silos, sugar refineries and coal mines have all suffered dust explosions.

Catalysts

A catalyst offers a different route with a lower barrier and is not used up. Enzymes in your body and the converter in a car exhaust are both catalysts. Topic 13 covers them in depth.

Chapter 08

Reactions that stop halfway

Many reactions can also run backwards. Products can collide and turn back into reactants. When the forward and backward speeds become equal, the mixture stops changing, even though molecules are still reacting in both directions. This is called chemical equilibrium. It looks like nothing is happening. In fact, two opposite processes are cancelling out.

In 1884 the French chemist Henri Le Chatelier stated a useful rule: if you disturb a system at equilibrium, it shifts in the direction that partly undoes the disturbance. Remove a product, and more forms. Squeeze a gas reaction, and it shifts towards the side with fewer gas molecules. Industrial chemistry is largely the art of pushing equilibria in the direction you want. The ammonia process that makes fertiliser runs at high pressure for exactly this reason. The full story is in Chemical equilibrium.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A handful of reactions run civilisation, and a few have destroyed parts of it

Almost every manufactured thing passed through a controlled reaction. Control is the key word. When it is lost, the same chemistry becomes a disaster.

That is why some industries cannot become carbon-free simply by switching to clean electricity. The chemistry of the reaction, not just the fuel, sets the emissions.

Chapter 10

When reactions run away

Remember that heat speeds reactions up. Now imagine a reaction that produces heat. It warms itself, so it speeds up, so it produces more heat, so it speeds up further. If the heat cannot escape fast enough, the result is thermal runaway. It is the basic mechanism behind most chemical explosions, many industrial fires and the rare lithium battery that bursts into flames.

On 4 August 2020, about 2,750 tonnes of ammonium nitrate, a fertiliser chemical that had been stored in a warehouse in the port of Beirut for six years, caught fire and exploded. More than 200 people died, thousands were injured, and much of the city was damaged. Ammonium nitrate is stable at normal temperatures. Heated strongly, especially in large quantities and with contaminants, it can decompose explosively.

The worst chemical accident in history was a different kind of loss of control. On the night of 2 to 3 December 1984, water entered a storage tank of methyl isocyanate at a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India. The reaction heated the tank and a large cloud of toxic gas escaped over the sleeping city. Thousands died within days, and estimates of the total deaths over the following years are far higher. Investigations found that several safety systems were not working at the time. Since then, process safety has become a discipline of its own, built around keeping hazardous inventories small and designing plants so that a failure cools down rather than heats up.

Chapter 11

The kitchen is a reaction lab

The safest place to watch reactions is the kitchen. Several different kinds happen there, and cooks control them by temperature and time.

Browning of meat, bread and coffee is the Maillard reaction between proteins and sugars, which Volumes 32 and 46 explain (Heat, and the reaction that makes food taste good). Caramelisation is a separate reaction: sugar alone breaking down under heat into hundreds of new flavour compounds. Fermentation is yeast breaking down sugars into carbon dioxide and alcohol, which raises bread and makes beer. The browning of a cut apple is an enzyme in the fruit using oxygen from the air, which is why lemon juice, an acid that slows the enzyme, keeps fruit pale.

A good cook, without using the word, is managing activation energies and reaction rates. High heat for a short time gives a brown crust with a raw middle. Low heat for a long time gives tenderness without a crust. It is the same chemistry, run at different speeds.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every useful reaction has by-products

Conservation of mass has a hard consequence. Every atom that goes in comes out somewhere. The question is always where.

The waste figure for medicines is not a sign of carelessness. Building a complex molecule means many separate reactions, each with solvents, purifications and leftover reagents. Reducing that waste is the main goal of Green chemistry.

Chapter 13

One chemist, both sides of the ledger

No single life shows the double edge of reactions more sharply than that of the German chemist Fritz Haber. Around 1909 he showed how to combine nitrogen from the air with hydrogen to make ammonia, the reaction Carl Bosch then scaled up for industry. Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it, awarded in 1919. Few discoveries have kept more people alive.

During the First World War, Haber also led Germany's chemical weapons programme and personally supervised the first large chlorine gas attack, at Ypres in 1915. Shortly afterwards his wife, the chemist Clara Immerwahr, took her own life; historians still debate how far his war work was the reason. In the 1930s, because he was of Jewish origin, Haber was forced out of Germany by the Nazi regime, and he died in exile in 1934.

The lesson is not that chemistry is good or bad. A reaction has no intentions. People choose what to make with it, and the same knowledge of pressures, temperatures and catalysts serves both choices.

A quieter trend is flow chemistry: running reactions continuously through narrow tubes instead of in large tanks. A small volume heats and cools quickly, so dangerous reactions are easier to control, and only a little hazardous material exists at any moment. It applies directly the lesson of Beirut and Bhopal. The safest reaction is one where there is never much of it happening at once.

The core idea

A reaction breaks bonds and forms new ones; atoms and mass are conserved. Whether it releases energy depends on the difference between bonds broken and bonds made. Whether it starts depends on the activation energy. Its speed depends on temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts.

The numbers worth knowing
  • One mole is about 6.02 × 1023 particles.
  • For many reactions, +10 °C roughly doubles the rate (a rule of thumb).
  • 16 g of methane burns with 64 g of oxygen to give 44 g of CO2.
The common mistake

Thinking that breaking bonds releases energy. Breaking always costs energy; making always pays. A fire's heat is the surplus from the new bonds being stronger.

Test yourself

Can you explain why flour dust can explode while a bag of flour barely burns? If you can, you have understood surface area, collision theory and thermal runaway together.

The words, in plain words
Chemical reaction
A process in which bonds between atoms break and new bonds form, making new substances.
Reactants and products
The substances a reaction starts with, and the ones it makes.
Chemical bond
The link between atoms, made by shared or transferred electrons.
Conservation of mass
The rule that a reaction ends with exactly the mass it started with.
Balanced equation
A written reaction with the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.
Mole
Chemistry's counting unit: about 6.02 × 1023 particles.
Stoichiometry
Calculating amounts of reactants and products from a balanced equation.
Exothermic / endothermic
Releasing heat overall / absorbing heat overall.
Activation energy
The energy barrier a reaction must get over before it can proceed.
Collision theory
The idea that molecules react only when they collide with enough energy and the right orientation.
Catalyst
A substance that lowers the activation energy and speeds a reaction without being used up.
Equilibrium
The state in which forward and backward reactions run at the same speed, so nothing seems to change.
Thermal runaway
A reaction that heats itself faster than it can cool, accelerating out of control.
Say this · five sentences you can use
  1. "In a chemical reaction nothing disappears. Atoms just change partners, and the mass at the end is the same as at the start."
  2. "Breaking a bond always costs energy. A fire is hot because the new bonds are stronger than the old ones."
  3. "Paper doesn't burst into flame on its own because of the activation energy. The match is just the push over the hill."
  4. "Flour dust can explode because powder has a huge surface area. Same chemistry, much faster."
  5. "Cement emits carbon dioxide from the limestone itself, not just from the fuel. That's why it's so hard to decarbonise."
