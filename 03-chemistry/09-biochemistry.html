Biochemistry. Life is chemistry that keeps itself going.

Every second, each of your cells runs thousands of chemical reactions. It breaks food into pieces, captures the energy, builds new proteins, copies DNA and throws out waste, all at body temperature, in water, without a flame or a pressure vessel in sight. Biochemistry is the study of how that works. Its answer is surprisingly concrete: a small set of molecule types, a large number of protein machines called enzymes, and a single energy currency that every living thing uses. There is no vital spark hiding in the gaps. There is only very clever chemistry.

Question 1 · What is it?

The chemistry of living things, with no special force required

Biochemistry is organic chemistry done by cells. It began when someone showed that a living process could keep going after the life had been removed.

Chapter 01

Fermentation without yeast

For most of the nineteenth century, fermentation was a puzzle. Yeast turned sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, but how? The great French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that living yeast cells were needed, and he believed that fermentation was inseparable from the life of the cell.

In 1897 the German chemist Eduard Buchner ground yeast with sand, squeezed out the juice, and filtered it so that no living cells remained. He added sugar to the juice, mainly to preserve it. The mixture began to bubble. The cell-free juice was fermenting the sugar on its own. Whatever did the work was a set of chemicals that could act outside the cell. Such substances had recently been given a name, enzymes, from the Greek for "in leaven", meaning in yeast.

Buchner received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1907. His experiment is often taken as the birth of biochemistry. It showed that the processes of life are chemical reactions that can be taken apart, studied and understood one at a time. The rest of the field has been doing exactly that ever since.

Chapter 02

Four kinds of molecule, and water

About half to two thirds of your body weight is water, depending on age and build. Almost all of the rest is made from four families of carbon-based molecules.

CarbohydratesSimple sugars such as glucoseQuick energy, energy storage, structure in plantsStarch, glycogen in your liver, cellulose in wood and cotton
Lipids (fats and relatives)Fatty acids and glycerol, and ring-shaped steroidsLong-term energy storage, cell membranes, some hormonesOlive oil, body fat, cholesterol, oestrogen

The list is short, and that is the point. A bacterium, an oak tree and a human are built from the same four families, using the same 20 amino acids and the same genetic letters. The differences between living things are differences in arrangement, not in raw materials.

Chapter 03

Small pieces, long chains

Three of the four families are polymers: long chains made by linking small units. Starch is a chain of glucose units. A protein is a chain of amino acids, often hundreds long. DNA is a chain of nucleotides, millions long. Cells build these chains with a reaction that removes a molecule of water at each link. Digestion does the reverse: enzymes add water back to break the links, a reaction called hydrolysis.

This explains a lot about food. The protein in a steak is not used as protein. Your gut breaks it down into amino acids, and your cells reassemble those into your own proteins, following your own DNA. Bread starch becomes glucose. You do not become what you eat; you take what you eat apart and rebuild yourself from the pieces.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Folded machines, a single currency, and reactions in chains

Three ideas explain most of what goes on inside a cell. Proteins fold into shapes that do jobs. Enzymes make reactions fast enough for life. And energy is moved around in one standard form.

Chapter 04

A chain that folds itself

A freshly made protein is a floppy chain of amino acids. Within moments it folds into a precise three-dimensional shape. Some amino acids are water-loving and some are oily. The oily ones bury themselves inside the fold, away from water, much as the oily tails of soap molecules hide inside a grease droplet. Other weak attractions between parts of the chain lock the shape in place.

In the 1950s and 1960s the American biochemist Christian Anfinsen unfolded an enzyme with chemicals and then removed the chemicals. The enzyme refolded itself and worked again. The sequence of amino acids contained all the information needed for the shape. Anfinsen shared the Nobel Prize in 1972. The catch was that nobody could predict the shape from the sequence. That problem stayed largely unsolved for about fifty years (see Chapter 11).

Shape is fragile. Heat or strong acid can unfold a protein, which is called denaturation. That is what happens when you boil an egg: the clear, runny proteins unfold and tangle into a white solid, and they cannot be untangled. Misfolding inside the body is also linked to disease. In Alzheimer's disease, certain proteins misfold and clump together in the brain, and in prion diseases such as "mad cow disease", a misfolded protein pushes normal copies into the same wrong shape.

Chapter 05

Enzymes: the reason life is fast enough

Most of the reactions in your body would be far too slow to keep you alive if left to themselves at 37 °C. Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts: they speed up a reaction without being used up. Many make their reaction millions of times faster, and some speed it up far more than that.

An enzyme has a pocket on its surface called the active site. Only molecules of the right shape and chemistry fit into it. In 1894 the German chemist Emil Fischer compared this to a lock and key. In 1958 the American biochemist Daniel Koshland refined the picture: the enzyme changes shape slightly as the molecule enters, gripping it like a glove closing around a hand. This "induced fit" strains the molecule's bonds and holds reacting partners in exactly the right position, which lowers the energy barrier of the reaction.

Because an enzyme's shape matters so much, each one works best in a narrow range of conditions. The stomach enzyme pepsin works best in strong acid. The enzymes that digest food in the intestine prefer nearly neutral conditions. Most human enzymes work best near body temperature. That is one reason a very high fever is dangerous. Catalysts covers the general chemistry.

Chapter 06

ATP, the currency of every cell

A cell cannot use the energy in a sugar molecule directly, any more than you can pay for coffee with a gold bar. It converts that energy into small, standard units first. The unit is a molecule called ATP, short for adenosine triphosphate. It carries three phosphate groups in a row. Breaking off the last one releases a convenient, standard amount of energy that the cell can spend on almost anything: contracting a muscle, pumping a chemical across a membrane, or building a protein.

ATP is not stored in large amounts. It is spent and rebuilt continuously. Estimates suggest that a resting adult recycles roughly their own body weight in ATP every day, while holding only a few tens of grams at any moment. Every known living thing, from bacteria to whales, uses ATP. It is one of the strongest pieces of chemical evidence that all life shares a common ancestor.

Chapter 07

Metabolism: burning food in small steps

Metabolism is the whole network of chemical reactions in a living thing. It has two halves. Catabolism breaks molecules down and releases energy. Anabolism uses energy to build molecules up. The two run all the time, connected by ATP.

How a glucose molecule becomes energy, roughly
  glucose (from food)
     |
     |  GLYCOLYSIS  in the cell fluid, no oxygen needed
     v  -> a little ATP
  two smaller molecules (pyruvate)
     |                                    if oxygen is short (a sprint):
     |                                    pyruvate -> lactate, a little ATP keeps coming
     v
  KREBS CYCLE  inside mitochondria
     |  carbon leaves as CO2 (what you breathe out)
     |  electrons loaded onto carrier molecules
     v
  ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN  on the mitochondrial membrane
     |  electrons passed to oxygen -> water
     v  -> most of the ATP

The middle stage is named after Hans Krebs, a German-born biochemist working in England, who worked out the cycle in 1937 and later shared a Nobel Prize. The final stage is the electron hand-off described in Oxidation and reduction. Fats enter the same pathway at the Krebs cycle, which is why fat and sugar can both fuel the same machinery.

The sprint branch explains a common myth. When you run flat out, oxygen cannot reach the muscles fast enough, so cells make lactate to keep glycolysis going. Many people blame this "lactic acid" for the soreness they feel a day or two later. That is wrong: lactate is cleared within about an hour and is even reused as fuel. Next-day soreness comes mainly from tiny damage to muscle fibres and the repair that follows.

Chapter 08

Membranes that build themselves

Every cell is wrapped in a membrane only two molecules thick. Its main ingredients are phospholipids: molecules with a water-loving head and two oily tails, much like soap. Put them in water and they arrange themselves without any help into a double layer, heads facing the water on both sides, tails hidden in the middle. The cell does not need to construct its wall. The chemistry assembles it.

The membrane is not just a bag. It is studded with proteins that act as gates, pumps and sensors, deciding what enters and leaves. Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids and keeps the membrane at the right stiffness. Cholesterol is not a poison; it is a building material, and the body makes most of its own. The trouble Volume 45 describes is about how it is carried in the blood (Cholesterol — the part everyone gets wrong).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicine, nutrition, and a fifty-year problem solved

Almost every blood test, most drugs and every vitamin label rest on biochemistry. In the 2020s it also produced one of the clearest scientific breakthroughs of the century so far.

Chapter 09

Medicine reads and rewrites your chemistry

When a doctor sends your blood to a laboratory, most of what comes back is biochemistry. Glucose and a related marker, HbA1c, show how well your body controls sugar. Certain enzymes leaking into the blood reveal damaged tissue: a protein called troponin shows heart muscle damage, and liver enzymes show liver stress. A blood test is a snapshot of thousands of reactions, read through a few markers.

Treatment often means supplying or blocking one molecule. In 1921 and 1922 in Toronto, Frederick Banting, Charles Best and their colleagues extracted insulin, the hormone that lets cells take up glucose. In January 1922 a teenage boy, Leonard Thompson, became the first person with type 1 diabetes to be treated with it. A disease that had been a death sentence became manageable. Statins, among the most prescribed drugs in the world, work by blocking one enzyme in the liver's cholesterol-making pathway. The first was found in the 1970s by the Japanese biochemist Akira Endo, who screened thousands of fungi for a substance that would do it.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Ask a doctor to explain your own test results and before starting or stopping any medicine.

Chapter 10

Vitamins are helpers for enzymes

In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind took twelve sailors with scurvy aboard HMS Salisbury and gave pairs of them different treatments. The two given oranges and lemons recovered fastest. It is often called one of the first controlled clinical trials. Nobody knew why it worked until the twentieth century, when vitamin C was identified.

The biochemical answer is that many enzymes cannot work alone. They need a small helper molecule or a metal ion sitting in the active site. Most vitamins are, or become, these helpers. Vitamin C helps the enzymes that build collagen, the protein that holds skin, gums and blood vessels together. Without it, collagen comes out weak, and gums bleed and old wounds reopen, the classic signs of scurvy. Many B vitamins become helpers in the energy pathways of Chapter 07. Because the helpers are reused, you need only small amounts, which is why most well-fed people get enough from food. Volume 33 covers supplements honestly (Supplements, honestly).

Chapter 11

The folding problem, and the machine that cracked it

Knowing a protein's shape tells you how it works and how a drug might block it. For decades, finding that shape meant months or years of laboratory work, mainly by crystallising the protein and scattering X-rays off it. Predicting the shape from the sequence alone, the problem Anfinsen had left open, was one of biology's great unsolved challenges.

Since 1994 a contest called CASP has tested prediction methods against newly solved structures that had not yet been published. In 2020 a system called AlphaFold, from the company DeepMind, predicted many structures with accuracy close to laboratory methods. By 2022 its makers had released predicted shapes for over 200 million proteins, covering almost every protein known to science, free for anyone to use. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold, and to the American biochemist David Baker for designing entirely new proteins.

Predictions are not perfect. They are less reliable for proteins that move a lot, for complexes of many molecules, and for how drugs bind. But what once took a doctoral thesis can now take minutes, and laboratories everywhere use these models as a starting point.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The power of taking life apart, and its limits

Reducing life to molecules has worked astonishingly well. It also has blind spots, and its language is easy to misuse for marketing.

Chapter 13

Biochemistry as a sales pitch

Biochemical words sell products. "Boosts your metabolism", "detoxifies", "rich in enzymes" and "balances your hormones" are common claims. Most collapse once you apply the chemistry in this file. Enzyme supplements swallowed as pills are proteins, and like any protein in food they are mostly broken down by digestion. The liver and kidneys already remove waste, and no juice cleanse has been shown to improve on them. Metabolic rate is set mainly by body size, muscle mass, age and activity, and foods marketed as "boosting" it change it very little.

There is a fair point on the other side. Some biochemical supplements do have evidence for specific groups: folic acid before and during early pregnancy, vitamin B12 for people on strict vegan diets, iron for people with diagnosed deficiency. The useful question is always: which molecule, for which person, with what evidence?

The deeper shift is from reading biochemistry to writing it. For a century the field mainly worked out what nature had already built. Now it is starting to design molecules nature never tried. That opens real possibilities, from better medicines to cleaner industry. It also raises questions about safety and misuse that scientists and governments are only beginning to address. The core idea

Life is built from four families of molecules plus water. Proteins fold into shapes set by their amino acid sequence, and many act as enzymes that make reactions fast. Metabolism breaks food down in small steps, and the energy is passed around as ATP.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 20 amino acids and 4 DNA letters, shared by all known life.
  • A resting adult recycles roughly their own body weight in ATP each day.
  • AlphaFold's database holds predicted shapes for over 200 million proteins (2022).
The common mistake

Thinking you absorb what you eat as it is. You take food apart and rebuild yourself from the pieces, which is why swallowed enzymes and proteins do not work as pills the way the labels suggest.

Test yourself

Can you explain why boiling an egg cannot be undone, but Anfinsen's enzyme refolded itself? If you can, you have understood protein folding, denaturation and what sequence controls.

The words, in plain words
Biochemistry
The study of the chemical processes in living things.
Enzyme
A protein that speeds up a specific chemical reaction without being used up.
Active site
The pocket on an enzyme where the reacting molecule fits.
Amino acid
One of the 20 small building blocks that are linked to make proteins.
Polymer
A long chain of repeated small units, such as starch, protein or DNA.
Hydrolysis
Breaking a bond by adding water, the main reaction of digestion.
Protein folding
The process by which a chain of amino acids takes up its working three-dimensional shape.
Denaturation
Loss of a protein's shape, for example through heat or acid.
ATP
Adenosine triphosphate, the molecule cells use as their standard unit of energy.
Metabolism
All the chemical reactions in an organism, both breaking down and building up.
Krebs cycle
A loop of reactions in mitochondria that strips carbon and electrons from food molecules.
Phospholipid
A soap-like molecule that forms cell membranes by arranging itself into a double layer.
Vitamin
A small molecule the body needs in tiny amounts, often as a helper for enzymes.
