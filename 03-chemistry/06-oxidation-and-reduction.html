Sharp Mind · Chemistry · Topic 06 of 20

Oxidation and reduction. Whenever something gives up electrons, something else takes them.

Rust on a bicycle, the flame of a candle, a green copper roof, bleach whitening a shirt, steel coming out of a blast furnace, and the breath you just took: all of these are the same kind of chemistry. In each one, electrons move from one substance to another. The one that loses electrons is oxidised. The one that gains them is reduced. Chemists call the pair "redox", and it is probably the single most important family of reactions on Earth. Once you can see it, you see it everywhere, including inside your own cells.

Question 1 · What is it?

A transfer of electrons, always in pairs

The words began with oxygen. The idea turned out to be bigger: any reaction in which electrons move from one substance to another.

Chapter 01

Why the Statue of Liberty is green

When the Statue of Liberty was unveiled in New York harbour in 1886, it was the colour of a new penny. Its skin is a thin layer of copper, hammered over an iron frame. Over the following decades, the copper slowly reacted with oxygen, water, carbon dioxide and sulfur compounds in the city air. It went dark brown, then black, and by the early twentieth century it had become the pale blue-green we know today.

That green coat is called a patina. It is a mix of copper compounds formed when copper atoms lose electrons to oxygen and other substances in the air. It is not damage in the usual sense. The patina sticks firmly to the metal underneath and shields it from further attack. There was talk early in the statue's life of painting it, and the idea was dropped. Leaving it alone was the better engineering.

The story contains the whole subject. Copper atoms gave up electrons; that is oxidation. Oxygen atoms took them; that is reduction. One cannot happen without the other, because electrons do not simply disappear. They have to go somewhere.

Chapter 02

From oxygen to electrons

The words are older than the modern idea. "Oxidation" first meant combining with oxygen, as iron does when it rusts. "Reduction" meant the reverse: removing oxygen from an ore to get the metal. The word came from the fact that the metal weighs less than the ore it came from, so the ore was "reduced".

In the twentieth century, once chemists understood electrons, they saw that what mattered was not oxygen itself but what oxygen does. Oxygen is greedy for electrons. When iron combines with oxygen, each iron atom hands electrons over. The same thing happens when sodium reacts with chlorine, with no oxygen anywhere. So the definitions were widened.

Oxidation

Losing electrons. The substance that loses them is oxidised. Because it gives electrons to something else, it is also called the reducing agent. Metals, hydrogen and carbon-rich fuels are typical reducing agents.

Reduction

Gaining electrons. The substance that gains them is reduced. Because it takes electrons from something else, it is the oxidising agent. Oxygen, chlorine, bleach and hydrogen peroxide are typical oxidising agents.

English-speaking students remember it with a short phrase: OIL RIG, "oxidation is loss, reduction is gain". The naming of agents is the confusing part, so it is worth saying slowly. The oxidising agent is the one that gets reduced. It oxidises its partner by taking that partner's electrons.

Chapter 03

Keeping the books: oxidation numbers

In many reactions electrons are not fully handed over. They are shared, but unequally. To track who has gained and who has lost, chemists use a bookkeeping device called the oxidation number (or oxidation state). It is the charge an atom would have if every shared electron went entirely to the greedier atom.

You do not need the rules to see the idea. In methane, carbon is surrounded by four hydrogens, which are less greedy, so carbon "owns" their electrons and is given an oxidation number of −4. In carbon dioxide, carbon is bonded to two very greedy oxygens and is given +4. So when methane burns, carbon's number rises by eight. A rising oxidation number means oxidation, a falling one means reduction. Fuels are fuels because their carbon sits at the reduced end and can release a lot of energy as it is pushed to the oxidised end.

This also explains why food and fuel are chemically similar. Fats, sugars, wood, coal and petrol all contain carbon bonded mainly to hydrogen and other carbon. Fats are especially reduced, which is why they store more than twice as much energy per gram as sugars.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Electrons flow from the generous to the greedy

Some elements hold their electrons loosely and some grab for more. Redox is what happens when the two meet, and the gap between them decides how much energy is released.

Chapter 04

The league table of metals

Chemists rank metals by how easily they give up electrons. The list is called the reactivity series. A metal higher in the list will push a lower one out of its compounds, because it gives up electrons more readily.

This one list explains a great deal of history. The order in which humans learned to use metals roughly follows the reactivity series from the bottom up. Gold and copper could be found or easily freed. Iron needed hotter furnaces and better chemistry, so the Iron Age came after the Bronze Age. Aluminium, near the top, could not be produced cheaply until the late nineteenth century.

Chapter 05

Rust, step by step

Rusting needs both oxygen and water. A nail kept in dry air, or in boiled water sealed from the air, barely rusts at all. With both present, a small electrical circuit forms on the metal surface. In one spot, iron atoms lose electrons and dissolve into the water as iron ions. The electrons travel through the metal to another spot, where oxygen and water take them up. The iron ions and the products of the oxygen reaction then meet and form hydrated iron oxide: rust.

Salt speeds this up because salty water conducts electricity much better, which makes the little circuit more efficient. That is why cars rust faster in countries that salt winter roads, and why ships and coastal structures need constant protection.

Rust has one property that makes it worse than copper's patina: it is crumbly and flakes off, exposing fresh iron underneath, so the attack continues until the metal is gone. Aluminium is more reactive than iron, yet an aluminium window frame lasts for decades. The reason is that its oxide forms a thin, hard, transparent film that seals the surface. This protective effect is called passivation. Stainless steel uses the same trick: it contains at least about 10.5 percent chromium, which forms a thin chromium oxide skin that repairs itself if scratched.

Chapter 06

Fire and breath are electron flows

In a flame, the electrons of a fuel's carbon and hydrogen move to oxygen all at once, and the energy comes out as heat and light. In your cells, the same electrons make the same journey, but in a long chain of small steps.

Your cells first strip electrons from the sugars and fats you eat and load them onto carrier molecules. Inside the mitochondria, the cell's power stations, the carriers pass those electrons down a series of proteins called the electron transport chain. At each step a little energy is released and used to pump particles across a membrane, a bit like water pumped uphill behind a dam. At the end of the chain the electrons are finally handed to oxygen, which combines with hydrogen to form water. That is what the oxygen you breathe is for: it is the last electron catcher at the end of the line.

This explains why cyanide kills so fast. It blocks the final protein in the chain, the one that passes electrons to oxygen. The whole chain backs up, and cells cannot use oxygen even though the blood is full of it. Photosynthesis is the same process run in reverse, using light energy to pull electrons off water, which releases oxygen as a by-product. The detail is in Biochemistry.

Chapter 07

Getting metals out of rock

Almost all metals in the Earth's crust are already oxidised. They sit in ores as oxides, sulfides or carbonates. Making a metal means reducing it: forcing electrons back onto the metal atoms. Something has to supply those electrons.

For iron, the supplier is carbon. In a blast furnace, coke made from coal takes the oxygen off the iron ore and leaves as carbon dioxide. Volume 15 tells that story and its climate cost. For metals higher in the reactivity series, carbon is not a strong enough reducing agent. They need electricity, which pushes electrons in directly. This is electrolysis.

Aluminium shows how much this matters. In the mid-nineteenth century it was so hard to make that it was a luxury metal. The French emperor Napoleon III is said to have served his most honoured guests on aluminium plates. In 1884 the Washington Monument was topped with a small aluminium pyramid, then a precious material. Two years later, Charles Martin Hall in the United States and Paul Héroult in France independently found a way to reduce aluminium oxide cheaply with electricity. The price collapsed. Today aluminium is a drinks can, and smelters are built near cheap power, because the reduction uses huge amounts of electricity.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A trillion-dollar enemy, a life-saving weapon, and a health myth

Redox decides how long bridges stand, whether tap water is safe, and whether the supplement on your shelf is doing anything at all.

Chapter 08

The bill for corrosion

Corrosion is oxidation of metals by their surroundings, and it never stops. An industry study published in 2016 by NACE, the main corrosion engineers' association, estimated the global cost at roughly 2.5 trillion US dollars a year, about 3 percent of world economic output. The figure includes repairs, replacement, protection and lost production. It is an estimate, but even rough versions put corrosion among the most expensive slow problems in engineering.

Its worst cost is in lives. On 15 December 1967 the Silver Bridge over the Ohio River, between West Virginia and Ohio, collapsed during rush hour, and 46 people died. Investigators traced the failure to a single steel link in its suspension chain, which had cracked from a small flaw made worse by corrosion. The disaster led directly to regular national bridge inspections in the United States.

Engineers fight back with redox itself. The simplest defence is a barrier: paint, oil or plastic keeps oxygen and water away. A cleverer one is sacrificial protection. Zinc gives up electrons more readily than iron. Coat steel with zinc, a process called galvanising, and the zinc corrodes first, feeding electrons to the iron and keeping it reduced. Ships' hulls and buried pipelines carry blocks of zinc or magnesium for the same reason. They are replaced when they have been eaten away.

Chapter 09

Oxidisers that keep you alive

Strong oxidising agents tear electrons out of the molecules that make up microbes, breaking their membranes and proteins. That makes them excellent disinfectants. Chlorine and the hypochlorite in household bleach, ozone, and hydrogen peroxide all work this way.

Chlorinating drinking water is one of the great public-health advances in history. In 1908 Jersey City in the United States began continuously chlorinating its public water supply, one of the first large systems to do so. As the practice spread, deaths from waterborne diseases such as typhoid and cholera fell steeply in the cities that adopted it. Volume 15 explains the steps a modern treatment plant uses (Water treatment).

Oxidisers also whiten. Bleach removes stains by oxidising the coloured molecules into colourless ones. Hair is lightened by hydrogen peroxide oxidising the pigment melanin. The same reactivity is why these products damage fabric and hair if overused, and why bleach must never be mixed with other cleaners.

Chapter 10

Free radicals, antioxidants, and a supplement that backfired

Some molecules end up with an unpaired electron. These are called free radicals, and many are highly reactive, snatching electrons from whatever is nearby. Your body produces them constantly, mostly as a by-product of the electron transport chain. They can damage fats, proteins and DNA. The body also uses some of them on purpose, for example to kill bacteria and to send signals between cells.

An antioxidant is a molecule that gives up an electron to a radical and calms it down, without becoming dangerous itself. Your cells make their own antioxidant enzymes, and food contains others, such as vitamins C and E. Food makers add antioxidants to stop oils going rancid, which is an oxidation reaction (Volume 41 explains: What preservatives and the rest are doing).

From the 1980s onward many people hoped that antioxidant pills would prevent cancer and heart disease. Large trials tested this. In a Finnish trial published in 1994, male smokers given beta-carotene had more lung cancer, not less. A large American trial, CARET, was stopped early in 1996 for a similar reason. Later reviews of antioxidant supplements have generally found no reliable benefit for well-nourished people. Antioxidants in food are part of a healthy diet; extra antioxidants in pills are not the same thing, possibly because the body's radical chemistry is a balance that high doses can disturb.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking high-dose supplements, especially if you smoke or take other medicines.

Chapter 11

Redox you can watch

Redox reactions often change colour, because the number of electrons on a metal atom changes how it absorbs light. Older roadside breath testers used orange potassium dichromate, which turns green when it oxidises alcohol on a driver's breath and is itself reduced. Wine left open turns to vinegar because bacteria oxidise its alcohol into acetic acid. Traditional photographic film worked by light helping to reduce silver compounds into tiny grains of black silver metal. Silverware tarnishes black because silver loses electrons to sulfur compounds in the air, forming silver sulfide.

Each of these is the same event in a different costume: an electron changes owner, and the substance changes character.

Chapter 13

The oxygen paradox

Oxygen is the most important oxidiser in our lives, and it was once a pollutant. For the first half of Earth's history the air contained almost no free oxygen. Then microbes evolved that split water using sunlight and released oxygen as waste. Around 2.4 billion years ago oxygen began to build up in the atmosphere, a change known as the Great Oxidation Event. Many microbes that could not cope with it were likely pushed into oxygen-free corners, where their descendants still live. Iron dissolved in the oceans was oxidised and settled out, forming some of the large iron ore beds that are mined today.

Life that survived learned to use oxygen, which releases far more energy from food than processes without it. But the danger never fully went away. In the 1950s doctors discovered that giving premature babies too much oxygen could damage the blood vessels in their eyes and cause blindness. Oxygen in hospitals is now dosed carefully. The molecule that keeps us alive is also reactive enough to harm us, and every oxygen-breathing cell spends energy managing that tension.

The bigger pattern is that industry is learning to supply electrons from electricity instead of from carbon. For most of history, the cheapest way to reduce an ore or drive a reaction was to burn something. With cheap renewable power, electrons can come from a wire. That shift, from carbon as the electron supplier to electricity, may be the defining chemical change of this century.

The core idea

Oxidation is losing electrons; reduction is gaining them, and the two always happen together. Electrons flow from substances that hold them loosely (metals, fuels) to ones that grab them (oxygen, chlorine). That flow powers fire and breathing, makes metals from ores, and drives corrosion.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Stainless steel needs at least about 10.5% chromium to protect itself.
  • Corrosion was estimated in 2016 to cost roughly US$2.5 trillion a year, about 3% of world output.
  • Oxygen began building up in the air about 2.4 billion years ago.
The common mistake

Thinking oxidation requires oxygen, or that more antioxidants are always better. Oxidation is about electrons, not oxygen, and high-dose antioxidant pills have failed in major trials.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a zinc coating protects steel even after it is scratched? If you can, you have understood the reactivity series, electron flow and sacrificial protection together.

The words, in plain words
Oxidation
Losing electrons. Originally, combining with oxygen.
Reduction
Gaining electrons. Originally, removing oxygen from an ore.
Redox
Short for reduction-oxidation: any reaction in which electrons move between substances.
Oxidising agent
The substance that takes electrons, and is itself reduced. Oxygen and bleach are examples.
Reducing agent
The substance that gives electrons, and is itself oxidised. Metals and carbon are examples.
Oxidation number
A bookkeeping charge that shows how many electrons an atom has, in effect, gained or lost.
Reactivity series
A ranking of metals by how readily they give up electrons.
Corrosion
The gradual oxidation of a metal by its surroundings.
Passivation
Protection of a metal by a thin, tight oxide film on its surface.
Galvanising
Coating steel with zinc, which corrodes first and protects the iron.
Electron transport chain
The series of proteins in mitochondria that passes electrons from food to oxygen in small steps.
Free radical
A molecule with an unpaired electron, often highly reactive.
Antioxidant
A molecule that neutralises free radicals by giving them an electron.
