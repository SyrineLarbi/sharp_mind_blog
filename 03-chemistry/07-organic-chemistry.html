Organic chemistry. One element, millions of molecules, and shape as destiny.

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. That sounds narrow until you list what it includes: every medicine in your cabinet, the fuel in your car, the dye in your shirt, the smell of a banana, the plastic of your phone, and the molecules of your own body. A single element builds more known compounds than all the others put together. The field's central lesson is that what a molecule does depends not only on which atoms it contains, but on how they are arranged, down to whether the molecule is left-handed or right-handed.

Question 1 · What is it?

The chemistry of carbon, and nothing mystical about it

The name is a leftover from an old belief that molecules from living things were special. They are not special. They are just built mostly from carbon.

Chapter 01

The urine that came out of a flask

In the early nineteenth century, many chemists believed in vitalism: the idea that substances made by living things contained a "vital force" and could only be made by life. Sugar, oil, alcohol and urea, the main waste chemical in urine, were "organic". Salt, metals and minerals were "inorganic". The two worlds were thought to be separate.

In 1828 the German chemist Friedrich Wöhler was trying to make a simple mineral salt, ammonium cyanate. When he heated it, he got crystals of urea instead. He wrote to his teacher that he could make urea without needing a kidney, whether of man or dog. A substance from a living body had been made from non-living chemicals.

Historians point out that vitalism did not die in a single afternoon. It faded over decades as more natural substances were made in laboratories. But Wöhler's urea became the symbol of the change. Today "organic" in chemistry simply means a compound built on carbon, usually bonded to hydrogen. It has nothing to do with "organic" food, which is a farming label. Petrol and plastic are organic chemicals.

Chapter 02

Why one element needs its own branch

Chemistry's main registry of known substances lists well over a hundred million, and the great majority contain carbon. No other element comes close. The reason, as Volume 26 explains, is that carbon has four outer electrons and forms four strong bonds, including to other carbon atoms. It can make chains, branches and rings of almost any size, and those skeletons are stable at everyday temperatures.

Carbon's partners matter too. Mostly it bonds to hydrogen. Add a few oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur or halogen atoms (fluorine, chlorine, bromine), and the number of possible molecules becomes effectively endless. Organic chemistry is the study of what those combinations do, and of how to make the ones we want.

Chapter 03

Skeletons, rings, and how chemists draw them

The simplest organic molecules are hydrocarbons, made only of carbon and hydrogen. A chain of one carbon is methane, the main part of natural gas. Two is ethane, three is propane (the gas in camping stoves), four is butane (lighter fuel). Longer chains give petrol, then diesel, then wax. Longer chains stick together more, so they move from gas to liquid to solid.

Carbon atoms can also share two pairs of electrons, making a double bond. Double bonds are more reactive and make molecules stiffer. They are the difference between saturated fats, which have none and are solid like butter, and unsaturated fats, which have some and are liquid like olive oil.

Then there are rings. The most famous is benzene, six carbons in a flat hexagon. Its structure puzzled chemists until the German chemist August Kekulé proposed the ring in 1865. Years later he told an audience that the idea came to him in a daydream of a snake biting its own tail. True or not, the ring was right. Benzene-type rings, called aromatic rings (a historical name; many have no smell), appear in a huge share of medicines and dyes.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Small groups decide behaviour, and shape decides everything else

An organic chemist reads a molecule the way a mechanic reads an engine: a frame of carbon, with a few working parts attached where the action happens.

Chapter 04

Functional groups: the working parts

A carbon-hydrogen skeleton is fairly unreactive. What gives a molecule its personality is a small cluster of other atoms attached to it, called a functional group. The same group behaves in much the same way whatever skeleton it is on. That is the field's great simplification: learn about thirty groups and you can predict the behaviour of millions of molecules.

You can see the logic in one example. Acetic acid (vinegar) is sharp. React it with an alcohol and you get an ester, which smells sweet: isoamyl acetate smells strongly of bananas and pear drops. Change the functional group and you change the character, even though most of the molecule stays the same.

Chapter 05

Same atoms, different molecule

Two molecules can contain exactly the same atoms and be completely different substances. They are called isomers. The formula C2H6O describes ethanol, the liquid in beer and wine. It also describes dimethyl ether, a gas at room temperature that has been used as an aerosol propellant. Same nine atoms, arranged differently, with different boiling points, different smells and very different effects if you swallow them.

The subtlest case is handedness, which chemists call chirality. When a carbon atom carries four different groups, the molecule can exist in two mirror-image forms, like a left and a right hand. They contain the same atoms connected in the same order, but they cannot be laid on top of each other. In most everyday chemistry the two behave identically. In the body they often do not, because the body's receptors and enzymes are themselves handed.

The molecule carvone shows this beautifully. One hand smells of spearmint. Its mirror image smells of caraway, the seed in rye bread. Your nose can tell left from right at the molecular level.

Chapter 06

The tragedy that made handedness famous

Thalidomide was sold from 1957, mainly in Europe, Australia and Japan, as a sedative and a treatment for morning sickness in pregnancy. Within a few years doctors linked it to severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs. More than ten thousand babies are thought to have been affected worldwide before it was withdrawn in the early 1960s. In the United States, the FDA reviewer Frances Kelsey had refused to approve it, asking for more safety data.

Thalidomide is a chiral molecule, and it was sold as a mixture of both hands. Research later suggested that one hand mainly produced the sedative effect and the other was linked to the birth defects. It is often said that selling only the "good" hand would have prevented the tragedy. That is probably wrong: inside the body thalidomide flips between its two forms, so a pure version would soon become a mixture anyway.

The real lessons were about testing. Thalidomide led to much stricter drug regulation in many countries, including requirements to test new medicines for effects on pregnancy. It also pushed drug makers to study each hand of a chiral drug separately, and many modern drugs are now made as a single hand. Thalidomide itself is still used, under strict controls, to treat some cancers and a complication of leprosy.

Chapter 07

Moving electrons, and building molecules backwards

Organic reactions follow a simple underlying rule: electrons flow from where there are too many to where there are too few. An oxygen or nitrogen atom with spare electrons attacks a carbon atom that is short of them, often because a greedy oxygen is pulling its electrons away. Chemists draw these movements with curved arrows, and a trained chemist can often predict the product of an unfamiliar reaction just by following them.

Making a complex molecule is like building a cathedral from bricks, one reaction at a time. The American chemist Elias James Corey formalised a way of planning this, called retrosynthesis: start with the target and work backwards, breaking it in your mind into simpler pieces until you reach chemicals you can buy. He received the Nobel Prize in 1990. Today computer programs do much of this planning, but they use the same logic.

Aspirin is a simple example. Willow bark had been used against pain and fever since ancient times. Its active ingredient, salicin, is turned into salicylic acid in the body, which works but irritates the stomach badly. In 1897 chemists at the German company Bayer attached a small acetyl group to salicylic acid, making acetylsalicylic acid. It was gentler and was sold as Aspirin from 1899. Who deserves the credit inside Bayer is still disputed. One small change to a functional group made one of the most-used medicines in history.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The molecules the modern world is made of

Organic chemistry turned oil and coal into an industry, gave medicine its drugs, and put colour into everyday life. Much of it began with one teenager's failed experiment.

Chapter 08

A failed experiment that started an industry

In 1856 William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old chemistry student in London, was trying to make quinine, the anti-malaria drug, from chemicals found in coal tar. He failed. Instead he got a black sludge. Cleaning his flask with alcohol, he noticed a brilliant purple colour. He had made the first synthetic dye, which became known as mauveine.

Until then, purple dyes were rare and expensive. Perkin left college, built a factory, and became rich. Mauve became a fashion craze. More importantly, his success showed that coal tar, an unwanted waste product of making gas for city lighting, was a treasure chest of organic molecules. Chemists across Europe raced to make new colours. Several of today's largest chemical and pharmaceutical companies, including BASF, Bayer and Hoechst, began as dye makers in Germany in the 1860s. The modern chemical industry grew out of the search for colours. Its skills then moved into medicines, explosives, fertilisers and plastics.

Chapter 09

Oil, the feedstock of almost everything

Coal tar was replaced in the twentieth century by oil and natural gas. A refinery separates crude oil into fractions by boiling point, then "cracks" large molecules into smaller, more useful ones. Most of the output is burned as fuel. A smaller share, the petrochemicals, becomes the raw material for plastics, synthetic fibres, solvents, detergents, paints, adhesives and many medicines.

This is why the end of oil as a fuel does not mean the end of oil. Even in scenarios where transport is electrified, the chemical industry still needs a source of carbon atoms. They could come from oil, from plants, from recycled plastic or, in principle, from carbon dioxide. Each option has very different costs, and the choice is one of the central questions in Green chemistry.

Chapter 10

Medicines are organic molecules

Most medicines are small organic molecules: a few dozen atoms built around carbon rings and chains, with functional groups placed so that the molecule fits a target in the body. Volume 44 explains the lock-and-key idea (Lock and key, and why side effects are unavoidable). Organic chemistry is the craft of making the keys.

Knowing a molecule's exact shape changed medicine. In 1945 the British chemist Dorothy Hodgkin used X-rays scattered by penicillin crystals to work out its structure. It contained an unusual, strained four-membered ring, which turned out to be the part that attacks bacteria. She went on to solve vitamin B12, which helped earn her the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and later insulin. Today chemists start drug design from the three-dimensional structure of the target protein, then build molecules to fit it.

Chapter 11

Smell, taste, and the identical copy

Smells and flavours are organic molecules too. The taste of vanilla comes mainly from one molecule, vanillin. Real vanilla pods are expensive and their supply is small and unstable, so the great majority of vanilla flavour in food is made industrially, from sources such as wood pulp by-products or petrochemicals. A vanillin molecule made in a factory is identical to one from a pod. Real vanilla tastes richer because the pod contains hundreds of other compounds as well.

This leads to an important point. A molecule does not remember where it came from. Synthetic vitamin C is the same molecule as the one in an orange. The question that matters is never "natural or synthetic?" but "which molecule, in what amount?"

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Molecules built to last, sometimes too well

Organic chemistry's power is that it can make molecules with almost any property. Its biggest mistakes came from molecules that were too stable, too active, or released too widely.

Chapter 12

What the field gave, and what it cost

ProductWhat it gaveWhat went wrong
CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons)Safe, non-flammable refrigerants and spray propellantsDrifted to the upper atmosphere and destroyed ozone. Phased out under the 1987 Montreal Protocol, and the ozone layer is slowly recovering.
DDTCheap, effective insecticide that cut malaria in many regionsBuilt up in food chains and thinned birds' eggshells. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring led to bans on farm use. Limited indoor use against malaria continues.
PFAS ("forever chemicals")Non-stick, waterproof and stain-proof coatings; firefighting foamsThe carbon-fluorine bond is so strong that they barely break down. They are now found in water and in most people's blood.
PlasticsLight, cheap, hygienic, versatilePersistence and pollution (see Vol 26)
MedicinesAntibiotics, painkillers, cancer drugs and much moreSide effects, and residues reaching rivers through wastewater

Notice the pattern. The properties that made each product useful were the same ones that caused the harm. CFCs were valuable because they were unreactive, and that is why they survived long enough to reach the ozone layer. PFAS repel everything because the carbon-fluorine bond is so strong, and that is why nothing in nature breaks them down.

Chapter 13

"Chemical-free" is not a thing

The mistakes above have left many people suspicious of "chemicals", and product labels exploit it with words like "chemical-free". Everything is made of chemicals, including water and you. Some of the most toxic substances known are entirely natural: botulinum toxin, made by a bacterium, and ricin, from castor beans. Many natural plant compounds are deliberate poisons that plants evolved to deter animals.

The opposite mistake is just as wrong. Synthetic is not automatically safe, as the table shows. The balanced position is the one toxicologists use: judge each molecule by what it does, at the dose people actually receive, and over how long it stays around. That is the subject of Toxicology.

Running through all three is a change in how the work is done. Software now suggests synthetic routes, robots run reactions, and models trained on millions of published reactions predict which ones will work. The craft that Wöhler, Perkin and Corey practised by hand is becoming partly automated. The questions stay the same: which skeleton, which functional groups, which hand, and at what cost to make?

The core idea

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Carbon forms skeletons of chains and rings; functional groups attached to them decide how a molecule behaves; and shape, including handedness, decides how it interacts with the body. Chemists build molecules by planning backwards from the target.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Wöhler made urea from non-living chemicals in 1828.
  • Known substances number well over a hundred million, mostly carbon-based.
  • Thalidomide affected more than 10,000 babies before withdrawal in the early 1960s.
The common mistake

Thinking "organic" means natural or safe, or that natural molecules differ from identical synthetic ones. A molecule does not remember where it came from. Dose and structure decide its effects.

Test yourself

Can you explain why making only one hand of thalidomide would probably not have prevented the disaster? If you can, you have understood chirality, the body's handedness and why drug testing matters.

The words, in plain words
Organic compound
A compound built on carbon, usually bonded to hydrogen.
Vitalism
The old belief that substances from living things needed a special "vital force" to be made.
Hydrocarbon
A molecule made only of carbon and hydrogen, such as methane or petrol.
Double bond
Two shared pairs of electrons between two atoms, more reactive than a single bond.
Aromatic ring
A flat ring of carbons, like benzene, with electrons shared around it.
Functional group
A small cluster of atoms on a molecule that gives it its typical behaviour.
Isomers
Molecules with the same atoms arranged differently.
Chirality
Handedness: a molecule existing in two mirror-image forms that cannot be superimposed.
Synthesis
Making a molecule through a planned series of reactions.
Retrosynthesis
Planning a synthesis by working backwards from the target molecule.
Petrochemicals
Chemicals made from oil and natural gas, used to make plastics, fibres and more.
PFAS
A large family of fluorine-containing chemicals that barely break down in the environment.
