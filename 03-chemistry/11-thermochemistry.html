Thermochemistry. The energy accounts behind every fire, battery and meal.

Shake a hand warmer and it gets hot. Squeeze an instant cold pack and it gets cold. Burn a litre of petrol and you can drive a car fifteen kilometres. All three are the same question in different clothes: where does the energy in a chemical change come from, and where does it go? Thermochemistry is the bookkeeping that answers it. It tells you how much heat a reaction gives off or takes in, and, with one extra idea called entropy, whether the reaction can happen at all. It does not tell you how fast.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of heat going in and out of chemical change

Every reaction rearranges atoms. Rearranging atoms always changes the energy stored in their connections, and the difference shows up as heat, light or electrical work.

Chapter 01

The hand warmer and the cold pack

A disposable hand warmer is a small bag of iron powder, salt, water and charcoal. Open the wrapper and air gets in. The iron starts to rust, fast, because the powder has an enormous surface. Rusting releases energy, and in a bag that size the energy comes out as a steady warmth for several hours.

An instant cold pack does the opposite. Inside is a solid, often ammonium nitrate or urea, and a pouch of water. Squeeze the pack, break the pouch, and the solid dissolves. Dissolving that solid takes in heat from the surroundings, so the water, the bag and your sprained ankle all get colder.

Chemists have two words for these. A change that releases heat to its surroundings is exothermic (from Greek "heat out"). A change that absorbs heat from its surroundings is endothermic ("heat in"). Thermochemistry is the measurement and prediction of that heat flow.

Chapter 02

Breaking bonds costs, making bonds pays

The most common misunderstanding in all of chemistry is the idea that energy is "stored in bonds" and released when they break. It is backwards. Breaking a chemical bond always costs energy. Forming one always releases energy. Pulling bonded atoms apart is like pulling two magnets apart: you have to do work.

So why does burning release so much heat? Because a reaction does both. When methane, the main part of natural gas, burns, the bonds inside methane and inside oxygen molecules are broken (a cost), and new bonds in carbon dioxide and water are formed (a payment). The bonds in carbon dioxide and water are much stronger than the ones that were broken. The payment exceeds the cost, and the difference comes out as heat. A fuel is not a parcel of stored energy. A fuel is a substance whose atoms can fall into a much more stable arrangement.

Chapter 03

Measuring it: a guinea pig in a bucket of ice

In the early 1780s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier and the mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace built an "ice calorimeter". Anything warm inside its chamber melted the surrounding ice, and the meltwater measured the heat. They put a guinea pig inside and compared the heat it gave off with the heat of burning charcoal that produced the same amount of carbon dioxide. It was early, powerful evidence that breathing is a slow form of burning.

The modern instrument is the calorimeter, any device that measures heat by the temperature change it causes in a known amount of water or metal. For fuels and foods, chemists use a "bomb calorimeter": a sealed steel container filled with pure oxygen and sunk in water. The sample is ignited electrically, burns completely, and the rise in water temperature tells you its energy.

The units

The scientific unit of energy is the joule (J). One older unit, the calorie, is the heat that warms one gram of water by one degree Celsius, about 4.18 joules. The "Calorie" on a food label is a thousand of those, a kilocalorie (kcal), about 4.2 kilojoules. Chemists usually quote reaction heats in kilojoules per mole, where a mole is a fixed, very large count of molecules (about 6 × 1023), chosen so that a mole of a substance weighs its formula weight in grams.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four ideas: enthalpy, Hess's law, entropy and the energy hill

Heat tells you how much energy changes hands. Entropy tells you which way things tend to go. Free energy combines them. The energy hill explains why possible things do not always happen.

Chapter 04

Enthalpy: heat with the bookkeeping done properly

Most reactions on Earth happen in open containers at roughly constant air pressure. Under those conditions the heat a reaction gives off or takes in has a name: the enthalpy change, written ΔH (the Greek letter delta means "change in"). ΔH is negative when the reaction releases heat, because the chemicals end up with less energy than they started with. It is positive when heat is absorbed.

Some typical values make the scale concrete. Burning one mole of methane (16 grams) releases about 890 kilojoules. Turning one mole of liquid water into steam (18 grams) absorbs about 41 kilojoules, which is why boiling a pan dry takes so long after it reaches boiling point.

Chapter 05

Hess's law: the route does not matter

In 1840 Germain Hess, a Swiss-born chemist working in St Petersburg, published a rule that sounds too simple to be important. The total heat of a reaction is the same whatever route you take from start to finish. Burn carbon straight to carbon dioxide, or burn it first to carbon monoxide and then burn that, and the total heat comes out the same.

Many reactions cannot be measured directly. They are too slow, or they produce a mess of side products. Hess's law lets chemists add and subtract reactions they can measure and get the one they cannot. This is how tables of "heats of formation" were built: one number for each compound, from which the heat of almost any reaction can be calculated on paper.

Chapter 06

Estimating heat from bond strengths

Chemists have measured roughly how much energy it takes to break each common kind of bond. A carbon-hydrogen bond needs about 410 kilojoules per mole. The double bond in an oxygen molecule needs about 500. The double bonds in carbon dioxide are much stronger, around 800 each, and an oxygen-hydrogen bond in water is about 460.

With those numbers you can sketch why fuels burn hot. Methane has four carbon-hydrogen bonds. Two oxygen molecules join in. Add up what you break, subtract what you make, and the answer comes out close to the measured 800 to 890 kilojoules. The estimate is rough, but it gives the intuition chemists use daily. The more hydrogen-carbon bonds a fuel has, and the less oxygen it already contains, the more energy it releases per gram. That is why fats carry more energy than sugars. Sugar molecules are already partly combined with oxygen, so they are partway down the hill.

Chapter 07

Why heat alone does not decide: entropy and free energy

If releasing heat were the only thing that mattered, the cold pack could not work. Its reaction absorbs heat, yet it happens on its own as soon as the water touches the crystal. Ice melts on a warm day, also absorbing heat. Something else is pushing.

That something is entropy, a measure of how many ways the energy and the particles in a system can be arranged. A crystal is a tidy lattice with few arrangements. The same ions floating in water can be arranged in vastly more ways. Nature drifts toward the states with the most arrangements, simply because there are more of them.

In the 1870s the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs combined the two drives into one quantity, now called Gibbs free energy, written G. Its change is:

The single most useful equation in this topic
   ΔG  =  ΔH  -  T × ΔS

   ΔG  change in free energy   negative = the reaction can run by itself
   ΔH  change in enthalpy      negative = heat released (helps)
   T   temperature in kelvin   (degrees Celsius + 273)
   ΔS  change in entropy       positive = more disorder (helps)

A reaction can proceed on its own when releasing heat and increasing disorder, taken together, come out favourable. Temperature sets the exchange rate between them. At high temperature, the entropy term counts for more. That is why ice melts above 0 °C and water freezes below it: melting absorbs heat (bad) but increases disorder (good), and the balance tips at exactly the melting point.

Chemists call a reaction with negative ΔG spontaneous. In chemistry the word means "able to happen without outside help", not "sudden", as the next chapter shows.

Chapter 08

The energy hill: why paper does not burst into flames

Paper and air together are far less stable than ash, carbon dioxide and water. Thermochemistry says paper "should" burn. Yet a book can sit on a shelf for three hundred years. The reason is that to get from one arrangement to the other, bonds must first be partly broken, and that costs energy up front. Picture a hill between two valleys: the far valley is lower, but you must climb first.

The height of that hill is the activation energy. A match supplies it for a few molecules, and the heat they release supplies it for their neighbours, and the fire spreads. In 1889 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius described how the rate of a reaction rises steeply with temperature, because more molecules have enough energy to get over the hill. A rough rule of thumb for many reactions near room temperature is that rates about double for every 10 °C rise, which is why food lasts longer in a fridge.

This is the boundary of thermochemistry. It tells you where the valleys are and how deep. It says nothing about how high the hill between them is. Lowering that hill without being used up is exactly what a catalyst does (Chemistry · Catalysts). Studying the hill itself, and how fast things cross it, is called kinetics (Chemistry · Chemical reactions).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fuel, food, climate and keeping factories in one piece

The same accounting that explains a cold pack sets the range of an electric car, the number on a cereal box, and the safety rules of every chemical plant.

Chapter 09

Energy density: why oil was so hard to replace

The energy a fuel releases per kilogram is its energy density. It explains a great deal of modern history. Approximate values, using the full heat of combustion where relevant:

An explosive does not contain unusual amounts of energy. It releases ordinary amounts extremely fast, because it carries its own oxygen and does not wait for air. Batteries look hopeless next to petrol, and yet electric cars work. An engine turns only about a quarter to a third of petrol's heat into motion, wasting the rest, while an electric motor converts around 90% of the battery's energy. The battery side is covered in What a battery actually is.

Chapter 10

The numbers on a food label

Food energy values come straight from thermochemistry, with a correction for biology. Around 1900 the American chemist Wilbur Atwater burned foods in calorimeters and measured what people actually absorbed and excreted. His rounded results are still printed on labels: about 4 kilocalories per gram of carbohydrate or protein, 9 per gram of fat, and 7 per gram of alcohol.

Your body does exactly what the bomb calorimeter does, ending with carbon dioxide and water from fat and sugar. By Hess's law, the route through dozens of enzyme steps releases the same energy as a flame. Protein is the exception. Your body cannot burn its nitrogen to the end point a flame would, and excretes it as urea, which is why protein's label value is lower than its calorimeter value.

Chapter 11

Runaway: when heat feeds the reaction that makes it

Put Chapters 04 and 08 together and you get the most important safety idea in industrial chemistry. An exothermic reaction releases heat. Heat raises the temperature. Higher temperature speeds the reaction. A faster reaction releases heat faster. If cooling cannot keep up, the loop accelerates on its own. This is called thermal runaway.

A large tank is more dangerous than a small flask of the same mixture. Heat is made throughout the volume, but it escapes only through the surface. Double the width of a tank and its volume rises about eight times, while its surface rises only four times. Big reactors are harder to cool, which is why scaling up a recipe that was safe on the bench is one of the riskiest steps in chemical engineering.

The same loop drives the rare but violent fires in lithium-ion batteries: a damaged or faulty cell heats, its internal chemicals start to break down exothermically, and the heat spreads to the next cell. It also played a part in the 1984 Bhopal disaster in India, where water entering a storage tank of methyl isocyanate set off a heat-releasing reaction that pushed the toxic gas out (Bhopal, the disaster with the highest toll).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful accountant with blind spots

Thermochemistry is one of the most reliable tools in science. Its limits come from what it chooses not to measure.

Chapter 12

What it tells you, and what it does not

FeatureStrengthWeakness
Based on energy conservationExtremely reliable. No exception has ever been found.It forbids things but cannot invent them. It tells you a reaction is possible, not how to make it work.
Route does not matter (Hess)You can calculate reactions nobody can measure directly.Errors in the tabulated values add up when you chain many steps together.
Free energy predicts directionTells you whether a reaction can run on its own, and how temperature changes that.Says nothing about speed. Diamond to graphite is "allowed" and never happens in practice.
Standard conditionsEveryone compares numbers on the same footing (usually 25 °C and one atmosphere).Real reactors run hot, under pressure, in mixtures. The numbers need correcting.

The deepest limit is speed. A huge share of the world around you exists only because reactions that "should" happen are stuck behind tall energy hills. Wood, paper and your own body, sitting in oxygen, are all unstable on paper and safe in practice. Life itself is a collection of reactions that are allowed but kept waiting, then released on command by enzymes.

Chapter 14

The quiet shift: moving heat instead of making it

For most of history, if you wanted heat you burned something. Thermochemistry sets a ceiling on that: a cubic metre of gas can only give you its heat of combustion, and no clever boiler can give you more. A heat pump breaks the frame. It uses electricity not to make heat but to move heat from the cold outside air or ground into the house, the way a fridge moves heat out of its box and into your kitchen (Why your fridge warms the room).

A good heat pump delivers about three to four units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses. That is not a violation of energy conservation: the extra comes from the outside environment (Heat pumps). The future of heating may depend less on what we burn and more on how cleverly we move energy that already exists. Industrial heat at several hundred degrees is harder, and burning will remain part of that answer for some time.

The core idea

Reactions are exothermic (heat out) or endothermic (heat in) depending on whether the bonds made are stronger than the bonds broken. The heat at constant pressure is the enthalpy change, and by Hess's law it does not depend on the route. Whether a reaction can run on its own depends on free energy, which weighs heat against entropy. How fast it runs is a separate question, set by the activation energy.

The numbers worth knowing
  • One food Calorie is about 4.2 kilojoules.
  • Food: about 4 kcal/g carbohydrate or protein, 9 fat, 7 alcohol.
  • Petrol holds about 45 MJ/kg, roughly ten times TNT and dozens of times a lithium-ion battery.
  • Many reaction rates roughly double per 10 °C.
The common mistake

Saying energy is "stored in bonds" and "released when they break". Breaking bonds always costs energy. Energy is released when new, stronger bonds form.

Test yourself

Can you explain why an instant cold pack works, even though its reaction absorbs heat? If you can, you have understood the difference between enthalpy and free energy.

The words, in plain words
Exothermic
A change that releases heat into its surroundings, like burning.
Endothermic
A change that absorbs heat from its surroundings, like melting ice.
Enthalpy change (ΔH)
The heat a reaction gives out or takes in at constant pressure. Negative means heat released.
Calorimeter
A device that measures heat from the temperature change it causes.
Mole
A fixed, very large count of particles, used so chemists can weigh out equal numbers of molecules.
Bond energy
The energy needed to break a particular kind of chemical bond, averaged over many molecules.
Hess's law
The total heat of a reaction is the same whatever route connects the start and the finish.
Entropy
A measure of how many ways a system's particles and energy can be arranged. It tends to increase.
Gibbs free energy (ΔG)
Heat and entropy combined into one number. Negative means the reaction can run on its own.
Spontaneous
In chemistry, "able to happen without outside help". It does not mean quick.
Activation energy
The energy hill a reaction must climb before it can run downhill.
Thermal runaway
A loop where a reaction's heat speeds it up until cooling can no longer keep pace.
Say this · five sentences you can use
  1. "Breaking a bond always costs energy. Fuels release heat because the new bonds they form are stronger than the old ones."
  2. "Explosives don't contain much energy. They just release it very fast. Petrol holds about ten times more per kilo than TNT."
  3. "Thermochemistry tells you whether a reaction is possible, not whether it's fast. That's why paper doesn't catch fire on its own."
  4. "A heat pump doesn't make heat, it moves it, which is why it can deliver three or four times the energy it uses."
  5. "For any new fuel, ask one question: how much energy goes in to make it, compared with what comes out?"
