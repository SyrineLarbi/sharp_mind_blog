Life. The cell, the code, the body, and the three pounds of tissue reading this.

Every living thing on Earth is built the same way, runs on the same code, and is descended from the same ancestor. That is not a poetic flourish — it is the single most striking finding in biology, and everything in this volume follows from it. No formulas, no Latin you have to memorise, just what is actually happening.

Part I

The unit

Chapter 01

Everything alive is made of the same thing

Bacteria, oak trees, mushrooms and people are all built from cells — small bags of watery chemistry wrapped in a membrane. You are somewhere around thirty trillion of them, cooperating.

A cell has to do a small number of jobs and every cell does all of them: keep the outside out and the inside in, take in fuel, get energy from it, build the parts it needs, hold instructions, and copy itself.

The membrane deserves a moment because it is beautifully simple. It is made of molecules with a water-loving head and a water-hating tail — exactly like the soap of the previous volume. Drop them in water and they spontaneously arrange into a double sheet, tails tucked inward away from the water, heads facing out on both sides. Nobody assembles it. The chemistry of the pieces makes the structure build itself, and that is probably how the first cells came about.

Bigger cells — yours, a plant's, a fungus's — also contain compartments doing specialised work. The one that releases energy from food has its own separate DNA and divides on its own schedule, because it is the descendant of a free-living bacterium that was swallowed and never digested. Two organisms became one, permanently, and every complex organism on Earth is a result of that merger.

Chapter 02

The code, and why it is the same everywhere

DNA is a long molecule made of four units repeated in some order. The order is the information, the way letters make a sentence.

The molecule is two strands twisted together, and the units pair in a fixed way — each one always opposite one particular partner. That single rule does two jobs at once. It means each strand contains enough information to rebuild the other, so copying is straightforward: unzip, and build a new partner against each half. And it means the information is stored twice, which helps with repair.

What the code actually specifies is proteins. Read three units at a time, and each triplet names an amino acid; string the named amino acids together and they fold into a shape; the shape does a job. Proteins are the machinery — they digest your food, carry oxygen, build your hair, fight infection and read the DNA itself.

Here is the remarkable part. The same triplets mean the same amino acids in a bacterium, a banana and you. There is no chemical reason it had to come out this way; many other assignments would work. The only sensible explanation for one shared code is one shared ancestor.

Chapter 03

Evolution, in four sentences

Individuals vary, and some of the variation is inherited. More offspring are produced than can survive. Variations that happen to help an individual survive and reproduce become more common in the next generation. Repeat for an extremely long time.

That is the entire mechanism. Nothing is choosing, nothing is aiming, and no individual evolves — a population does, across generations.

Two misunderstandings are worth clearing. Evolution is not about the strongest; it is about whatever fits the current circumstances, which might be smallest, or dullest, or best at hiding. And it has no goal and no upward direction — organisms have lost eyes, legs and complexity when those cost more than they returned.

The evidence is not one line but several that agree. Fossils appear in a consistent order. Anatomy shows the same underlying bone arrangement modified for entirely different uses — a bat's wing, a whale's flipper and your hand have the same bones in the same order, which makes sense as modification and no sense as design. And DNA comparison independently produces the same family tree that fossils and anatomy suggested, which is a genuinely severe test that it passed.

The leftovers

You get goosebumps, which fluff up fur you no longer have. You have an appendix, muscles for moving ears that do not move, and the remnants of a tail. Whales have hip bones inside them, connected to nothing. These make sense as inherited leftovers and make no sense otherwise, which is why they are some of the most persuasive evidence there is.

Part II

The body

Chapter 04

What your organs are actually for

Almost every organ is solving one of four problems: getting fuel in, getting oxygen in, moving both around, or getting waste out.

Digestion is chemical dismantling. Food is made of molecules too large to cross into your blood, so the gut takes them apart — stomach acid and enzymes breaking proteins, fats and starches into pieces small enough to absorb through the intestine wall, which is folded and refolded until its surface area is roughly that of a tennis court.

Lungs are surface area too. Their job is to put air next to blood with the thinnest possible barrier between, and they do it by branching into hundreds of millions of tiny sacs. Oxygen crosses into the blood and carbon dioxide crosses out, purely because each moves from where it is plentiful to where it is scarce. No pumping is involved — it is simply spreading.

The heart is a pump with two circuits, one sending blood to the lungs to collect oxygen and one sending it round the body to deliver it. And the kidneys are a filter with a clever design: they dump almost everything out of the blood and then carefully reabsorb what is worth keeping, which sounds wasteful and is far easier to regulate than picking out individual poisons.

Chapter 05

How your body defends itself

There are two systems and they work quite differently.

The first is general-purpose and immediate. Skin and mucus keep things out. If something gets in, cells that eat intruders arrive within minutes, and the area becomes hot, red and swollen — which is not the infection but your response to it, widening blood vessels to bring more defenders.

The second is specific and slow, and it is the interesting one. Your body carries an enormous variety of cells, each shaped to recognise one particular intruder. Almost all of them will never meet theirs. When one does, it multiplies rapidly into an army tailored to that exact threat.

That takes days, which is why you are ill for a week. But afterwards some of those cells remain, and the second time they respond so quickly you never notice. That is immunity, and it is why you get chickenpox once.

A vaccine is simply a way of showing your immune system the shape of a threat without the threat — a fragment, or a disabled version, or instructions for your own cells to make one harmless piece of it. The memory forms, and the first real encounter is met with the second-time response.

Two things go wrong. Sometimes the system attacks the body's own tissue, which is autoimmune disease. Sometimes it reacts violently to something harmless — pollen, peanuts — which is allergy, an immune system fighting extremely hard against a threat that is not there.

Chapter 06

The passengers

You are carrying roughly as many bacterial cells as human ones, mostly in your gut, and they are not freeloaders.

They digest fibre you cannot, manufacture certain vitamins, crowd out dangerous species by occupying the space, and train your immune system about what is harmless. Animals raised entirely germ-free are not healthier; they are distinctly unwell.

The population varies with diet, and it shifts within days of changing what you eat. It is disrupted by antibiotics, which cannot distinguish the bacteria making you ill from the ones helping you, and recovery takes weeks or months.

There is good evidence connecting gut bacteria to digestion, immunity and weight, and suggestive but much less settled evidence connecting them to mood and behaviour. The field is genuinely exciting and currently oversold, and a reasonable position is interested scepticism.

Part III

The brain

Chapter 07

How a thought physically happens

A neuron is a cell with a long tail and a bushy end, and it works electrically along its own length and chemically between itself and the next one.

A signal travels down the tail as a wave of electrical change, arrives at the end, and causes chemical messengers to be released into the small gap before the next neuron. Those messengers cross and either encourage or discourage the next cell from firing. Each neuron sits collecting thousands of these votes, and fires when the encouragement outweighs the discouragement.

There are something like eighty-six billion of them, each connected to thousands of others. The connections are where everything is: you are not your neurons so much as the pattern of strength between them.

Which is also what learning is. Connections that are used repeatedly become stronger and easier to trigger; ones that are not used weaken. Practising a skill is physically thickening certain routes. This continues for life, though more readily when young.

Chapter 08

Why you cannot trust what you see

It feels as though your eyes deliver a picture and you watch it. That is not remotely what happens.

Your eye has a blind spot where the nerve leaves, and you never see a hole because your brain fills it in with a guess based on the surroundings. Only a small central patch of your vision is detailed; the rest is vague, and you do not notice because your eyes dart constantly and the brain assembles the impression of a complete sharp scene that you never actually received.

Colour is constructed too. A white shirt looks white in warm evening light and in blue daylight, even though the light reaching your eye is completely different, because your brain corrects for the illumination. That correction is a decision, and when two people's brains make different decisions about an ambiguously lit photograph they will disagree furiously about its colours — as one notorious dress once demonstrated.

So perception is not recording. It is your brain's best running hypothesis about what is out there, built from fragments and heavily edited. Usually it is right. When it is wrong, it is wrong with total confidence, which is exactly why eyewitness testimony is far less reliable than juries assume, and why the psychology volume on the shelf next to this one matters.

Reference

What to carry forward

The finding that organises everything

Every living thing uses the same genetic code — the same triplets meaning the same building blocks in a bacterium, a banana and you. There is no chemical necessity to it. One shared code means one shared ancestor.

Copying, and evolution

DNA's fixed pairing rule means each strand can rebuild the other, which is how copying works. Evolution is four sentences: variation, more born than survive, helpful variations spread, repeat. Nothing aims at anything, and the leftovers — goosebumps, whale hip bones — are the giveaway.

The body, and the defence

Most organs are surface area or filtration solving one of four problems. Immunity works by keeping a huge variety of cells that each recognise one threat — and remembering, which is all a vaccine exploits.

The brain

You are the pattern of connection strengths, and learning physically thickens the routes you use. And perception is not recording but hypothesis — filled in, corrected, edited, and confidently wrong when it is wrong.
