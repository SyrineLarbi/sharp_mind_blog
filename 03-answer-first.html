Sharp Mind 03 · the delivery

Answer first. Sounding organised is a structure, not a talent.

Most people tell you how they got there and then what they concluded. That order is natural, generous, and wrong — it spends your listener's attention on the journey and delivers the destination after they have stopped paying for it. Reverse it and two things happen at once: you become markedly easier to follow, and you become able to think while you speak, because the shape came out of your mouth before the content did.

Why this handbook is worth the most per page

File 01 gave you the fifteen-second emergency skeleton — position, reason, qualifier, handback. This handbook is that skeleton scaled up until it works for a Slack message, a written recommendation, a thirty-minute case interview and a board update, all from one underlying shape.

It is also the handbook with the fastest visible payoff. The techniques in 01 change what happens in your head, and nobody can see that. This one changes the first sentence out of your mouth, and everybody can.

Part I · The principle

Why the answer goes first

Four sections on the underlying structure. It comes from Barbara Minto, who built it at McKinsey in the 1960s and wrote it up as The Pyramid Principle — the full extraction of that book is in file 07.

Answer First 01

Why suspense fails in professional communication

Here is how almost everyone answers a question they have thought about carefully:

The natural order — and why it costs you

"So I looked at the last three months of returns data, and I also spoke to the warehouse team, and there were a few things going on — some of it was the packaging change, some of it was that one courier, and I compared it against last year as well, which was complicated because the volumes were different, and... so I think we should probably switch couriers."

Every word of that is true and the conclusion is sound. It still fails, for a mechanical reason: by the time the recommendation arrives, the listener has spent forty seconds holding unlabelled fragments in working memory with no idea what they are for. They cannot file anything, because they do not yet know what the file is called.

This is the same working-memory constraint from file 01, §02, seen from the other side. There you were the one holding four chunks. Here you are the one handing someone four chunks — and if you hand them over without a label, they fall on the floor.

THE SAME CONTENT, TWO ORDERS SUSPENSE ORDER ANSWER-FIRST ORDER ────────────── ────────────────── evidence ─┐ CONCLUSION ─┐ evidence ─┤ ← listener holds │ │ ← listener now has evidence ─┤ all of this with │ │ a container. Every evidence ─┘ no container │ │ fact that follows │ ▼ ▼ goes straight into it ▼ evidence CONCLUSION evidence │ evidence ▼ │ listener re-runs the ▼ whole thing to see if listener is evaluating it supported the point AS THEY GO (they will not bother) ⇒ SAME WORDS. THE SECOND ONE IS UNDERSTOOD, THE FIRST ONE IS ENDURED.

There is a second reason, and in practice it is the more important one: you will be interrupted. Meetings run over, someone has a hard stop, your manager gets a message. If your conclusion is at the end, an interruption at minute two means it was never said at all. If your conclusion was first, an interruption at minute two means they got the whole point and missed some supporting detail. Section 08 is entirely about this.

The one exception

Suspense is right when the conclusion will be rejected on sight and the reasoning is what earns it a hearing — some bad news, some genuinely unpopular recommendations. Even then the fix is not full suspense; it is the SCQA opening in section 04, which builds the case in three sentences and then still delivers the answer early. Full suspense is almost never correct in working life, and people reach for it far more often than the exception justifies.

Answer First 02

The pyramid

Minto's structure is one idea, applied recursively. Every communication is a pyramid: a single governing statement at the top, supported by a small group of statements beneath it, each of which may itself be supported by a group beneath it.

THE PYRAMID ┌──────────────────────────┐ │ THE ANSWER — one │ │ sentence, said first │ └────────────┬─────────────┘ ┌──────────┼──────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ ┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐ ┌─────────┐ │ reason │ │ reason │ │ reason │ │ 1 │ │ 2 │ │ 3 │ └────┬────┘ └────┬────┘ └────┬────┘ │ │ │ ┌────┴───┐ ┌────┴───┐ ┌────┴───┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ ▼ ▼ ▼ fact fact fact fact fact fact THREE RULES THAT MAKE IT WORK ① EVERY BOX SUMMARISES THE BOXES BELOW IT If the three reasons do not add up to the answer, the answer is wrong or a reason is missing. ② BOXES IN A GROUP ARE THE SAME KIND OF THING Three reasons, or three steps, or three options. Never two reasons and a step — that is the most common structural error people make. ③ A GROUP HAS A LOGICAL ORDER by importance · by time · by structure. Never "the order I thought of them in".

You do not need to draw a pyramid to use it. In speech it collapses to something very simple: say the top box, say how many boxes are underneath, then say them. That is the whole technique, and it is section 05.

Finding the top box

The hard part is not the structure. It is that most people do not know what their own top box is — they have five things to say and no view about which one governs. The test is a single question:

The top-box test

"If they only hear one sentence, which one do I want it to be?"

Not the most interesting sentence. Not the most defensible. The one that, on its own, would change what they do. If you cannot answer this, you are not ready to speak yet — and the honest move is one of the stalls from file 01, §09 rather than starting to talk and hoping the point arrives.

Answer First 03

Grouping that holds — MECE without the jargon

The supports under your answer have to be a proper group. Consultants call the standard MECE — mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive — which is off-putting jargon for two ordinary requirements.

The requirementIn plain wordsWhat it fails like
Mutually exclusive The items do not overlap. Nothing is counted twice. "Three reasons: cost, budget, and the price." The listener senses padding even if they cannot name why, and the argument feels thinner than it is.
Collectively exhaustive Together they cover the ground you claimed to cover. "There are two ways to fix this" — when there are four. Someone names the third, and your whole structure looks careless.

In practice you rarely need perfect exhaustiveness. What you need is to not claim more than you have covered. The fix is a single word:

Hedging the claim, not the content
❌ "There are three reasons."
   → invites: "what about X?" and now you are on the back foot

✓  "There are three main reasons."
✓  "The three that matter most are —"
✓  "There are a few; the two big ones are —"

⇒ ONE WORD ("main", "biggest", "that matter") CONVERTS AN
  EXHAUSTIVE CLAIM INTO A PRIORITISED ONE. You keep the
  structure and drop the exposure.

⚠ NOTE THE DIFFERENCE FROM HEDGING YOUR CONTENT.
  "I think maybe there are possibly three reasons" weakens
  the ANSWER — that is the hedging file 04 tells you to cut.
  "The three main reasons" bounds the CLAIM. Bounding a claim
  is precision. Hedging a position is weakness. They look
  similar on paper and land in completely opposite ways.

How many boxes

Two or three. Occasionally four. Never seven.

This is not a stylistic preference — it is the working-memory limit again. Your listener can hold about four chunks (file 01, §02). Give them three reasons and they retain the structure. Give them seven and they retain a general impression that you had a lot of reasons, which is a strictly worse outcome than having given three.

And as file 01 §07 noted from the other direction: a long list is judged near its weakest member, because the weakest is the one that gets attacked.

Answer First 04

SCQA — the four sentences that earn attention first

Answer-first has one real weakness: dropped cold, a conclusion can land on someone who is not yet holding the problem it solves. "We should switch couriers" said to someone who did not know there was a courier issue produces confusion, not clarity.

Minto's fix is a four-part opening that builds the question in the listener's head so that your answer arrives as a resolution rather than an announcement. It takes about twenty seconds.

SCQA · situation · complication · question · answer
① SITUATION     something they already agree with
                "We've been with the same courier for four years."
                ⇒ must be uncontroversial. This is not information,
                  it is common ground. If they nod, it worked.

② COMPLICATION  what changed, or what is now wrong
                "Since the March packaging change, our damage
                 rate on their route has tripled."
                ⇒ this is where the tension enters. One sentence.

③ QUESTION      the question now live in their head
                "So: do we fix it with them, or move?"
                ⇒ often left implicit in speech. Say it out loud
                  when the room is distracted or the stakes are
                  high — it forces everyone onto the same question.

④ ANSWER        your position — and now it lands
                "I think we move, and I'd start the tender
                 this month."

⭐ THE WHOLE THING IS FOUR SENTENCES AND ABOUT 20 SECONDS.
  Then the pyramid: "Two reasons —" and you are away.

⇒ WHY IT WORKS: you have manufactured the question that your
  answer answers. An answer to a question the listener is
  actually holding is heard completely differently from an
  answer to nothing.

WHEN TO USE IT
  ✓ writing to someone senior who lacks your context
  ✓ opening a meeting you called
  ✓ a recommendation that will be resisted
  ✗ a quick verbal answer — too slow, use the skeleton
    from file 01 §07 instead
The most common SCQA mistake

Putting real information in the Situation. The Situation must be something the listener already knows and would nod at — its entire job is to establish shared ground so the Complication has something to disrupt. If your Situation contains news, the Complication has nothing to push against and the structure collapses into an ordinary list of facts.

Part II · Speaking

Out loud, in real time

Written structure is easy — you can revise. The skill is producing the structure live, under pressure, in the two seconds after someone asks you something.

Answer First 05

The "two things" opener

This is the single most useful verbal habit in this library, and it does two entirely different jobs at once.

Announcing the shape before you have the content
THE MOVE
  "Two things —"        then say the first one
  "Three quick things." then say them
  "One thing, then a question."

JOB ONE · IT MAKES YOU EASY TO FOLLOW
  The listener now has a container and a length. They know
  when you will be finished, which means they stop half-
  listening for an exit and start actually listening.

JOB TWO · IT BUYS YOU THE FIRST ITEM'S WORTH OF THINKING
  ⭐ THIS IS THE PART NOBODY NOTICES.
  You commit to "two things" while knowing only the first.
  Saying "Two things —" takes about a second, and saying the
  first one takes eight more. That is nine seconds in which
  to find the second, while sounding completely composed.

  It works because you are not stalling — you are structuring.
  Nobody can tell the difference from outside, including
  people who use the technique themselves.

⚠ THE ONE RULE: COMMIT TO A NUMBER YOU CAN LAND.
  Say "two" and produce one, and the trick reverses on you —
  everyone is waiting for a second thing that never comes.
  When genuinely unsure, "a couple of things" or "two, maybe
  three" is honest and buys the same time.

⚠ AND DO NOT SAY "TWO THINGS" AND THEN SAY FIVE. That is
  worse than no structure at all, because you promised one
  and delivered another.
Variants worth having
  • "Short answer, then the caveat —" (perfect when the honest answer needs a boundary)
  • "I'll give you the headline and you can pull on whichever bit you want." (excellent upwards; it hands them control of the depth)
  • "There's a version of this that's thirty seconds and a version that's ten minutes — which do you want?" (the scope check from file 01 §09, doing double duty as structure)
  • "One thing, then a question for you." (guarantees you end on a handback rather than trailing off)
Answer First 06

Thirty seconds, three minutes, thirty minutes

Anything worth being able to say, you should be able to say at three lengths. Not three different speeches — the same pyramid, cut at three depths. That is the point of the structure: each level down is more detail on the same skeleton, so the versions cannot contradict each other and you never have to prepare them separately.

One pyramid, three cuts — worked all the way through
THE 30-SECOND VERSION — top box + the reason count
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  "I think we should switch couriers, mainly because the
   damage rate on their main route has tripled since March
   and they've missed two remediation dates. I'd start the
   tender this month."

  ⇒ answer · one reason · next action. This is the version
    you give in a corridor, in a lift, or when someone asks
    "what's the headline?"

THE 3-MINUTE VERSION — top box + all reasons + one fact each
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  "I think we should switch couriers. Two reasons.

   First, the damage rate. It went from 1.1% to 3.4% after
   the March packaging change and it hasn't come back down —
   that's about 60k a quarter at current volume.

   Second, the response. We raised it in April, they
   committed to a fix by June, and we're now on the second
   missed date with no root cause.

   The main argument against is switching cost, which I'd
   put at one quarter of the saving — so it pays back fast.

   I'd start the tender this month. Do you want to see the
   damage numbers before I do?"

  ⇒ answer · two reasons · a number in each · the strongest
    counter-argument handled · a next step · a handback.
    This is the default. Most things are three minutes.

THE 30-MINUTE VERSION — the same shape, one level deeper
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  same top box · same two reasons · but now each reason gets
  its own supporting group: the data by route and month, what
  the warehouse team observed, the contract terms, the tender
  timeline, the migration risk, the two alternatives
  considered and why they lost.

  ⇒ NOTHING NEW IS INTRODUCED AT THE TOP. If the 30-minute
    version has a different conclusion from the 30-second
    version, you did not have a pyramid — you had a pile.

⭐ THE TEST THAT MATTERS
  Can you cut it at any of the three lengths WITHOUT
  REWRITING? If yes, it is structured. If the short version
  needs different words rather than fewer words, it is not.
Prepare the 30-second version, always

Whatever you are walking into, know your thirty-second version cold. It is what you will actually be asked for — "give me the short version" is the most common request in professional life — and it is the one people are least ready for, because preparation instinctively goes into the long version. The three-minute and thirty-minute versions can be improvised from a well-built pyramid. The thirty-second version really cannot; it has to be cut in advance.

Answer First 07

Signposting — saying where you are

In writing, structure is visible: headings, paragraphs, white space. In speech it is invisible unless you say it. Signposting is speaking the punctuation out loud.

SignpostSayWhat it does
Opening the count"Two things —"Sets the container and the length
Moving between items"That's the first. Second —"Tells them one box closed and another opened. Prevents everything blurring into one long paragraph
Changing level"One level down on that —" · "Stepping back —"Signals you are moving down or up the pyramid rather than sideways. Very strong; almost nobody does it
Flagging the caveat"The honest caveat is —"Marks the boundary of what you know as deliberate rather than as a wobble
Landing"So, net: —" · "Bottom line —"Tells them the conclusion is arriving, which pulls attention back for the sentence that matters most
Closing"That's it from me. What's your read?"Ends deliberately instead of trailing off. Trailing off undoes a good answer — see file 04
The highest-value signpost

"Stepping back —" is the one that changes how you are perceived. It signals that you know which altitude you are at and that you are choosing to change it. It is also genuinely useful when a conversation has descended into detail and someone needs to lift it — and the person who lifts it is remembered as the one who could see the whole thing.

Answer First 08

Surviving interruption

Answer-first is not primarily a clarity technique. It is a robustness technique. Its real value shows up when things go wrong — when you are cut off, when the meeting overruns, when someone's attention leaves halfway through.

WHAT AN INTERRUPTION AT 40 SECONDS COSTS YOU SUSPENSE ORDER ANSWER-FIRST ORDER ────────────── ────────────────── 0:00 context 0:00 ⭐ THE ANSWER 0:20 evidence 0:10 reason one 0:40 evidence 0:30 reason two ✂ INTERRUPTED ✂ INTERRUPTED 1:10 evidence 0:50 the caveat 1:30 THE ANSWER 1:10 next step (never said) THEY LEAVE WITH: THEY LEAVE WITH: some facts, no point the point, most of the → and a vague sense that support, and a clear you did not get to it sense you were on top of it ⇒ THE STRUCTURE IS INSURANCE. You pay a small cost in naturalness for a large reduction in variance.

Three recoveries

When you are cut off mid-item

Do not restart. Land the current box and stop: "— just to close that one: it's about 60k a quarter. Happy to come back to the second point whenever." You have finished a unit rather than abandoning a fragment, which reads as control rather than being steamrolled.

When time is called early

"Then let me give you the one-line version: I'd switch couriers, and I need a decision by Friday to hit the tender window." You always have the thirty-second cut available — that is what section 06 was for.

When the room goes off on item one

Let it. Then reclaim: "Before we move on — there was a second thing, and it's the shorter one." Because you announced two, the second one is owed to you. That is the quiet structural benefit of the count: it reserves your slot.

When you realise mid-sentence you were wrong

Say so and re-top: "Actually — scrap that, I think the real answer is X." Delivered flatly, this reads as thinking rather than floundering. Delivered apologetically it reads as collapse. Which is file 04's subject.

Part III · Writing

On the page

The same pyramid, where you get to revise — which means there is no excuse. Written communication is also where the habit is easiest to build, because you can see the structure fail.

Answer First 09

Email, Slack, and the one-pager

Three formats, one shape
EMAIL — the answer is the subject line
──────────────────────────────────────
  Subject: Recommend switching couriers — need a decision
           by Fri

  Hi ___,

  Short version: I think we should move couriers, and I
  need a yes/no by Friday to hit the tender window.

  Two reasons:
  · Damage rate tripled after March (1.1% → 3.4%), ~60k
    a quarter at current volume.
  · Two missed remediation dates, no root cause given.

  Switching cost is roughly one quarter of the saving.

  Happy to send the full analysis — say the word.
  ___

  ⭐ THE SUBJECT LINE CARRIES THE ANSWER AND THE ASK.
    Most people write "Courier update" and bury the request
    in paragraph four. The subject line is the top of the
    pyramid, and it is the only part guaranteed to be read.

SLACK — one message, answer first, detail in the thread
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  ✗ "hey — quick q"
    [30 seconds of typing indicator]
    "so I was looking at the courier data..."

  ✓ "Recommend we switch couriers — need a call by Fri.
     Damage rate tripled since March (~60k/qtr) and they've
     missed two fix dates. Detail in thread 🧵"

  ⇒ ONE MESSAGE. NEVER SPLIT A THOUGHT ACROSS FIVE PINGS —
    each one interrupts them again, and the answer arrives
    in the one they read last.

ONE-PAGER — headings ARE the pyramid
─────────────────────────────────────
  TITLE       the recommendation, as a sentence, not a topic
              ✓ "Switch couriers before the Q3 peak"
              ✗ "Courier review"
  ¶1          the answer and the ask, four lines
  HEADING 1   reason one — as an assertion, not a label
              ✓ "Damage has tripled and has not recovered"
              ✗ "Damage analysis"
  HEADING 2   reason two — same rule
  HEADING 3   "What we'd give up" — the counter-case, owned
  LAST ¶      the decision needed, from whom, by when

  ⭐ THE HEADING RULE IS THE WHOLE TECHNIQUE:
    IF SOMEONE READS ONLY YOUR HEADINGS, THEY SHOULD GET
    YOUR ARGUMENT. Headings that are topics ("Background",
    "Analysis", "Next steps") tell the reader nothing and
    waste the one thing they will definitely look at.
The heading test, applied to anything you write

Read only your headings, top to bottom. Do they form an argument? If they form a table of contents instead, rewrite each one as a claim. This single edit is the difference between a document that gets skimmed and dismissed and one that gets skimmed and acted on — and skimming is what actually happens to your documents, whatever you would prefer.

Answer First 10

Twelve rewrites

Structure is learned by rewriting, not by reading about it. Here are twelve real shapes, before and after. Read the before, cover the after, write your own, then compare.

Rewrites 1–4 · the status update
① ✗ "Just wanted to give you an update on where we are with
     the integration — we've had a few blockers this week
     and the team's been working through them, and there's
     been some back and forth with the vendor..."
  ✓ "Integration slips a week. The vendor's auth change
     landed late; we've absorbed three days of it and I
     can't absorb the rest. New date is the 22nd."

② ✗ "I don't know if this is a problem or not but I noticed
     something in the numbers that seemed a bit off?"
  ✓ "I think we have a data problem. Returns and refunds
     disagree by about 8% for October. Worth an hour to
     find out which is right before the board pack."

③ ✗ "Everything's on track!"
  ✓ "On track for the 22nd. The one thing I'm watching is
     the vendor's auth change — if that slips again we lose
     the date, and I'd know by Thursday."
     ⇒ "on track" tells your manager nothing and costs you
       credibility later. Naming the one risk is what a
       trustworthy update looks like.

④ ✗ "Sorry for the delay in getting back to you! I've been
     meaning to look at this but things have been busy..."
  ✓ "I haven't got to this yet — it's behind the migration.
     I can have it Thursday, or I can drop the migration
     for a day. Which do you want?"
     ⇒ no apology, a date, and a trade-off they can decide.
Rewrites 5–8 · the recommendation
⑤ ✗ "There are lots of options here and they all have
     pros and cons, so I've laid them out for you."
  ✓ "I'd go with option B. Two reasons — it's reversible,
     and it doesn't need Legal. A and C are in the appendix
     if you want them."
     ⇒ handing someone an unranked list of options is
       handing them your job. A recommendation with the
       alternatives attached is doing it.

⑥ ✗ "I think we should probably maybe consider possibly
     moving the deadline."
  ✓ "I'd move the deadline two weeks. If we don't, we ship
     without the reconciliation step, and that's the part
     Finance actually needs."

⑦ ✗ "This is going to be really difficult and expensive
     and I'm not sure we should do it."
  ✓ "I'd say no. It's roughly two engineer-months for a
     feature three customers asked for. If those three are
     strategic, tell me and I'll change my answer."
     ⇒ a number and a named condition under which you would
       change your mind. Both raise your credibility;
       adjectives lower it.

⑧ ✗ "As discussed in our meeting last Tuesday with the
     wider team, following on from the analysis that was
     circulated, I wanted to follow up regarding the..."
  ✓ "Following Tuesday: I recommend we cancel the pilot."
     ⇒ context is not an opening. It is a subordinate clause.
Rewrites 9–12 · the hard ones
⑨ DELIVERING BAD NEWS
  ✗ "So there have been a few developments and I want to
     walk you through the timeline of what happened..."
  ✓ "We've lost the Denver data for October. It's not
     recoverable. Here's what it affects, and here's what
     I need from you."
     ⇒ people can handle bad news. What they cannot handle
       is realising, ninety seconds in, that bad news is
       coming and they are being eased into it. The easing
       is what costs you their trust, not the news.

⑩ SAYING NO
  ✗ "I'd love to help but I'm quite stretched right now
     and I've got a lot on, although maybe I could try to
     find some time..."
  ✓ "I can't take this on this month. If it moves to
     November I can. Or if it's urgent, tell me what to
     drop."
     ⇒ a clean no with a lever. No apology, no explanation
       of your workload, no false maybe.

⑪ DISAGREEING WITH SOMEONE SENIOR
  ✗ "That's a really interesting point and I think there's
     a lot of merit in it, although I wonder whether
     perhaps we might also want to consider..."
  ✓ "I see it differently, and I might be missing context.
     My read is that the cost lands on the warehouse team
     rather than on us. What would have to be true for it
     to work the way you're describing?"
     ⇒ position stated · humility that is real rather than
       decorative · and the question from file 02 §03 doing
       the actual work.

⑫ ADMITTING YOU DO NOT KNOW
  ✗ "Um, that's a good question, I think it might be
     something to do with... I'd have to check but possibly
     it's related to..."
  ✓ "I don't know. I'll find out and come back to you
     Thursday morning."
     ⇒ four seconds, and it raises your credibility rather
       than lowering it. Why that is true, and why almost
       nobody believes it, is file 04 §05.
Answer First 11

The drills

1 · The top-box check · every message, all week

Before sending anything longer than two lines, ask: is my conclusion in the first sentence? If not, move it. Do this for one week and it stops being a check and becomes how you write.

2 · Subject lines as answers · daily

Every email subject this week states a conclusion or an ask, never a topic. "Courier review" becomes "Recommend switching couriers — decision needed by Fri". The fastest-feedback drill here: reply rates change visibly.

3 · The thirty-second cut · before each meeting

Write the thirty-second version of whatever you are bringing. Out loud, timed. If it runs over forty seconds you have not found your top box yet.

4 · "Two things" · once a day, on purpose

Use the opener at least once a day even when you do not need it. The goal is for it to arrive automatically when you do — which is precisely when you will be too rattled to remember it.

5 · The heading test · on anything over a page

Read your headings alone. Do they make the argument? Rewrite every topic-heading as a claim.

6 · Rewrite one thing you already sent · weekly

Take one message from your sent folder and restructure it answer-first. Seeing your own words improve teaches more than any example written by someone else — including the twelve above.

The minimum viable version

Drill 1 and drill 4. The top-box check fixes your writing, which is most of how people who are not in the room form their view of you. "Two things" fixes your speaking, and it is the one that also buys you thinking time — so it pays twice.

Reference

Term bank

The vocabulary from this handbook
the pyramid
Minto's structure: one governing statement on top, supported by a small group beneath it, each of which may be supported in turn. Every box summarises the boxes below it.
the top box
Your single governing sentence. Found by asking: if they hear only one sentence, which one changes what they do?
answer-first
Conclusion before reasoning. Gives the listener a container to file evidence into, and makes your point survive an interruption.
MECE
Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Items in a group must not overlap and must cover what you claimed. In practice, bound the claim with one word — "the three main reasons".
bounding vs hedging
"The three main reasons" bounds a claim — precision. "I think maybe there are possibly three" hedges a position — weakness. They look similar and land opposite.
SCQA
Situation · complication · question · answer. Four sentences that build the question in the listener's head so your answer lands as a resolution. The Situation must contain no news.
the three cuts
Thirty seconds, three minutes, thirty minutes — the same pyramid at three depths. If the short version needs different words rather than fewer, it is not structured.
signposting
Speaking the punctuation out loud: "two things", "that's the first", "stepping back", "so, net". Structure is invisible in speech unless you say it.
the heading test
Read only the headings. If they form a table of contents rather than an argument, rewrite each as a claim.
reserving your slot
The structural side-benefit of announcing a count: having said "two things", the second is owed to you and you can reclaim it after a digression.

  Cannot answer it? Then stall (file 01 §09) — do not start
  talking and hope the point arrives.

⭐ BOUND THE CLAIM, DON'T HEDGE THE POSITION
  "the three MAIN reasons" = precision
  "I think maybe possibly three" = weakness

⭐ SCQA WHEN THEY LACK CONTEXT
  situation (no news in it) · complication · question · answer
  ~20 seconds, and your answer arrives as a resolution

⭐ "TWO THINGS —"
  makes you followable AND buys nine seconds to find the
  second one. Commit only to a number you can land.

⭐ THREE CUTS OF ONE PYRAMID
  30 sec · 3 min · 30 min — same conclusion, more depth.
  Prepare the 30-second one; it is what you get asked for.

⭐ SIGNPOST OUT LOUD
  "that's the first" · "stepping back" · "so, net"
  → "stepping back" is the one that changes how you're read

⭐ HEADINGS ARE ASSERTIONS, NOT TOPICS
  ✓ "Damage has tripled and has not recovered"
  ✗ "Damage analysis"

You can now diagnose (01), open (02) and structure (03). What none of that controls is whether the room believes you — and the same sentence, delivered two ways, gets two different receptions. File 04 is the delivery layer: warmth and competence, the hedging audit that strips the apologies out of your sentences, what to do with a question you cannot answer, and how to be wrong in public without paying for it.

On sourcing. The pyramid, SCQA and MECE are Barbara Minto's, developed at McKinsey from 1963 and published as The Pyramid Principle in 1987 — the extraction is in file 07, and it is one of the few books in this library whose verdict is worth reading fully. The working-memory reasoning behind the two-or-three rule is the Miller/Cowan research cited in file 01. The signposts, the three cuts and the twelve rewrites are craft rather than research: widely used, field-tested, and to be judged by whether they work for you.
