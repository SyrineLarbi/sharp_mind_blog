Part I · The machine that writes

What is actually happening

Three topics that belong together: what the model does, what it runs on, and why what it runs on has become an energy story.

How it works 01

What a language model actually does

In one sentence

It predicts the next chunk of text, over and over, extremely well.

Think of it like…

Your phone keyboard. You type "how are" and it offers "you". That is a tiny language model, doing exactly the same job.

An LLM is the same idea, scaled up until the prediction is good enough to write a letter, explain an idea, or find a bug in code.

And here is the genuinely surprising part, which is worth stating carefully: nobody expected that predicting the next word, done well enough, would look like this. It was not designed to reason. It was designed to predict. The reasoning-like behaviour appeared as a side effect of doing prediction at enormous scale, and that remains one of the more unsettling facts in the field.

How it works · from your question to its answer
① TOKENS — text is cut into pieces
   A token is roughly a word, sometimes part of one.
   "unhappiness" might be three tokens: un + happi + ness.
   ⇒ YOU PAY PER TOKEN, and the model reads and writes in
     tokens, not letters.
   ⚠ AND THIS IS NOT FAIR ACROSS LANGUAGES. English is
     about 0.75 words per token. Arabic, Hindi and many
     others often need TWO OR THREE TOKENS PER WORD,
     because the models were trained mostly on English.
     So the same paragraph costs more, runs slower, and
     fits less well in the same window. A quiet, real
     inequality that almost nobody mentions.

② EMBEDDINGS — each token becomes a list of numbers
   A position in a space with thousands of dimensions.
   Tokens used in similar ways end up near each other.
   ⇒ the model's "meaning" is geometry. Nearness IS
     relatedness. There is nothing else in there.

③ ⭐ ATTENTION — the 2017 invention that made all this
   possible
   For every word, the model works out which OTHER words
   in the text matter for understanding it.
   Take: "the trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because
   it was too big."
   What does "it" refer to? You know instantly. Attention
   is the mechanism that lets the model connect "it" to
   "trophy" rather than "suitcase" — by weighing every
   word against every other word.
   ⇒ and it does all those comparisons AT THE SAME TIME,
     which is exactly why this runs well on GPUs and why
     the whole field took off when it did.

④ LAYERS — dozens to hundreds of those blocks, stacked
   Each one refines the representation a little further.

⑤ OUT COMES A PROBABILITY FOR EVERY POSSIBLE NEXT TOKEN
   Then one is chosen.
   TEMPERATURE controls how adventurous that choice is.
     LOW  = always take the most likely. Repeatable,
            safe, a bit flat
     HIGH = sometimes take a less likely one. Creative,
            and less reliable on facts

⑥ APPEND IT AND RUN THE WHOLE THING AGAIN
   One token at a time. That is the entire loop. What
   looks like a considered answer is this, repeated a
   few hundred times.

⭐ AND TRAINING IS TWO SEPARATE THINGS
  PRE-TRAINING  predict the next token across an
                enormous amount of text. THIS IS WHERE
                THE KNOWLEDGE COMES FROM, and it costs
                tens to hundreds of millions in compute
  FINE-TUNING   then it is shaped, with human feedback,
                to follow instructions and be useful
                ⇒ THIS IS WHERE "BEING AN ASSISTANT"
                  COMES FROM. The raw pre-trained model
                  is not an assistant. It is a
                  text-continuer.
What goes wrong
  • Hallucination is structural, not a bug. This is the single most important thing in this entry. The model produces plausible continuations. A confident right answer and a confident wrong answer are produced by exactly the same process, and the model has no internal flag distinguishing "I know this" from "this sounds right". Confidence carries no information. That is why it can invent a citation in the same tone it uses for a fact.
  • It cannot reliably count letters or do exact arithmetic, because it works in tokens, not characters or numbers. Asking how many r's are in a word is asking a question in a unit it does not perceive.
  • No memory between conversations unless something outside the model stores and re-sends it.
  • A training cutoff. It does not know recent events unless you give them to it.
  • Prompt injection, which is genuinely unsolved. Instructions and data arrive through the same channel — as text. So text hidden inside a document or a web page can act as instructions to the model reading it. There is no clean fix, because there is no separate wire for "this is data, not orders". Anyone telling you this is handled is overselling.
And the one piece of jargon worth owning · RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation. Before answering, the system searches a document store and pastes the relevant passages into the prompt. The model then answers using those.

Think of it like the difference between a closed-book and an open-book exam. Without it, the model answers from memory and fills gaps with plausible invention. With it, the relevant page is in front of it.

This is how models are given current news, company documents or private data without retraining them. If someone says "we're using our own data", this is almost always what they mean.

Say this

"It predicts the next chunk of text, over and over. The surprise wasn't that it works — it's that doing it well enough started to look like understanding."

"Hallucination isn't a bug you can patch. The same process makes the right answers and the wrong ones, and it can't tell them apart."

"Without RAG it's a closed-book exam. With RAG it's open-book."

How it works 02

GPUs, and the memory wall

In one sentence

A GPU does an enormous number of simple sums at once — and the hard part now is feeding it numbers fast enough.

Think of it like…

A CPU is a few extremely clever people. Each can do anything: read a form, make a judgement, handle an exception.

A GPU is ten thousand people who can only do arithmetic, all working at the same moment on different numbers.

For most jobs the clever few win easily. But a neural network is, underneath everything, multiplying enormous grids of numbers — and for that, the ten thousand win by a margin so large it changed the industry.

How it works, and where the bottleneck went
① A NEURAL NETWORK IS MATRIX MULTIPLICATION
   Rows of numbers multiplied by columns of numbers,
   billions of times. That is genuinely most of it.

② A GPU HAS THOUSANDS OF SMALL CORES
   All performing the same operation on different data
   simultaneously.

③ ⭐ AND THEN YOU HIT THE MEMORY WALL
   The arithmetic units can do sums far faster than
   memory can deliver numbers to them.
   So the expensive chip sits IDLE, waiting.
   ⇒ THE BOTTLENECK MOVED FROM COMPUTE TO MEMORY
     BANDWIDTH, and most commentary has not caught up.
     People still talk as if the race is about raw
     processing speed. It has not been for a while.

④ THE FIX IS HBM — HIGH BANDWIDTH MEMORY
   Instead of memory chips sitting across the board,
   connected by a narrow path: memory chips STACKED
   VERTICALLY, right beside the processor, connected by
   thousands of tiny wires running straight through the
   silicon.
   Shorter distance. Far wider pipe.

⑤ ⚠ AND HBM IS THE ACTUAL SCARCE THING
   Made by essentially three companies: SK Hynix,
   Samsung, Micron.
   Plus "advanced packaging" — physically assembling
   the logic chip and the memory stacks onto one
   substrate — which has been its own bottleneck at
   TSMC.
   ⇒ SO WHEN AI CHIPS ARE SHORT, IT IS OFTEN NOT THE
     PROCESSOR THAT IS SHORT. It is the memory next to
     it, and the machine that glues them together.
     Almost nobody reports it that way.
The numberWhat it means
Tens of thousands of chips, for weeksTo train a frontier model. This is why only a handful of organisations do it, and why the cost of entry keeps rising.
$25,000-40,000+ per acceleratorFor a top AI chip. A single rack of them costs more than a house.
700W-1,200W+ eachPower draw per chip, and rising. Hold onto this number — it is the reason the next section exists.
What goes wrong
  • Training versus inference get confused constantly. Training is teaching the model once, at enormous cost. Inference is answering a question, which is far cheaper individually — and happens billions of times. Inference is now the larger share of total compute spent, which changes where the money and the hardware design effort go.
  • Export controls target these chips specifically, by performance thresholds. Which creates an ongoing game of designing chips that fall just under the line.
  • Power per chip is rising fast, which forces liquid cooling and which is the beginning of the next entry.
Say this

"A CPU is a few geniuses. A GPU is ten thousand people who can only do arithmetic — and a neural network is arithmetic."

"The bottleneck isn't the processor any more, it's feeding it. The scarce part is the stacked memory next to the chip."

How it works 03

Data centres, and why AI became an electricity story

In one sentence

A data centre is a building full of computers, and the thing now stopping you building one is electricity.

Think of it like…

A factory where the raw material is electricity and the product is heat, plus a little computation.

That is not a joke. Essentially one hundred percent of the electricity a data centre consumes comes back out as heat. Computation does not consume energy in any permanent sense — it warms the room. So roughly half the engineering of a data centre is not computing at all. It is getting the heat out.

How it works
① RACKS OF SERVERS
   For AI work: racks of the accelerators from §02.

② POWER, WITH TWO BACKSTOPS
   Grid supply, then batteries (a UPS) to ride through
   the seconds of a dip, then diesel or gas generators
   for a real outage. The batteries exist to keep things
   alive long enough for the generators to start.

③ ⭐ COOLING, AND WHY IT CHANGED
   A traditional server rack drew about 5-10 kilowatts.
   Air could carry that away.
   AN AI RACK DRAWS 40 TO 130 KILOWATTS.
   Air physically cannot move that much heat out of that
   small a space.
   ⇒ so: LIQUID COOLING. Cold plates pressed directly
     onto the chips, or whole servers IMMERSED in a
     non-conductive fluid.
   ⇒ this is a genuine break with forty years of data
     centre design, and it is happening because of one
     number in §02.

④ PUE — THE EFFICIENCY MEASURE YOU WILL HEAR
   Total power used ÷ power that reached the computers.
   1.0 would be perfect (no overhead at all).
   Good modern sites: about 1.1-1.2.
   Old sites: 1.8 or worse — nearly as much energy on
   cooling as on computing.
The numberWhat it means
Roughly 1-2% of world electricityData centres, on common estimates, with AI pushing it up quickly. Forecasts for where it goes vary enormously and the honest position is that nobody knows.
100+ MW for one AI clusterA single large training site can draw as much as a small city. That is a scale of connection that utilities plan years ahead for.
Grid connection queues in yearsIn several regions. This, not chips, is now the binding constraint on building AI capacity.
What goes wrong
  • You cannot buy your way past a grid connection. Several announced data centres simply cannot be built where they were announced, because there is no capacity and no way to add it quickly. This is why technology companies started signing long-term nuclear power agreements and funding the restart of retired plants — a development that would have sounded absurd five years ago, and which is the clearest evidence of how binding the constraint is.
  • Water. Evaporative cooling uses a great deal of it, and in dry regions this has become a bigger local political problem than the electricity. Communities that shrugged at power use do not shrug at water use.
  • The heat is almost all wasted. A few places pipe it into district heating for homes. Most vent it to the sky, which is an enormous quantity of low-grade energy thrown away because the buildings were sited for connectivity rather than for anyone needing warmth nearby.
  • The forecasts are unreliable in both directions. Efficiency has repeatedly improved faster than predicted, and demand has repeatedly grown faster than predicted. Be suspicious of confident numbers for 2030 from anyone.
Say this

"A data centre turns electricity into heat and a little computation. Getting the heat out is half the engineering."

"The limit on AI right now isn't chips, it's grid connections. That's why tech companies are buying nuclear power."

"AI racks got too hot for air. They're liquid-cooled now — that's a forty-year break with how these buildings were designed."

Renting computers

One topic, and the most consequential business decision most companies make without understanding it.

How it works 04

The cloud, and what it actually costs

In one sentence

Renting computers by the hour instead of buying them.

Think of it like…

Taking taxis instead of owning a car.

Brilliant if you travel occasionally, at unpredictable times, to unpredictable places. You pay nothing when you are not moving, and you never think about maintenance, insurance or parking.

Ruinous if you commute the same route twice a day for three years.

Almost every horrified cloud-bill story is somebody who took taxis to work. And almost every "cloud is a scam" article is written by someone whose workload was a commute, generalising to everyone.

How it works, and where the money actually goes
① THREE LAYERS, AND THE NAMES ARE WORTH KNOWING
   IaaS  raw machines. You manage everything above
   PaaS  managed services — a database that someone else
         patches, backs up and scales
   SaaS  finished software you just log into
   ⇒ moving up the stack costs more per unit and needs
     fewer of your people. That is the actual trade.

② YOU PAY FOR THREE THINGS
   COMPUTE TIME   per second or hour a machine exists
   STORAGE        per gigabyte per month
   ⭐ DATA TRANSFER OUT — the one that surprises everyone

③ ⭐ EGRESS FEES, AND WHY THEY EXIST
   Moving data IN is usually free.
   Moving data OUT costs money, per gigabyte.
   ⇒ THIS IS NOT AN ACCIDENT OF PRICING. It makes
     leaving expensive, and it makes leaving MORE
     expensive the more successful you have been,
     because you have more data. Regulatory pressure in
     the EU has forced some of these fees down for
     customers who are actually switching provider.

④ COMMITMENT DISCOUNTS
   Promise to spend a certain amount for one or three
   years and prices drop substantially.
   ⇒ which also deepens the lock-in you just paid for.

⑤ THREE PROVIDERS DOMINATE
   AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud.
   ⚠ and a single REGION going down takes a
     startling share of the internet with it, several
     times a year. Everyone is resilient inside one
     provider and nobody is resilient across them.
What goes wrong
  • Nobody understands the bill. Hundreds of line items, per-second pricing, dozens of service names. An entire job — "FinOps" — exists purely because organisations cannot tell what they are spending money on. That is a remarkable thing to be true of a utility.
  • Most of a typical bill is things nobody is using. Machines left running, over-provisioned databases, storage from a project that ended two years ago. The first cloud cost review at any company usually finds a large double-digit percentage of pure waste.
  • The honest summary, which neither side gives you: cloud is genuinely cheaper for workloads that are variable, unpredictable, small, or growing fast. It is usually more expensive for workloads that are steady, predictable and large. Both camps are describing their own situation and assuming it generalises. A few well-known companies have moved back to their own hardware and reported large savings — and their workloads were the steady, predictable, large kind.
Say this

"Cloud is taxis instead of a car. Great if you travel sometimes. Expensive if you commute."

"Getting your data in is free. Getting it out costs. That's not an accident."

"Most of a cloud bill is usually things nobody is using."

How it works 05

Encryption, and what quantum computers would break

In one sentence

Encryption scrambles a message so only the intended reader can unscramble it — and quantum computers threaten one specific half of how that is set up.

Think of it like…

There are two separate problems, and almost every confused conversation about encryption is someone mixing them up.

Problem one — the safe. You and I share a secret key. I lock the message, you unlock it. Straightforward, fast, and it has worked for thousands of years.

Problem two — the padlock. You and I have never met. We are shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear. How do we agree on a secret key, in public, without anyone listening learning it?

That second problem sounds impossible. Solving it in the 1970s is what made the internet possible — and it is the half that quantum computing threatens.

How it works
① SYMMETRIC ENCRYPTION — the safe
   One shared key locks and unlocks. The standard is AES.
   Fast, and it does the actual work of scrambling your
   data.

② ASYMMETRIC / PUBLIC-KEY — the padlock
   TWO keys, mathematically linked.
   Your PUBLIC key you give to everyone. Anyone can use
   it to lock a message to you.
   Your PRIVATE key you never share. Only it can unlock.
   ⇒ built on problems that are EASY ONE WAY AND HARD
     THE OTHER. Multiplying two enormous prime numbers
     is easy. Taking the answer and finding which two
     primes made it is, with ordinary computers,
     effectively impossible.

③ IN PRACTICE YOU USE BOTH
   Use the slow asymmetric method ONCE, to agree a
   shared symmetric key. Then use the fast symmetric
   method for the actual conversation.
   ⇒ that is what the padlock icon in your browser is
     doing, every time, in about a tenth of a second.

④ HASHING IS A THIRD, DIFFERENT THING
   A one-way fingerprint. You cannot reverse it.
   Used for storing passwords and checking a file has
   not been altered. NOT for hiding a message — there is
   nothing to unhide.

⭐ NOW THE QUANTUM PART
  A large enough quantum computer, running Shor's
  algorithm, breaks step ② — RSA and elliptic curve —
  not by brute force but by a fundamentally different
  method that ordinary computers cannot use.
  ⚠ BUT IT BARELY TOUCHES STEP ①. The best known
    quantum attack on symmetric encryption roughly
    halves the effective key length, so AES-256 remains
    comfortably safe.
  ⇒ SO THE HONEST SENTENCE IS: QUANTUM THREATENS THE KEY
    EXCHANGE, NOT THE BULK ENCRYPTION. Almost all
    reporting says "quantum breaks encryption", which is
    wrong in a way that matters.

⭐⭐ AND HERE IS WHY IT IS URGENT DESPITE NO SUCH MACHINE
  EXISTING YET — "HARVEST NOW, DECRYPT LATER"
  Someone can record your encrypted traffic TODAY,
  store it, and decrypt it in fifteen years when the
  machine arrives.
  ⇒ so anything that must stay secret for decades —
    state secrets, medical records, identity documents —
    IS ALREADY AT RISK, right now, today.
  ⇒ which is why NIST standardised post-quantum
    algorithms in 2024, and why browsers and messaging
    apps have already started deploying them alongside
    the old ones. The migration is happening before the
    threat, on purpose.
What goes wrong
  • The maths is almost never what fails. Real systems are broken through implementation bugs, reused keys, bad random number generation, expired certificates and stolen credentials. Nobody has factored a large number to get into a bank; plenty of people have phished a password.
  • Key management is the actual hard problem everywhere. Generating, storing, rotating and revoking keys, at scale, without losing any. This is unglamorous and it is where the difficulty lives.
  • Migration is slow and touches everything — every browser, server, payment terminal, passport chip and car. It will take a decade and it has started.
Say this

"Encryption has two halves: agreeing a secret key, and using it. Quantum threatens the first one, not really the second."

"Harvest now, decrypt later. People are recording encrypted traffic today to read it in fifteen years — which is why the migration is happening before the machine exists."

"Real systems are almost never broken by the maths. They're broken by a stolen password."

How it works 06

Ransomware

In one sentence

Criminals encrypt your files and sell you the key.

Think of it like…

Someone changing the locks on your house while you are out, and then selling you the new key.

They have not taken anything. Everything you own is still in there. They have simply made it unreachable — which turns out to be just as effective and far easier than stealing it.

How an attack actually runs — and it is slower than people imagine
① GET IN
   Phishing, a stolen password, or an unpatched system
   facing the internet.
   ⭐ INCREASINGLY: THEY BUY THE WAY IN. "Initial access
     brokers" are specialists who break in and sell the
     foothold to whoever wants it. This is a supply
     chain with division of labour.

② MOVE AROUND QUIETLY
   Often for days or weeks. Escalate privileges. Map the
   network. Find where everything important lives.

③ ⭐ FIND AND DESTROY THE BACKUPS. FIRST.
   This is the step that decides whether you pay.
   An attacker who has your backups has your business.
   ⇒ which is why backups must be OFFLINE or IMMUTABLE —
     on media that cannot be altered even by someone
     with full administrator rights.

④ STEAL THE DATA
   Copy it out before doing anything visible.

⑤ THEN ENCRYPT EVERYTHING, AND LEAVE THE NOTE
   The visible part of the attack is the last part, and
   by then it is essentially over.

⭐ DOUBLE EXTORTION — why good backups stopped being enough
  Pay once to get the decryption key.
  Pay again so we do not publish what we stole.
  ⇒ THIS CHANGED THE WHOLE CALCULATION. Perfect backups
    solve the encryption problem completely and do
    nothing at all about the leak. An organisation with
    excellent backups can still be facing the publication
    of its customers' data.

⚠ AND IT IS AN INDUSTRY, NOT A PERSON
  "Ransomware as a service": developers build the
  malware and rent it to affiliates for a share of the
  proceeds. There are brand names, reputations,
  negotiation teams, and customer support — because a
  group known for not supplying working keys stops
  getting paid.
What actually defends against it
  • Multi-factor authentication on everything reachable from the internet. An enormous share of intrusions begin with a password alone. This is the single highest-value control and it is boring.
  • Backups that are offline or immutable — and tested. Most organisations discover their backups do not restore during the incident. An untested backup is a belief, not a backup.
  • Patch internet-facing systems fast. The gap between a vulnerability being published and being exploited at scale is now often days.
  • Segment the network so that one compromised laptop is not the entire estate.
  • On paying: it often does not fully work — decryption is slow and incomplete — it funds the next attack, and depending on who the group is it may be illegal. A great many organisations pay anyway, because the alternative is going out of business. It is an easy thing to have a firm opinion about from outside.
Say this

"They don't steal your files, they lock them. And the first thing they do is kill your backups."

"Double extortion changed everything. Good backups stop the encryption problem and do nothing about the leak."

"They're usually in your network for weeks before anything visible happens."

Part IV · You, digitised

Your money and your body, as data

Two systems you use constantly and have probably never had explained.

How it works 07

Digital payments

In one sentence

Moving money is mostly messaging. The money itself moves later, in bulk.

Think of it like…

When you tap your card, no money moves.

A message goes out saying, in effect: this account is good for thirty euros, and we have set that amount aside. Everyone acts on the message. The actual money moves the next day, in one large batch, together with everyone else's.

The speed you experience is the speed of the message. It has almost nothing to do with the speed of the money.

One card payment, step by step
① YOU TAP. THE TERMINAL ASKS FOR AUTHORISATION.
② IT TRAVELS THROUGH THREE PARTIES
   the ACQUIRER   the shop's payment processor
   the NETWORK    Visa, Mastercard
   the ISSUER     your bank
③ YOUR BANK CHECKS FUNDS AND RISK, SAYS YES, AND PLACES
   A HOLD
   All of that in about one to two seconds.
④ CLEARING AND SETTLEMENT HAPPEN LATER
   Usually the next day, in batches. THIS is when money
   genuinely moves between banks.

⭐ INTERCHANGE — where your cashback comes from
  The shop pays a fee on every card transaction. Most of
  that fee goes to YOUR bank, not to Visa.
  ⇒ that is what funds your points, your cashback and
    your airport lounge.
  ⇒ AND IT IS CAPPED BY LAW IN THE EU AND NOT IN THE US.
    Which is the entire reason American card rewards are
    lavish and American merchant fees are high, and
    European rewards are meagre and European merchant
    fees are low. Same system, one regulation, opposite
    outcomes. It is one of the cleanest examples of
    policy shaping an everyday experience.

⭐ AND THE NEWER RAILS, WHICH SKIP ALL OF THIS
  UPI in India. Pix in Brazil. SEPA Instant in Europe.
  FedNow in the US.
  Bank to bank, in seconds, at very low or zero fee —
  bypassing the card networks entirely.
  ⇒ Pix went from nothing to the majority of Brazilian
    transactions in a few years. Where a central bank
    built the rail and mandated participation, adoption
    was extremely fast. This is the most significant
    thing happening in payments and it gets a fraction
    of the attention that cryptocurrency does.

TOKENISATION — why paying by phone is safer
  Your phone does not hold your card number. It holds a
  device-specific token that is useless anywhere else.
  Stolen in a breach, it buys nothing.
What goes wrong
  • Card-not-present fraud — online, without the physical card — is the bulk of card fraud. Extra verification steps help, and every step of friction also loses genuine sales, so there is a constant tension between the fraud team and the sales team.
  • Chargebacks. In a dispute the merchant usually loses, and pays a fee on top. Small merchants carry a risk they rarely understand when they start.
  • Cross-border remittances remain expensive — frequently in the range of 5-7% — charged disproportionately to migrant workers sending money home. This is one of the clearest cases where the technology has existed for years and the cost has not fallen, because the difficulty is regulatory and commercial rather than technical.
  • Going cashless excludes people — the unbanked, some elderly people, anyone without a smartphone. Countries that moved fastest have had to legislate to keep cash accepted.
Say this

"When you tap your card, no money moves. A message moves. The money follows the next day, in a batch."

"Your cashback is paid by the shop, through interchange. It's capped in Europe and not in America — that's the whole difference."

"The big change in payments isn't crypto. It's Pix and UPI — bank to bank, instant, nearly free."

How it works 08

Biometrics

In one sentence

Using your body as your password.

Think of it like…

A password you can never forget — and can never change.

Both halves of that matter, and people only ever repeat the first one. It is wonderfully convenient for as long as it holds, and if it is ever compromised it is compromised permanently. You cannot issue yourself a new fingerprint.

How it works
① ENROLMENT — and it does NOT store a picture
   The sensor captures your fingerprint or face, then
   extracts a TEMPLATE: a mathematical summary of
   distinctive features. The original image is
   discarded.
   ⇒ so a stolen template is not a photograph of your
     face. It is also not nothing.

② MATCHING
   Capture again, extract again, compare the two
   templates.

③ ⭐ AND IT IS NEVER AN EXACT MATCH
   Your finger is at a slightly different angle, drier,
   colder. The two templates are always a little
   different.
   So the system uses a THRESHOLD: close enough counts.

④ ⭐⭐ WHICH MEANS YOU ARE CHOOSING BETWEEN TWO ERRORS
   FALSE ACCEPT  the wrong person is let in
   FALSE REJECT  the right person is refused
   Tighten the threshold and you get fewer false
   accepts and MORE false rejects. Loosen it and the
   reverse.
   ⇒ THERE IS NO SETTING THAT ELIMINATES BOTH. Ever. A
     bank and a phone choose opposite ends of this dial,
     deliberately, and both are correct.
   ⇒ this is the single most useful thing to understand
     about biometrics, and it applies to every detection
     system there is, including the medical AI in
     file 13.

⑤ THE TWO KINDS OF FACE RECOGNITION ARE NOT THE SAME
   Phone unlock projects thousands of infrared dots to
   build a 3D depth map — so a photograph fails.
   Cheap 2D face recognition from an ordinary camera is
   very much easier to fool, and is what most public
   surveillance systems use.

⑥ ⭐ AND THE DISTINCTION THAT MATTERS MOST
   ON-DEVICE  your phone keeps the template in a
              dedicated secure chip, and it never leaves
              the handset. Not even to the manufacturer
   CENTRAL    a government or company holds millions of
              templates in one database
   ⇒ THESE HAVE COMPLETELY DIFFERENT RISK PROFILES AND
     ARE BOTH CALLED "BIOMETRICS". Most public argument
     conflates them, and the conflation usually serves
     whoever is making the argument.
What goes wrong
  • You cannot revoke a fingerprint. A leaked password is changed in thirty seconds. A leaked biometric database is permanent, for everyone in it, for the rest of their lives.
  • Spoofing is an arms race. Lifted prints, printed masks, and now deepfaked video against remote identity checks. "Liveness detection" improves, attacks improve, and neither side finishes.
  • Demographic bias is documented, not theoretical. Large-scale testing has repeatedly found error rates differing substantially by skin tone, age and sex across many systems. It has improved considerably and has not disappeared — and the consequence of a false match is not evenly distributed either.
  • Function creep. A system built for one purpose gets used for another, and the second purpose was never debated. This is the normal life cycle of an identity database, not an aberration.
  • Coercion. You can refuse to give up a password. A finger can be pressed to a sensor while you are asleep or unconscious, and in several jurisdictions the legal protection for a biometric is weaker than for something you know.
Say this

"A biometric is a password you can't change. That's the whole trade."

"Your phone's fingerprint never leaves your phone. A central government database is a completely different thing with the same name."

"There's no threshold that prevents both false accepts and false rejects. You only get to choose which error you'd rather have."

The words, in plain words

When they say…They mean
TokenA piece of a word. Models read, write and charge in these.
Context windowHow much text the model can look at in one go. Like a desk: put a new page on and an old one falls off.
TemperatureHow adventurously it picks the next word. Low = safe and repeatable. High = creative and less reliable.
HallucinationConfidently wrong. Not a malfunction — the same mechanism that produces correct answers.
RAGSearching documents and pasting the relevant bits into the prompt before answering. Closed-book exam → open-book exam.
Fine-tuningFurther training to change behaviour. RAG gives knowledge; fine-tuning gives manner. People mix these up constantly.
InferenceUsing a trained model to answer. As opposed to training, which is building it.
ParametersThe numbers inside the model that were adjusted during training. More is roughly more capable and definitely more expensive to run.
Open weightsThe trained numbers are published, so anyone can run it themselves. Often loosely called "open source", which is not quite the same thing — the training data usually is not released.
Prompt injectionHiding instructions inside content the model reads, so it obeys them. Unsolved.
LatencyDelay before a response starts. Distinct from throughput, which is how much you get per second.
Zero-dayA vulnerability with no patch available yet. Named for how many days the defender has had.
MFA / 2FANeeding more than a password. The single most effective ordinary security control there is.
End-to-end encryptedOnly the two people talking can read it — not even the company carrying the messages.
EgressData leaving. The part of a cloud bill nobody budgets for.

  A language model sounds exactly as certain when it is
