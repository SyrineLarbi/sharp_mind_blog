Part I

How a medicine works at all

Chapter 01

Lock and key, and why side effects are unavoidable

Almost every medicine works by binding to something in the body — a receptor, an enzyme, a channel — and either switching it on, blocking it, or getting in the way of something else that would have bound there. Shape is what determines where a molecule goes.

Which immediately explains side effects, and explains why they are not a sign of a badly made drug. The same target usually exists in more than one place, and no molecule fits only one shape perfectly. A drug that calms an overactive process where you want it calmed will also reach everywhere else that process happens. Side effects are the drug doing exactly what it does, somewhere you did not need it.

Three further consequences follow, and they cover most of what confuses people about taking medicines.

Dose is everything. Too little does nothing; too much overwhelms the systems that clear it. The useful range between those is called the therapeutic window, and it is wide for some drugs and alarmingly narrow for others — which is why some medicines are sold freely and others require monitoring.

Timing is not arbitrary. A drug is cleared continuously, mostly by the liver and the kidneys, and dosing intervals are calculated to keep the amount in the useful range. Skipping and then doubling up does not average out.

"With food" means something specific — usually either that food slows absorption to avoid a spike, or that the drug irritates the stomach lining. "On an empty stomach" usually means food blocks absorption. Neither is a suggestion.

Chapter 02

The painkillers, and the one that is genuinely different

The common over-the-counter painkillers are not interchangeable, and knowing which does what is genuinely useful.

Paracetamol — acetaminophen in some countries — treats pain and fever and does essentially nothing for inflammation. It is gentle on the stomach, which is why it is the usual first choice, and it is the single most dangerous thing in most medicine cupboards for the reason in the box below.

Ibuprofen and the related anti-inflammatories treat pain, fever and inflammation, which makes them better for injuries, swelling, period pain, dental pain and arthritis. The cost is that they irritate the stomach lining and can cause bleeding with regular use, they strain the kidneys, and they are a poor idea for people with certain heart, kidney or stomach conditions, or during much of pregnancy. Taking them with food helps a little; taking them for weeks on end without advice does not.

Aspirin is in the same family with an additional effect on blood clotting, which is why low doses are prescribed long-term to some people for entirely different reasons. It should not be given to children because of a rare but severe liver and brain complication.

Two practical notes that matter more than the choice. Paracetamol and ibuprofen work by different mechanisms and can generally be used together or alternated — a genuinely useful thing to know for bad pain. And for most acute pain, taking a proper dose promptly works better than waiting until the pain is severe, because it is easier to prevent than to catch up with.

The paracetamol problem — the most important paragraph in this volume

Paracetamol is extremely safe at the right dose and has an unusually narrow margin above it. Moderate excess — not a dramatic overdose, just consistently a bit too much over a couple of days — can cause severe liver damage.

The danger is that it is hidden in a great many combination products: cold and flu remedies, hot lemon sachets, night-time medicines, some prescription painkillers. Taking a cold remedy and paracetamol together, both as directed, can exceed the limit without either packet being misused. Read every packet for it before combining anything.

And the part that kills people: the symptoms are delayed. Someone can take a dangerous amount, feel more or less fine for a day, and have irreversible liver damage underway. There is an antidote and it works best early. Suspected excess is an immediate medical matter regardless of how well the person feels. Alcohol makes all of this considerably worse.

Chapter 03

Antibiotics, and why the advice changed

Antibiotics kill bacteria. They do nothing whatsoever to viruses — so they do nothing for colds, influenza, most sore throats, most coughs and most sinus infections, which are overwhelmingly viral. Taking one anyway carries the side effects with none of the benefit, and contributes to the larger problem.

That problem is straightforward and serious. Bacteria vary; a few survive any given exposure; those few reproduce. Resistance is evolution happening on a timescale you can watch, and it is driven by every unnecessary course taken anywhere, including in farmed animals. The endpoint is routine infections and routine surgery becoming dangerous again, which is the world described in Volume 28 before antibiotics existed.

Two pieces of practical advice worth updating. The instruction to always finish the course has softened — for several common infections the evidence now favours shorter courses, and the right answer is what your prescriber specifies for that infection rather than a universal rule. Do not stop early on your own judgement, and do not assume longer is safer either.

And never take leftover antibiotics, or someone else's. The drug must match the organism, the dose must match the infection, and a partial course from last year is the ideal way to breed something resistant without treating anything.

On the side that does affect you immediately: antibiotics disrupt the useful bacteria in the gut, which is why they commonly cause digestive upset, and they can interact with other medicines — including reducing the effectiveness of some contraceptives, which is worth asking about rather than assuming.

Chapter 04

Interactions, generics, storage and dates

The practical housekeeping, briefly, because each item causes real problems.

Interactions happen when one substance changes how another is absorbed or cleared. The liver processes many drugs through the same machinery, so anything that speeds it up or slows it down changes the level of everything else. Grapefruit is the famous example and it is not a myth — it blocks one of those pathways and can substantially raise the level of a long list of medicines, for many hours. Alcohol is the most common and most underestimated interaction: with sedatives and strong painkillers it is genuinely dangerous, and with paracetamol it compounds the liver risk. And herbal products are not exempt — St John's wort in particular accelerates the clearance of a wide range of drugs, including contraceptives and several essential medications. Tell your doctor and pharmacist everything you take, including supplements and herbal remedies.

Generics contain the same active ingredient at the same dose and must demonstrate that they behave the same way in the body. The differences are in the inactive ingredients, the shape and the price. The confusion they cause is real and worth guarding against: the same drug appears under several names, and taking a branded and a generic version of the same thing is a straightforward way to double a dose by accident.

Storage. Heat, light and humidity degrade medicines, which makes the bathroom cabinet — the warmest, dampest room in the house — the worst possible location. A cool, dry, dark place out of children's reach is the answer.

Expiry dates mark the point up to which full potency is guaranteed. For most solid tablets, modest expiry means gradually reduced strength rather than danger. For liquids, eye drops, anything refrigerated, antibiotics, insulin and adrenaline injectors, the date should be respected strictly — and for anything where reduced potency would itself be an emergency, there is no argument at all. Dispose of old medicines through a pharmacy rather than the bin or the toilet.

Part II

Judging a claim

Chapter 05

Why bad treatments feel like they work

This is the foundation, and it has nothing to do with the treatment. Four things conspire to make anything at all look effective.

Most things get better anyway. Colds end, backs settle, headaches pass. Whatever you took shortly beforehand receives the credit, and a treatment that does nothing at all will appear to work in the great majority of self-limiting conditions.

You try remedies at your worst. Which is precisely when regression to the mean from Volume 39 guarantees improvement. This is the single most powerful generator of false belief in ineffective treatments, and it is invisible from inside.

The placebo effect is real, and more interesting than "it is all in your head". Expectation genuinely reduces perceived pain and produces measurable physiological changes. What it does not do is shrink a tumour or clear an infection. It is strongest exactly where symptoms are subjective — pain, nausea, fatigue, mood — which is exactly where alternative remedies claim their successes.

You remember the hits. The times it worked are stories; the times it did not are forgotten, or explained by not having used it properly. This is the availability bias and the confirmation bias from Volume 35, operating on your own body.

Put together, these mean that personal experience cannot distinguish an effective treatment from an ineffective one. That is not a slight on anyone's intelligence — it is why controlled trials had to be invented, and why the entire apparatus of comparison groups and blinding exists.

Chapter 06

What makes evidence good

You do not need to read studies to use this. You need to know what the words in the headline imply.

A comparison group is the minimum. Without one, you cannot tell the treatment from the four effects in the last chapter.

Random allocation is what stops the healthier, wealthier or more motivated people ending up in one group. If people chose their group, any difference may be about them rather than the treatment.

Blinding — neither the participant nor ideally the assessor knowing who got what — stops expectation affecting both the experience and the measurement.

Size and duration. Small samples produce extreme results, exactly as Volume 39 described. Short trials cannot see long-term harms.

Replication is the one that matters most and gets the least coverage. A single striking study is a hypothesis. A finding that several independent groups reproduce is knowledge. A great many famous results have failed to replicate, and the failure rarely makes the news.

The hierarchy, roughly: a systematic review pooling many good trials sits at the top; a single large randomised trial below it; observational studies below that, because they show association and cannot establish cause; then animal and laboratory work, which is where most exciting headlines actually come from; and anecdote at the bottom, however vivid.

Chapter 07

Reading a health headline

A short set of questions handles almost everything you will meet.

Was it in people? An enormous share of "X causes cancer" and "Y cures Z" stories are cells in a dish or mice. Findings in those settings frequently fail in humans, and the headline rarely says which it was.

From what, to what? The relative-risk question from Volume 39. A doubled risk of something that affects one person in a hundred thousand is a different story from a doubled risk of something common. Any figure given only as a percentage change is incomplete.

Correlation or cause? Observational studies find associations, and the honest language is "associated with" or "linked to". When a headline converts that into "causes", the conversion was done by the journalist. Ask what else might explain it — people who do X often also do Y, and often differ in wealth, age and health to begin with.

Who paid, and who is selling? Funding does not make a result wrong, and it does predictably shift what gets studied and what gets published. A study funded by an industry with a stake warrants the extra scrutiny you would apply to any interested testimony.

Does one study overturn a field? Almost never. When a single result contradicts a large body of work, the prior probability is that the single result is wrong.

Chapter 08

Supplements and alternative remedies

The honest position has three parts, and most arguments about this go wrong by holding only one.

Some supplements have real evidence for specific people. Vitamin D where sunlight is limited, folic acid before and during early pregnancy, iron in diagnosed deficiency, B12 for those who do not eat animal products, and specific clinical situations. These are genuine and worth taking seriously.

Most supplements, in people who are not deficient, do nothing. Large trials of multivitamins and antioxidants in well-nourished populations have repeatedly failed to show benefit, and several have found harm at high doses — beta-carotene in smokers being the most sobering example. More is not better; the dose principle from Volume 41 does not stop applying because something is marketed as natural.

The regulation is much lighter than for medicines. In most places supplements need not demonstrate effectiveness before sale, and testing has repeatedly found products containing less than stated, more than stated, or substances not on the label at all.

On alternative medicine specifically, the useful distinction is between unproven and disproven. Some herbal preparations have real pharmacological activity — which also means real interactions and real side effects, and being plant-derived confers no safety. Some practices have modest evidence for subjective symptoms, consistent with the mechanisms in Chapter 05. And homeopathy is a special case that deserves naming plainly: the preparations are diluted past the point where any original substance remains, its claimed mechanism contradicts basic chemistry, and it has been tested extensively with no effect beyond placebo.

The real harm is rarely the remedy itself. It is delay — treatable conditions becoming untreatable while something ineffective is tried — and money, and interactions that nobody knew about because the patient did not think a herbal product counted as medicine.

Reference

What to carry forward

The cupboard

Paracetamol for pain and fever; ibuprofen when inflammation is involved; they work differently and can be combined. Paracetamol is hidden in cold and flu remedies, its margin is narrow, and the symptoms of excess are delayed by a day — check every packet before combining.

Antibiotics

Useless against viruses, which is most coughs, colds and sore throats. Resistance is evolution you can watch. Never use leftovers or someone else's, and follow the course your prescriber specified rather than a universal rule.

Interactions and housekeeping

Tell them everything you take, herbal included — St John's wort and grapefruit both alter drug levels substantially. Alcohol is the most underestimated interaction. Store cool, dry and dark, not in the bathroom.

Judging claims

Personal experience cannot distinguish an effective treatment from an ineffective one — things improve anyway, you try remedies at your worst, expectation is real, and you remember the hits. So ask: in people or in mice? From what, to what? Correlation or cause? Has anyone replicated it?
