Sharp Mind · Public Health & Society · Topic 09 of 20

Health inequality. Your postcode predicts your lifespan better than your doctor does.

In almost every country, richer people live longer than poorer people, and they spend more of those years in good health. That much sounds obvious. The surprise is the shape of the pattern. It is not a line between the poor and everyone else. It is a slope that runs all the way up the social ladder, so that even people in comfortable middle positions die earlier than those one step above them. Explaining that slope, and deciding what to do about it, is one of the central problems of public health.

Question 1 · What is it?

Differences in health that follow social position

Health inequality means systematic differences in health between groups of people, linked to income, education, job, ethnicity, gender or place. Not every difference counts. The ones that matter are those society could change.

Chapter 01

The civil servants who proved the slope

In 1967 researchers in London began following about 18,000 male British civil servants. These were not poor men. They all had steady jobs, offices, pensions and access to the free National Health Service. The study, led for many years by the epidemiologist Michael Marmot, became known as the Whitehall study, after the street where many government departments sit.

The result was striking. Men in the lowest grades, such as messengers and doorkeepers, had death rates roughly three times higher than men in the top administrative grades over the following years. But the finding that changed the field was the middle. Each grade had worse health than the grade above it, all the way to the top. Clerical officers did worse than executives. Executives did worse than senior administrators. None of them was poor, and none lacked a doctor. Something about rank itself was linked to health.

A second study, Whitehall II, started in 1985 and included women. It found the same slope, and pointed to one important ingredient: how much control people had over their work. Those with low control, high demands and little say had more heart disease, even after accounting for smoking, blood pressure and cholesterol.

Chapter 02

Inequality, inequity and the social gradient

Three terms need to be separated. A health inequality is any measurable difference in health between groups. Some are unavoidable: older people have more disease than younger people. A health inequity is a difference that is avoidable and unfair, such as poorer children having more tooth decay because their area has no dentist. Many writers use "inequality" for both, but the distinction matters for policy, because only inequities are a target.

The third term is the social gradient: the step-by-step pattern in which health improves with each rise in social position. It means health inequality is not only about poverty. Helping only the poorest would shrink one part of the gap and leave the slope in place.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The causes of the causes

Doctors treat heart attacks, lung cancer and diabetes. Public health asks why those diseases cluster in some groups. The answer usually lies several steps back from the clinic.

Chapter 03

The social determinants of health

The social determinants of health are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age. A well-known diagram by Göran Dahlgren and Margaret Whitehead, from 1991, draws them as layers around a person, like the rings of an onion.

The key point is that the layers are connected. Smoking is an individual habit, but who smokes is shaped by stress, by advertising, by what friends do, by price and by how hopeful people feel about the future. Behaviour is real, but it is not free-floating; it grows out of circumstances. Public health experts sometimes call these deeper factors "the causes of the causes".

Chapter 04

Four pathways from status to illness

Researchers usually describe several routes, which work together.

Material conditions

Damp housing, polluted air, dangerous jobs, cheap calorie-dense food, less safe space to exercise. These act directly on the body. Children in cold, mouldy homes have more asthma. Workers in manual jobs have more injuries.

Behaviour

Smoking, heavy drinking, poor diet and inactivity are more common lower down the social ladder in most rich countries. In England, smoking alone explains a large share of the gap in deaths between richest and poorest areas.

Chronic stress and control

Insecure work, debt and low control keep the body's stress systems switched on. Over years this "wear and tear", sometimes called allostatic load, is linked to raised blood pressure, inflammation and heart disease. Whitehall II suggested this route is important.

Access to care

Poorer groups often get less and later care, even where it is free. They may struggle to take time off work, travel, or navigate the system, and services are often thinnest where need is greatest.

How much each pathway contributes is debated, and the answer differs by country and disease. A figure often quoted is that medical care explains only 10 to 20% of differences in health outcomes, with the rest due to behaviour, social conditions, environment and genes. Such figures are rough and disputed, but almost all researchers agree that health care alone cannot close the gap. Britain is the proof: the NHS has been free at the point of use since 1948, and the gradient is still steep.

Chapter 05

The inverse care law

In 1971 Julian Tudor Hart, a family doctor in a Welsh mining village, wrote a short paper in The Lancet with a phrase that stuck. "The availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served." He called it the inverse care law. Areas with the most illness had the fewest doctors, the oldest buildings and the most overworked staff. Areas with the least illness had the most.

The law still holds in many places. Doctors prefer to work where life is pleasant and patients are easier to treat. Private markets follow money, not need. Where public systems pay by headcount rather than by need, deprived areas are under-served. Market forces in health care, left alone, tend to widen gaps, because care flows towards people who can pay or complain. Tudor Hart argued this happens most where care is most exposed to the market, and less where it is publicly planned. Healthcare systems in general are covered in How healthcare systems work.

Chapter 06

How inequality is measured

Measuring a gap sounds simple. In practice, the choice of measure can change the story.

The absolute gap is the simple difference: if the poorest group has 20 deaths per 1,000 and the richest 10, the gap is 10. The relative gap is the ratio: the poorest group's rate is twice the richest. Now suppose both rates halve, to 10 and 5. The absolute gap shrinks to 5. The relative gap stays at two. Has inequality fallen? By one measure yes, by the other no. Honest reports give both.

Analysts also count healthy life expectancy, the years lived in good health. Gaps here are usually larger than gaps in total lifespan. In England, people in the most deprived areas can expect close to two decades fewer years in good health than those in the least deprived, according to official statistics. They live shorter lives and spend more of them ill.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Lost years, lost money, and a question of fairness

Health inequality costs lives, strains public budgets and shapes politics. It also raises an ethical question that every society answers, whether it admits it or not.

Chapter 07

Deaths of despair

In 2015 the economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton published a finding that surprised many. Death rates among middle-aged white Americans without a university degree had stopped falling around the turn of the century and started to rise, while they kept falling in other rich countries. The increase came from drug overdoses, suicide and alcohol-related liver disease. Case and Deaton called them deaths of despair.

Their explanation, set out in a 2020 book, was that the loss of stable, well-paid jobs for people without degrees had eroded not just income but status, marriage, community and meaning. The opioid epidemic, fuelled by aggressive marketing of prescription painkillers, then turned that despair into deaths. Their account has been debated, especially on how much is economic and how much is about drugs and supply, but the core data are not in dispute. US life expectancy fell for several years in the late 2010s, something rich countries had rarely seen outside war or pandemic. A widening social gap can show up first in the death statistics.

Chapter 08

COVID-19 as a stress test

The pandemic revealed existing inequalities sharply. In many countries, death rates were higher in poorer areas and in some ethnic minority groups. The reasons were mostly structural. People in these groups were more likely to work jobs that could not be done from home, such as care work, transport and food retail. They were more likely to live in crowded housing, often across generations. And they had more of the conditions, such as diabetes and heart disease, that made COVID-19 more dangerous. The virus did not discriminate; society had already sorted people by exposure and vulnerability. The pandemic itself is the subject of Pandemics.

Chapter 09

The economic case and the moral case

The economic argument is that ill health among working-age people means lost output, lost tax, higher benefit spending and higher healthcare costs. Governments that set out to reduce health gaps often frame it this way, because it appeals across party lines.

The moral argument is older and divides people more. One view, associated with Marmot and the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health (whose 2008 report was titled Closing the Gap in a Generation), is that avoidable health differences are a matter of social justice. If a child born in one neighbourhood can expect to live a decade less than a child born a few kilometres away, that is a fact about how society is organised, not about the child.

Another view puts more weight on personal responsibility and freedom. It agrees that children should not be penalised for their birth, but argues that adults' choices about smoking, food and drink are their own, and that heavy state intervention in them is paternalistic. Most real policy sits between these positions. The question is rarely whether to act, but how far the state should reach into how people live.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What has worked, and what has not

Many countries have tried to narrow health gaps. A few strategies have clear successes. Others have shown how stubborn the gradient is.

The phrase proportionate universalism comes from the 2010 Marmot Review for England, Fair Society, Healthy Lives. It tries to combine the two traditions: keep services universal, so everyone has a stake in them, but give more to those with greater need.

Chapter 11

England's experiment, and good health at low cost

England ran one of the most ambitious national strategies to narrow health gaps, from the late 1990s to about 2010, following an earlier government inquiry, the Black Report of 1980. It set targets for reducing gaps in life expectancy and infant mortality. The results were mixed. Some studies found the gaps narrowed modestly in the targeted areas in the later years. Others noted that headline targets were missed. Since then, gaps in England have widened again, and in the 2010s improvements in life expectancy stalled, especially for women in poorer areas. The causes of the stall, including austerity policies, are still argued over.

Meanwhile, a 1985 conference titled Good Health at Low Cost studied places such as Sri Lanka, Costa Rica, the Indian state of Kerala and China, which had achieved high life expectancy on low incomes. The common threads were investment in girls' education, primary health care, and broad political commitment to basic services for all. Money matters, but how a society spends it, and on whom, matters more.

Chapter 13

The live debates

Three questions will shape the next decade. The first is whether overall inequality itself damages health. In their 2009 book The Spirit Level, Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett argued that more unequal rich countries have worse health and social problems across the board, not just among the poor. Critics questioned their choice of countries and methods. The debate about whether inequality harms health beyond its effect through poverty remains open.

The second is how to treat the new drugs, such as the GLP-1 medicines for obesity (see GLP-1 drugs). Obesity is more common in poorer groups in rich countries. If these drugs reach everyone, they could narrow a major gap. If only the well-off can afford them, they could widen it.

The third is about who measures what. Inequality hidden in national averages stays invisible. Better data, broken down by area, income and ethnicity, is a condition for any progress. A gap that is not measured is a gap that nobody is responsible for. The wider social picture is in Poverty and Social class.

The core idea

Health follows a social gradient: each step up in income, education or status brings longer, healthier life. The causes lie mostly outside the clinic, in housing, work, stress, habits and place, which is why universal health care narrows but does not close the gap.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US: about 15 years between the life expectancy of the richest and poorest 1% of men (Chetty, 2016).
  • Glasgow: a gap of about 28 years between two areas (WHO, 2008).
  • Between countries: around 30 years separate the highest and lowest life expectancy.
The common mistake

Thinking health inequality is only about the very poor. The gradient runs through the middle, so fixing only the bottom leaves most of the slope in place.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the Whitehall civil servants, all employed and all with free health care, still showed a health gradient? If you can, you understand why health inequality is a social problem, not only a medical one.
