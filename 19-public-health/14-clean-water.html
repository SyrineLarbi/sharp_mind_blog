Clean water. The tap is the end of a long chain, and every link can fail.

Turning on a tap and drinking without a second thought is one of the great achievements of public health. It depends on protected sources, treatment plants, thousands of kilometres of pipes, constant testing, and institutions that pay for all of it. More than two billion people still do not have water they can fully rely on. And even in rich countries, the chain sometimes breaks, in ways that show how much trust we place in a system we never see.

Question 1 · What is it?

Water that is safe, close, and there when needed

"Clean water" in public health means drinking water free from harmful germs and chemicals, and also available close to home, in enough quantity, whenever people need it.

Chapter 01

Two cities, one river, one epidemic

In 1892 cholera struck Hamburg, in Germany. In a few weeks it killed about 8,600 people. Next door was the town of Altona, which was then a separate town in Prussia. It took its water from the same river, the Elbe, and from further downstream, where the water was dirtier. Yet Altona had very few cases. The difference was that Altona passed its water through slow sand filters before supplying it. Hamburg did not. In some streets that ran along the border, houses on the Hamburg side had cholera and houses on the Altona side did not.

The scientist Robert Koch, who had identified the cholera bacterium a few years earlier, investigated. The contrast became one of the clearest demonstrations ever that treating drinking water prevents epidemics. Hamburg built a filtration plant soon afterwards.

The story echoes John Snow's work in London in the 1850s, told in Volume 28. Snow also compared households supplied by two different water companies, one drawing water from a polluted stretch of the Thames and one from further upstream. Deaths from cholera were many times higher among customers of the first. Both stories make the same point. The source and the treatment of water decide who lives.

Chapter 02

The drinking water ladder

As with sanitation, the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme ranks drinking water on a ladder.

RungWhat it meansExample
Safely managedAn improved source on the premises, available when needed, and free from contaminationA tested piped supply in the home
BasicAn improved source within a 30-minute round trip, including queuingA public standpipe or protected borehole nearby
LimitedAn improved source more than 30 minutes awayA protected well an hour's walk away
UnimprovedAn unprotected well or springAn open well with no cover
Surface waterDirectly from a river, lake, pond or canalCollecting water from a stream

In the early 2020s, about 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water, and more than 100 million still drank directly from surface water. Note that "improved" is not the same as "safe". A borehole or a tap can still be contaminated. That is why the top rung requires testing. Access to a source is not the same as access to safe water.

Distance matters because someone has to carry the water. In most households without water at home, that person is a woman or girl. The hours spent fetching water are hours not spent at school or at paid work. A family needs a minimum of around 20 litres per person per day for drinking, cooking and basic hygiene, according to WHO guidance. Twenty litres weighs twenty kilograms.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Multiple barriers, from catchment to cup

A safe water system does not rely on any single step. It stacks several barriers so that if one fails, others still protect people.

Since 2004, WHO has recommended that water suppliers draw up water safety plans. Instead of only testing water at the end, a supplier maps every point from source to tap where something could go wrong, and puts a control on each. It is the same logic used in food safety and aviation: manage risks before they become failures, rather than discovering them afterwards.

Chapter 04

Chlorine, and how big its effect really was

In 1908 Jersey City, in the US, became one of the first cities to add chlorine continuously to its public water supply. It was controversial: a chemical added to everyone's drinking water. Within a few decades chlorination was standard across rich countries, and deaths from typhoid in cities fell dramatically.

How much of the early twentieth century's fall in deaths it caused is a genuine research debate. A much-cited 2005 study by the economists David Cutler and Grant Miller estimated that clean water technologies, filtration and chlorination, explained nearly half of the fall in total death rates in major US cities between 1900 and 1936, and most of the fall in infant deaths. Later studies using more data have found smaller effects, while still finding real benefits, especially against typhoid. Everyone agrees chlorination saved huge numbers of lives; researchers disagree on how large its share was compared with other changes.

Chapter 06

When people cannot rely on the tap

Where piped water is unsafe or absent, families can treat water at home. Boiling kills germs but uses fuel and time. Chlorine tablets or liquid are cheap and leave protective residual chlorine. Ceramic and other filters remove most bacteria and parasites. Solar disinfection, known as SODIS, leaves clear plastic bottles of water in the sun for several hours so that ultraviolet light and heat kill germs.

Studies find that household treatment can reduce diarrhoea, but there is a catch. People must use it every day, consistently, which many do not, and some of the most positive early results came from studies where people knew they were being observed. A method that relies on daily effort from every family is weaker than a system that delivers safe water to the tap. Household treatment is a valuable bridge, not a destination.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

When the chain breaks

The value of a water system is clearest when it fails. Three disasters, in very different places, show different kinds of failure.

Chapter 07

Bangladesh: solving one problem, creating another

From the 1970s, aid agencies and the government of Bangladesh drilled millions of shallow tube wells, so that people could drink groundwater instead of germ-laden surface water from ponds and rivers. It worked against diarrhoea. But in the 1990s, tests showed that the groundwater in large parts of the country contained naturally occurring arsenic at dangerous levels. Tens of millions of people had been drinking it for years. A 2000 paper in the WHO's own Bulletin described it as the largest mass poisoning of a population in history.

The lesson was painful. The wells had been tested for germs, not for arsenic, because nobody expected it. A safe-looking water source can carry a hidden chemical danger, and water programs now test for local chemical risks as well as germs. Many wells have since been tested and painted red or green, and deeper wells or other sources have been provided, but exposure has not been eliminated.

Chapter 08

Flint and Walkerton: failures in rich countries

In 2014 the city of Flint, Michigan, under state-appointed emergency management to cut costs, switched its water source to the Flint River. The new water was more corrosive, and the city did not add the corrosion-control treatment that would have protected its old pipes. Lead leached from pipes into homes. Residents complained for months about discoloured, foul water and were told it was safe. Lead levels in children's blood rose. An outbreak of Legionnaires' disease, a severe pneumonia, linked by many investigators to the water switch, killed at least twelve people. The city switched back in 2015. Several officials faced criminal charges, though most were later dropped, and the state agreed a large legal settlement with residents.

Flint is now a byword for two things. One is technical: old lead pipes are safe only as long as water chemistry keeps them coated. The other is about trust and inequality. Flint is a poor, majority-Black city, and an official investigation concluded that residents' concerns were dismissed for too long. That connects to Health inequality.

Fourteen years before Flint, in 2000, the small town of Walkerton, in Ontario, Canada, suffered an outbreak after manure-contaminated water entered a well and the operators failed to chlorinate properly and falsified records. Seven people died and about 2,300 fell ill. The inquiry that followed led Ontario to rebuild its water safety rules around the multiple-barrier approach. In rich countries, water disasters are usually failures of institutions, not of technology.

Chapter 09

Guinea worm: a disease beaten with water alone

Guinea worm disease is caught by drinking water containing tiny water fleas that carry the larvae of a parasitic worm. About a year later, a worm up to a metre long emerges painfully through the skin, usually on the leg. There is no vaccine and no drug. In 1986, when the Carter Center, the organisation founded by former US President Jimmy Carter, began leading a global eradication campaign, there were an estimated 3.5 million cases a year in Africa and Asia.

The campaign used simple tools: cloth and pipe filters to strain the water, education about not entering water sources while a worm is emerging, and safe wells. By the mid-2020s, cases had fallen to around a dozen or so a year. If it is completed, it would be the second human disease eradicated, after smallpox, and the first without a vaccine or medicine. Clean water, by itself, can beat a disease.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Who runs the taps, who pays, and what goes in

Water systems raise hard questions about ownership, pricing and what, if anything, should be added to everyone's water.

Chapter 10

Real cases show the stakes. In Cochabamba, Bolivia, in 2000, a private concession raised water prices sharply and triggered mass protests, known as the Water War, and the contract was cancelled. England and Wales privatised their water industry in 1989; in the 2020s it faced heavy criticism over sewage spills, high debts and dividends, and the near-collapse of the largest company, Thames Water. Paris, by contrast, took its water back into public management in 2010 after a century of private contracts. Supporters of each model can point to successes elsewhere. The evidence suggests that strong regulation and steady investment matter more than who owns the pipes.

Chapter 11

The fluoride debate

Since Grand Rapids, Michigan, began in 1945, many countries have added small amounts of fluoride to public water to reduce tooth decay. The US, Australia, Ireland and parts of the UK fluoridate much of their water. Most of continental Europe does not, often using fluoridated salt or toothpaste instead. Public health bodies have long described community fluoridation as safe and effective at the recommended level, around 0.7 milligrams per litre in the US.

The debate has sharpened. Reviews in the 2010s and 2020s suggested that the benefit is smaller today than in the past, because fluoride toothpaste is now widespread. A 2024 US government review concluded, with moderate confidence, that fluoride at levels above 1.5 milligrams per litre, about twice the US recommended level, is linked to lower IQ in children. It did not reach a conclusion about the lower levels used in fluoridation. In 2025 two US states, Utah and Florida, banned community fluoridation. Supporters point to decades of evidence of benefit at low doses and to the risk of more tooth decay among poor children with less access to dentists. Opponents raise consent and the uncertainty about long-term effects. It is a genuinely contested question about a small benefit and a disputed risk, and countries have reached different answers.

Chapter 13

Water for health, water for everything else

Drinking water is a small share of all the water humans use. Farming takes about 70% of freshwater withdrawals worldwide, and industry and energy much of the rest. When supplies tighten, drinking water competes with irrigation, power stations and factories. Getting that balance right is a question for Climate adaptation as much as for public health.

Two newer ideas may shape the future. One is water reuse: treating wastewater to drinking standard. Singapore produces a large share of its supply this way, under the brand NEWater, and Windhoek in Namibia has done so since 1968. The main barrier is not technology but public acceptance, sometimes called the "yuck factor". The other is smart networks, with sensors that detect leaks and contamination in real time, which could cut both water losses and response times.

The core lesson stays the same as in Hamburg in 1892. Clean water is not a product you buy once; it is a system that must be run, paid for and trusted every day.

The core idea

Clean water is a system of barriers: protected sources, treatment, sealed pipes, testing and safe storage. It must be safe, close and reliable. Germs are the biggest danger worldwide, but chemicals such as arsenic and lead cause hidden, long-term harm. Most failures are failures of institutions, not technology.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water in the early 2020s.
  • Basic needs: around 20 litres per person per day (WHO guidance).
  • Guinea worm: from about 3.5 million cases in 1986 to around a dozen a year by the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Assuming a well or a tap means safe water. Access is not safety: Bangladesh's wells were free of germs but full of arsenic, and Flint had taps that delivered lead.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Altona escaped the 1892 cholera epidemic while Hamburg did not, and what Flint teaches that Hamburg does not? If you can, you understand both the technology and the institutions behind clean water.

The words, in plain words
Safely managed drinking water
Water from an improved source at home, available when needed and free from contamination.
Improved source
A water source protected from outside contamination by its design, such as a piped supply, borehole or protected well.
Surface water
Water taken directly from rivers, lakes, ponds or canals.
Multiple-barrier approach
Protecting water with several independent safeguards, so one failure does not cause harm.
Water safety plan
A supplier's map of every risk from source to tap, with a control for each.
Chlorination
Adding chlorine to water to kill germs, leaving a small amount to protect it in pipes.
Slow sand filter
A bed of sand through which water passes slowly, removing germs and particles.
Corrosion control
Adjusting water chemistry so it does not dissolve metal, such as lead, from pipes.
Arsenic
A natural element found in some groundwater that causes cancer and other disease after long exposure.
PFAS
Long-lasting industrial chemicals, called "forever chemicals", found in many water supplies.
Fluoridation
Adding a small amount of fluoride to public water to reduce tooth decay.
Water reuse
Treating wastewater so it can be used again, including for drinking.
