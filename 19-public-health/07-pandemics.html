Pandemics. Not a question of whether the next one comes, but of how ready we are when it does.

A pandemic is an outbreak that crosses the whole world. It begins with something tiny, often a virus jumping from an animal to one person, and it can end up closing schools on every continent. Pandemics have shaped history more than most wars. They are also surprisingly predictable in outline, even when they are unpredictable in detail. The same arithmetic, the same hard choices and the same human reactions appear again and again, from the Black Death to COVID-19.

Question 1 · What is it?

An epidemic that has gone global

The word describes how far a disease has spread, not how deadly it is. A mild disease can cause a pandemic, and a deadly one may never become one.

Chapter 01

From outbreak to pandemic

Public health uses a ladder of words to describe how widely a disease is spreading.

TermMeaningExample
EndemicAlways present in a region at a fairly steady, expected levelMalaria in parts of sub-Saharan Africa; the common cold
OutbreakMore cases than expected, usually in one placeFood poisoning after one wedding
EpidemicA larger rise in cases across a community or regionThe West African Ebola epidemic of 2014–2016
PandemicAn epidemic spreading across many countries and continentsInfluenza in 1918; COVID-19 from 2020

Under the International Health Regulations, a treaty updated in 2005, the WHO can declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC). This is a formal alarm that obliges countries to coordinate. The WHO declared COVID-19 a PHEIC on 30 January 2020 and described it as a pandemic on 11 March 2020. It ended the emergency status in May 2023. Amendments agreed in 2024 added a higher alarm level, a "pandemic emergency".

Note the important point: "pandemic" is about geography, not severity. The 2009 H1N1 influenza was a pandemic but killed fewer people than many expected. Ebola is far deadlier per case, but it has caused regional epidemics, not pandemics.

Chapter 03

Where new diseases come from

Most new human diseases come from animals. A disease that passes from animals to humans is a zoonosis, and the moment it first jumps is called a spillover. Researchers commonly estimate that around three-quarters of emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic. HIV came from chimpanzees and other primates in central Africa. Influenza strains circulate in wild birds and pass through pigs and poultry. The 2003 SARS virus and the MERS virus, first identified in 2012, are coronaviruses linked to bats, with other animals such as civets and camels acting as intermediate hosts.

Spillovers happen more often where people and animals meet in new ways: forests cleared for farming, wildlife traded in markets, huge numbers of farm animals kept close together. In 2024 an H5N1 bird flu spread among dairy cattle in the United States, a reminder that viruses keep finding new hosts. Most spillovers go nowhere. A pandemic needs a germ that can also spread efficiently from person to person, and that combination is rare, which is why pandemics happen every few decades rather than every year.

The origin of COVID-19 itself remains contested. One view holds that the virus spilled over from animals at or near a market in Wuhan, China. Another holds that it may have escaped from a research laboratory in the same city. Scientists, governments and intelligence agencies have reached different conclusions, and as of 2026 there is no consensus, partly because key early data have not been made available.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The arithmetic of spread, and the tools to bend it

A few numbers decide how hard a pandemic will be to control. The tools to fight it all work by changing those numbers.

Chapter 04

R, and the threshold for herd immunity

Volume 28 explains the reproduction number, R: the average number of people each infected person infects. Above one, cases grow; below one, they shrink. The starting value in a population with no immunity is called R0 ("R nought"). Seasonal flu has an R0 of roughly 1.3. The original COVID-19 virus was around 2 to 3. Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known, with an R0 often quoted between 12 and 18.

R0 leads to a useful formula. To stop sustained spread, enough people must be immune that each case infects fewer than one other. The share needed is roughly 1 − 1/R0. For a disease with R0 of 2, about half the population must be immune. For measles, with R0 around 15, it is over 90 percent. That is why measles returns as soon as vaccination rates slip a few points, as Vaccination programs explains.

R is not fixed by the germ alone. It also depends on how people behave, how crowded they live, and how many are immune. Every pandemic measure, from handwashing to vaccines, is an attempt to push R below one.

Chapter 05

Why some germs are much harder to stop

The 2003 SARS outbreak infected around 8,000 people and killed about 800 before it was contained, within months. COVID-19, caused by a closely related virus, could not be contained. Why?

The key difference was timing. People with SARS became most infectious only after they were clearly ill, so isolating sick people broke the chains of infection. People with COVID-19 could pass the virus on before they had symptoms, or while having very mild ones. By the time anyone knew they were ill, they had often already infected others. A germ that spreads before symptoms appear is far harder to stop than one that spreads after.

A second factor is superspreading. In many outbreaks, most infected people infect no one or very few, while a small number infect many, often at crowded indoor events. This means that the average R hides a lot of variation, and that targeting crowded, poorly ventilated settings can have an outsized effect. A third factor is severity. A germ that kills or disables quickly may limit its own spread. One that leaves most people only mildly ill keeps moving.

Chapter 06

The toolkit before vaccines

When a new germ appears, there is no vaccine and usually no proven drug. Countries must rely on non-pharmaceutical interventions: measures that do not involve medicines.

Find and isolate

Testing to find cases, isolation of the sick, contact tracing to find the people they may have infected, and quarantine of those contacts. This is precise and works best early, when cases are few.

Reduce contact for everyone

Closing large gatherings, schools or workplaces, working from home, and, in the strongest form, orders to stay at home ("lockdowns"). These are blunt and costly, used when cases are too many to trace.

Make each contact safer

Masks, ventilation, distance, hand hygiene, and meeting outdoors. Each reduces the chance of transmission a little; together they can add up.

Control borders

Travel screening, quarantine for arrivals, or closures. These can delay spread, buying time, but rarely stop a highly transmissible germ for long.

Countries that acted early and decisively, with strong testing and tracing, often kept deaths low in the first year of COVID-19. But the right mix depends on the germ, the stage of the outbreak, and what a society can sustain. The aim of early measures is usually to buy time: to protect hospitals and to wait for vaccines and treatments.

Chapter 07

Vaccines and drugs at pandemic speed

Before COVID-19, developing a new vaccine typically took many years. In January 2020, Chinese scientists published the genetic sequence of the new virus. Within days, companies had designed candidate vaccines, and the first was authorised in the United Kingdom in early December 2020, less than a year later. Several technologies reached the finish line, including mRNA vaccines (explained in Volume 13) and viral vector vaccines.

This speed came from decades of earlier research, huge public funding, and running trial stages and manufacturing in parallel rather than one after another. Safety steps were not skipped; what was removed was waiting time between them. The vaccines greatly reduced severe illness and death. One modelling study estimated they prevented many millions of deaths in their first year, though such estimates depend on assumptions. Treatments improved too: cheap steroids such as dexamethasone were shown in a large UK trial in 2020 to reduce deaths among patients needing oxygen.

The weak point was distribution. Rich countries secured most early doses, and many poorer countries waited months longer. Science moved at record speed; fairness did not.

Chapter 08

How pandemics end

Pandemics rarely end with a clear date. Most often, the germ does not disappear but settles into an endemic pattern. Enough people gain immunity, through infection, vaccination or both, that the disease becomes a familiar, recurring illness rather than an emergency. The 1918 flu virus's descendants circulated as seasonal flu for decades. COVID-19 has followed a similar path.

A few diseases can be eliminated, and one, smallpox, has been eradicated worldwide, with the last natural case in 1977. That required a vaccine, a germ with no animal reservoir, and an enormous global campaign. For most pandemic germs, the realistic end is living with them at a level health systems can manage. Historians sometimes add that pandemics also end socially, when people decide the emergency is over, which may come before or after the medical end.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Counting the dead, and everything else

The toll of a pandemic is larger than its death certificates, and it falls unevenly.

Chapter 09

Excess deaths: the honest count

Reported deaths depend on testing and on how death certificates are filled in. In places with little testing, many COVID-19 deaths were never recorded as such. So researchers use excess mortality: the number of deaths from all causes, compared with how many would normally be expected for that time of year.

Excess deaths include people who died of the disease without a test, and people who died because health services were overwhelmed or avoided. They also subtract deaths that did not happen, for example fewer road deaths during lockdowns. The WHO estimated about 14.9 million excess deaths worldwide in 2020 and 2021, roughly three times the COVID-19 deaths officially reported for those years. Excess mortality is the best single measure of a pandemic's full impact, though it too carries uncertainty where death registration is weak.

Chapter 10

The costs beyond death

The global economy shrank sharply in 2020. Hundreds of millions of jobs were lost or reduced, at least for a time, and tens of millions of people fell back into extreme poverty. Schools closed for months in many countries, and studies of learning found significant losses, larger for poorer children. Delayed operations and cancer diagnoses created backlogs that lasted years. Some people developed long COVID, symptoms lasting months after infection, whose frequency and causes are still being studied.

The burden was unequal. People in crowded housing and in jobs that could not be done from home, such as care work, transport and food production, were more exposed. Older people and those with chronic illnesses faced the highest risk of death. In many countries, poorer neighbourhoods and some ethnic minorities had higher death rates. Pandemics magnify the inequalities a society already has, a theme of Health inequality.

There was also an information crisis. The WHO used the word "infodemic" for the flood of rumours, false cures and conflicting claims. Trust in health authorities, already uneven, became sharply divided along political lines in some countries, with effects on later vaccination programmes.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every measure saves something and costs something

Pandemic decisions are made fast, with poor information, and their costs fall on different people. Reasonable people still disagree about many of them.

Chapter 12

What went right and what went wrong with COVID-19

What went right

The virus was sequenced and shared within weeks. Vaccines arrived in under a year and prevented enormous numbers of deaths. Large, simple trials, such as the UK's RECOVERY trial, quickly identified treatments that worked and others that did not. Scientists shared data openly at unprecedented speed. Wastewater and genomic surveillance matured into lasting tools.

What went wrong

Many countries had plans that existed on paper but lacked tests, protective equipment and tracing capacity. Early warnings were slow. Care homes were badly protected in several countries. Vaccines reached poor countries late. Messages were sometimes inconsistent, and uncertainty was presented as certainty, which damaged trust when advice changed. The balance of costs and benefits of long restrictions remains disputed.

A useful lesson from preparedness reviews before 2020: several rich countries had ranked highly on pandemic-readiness indexes and still did poorly. Having a plan was less important than having capacity, fast decisions and public trust.

Chapter 14

What preparedness actually means

Pandemic preparedness sounds abstract. In practice it is a list of unglamorous things that must exist before the crisis: laboratories that can scale up testing, stockpiles of protective equipment that are kept in date, public health teams trained in tracing, hospitals with surge capacity, vaccine factories that can switch production, legal powers that are clear in advance, and data systems that talk to each other.

The hardest part is political. Preparedness costs money every year to guard against something that may not happen for decades. After each pandemic, attention and funding rise, then fade. Historians call this the cycle of panic and neglect. The 1918 pandemic was quickly forgotten. Lessons from SARS in 2003 shaped strong responses in parts of East Asia but were ignored elsewhere.

The best predictor of how a country handles the next pandemic may be whether it keeps paying for readiness when no one is afraid.

The core idea

A pandemic is an epidemic that has spread worldwide. Most begin as spillovers from animals. Their course depends on R0, on whether people spread the germ before symptoms, and on superspreading. Early tools buy time; vaccines and immunity usually turn the pandemic into an endemic disease.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Herd immunity threshold ≈ 1 − 1/R0: about 50% for R0 = 2, over 90% for measles.
  • COVID-19: about 7 million reported deaths; about 15 million excess deaths in 2020–2021 (WHO estimate).
  • First COVID-19 vaccine authorised in under a year (December 2020).
The common mistake

Thinking "pandemic" means "very deadly". It describes how far a disease has spread, not how severe it is.
