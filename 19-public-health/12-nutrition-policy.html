Nutrition policy. Changing what millions of people eat without asking each of them to try harder.

Telling people to eat better rarely works on its own. Most people already know that vegetables are good and sugary drinks are not. What they eat is shaped by price, habit, advertising, time, and what is on the shelf in front of them. Nutrition policy works on those forces instead of on willpower. Some of its tools are so quiet that you have never noticed them, such as the iodine in your salt. Others, such as taxes on sugary drinks, start fierce political fights. Today the same countries often face both hunger and obesity at once.

General education

This file explains public policy, not personal diet. Individual dietary needs, especially in pregnancy, childhood or illness, are a matter for a doctor or registered dietitian.

Question 1 · What is it?

Public action on what people eat

Nutrition policy is any deliberate government action meant to improve diets across a population. It ranges from adding vitamins to flour to taxing soft drinks and feeding children at school.

Chapter 01

The goitre belt and the cheapest fix in history

In the early twentieth century, large parts of Switzerland, the American Midwest and many other inland regions had a visible problem. Many people had a goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck. Some children were born with severe mental disability, a condition then called cretinism. The cause was a lack of iodine, a mineral that the thyroid needs and that is scarce in the soil of many mountain and inland areas far from the sea.

The fix was simple. In the 1920s Switzerland and then the US began adding small amounts of iodine to table salt. Goitre rates fell sharply within a generation. From the 1990s, WHO and UNICEF pushed universal salt iodisation worldwide, and today most of the world's households use iodised salt. Iodine deficiency is still the leading preventable cause of brain damage in children worldwide, but it is far rarer than it was.

Salt iodisation shows nutrition policy at its best. Nobody had to change their habits. The healthy choice became the default choice. Most people never knew it had happened.

Chapter 02

The two faces of malnutrition

"Malnutrition" means bad nutrition in either direction. Undernutrition covers not getting enough energy or protein (leading to wasting and stunting in children) and micronutrient deficiencies, sometimes called hidden hunger, where people have enough calories but lack iron, iodine, vitamin A or zinc. Overnutrition covers overweight, obesity and diets high in salt, sugar and unhealthy fats, which raise the risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes and some cancers.

Many countries now have both at once, which WHO calls the double burden of malnutrition. The same town can have stunted children and obese adults. Even the same household can. As incomes rise and diets shift to cheap processed food, countries move from hunger to obesity, often passing through a stage where both are common.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A ladder of tools, from gentle to forceful

Policies range from giving information, to changing what is sold, to changing what it costs, to banning things outright. Each step is more effective and more controversial.

Chapter 03

The intervention ladder

A British ethics body, the Nuffield Council on Bioethics, described public health policy in 2007 as a ladder. The higher the rung, the more it restricts choice, and the stronger the justification needed.

Chapter 04

Fortification: changing food without changing habits

Fortification means adding vitamins or minerals to common foods. Iodised salt is one example. Others include vitamin D in milk or margarine against rickets, iron in flour against anaemia, and vitamin A in cooking oil or sugar in parts of Latin America and Africa.

The best-studied example is folic acid in flour. Folate, a B vitamin, taken around conception, sharply reduces the risk of neural tube defects, such as spina bifida, in which a baby's spine or brain does not form properly. But many pregnancies are unplanned, and the critical window is in the first weeks, before many women know they are pregnant. So supplements alone reach too few women in time. The US made folic acid fortification of flour mandatory in 1998. Neural tube defects fell by roughly a quarter to a third. More than 60 countries now require it. The UK debated it for years and decided in 2021 to follow, with the rule due to take effect around 2026.

Biofortification is a newer approach: breeding crops that are naturally richer in nutrients, such as orange-fleshed sweet potatoes high in vitamin A, or iron-rich beans. It reaches rural families who grow their own food and never buy fortified flour.

Chapter 05

Feeding programs: school meals and food aid

School meals have a long history. In Britain, the poor health of men volunteering for the Boer War around 1900 alarmed the government, and in 1906 Parliament allowed local authorities to provide school meals. The US created its National School Lunch Program in 1946, partly on similar grounds of national fitness. Today India runs one of the largest school feeding programs in the world, giving a cooked midday meal to more than 100 million children. Brazil requires a share of its school food budget to be spent on food from local family farms.

School meals do several jobs at once: they feed children, encourage attendance, especially for girls, and support local agriculture. The evidence for effects on attendance and learning is fairly good. The evidence for effects on growth is weaker, because by school age much stunting has already happened. School meals help children learn, but the first thousand days decide growth. See Child health.

For poor households, governments provide food or money. The US Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP, formerly food stamps) gives eligible families electronic credit to buy food. India runs a vast public distribution system of subsidised grain. Brazil's Bolsa Família gives cash to poor families on condition that children attend school and health checks. Cash is increasingly favoured over food handouts where markets work, because it is cheaper to deliver and lets families buy what they need.

Chapter 06

Taxes, labels and reformulation

For overnutrition, the main tools target the food environment. Three have the best evidence.

Sugary drink taxes

Mexico introduced a tax of one peso per litre in 2014, about 10% of the price. Purchases of taxed drinks fell, more among poorer households. Dozens of countries and cities now have such taxes.

Warning labels

Chile's law of 2016 put black octagon "HIGH IN" warnings on packaged foods high in sugar, salt, saturated fat or calories, and banned marketing them to children and selling them in schools. Purchases of the labelled drinks fell markedly.

Reformulation

Making manufacturers change recipes. The UK's salt reduction program from the early 2000s set targets for bread, soups and other foods, and average salt intake fell, though by less than the targets.

The UK's Soft Drinks Industry Levy, introduced in 2018, was cleverly designed. It taxed manufacturers by the sugar content of their drinks, in tiers, and was announced two years ahead. Many companies cut sugar in their recipes before the tax started, so they would pay less or nothing. The tax worked mainly by changing the product, not the price. The amount of sugar sold in soft drinks fell substantially.

Two more interventions have strong evidence on specific nutrients. Trans fats, industrially made fats used in margarine and fried foods, raise heart disease risk. Denmark limited them in 2003. The US effectively banned them in a process completed in 2018. WHO launched a plan called REPLACE in 2018 to eliminate them worldwide. And Finland's North Karelia project, started in 1972 in a region with one of the world's highest rates of heart disease, combined community campaigns, food industry change and later national policy. Heart disease deaths fell dramatically over the following decades, though how much was due to the project itself and how much to wider trends is debated.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Diet is now a leading cause of death

Bad diets cause more ill health worldwide than tobacco, according to major global studies. And the food system is shaped by policy whether governments admit it or not.

Chapter 07

The scale of diet-related disease

The Global Burden of Disease study, a large international research effort, estimated in a 2019 paper that poor diet was linked to about one in five adult deaths worldwide, mainly through heart disease. The biggest problems it identified were not only too much sugar or fat, but too much salt and too little whole grain, fruit, nuts and vegetables. Such estimates depend on modelling and have wide uncertainty, but they place diet among the largest health risks there are.

Diet-related disease is expensive. Diabetes, heart disease and obesity-related illness consume a growing share of health budgets in rich and middle-income countries. Obesity is covered as a condition in Obesity. Policy matters because individual advice has a weak track record against a food environment designed to sell more food. When the cheapest, most advertised and most convenient food is the least healthy, telling people to choose better is pushing against a current.

Chapter 08

Famines are political

Hunger, at its worst, is famine. The economist Amartya Sen, who lived through the Bengal famine of 1943 as a child, showed in his work that many famines happened while there was enough food in the country. People starved because they lost the means to get it, through falling wages, rising prices or lost harvests, while food moved elsewhere. He also argued that no major famine had occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that must face voters and newspapers cannot ignore mass starvation.

Sen's insight changed food policy. It shifted attention from total food supply to people's access to food: incomes, prices, jobs and safety nets. Modern famines and near-famines, in places such as Somalia, Yemen, Sudan and Gaza in recent decades, have been closely linked to conflict and blocked aid, not only to drought.

Chapter 09

Industry, evidence and trust

Food is a huge industry, and industries lobby. In 2016 researchers published internal documents showing that in the 1960s a sugar industry group had paid Harvard scientists to write a review that played down the role of sugar in heart disease and emphasised fat. The episode is often cited as a warning about conflicts of interest. Industry groups have fought sugar taxes, labels and marketing limits in many countries, sometimes successfully. Denmark introduced a tax on saturated fat in 2011 and abolished it about a year later, after complaints about costs, cross-border shopping and administrative burden.

Nutrition science itself has made public trust harder. Much of it relies on observational studies, which link diets to outcomes but cannot easily prove cause and effect. Advice on fat, eggs, salt and other topics has shifted over the decades. One of the few strong experiments came in 2019, when Kevin Hall and colleagues at the US National Institutes of Health fed volunteers either an ultra-processed or an unprocessed diet for two weeks each. On the ultra-processed diet, people ate about 500 more calories a day and gained weight. Small, careful trials like this are rare and valuable, and policy often has to act on imperfect evidence.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Nanny state or necessary state?

Every rung up the intervention ladder trades some freedom of choice for some health gain. Reasonable people weigh that trade differently.

Chapter 11

The main arguments on each side

Critics of strong nutrition policy make several serious points. Adults have the right to make their own choices about food, including unhealthy ones. Food taxes take a bigger share of poor people's income, so they are regressive. Nutrition science has changed its mind before, so governments should be cautious. And heavy regulation can hurt small producers more than big ones.

Supporters respond that food choices are already shaped by marketing, prices and subsidies, so the choice is not between freedom and interference but between different kinds of influence. They note that poorer people suffer most from diet-related disease, so they also gain most from effective policy. And they point to tax revenue being spent on the same communities, for example on school sport or free fruit. Both sides agree that children deserve more protection than adults, which is why marketing limits and school food rules are among the least contested measures.

Chapter 13

Feeding ten billion

The world's population is projected to approach ten billion later this century. Climate change threatens crop yields in hot regions, while farming itself is a major source of greenhouse gases, especially from livestock. Food policy is therefore becoming climate policy, and the reverse. Some proposals, such as the 2019 EAT-Lancet "planetary health diet", argue for diets rich in plants and lower in red meat in rich countries. These proposals are contested on cost, culture and nutrition grounds, especially for poorer people who need more, not less, animal protein. The farming side is covered in Sustainable agriculture.

The lasting lesson is the one from iodised salt. The most successful nutrition policies change the environment so the healthy option is the easy one. The policies that ask most of individuals, such as education campaigns alone, have usually done least.

The core idea

Nutrition policy changes the food environment rather than relying on willpower. Tools range from fortification and school meals to labels, taxes and bans. Many countries face the double burden of hunger and obesity at once.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 700 million people chronically hungry, and more than 1 billion living with obesity (mid-2020s).
  • US folic acid fortification (1998) cut neural tube defects by roughly a quarter to a third.
  • Poor diet linked to about 1 in 5 adult deaths worldwide (Global Burden of Disease, 2019).
The common mistake

Thinking more information will fix diets. People mostly know what is healthy; price, convenience and marketing decide what they buy.

The words, in plain words
Malnutrition
Bad nutrition of any kind, too little or too much.
Undernutrition
Too little energy, protein or nutrients, causing wasting, stunting or deficiencies.
Hidden hunger
Lack of vitamins and minerals even when calorie intake is enough.
Double burden of malnutrition
Undernutrition and obesity existing together in the same population.
Fortification
Adding vitamins or minerals to common foods such as salt, flour or oil.
Biofortification
Breeding crops that are naturally richer in nutrients.
Goitre
A swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck, often caused by lack of iodine.
Neural tube defect
A birth defect of the spine or brain, such as spina bifida, made less likely by folate.
Reformulation
Changing a product's recipe to reduce salt, sugar or fat.
Trans fats
Industrially made fats that raise heart disease risk, now banned or limited in many countries.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a larger share of income from poorer people.
Intervention ladder
A way of ranking policies from least to most restrictive of personal choice.
Ultra-processed food
Industrial products made mostly from refined ingredients and additives, rather than from whole foods.
