Sharp Mind · Public Health & Society · Topic 01 of 20

How healthcare systems work. The same broken leg costs nothing in one country and a month's wages in another.

A healthcare system is not just hospitals and doctors. It is a set of answers to three hard questions: who pays, who provides the care, and who decides what is covered. Every country answers them differently, mostly because of its history rather than careful design. Those answers explain why a British patient never sees a bill, why a German one belongs to a "sickness fund", and why an American can be fully insured and still go bankrupt. Once you can see the three questions, you can understand any system in the world in a few minutes.

Question 1 · What is it?

Everything a society does on purpose to keep people healthy

The phrase sounds like a building. It is really an arrangement of money, people, rules and information, and the arrangement matters as much as the medicine.

Chapter 01

One broken leg, four countries

Imagine a woman of forty who slips on ice and breaks her leg. The injury is identical wherever it happens. The bone needs an X-ray, a cast or an operation, some painkillers, and a few follow-up visits. What changes from country to country is everything around the bone.

In the United Kingdom she goes to a public hospital's emergency department. She gives her name and address, she is treated, and she goes home. She never sees a bill, because the National Health Service is paid for out of general taxes. In Germany she shows a card from her sickness fund, a non-profit insurer that she and her employer pay into every month. The hospital sends the bill to the fund, not to her. In the United States, what she pays depends on her insurance plan. She may owe a fixed "deductible" before the plan pays anything, and a share of the rest after it. In rural India, if there is no well-equipped public hospital nearby, she may go to a private clinic and pay in cash, perhaps by borrowing from relatives.

Same leg, same medicine, four completely different experiences. The difference is not the doctors. It is the system that decides how care is paid for and who can reach it.

Chapter 02

The six building blocks

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines a health system broadly: all the organisations, people and actions whose main purpose is to promote, restore or maintain health. That includes a vaccination team, a pharmacy, a food-safety inspector and a ministry statistician, not only a surgeon. In 2007 the WHO described six "building blocks" that every system needs. They are a useful checklist when you look at any country.

Building blockWhat it meansWhat failure looks like
Service deliveryClinics, hospitals, community health workers, emergency careA clinic exists but is closed, too far away, or has a three-month waiting list
Health workforceEnough trained doctors, nurses, midwives and technicians, in the right placesAll the doctors are in the capital; villages have none
InformationRecords, disease surveillance, statistics on births and deathsNobody knows what people are dying of, so money goes to the wrong problems
Medicines and technologyReliable supply of drugs, vaccines and equipment, of good qualityStock-outs, fake medicines, broken machines with no spare parts
FinancingRaising money and paying providers so people are not ruined by illnessFamilies sell land or skip treatment because of the cost
Leadership and governanceRules, regulation, planning and accountabilityNo one checks quality, and corruption drains the budget

The blocks depend on each other. A new hospital without nurses is an empty building. A system is only as strong as its weakest block, which is why aid projects that build one shiny thing often disappoint.

Chapter 03

What a system is for: three goals

If a system is a machine, what is it supposed to produce? The WHO's World Health Report of 2000 named three goals, and they still frame most debates.

The first is better health: longer lives, fewer babies and mothers dying, less disability. The second is financial protection: nobody should be pushed into poverty because they or a family member fell ill. The third is responsiveness: treating people with dignity, giving them some choice, and not making them wait without reason. Economists add a fourth that sits over all three: efficiency, meaning how much of the first three you get for each unit of money.

The goals pull against each other. Long waiting lists keep costs down but hurt responsiveness. Generous coverage protects families but can encourage overuse. Every system is a particular compromise between these goals, and arguments about healthcare are usually arguments about which goal to sacrifice a little of.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Who pays, who provides, who decides

Strip away the names and every system in the world is built from the same few parts. Four classic models show the main ways of putting them together.

Chapter 04

The four classic models

The American journalist T.R. Reid popularised a simple way to sort the world's systems in his 2009 book The Healing of America. Real countries mix the models, but the four types are a very good starting map.

The Beveridge model (tax-funded, mostly public)

Named after William Beveridge, whose 1942 report shaped Britain's post-war welfare state. The government raises taxes, owns most hospitals and employs many of the staff. Care is free or nearly free when you use it. The UK's National Health Service, launched in July 1948 under the health minister Aneurin Bevan, is the classic case. Spain, Italy, the Nordic countries and New Zealand are broadly similar.

The Bismarck model (social insurance)

Named after Otto von Bismarck, whose Germany passed a compulsory sickness insurance law in 1883. Workers and employers pay contributions into non-profit funds, and the funds pay doctors and hospitals, many of which are private. Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland and Japan are variations on this theme.

National health insurance (single payer)

A mix of the two. One public insurer, funded by taxes or premiums, pays for everyone, but the doctors and many hospitals are private. Canada built this province by province, starting in Saskatchewan, between the 1940s and the early 1970s. Taiwan created its version in 1995, and South Korea runs one national insurer. Because there is one buyer, it has strong power to bargain prices down.

Out-of-pocket

No real system at all for most people: you pay the doctor or pharmacist directly, if you can. This is the reality for much of the population in many low-income countries. It is the most unequal model, because care goes to those with cash, and it is a major cause of poverty.

The United States does not fit one box. Older people are covered by Medicare, a single-payer programme since 1965. Many poorer people are covered by Medicaid, funded by federal and state taxes. Veterans use a Beveridge-style system run by the government. Most working-age people get private insurance through their employer, which is a little like Bismarck but run largely for profit. And the uninsured, still about one in twelve Americans in the mid-2020s, often fall back on out-of-pocket payment. The US runs all four models at once, for different groups, which is a large part of why it is so complicated.

Chapter 05

The triangle: patient, payer, provider

The most useful picture in health policy is a triangle. At one corner is the patient, who needs care. At another is the provider: a doctor, hospital or clinic that gives it. At the third is the payer: a government, an insurer or a fund that pays the bill. Behind the payer stands the population, who supply the money through taxes or premiums.

The triangle exists because of a basic problem economists call information asymmetry: the provider knows far more than the patient about what care is needed. If the patient paid directly, they could not judge whether an operation was necessary. The payer acts as an informed buyer on the patient's behalf. It decides which treatments are covered, how much each is worth, and how to check quality. Much of what makes systems differ is how strong and well-informed the payer is. A single national payer can say no to an overpriced drug. A patient in a doctor's office cannot.

Chapter 06

Levels of care, and the gatekeeper

Systems arrange care in levels, like a pyramid. At the broad base is primary care: the family doctor, the community nurse, the local clinic. It handles most problems, most of the time, close to home. In the middle is secondary care: specialists and general hospitals, such as a cardiologist or an orthopaedic surgeon. At the narrow top is tertiary care: highly specialised centres for things like organ transplants, childhood cancers and major burns.

Many countries, including the UK, the Netherlands and Denmark, make the family doctor a gatekeeper. You cannot see a specialist (except in an emergency) without a referral from your general practitioner. Other countries, such as Germany and, traditionally, France, let patients go more freely to specialists, though France now rewards people for registering with a regular doctor.

Gatekeeping has a clear logic. Specialists are expensive and see a narrow slice of medicine. A generalist who knows you can solve most problems and send only the right cases upward. The cost is a feeling of being blocked, and sometimes a delay. More in Primary healthcare and Hospitals.

Chapter 07

Deciding what is worth paying for

No system can pay for everything. New drugs and devices arrive every year, and some cost hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient. So someone must decide which ones are covered. Many countries now use health technology assessment: a formal review of whether a treatment works, how much benefit it gives, and whether that benefit is worth its price compared with other uses of the money.

England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), set up in 1999, is the best-known example. It often measures benefit in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). One QALY is one year of life in full health. A year in poor health counts as a fraction. NICE then asks roughly how much a treatment costs per QALY gained, and treatments far above its threshold are usually not recommended unless special rules apply.

This is often attacked as "rationing". But every system rations. A tax-funded system rations by budgets and waiting lists. A market system rations by price, so people who cannot pay go without. The honest choice is not whether to ration but how openly and fairly to do it. Explicit rules at least let the public see and argue with the decisions.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A tenth of the economy, and a large part of whether you live or die

Health systems are among the biggest things governments run. The way they are built decides who gets care, who goes bankrupt, and how much a country spends for each year of life.

Chapter 08

Twice the money, shorter lives

Rich countries typically spend around a tenth of their national income (GDP) on health. The average across the OECD, a club of mostly wealthy democracies, was a little over 9 percent in the early 2020s. The United States spends far more: around 17 percent of GDP, which is roughly twice the OECD average per person once you adjust for prices.

Yet American life expectancy is several years shorter than in Japan, Spain, Switzerland or Australia, and below most other rich countries. Why? The reasons are debated, but researchers point to a mix. Prices are much higher in the US for the same drugs, scans and procedures. Administrative costs are high, because thousands of insurance plans each have their own forms and rules. Some people delay care because of cost. And part of the gap has nothing to do with medical care at all: higher rates of obesity, gun deaths, road deaths and drug overdoses.

American medicine at its best is among the world's finest. The lesson is that spending more on medical care does not automatically buy more health. How the money flows matters more than how much there is.

Chapter 09

Illness as a road to poverty

In a system that relies on out-of-pocket payment, a serious illness hits a family twice. The sick person stops earning, and at the same moment the family must find cash for treatment. They sell animals or land, take children out of school, or borrow at high interest. Health economists call spending that swallows a large share of a household's budget catastrophic health spending.

WHO and World Bank monitoring reports estimate that around two billion people face financial hardship from health costs, and that hundreds of millions are pushed into extreme poverty by them. The same reports say that roughly half the world's population is not fully covered by essential health services. These are estimates built from household surveys, so treat the exact figures with care, but the scale is not in doubt.

Whether getting sick can ruin you is one of the most important differences between systems, and it is invisible in league tables of life expectancy.

Chapter 10

Universal health coverage: the goal almost everyone signed

In 2015 all United Nations member states adopted the Sustainable Development Goals. Target 3.8 is universal health coverage (UHC): everyone can get the health services they need, of good quality, without financial hardship. UHC does not mean "everything free for everyone". It means a defined package of essential care that everybody can reach and afford.

Middle-income countries have shown it can be done without being rich. Thailand launched its Universal Coverage Scheme in 2002, at first charging a token fee of 30 baht per visit. It extended cover to tens of millions of people who had none, paid mainly from taxes, and is widely studied as a success. Rwanda built a community-based health insurance scheme after the 1994 genocide and reached high enrolment. Turkey and Indonesia have also run large coverage reforms.

Three dimensions of coverage

The WHO pictures UHC as a box with three sides. Who is covered (breadth), which services are covered (depth), and what share of the cost is paid from pooled funds rather than by the patient (height). A country can cover everyone for very little, or a few people for almost everything. Progress means making the box bigger on all three sides.

Every model buys something and pays for it somewhere else

There is no system without complaints. The useful question is which complaints you prefer to live with.

Chapter 12

The iron triangle

Health policy experts talk about an iron triangle of access, quality and cost. The rule of thumb is that you can improve any two, but usually at the price of the third. Cover more people with better care, and costs rise. Hold costs down and cover everyone, and waits or quality may suffer. Keep quality high and costs low, and you probably restrict access.

Good systems loosen the triangle a little through efficiency: generic drugs, more care in the community, fewer duplicated tests. No system has broken it. When a politician promises more access, better quality and lower cost all at once, ask which corner will quietly give.

A further honest point: international rankings of systems are contested. The WHO's 2000 report ranked France first and caused years of argument about its methods. It has not been repeated. Different weights on the goals give different winners.

Chapter 14

Why systems change so slowly

Germany's system still carries the shape of an 1883 law. Britain's still reflects decisions made in 1948. Political scientists call this path dependence: once millions of people, jobs and institutions are built around one arrangement, changing it becomes extremely costly. Every hospital, insurer, union and patient group has something to lose.

That is why big reforms are rare and usually happen at special moments: after a war, a crisis or a landslide election. The US Affordable Care Act of 2010 expanded coverage to millions but kept the mixed private structure, because replacing it outright was politically impossible. Systems evolve by adding layers, not by starting again. When you read about a country's healthcare debate, look first at its history.

The core idea

A healthcare system answers who pays, who provides and who decides. The four classic answers are Beveridge (tax-funded, public), Bismarck (social insurance funds), single payer (one public insurer, private providers) and out-of-pocket. Every system trades off health, financial protection, responsiveness and cost.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Rich countries spend about 9–11 percent of GDP on health; the US about 17 percent (early 2020s).
  • Germany's social insurance dates from 1883; the UK's NHS from 1948.
  • Roughly half the world's people lack full coverage of essential services, by WHO and World Bank estimates.
The common mistake

Thinking "public" or "private" describes a system. Most systems mix public money with private providers. Ask separately who pays and who provides; the answers are often different.
