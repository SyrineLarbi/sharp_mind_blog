Maternal health. Most deaths in childbirth are preventable with knowledge that is decades old.

For most of history, giving birth was one of the most dangerous things a woman would ever do. In rich countries today it is very safe, so safe that a death in childbirth is investigated as a failure. In parts of the world it is still a real danger. The difference is not mysterious science. Nearly all of it comes down to whether a trained person, a few cheap medicines and a working hospital are within reach in the hours that matter.

This file covers maternal health as a public health problem: what kills mothers, what prevents it, and why the risk is still so unequal.

General education

This file explains maternal health at the level of populations and health systems. It is not medical advice. Anyone who is pregnant, or has recently given birth, should take questions and warning signs to a midwife, doctor or emergency service.

Question 1 · What is it?

The health of women before, during and after birth

Maternal health covers pregnancy, childbirth and the weeks after. Its most common measure is a grim one, deaths, but it is about much more than survival.

Chapter 01

A death every two minutes, mostly in one region

The United Nations estimates that about 260,000 women died from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth in 2023. That is roughly one every two minutes. The number has fallen by around 40% since 2000, which is real progress. But the deaths are not spread evenly. About seven in ten happened in sub-Saharan Africa, and most of the rest in South Asia. Countries in conflict or crisis carry a large share.

The gap between countries is wider than for almost any other common health measure. In Norway or Japan, a few women die per 100,000 births. In the countries with the highest rates, the figure is several hundred, and in a few above a thousand. A woman's lifetime risk, which counts the fact that women in poorer countries also have more pregnancies, is estimated at around 1 in 40 in sub-Saharan Africa, compared with one in several thousand in most rich countries.

Chapter 02

How it is counted

A maternal death is the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the end of a pregnancy, from a cause related to or made worse by the pregnancy, but not from accidents. The standard measure is the maternal mortality ratio: maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. The world's goal, under the UN Sustainable Development Goals, is to bring the global ratio below 70 by 2030. In 2023 it was close to 200, so the target is far off.

Counting is hard. Many poor countries do not record every death or its cause. A woman who dies at home after a haemorrhage, or after an unsafe abortion, may never appear in official records. Estimates are built from surveys, models and partial records, and carry wide margins of error. Even rich countries undercounted until they added a pregnancy checkbox to death certificates. The worst-off places are also where the data are weakest.

Deaths are the tip of a larger problem. For each death, many more women suffer severe complications: a near-fatal haemorrhage, a lasting injury such as an obstetric fistula (a hole between the birth canal and the bladder or bowel, caused by prolonged obstructed labour), anaemia, or depression. Maternal health in its full sense covers all of these.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A few causes, a few cures, and the clock

Most maternal deaths come from a short list of complications. Most can be treated. What kills is delay.

Bleeding is the most important to understand. A woman can lose a dangerous amount of blood within an hour after birth. In postpartum haemorrhage, the difference between life and death is often measured in minutes. That is why the place of birth, and who is present, matters so much.

Chapter 04

The three delays

In 1994 the public health researchers Sereen Thaddeus and Deborah Maine published a simple model that is still the standard way to think about maternal deaths. They asked: once a complication starts, what stops a woman getting the care that would save her? They found three kinds of delay.

The model is useful because each delay has different fixes. Delay one needs education, community health workers and attention to who makes decisions in a household. Delay two needs roads, ambulances, phones, and sometimes "maternity waiting homes" near hospitals, where women from remote areas can stay in the last weeks. Delay three needs well-run facilities. A brilliant hospital saves nobody who cannot reach it in time.

Chapter 05

The core package of care

Public health experts agree on a fairly short list of what works.

Antenatal care

Check-ups during pregnancy to measure blood pressure, test for anaemia, infections and diabetes, give iron and folic acid, and plan the birth. WHO recommended at least eight contacts in 2016, up from four.

A skilled birth attendant

A midwife, nurse or doctor trained to manage a normal birth and to recognise and act on complications. The share of births with one present is a key measure of progress.

Emergency obstetric care

Facilities that can give the life-saving drugs, remove retained tissue, perform caesarean sections and give blood transfusions. Without these, a skilled attendant can recognise a crisis but not resolve it.

Postnatal care

Checks in the first days and weeks, when many deaths happen. This stage was long neglected and is now a priority.

Two further pieces sit around the core. Family planning lets women avoid pregnancies that are too early, too late, too close together or unwanted, which reduces maternal deaths directly. And girls' education tends to delay first births and makes women more likely to seek care.

Chapter 06

Cheap medicines, proved in large trials

Several of the most important tools are old, cheap drugs whose value was proved in large international trials. Magnesium sulphate, a simple salt, was shown in trials in the 1990s and early 2000s to prevent and treat the seizures of eclampsia better than the alternatives. Oxytocin, given as a routine injection right after birth, helps the womb contract and reduces bleeding. Tranexamic acid, which helps blood clot, was shown in the large WOMAN trial, published in 2017, to reduce deaths from bleeding when given within three hours.

Oxytocin has a cold-chain problem: it loses strength in heat. A heat-stable alternative, carbetocin, has been recommended by WHO for settings where oxytocin cannot be kept cold. And in 2023 the E-MOTIVE trial showed that a simple package, including a calibrated plastic drape that measures blood loss accurately and a bundle of treatments started immediately, cut severe bleeding outcomes by more than half. The insight was humble: staff routinely underestimated blood loss by eye, so they started treatment too late. Measuring it changed behaviour.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A test of whether a health system works at all

A maternal death is often called a "sentinel" event. It reveals failures across transport, staffing, supplies and respect, all at once.

Chapter 07

How rich countries made childbirth safe

In England and the US in the early twentieth century, maternal mortality was still several hundred per 100,000 births, similar to the poorest countries today. Then, from the late 1930s, it fell steeply. The causes were a cluster: sulphonamide drugs and then penicillin against infection, safe blood transfusion, better obstetric training, and the move to supervised births. Within a few decades the rate fell to a small fraction of its former level.

Some countries did well early with a different tool. Sweden had high standards of midwifery training from the nineteenth century and lower maternal mortality than many richer nations. In more recent times, Sri Lanka brought maternal deaths down sharply on a modest income, largely by building a network of trained public midwives, free hospital births and good records. Midwives, not high technology, have repeatedly been the backbone of rapid progress.

Britain also pioneered a habit worth copying. Since 1952 it has investigated maternal deaths through confidential enquiries, which review each case to see what went wrong, without assigning blame to individuals. It is one of the longest-running audits of its kind in the world, and many countries now run similar maternal death reviews.

Chapter 08

The rich country with a problem

The United States has the highest maternal mortality ratio among rich countries. The figure rose sharply during the pandemic and then fell back, to roughly 20 per 100,000 births in 2023, still several times higher than in countries such as Norway or Japan. Part of the rise over earlier decades reflects better counting. Part reflects real risk: older mothers, more obesity, diabetes and high blood pressure, gaps in insurance, and many deaths in the weeks and months after birth, after women have left hospital.

The gap by ethnicity is stark. Black women in the US die at roughly three times the rate of white women, and the gap persists even among those with university degrees. The causes are debated, but most researchers point to a mix of differences in access and quality of care, chronic health conditions, stress, and experiences of not being taken seriously by clinicians. The wider pattern is explored in Health inequality.

Chapter 09

Mothers, babies and families

A mother's health is closely tied to her baby's. Many of the same complications that kill mothers also kill newborns or cause stillbirths. Good antenatal and birth care saves both at once. When a mother dies, the surviving children, especially newborns, face much higher risks of death and malnutrition. Maternal health is the starting point of child health, which is the subject of the next file, Child health.

Mental health is the part most often missed. Depression during pregnancy or after birth affects a substantial minority of women everywhere, and in some rich countries suicide is a leading cause of death in the year after birth. Screening and support for perinatal mental health have become a growing focus of maternal care.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Too little, too late; too much, too soon

A 2016 series in The Lancet named the two failures of maternal care. Some women get too little care, too late. Others get too many interventions, too soon.

Chapter 11

The caesarean paradox and respectful care

The caesarean section shows the two failures side by side. In parts of sub-Saharan Africa, very few births are by caesarean, too few to meet real need, and women die of obstructed labour. In Brazil, the Dominican Republic and parts of other middle-income countries, more than half of births are caesareans, many scheduled for convenience or fear of labour. WHO stated in 2015 that, at the population level, rates above about 10 to 15% are not linked to fewer maternal or newborn deaths. The right number is neither "as many as possible" nor "as few as possible"; it is "whenever needed, and rarely otherwise".

A second, more uncomfortable finding concerns how women are treated. Studies in many countries have documented women being shouted at, slapped, neglected or detained in facilities over unpaid bills. Where women expect this, they stay home, and the first delay grows. WHO now treats respectful maternity care as part of quality, not an optional extra.

Abortion law is the most contested area. Countries differ deeply, for moral and religious reasons that many people hold sincerely. Public health research consistently finds that where safe services are unavailable, some women still end pregnancies by unsafe means, and some die. Supporters of legal access stress this toll. Opponents stress the moral status of the fetus and argue for other measures, such as support for mothers. Almost all sides support treating the complications of an abortion, whatever its legality, which WHO calls post-abortion care.

Chapter 13

The threats on the horizon

Two pressures could reverse gains. The first is money. Much maternal health work in the poorest countries depends on donor funding, and several large donors cut aid sharply in the mid-2020s, affecting supplies of contraceptives, midwife training and data collection. The second is the environment. Extreme heat is linked to higher risks of preterm birth and some pregnancy complications, and climate-driven crises, from floods to conflict, tend to break down exactly the transport and services that maternal care relies on.

The hopeful lesson is that the tools are known, and they are cheap. The problem is mostly delivery: getting a trained midwife, a few medicines, a vehicle and a functioning operating theatre within reach of every woman. That is a problem of health systems, which is the theme of Primary healthcare and Healthcare access in developing countries.

The core idea

Most maternal deaths come from a few causes, above all bleeding, high blood pressure disorders and infection. Most are preventable with known, cheap care. Women die because of delays: in deciding to seek care, in reaching it, and in receiving it once they arrive.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 260,000 maternal deaths worldwide in 2023, down roughly 40% since 2000.
  • About 7 in 10 of them in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Global target: below 70 deaths per 100,000 births by 2030; the 2023 level was near 200.
The common mistake

Thinking the answer is advanced medicine. Most lives are saved by midwives, a few cheap drugs and fast transport, not by new technology.

The words, in plain words
Maternal death
The death of a woman during pregnancy or within 42 days after it, from a cause linked to the pregnancy.
Maternal mortality ratio
Maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
Lifetime risk
The chance that a woman will die of a maternal cause over her whole life, taking into account how many pregnancies women typically have.
Postpartum haemorrhage
Heavy bleeding after birth, the leading cause of maternal death.
Pre-eclampsia
A pregnancy disorder with high blood pressure and organ strain, which can lead to seizures (eclampsia).
Sepsis
A life-threatening body-wide reaction to infection.
Obstetric fistula
A hole between the birth canal and bladder or bowel caused by prolonged obstructed labour.
Antenatal care
Health checks and care during pregnancy.
Skilled birth attendant
A midwife, nurse or doctor trained to manage births and spot complications.
Emergency obstetric care
The drugs, surgery and blood transfusion needed to treat birth complications.
Three delays model
A way of analysing maternal deaths by the delays in seeking, reaching and receiving care.
Respectful maternity care
Care that protects women's dignity, privacy and choices during birth.
