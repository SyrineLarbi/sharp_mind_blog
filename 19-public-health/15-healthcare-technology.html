Healthcare technology. The cheapest tools saved the most lives. The most expensive ones get the headlines.

When people hear "healthcare technology" they picture robots in operating rooms and scanners the size of a car. Those exist, and some are wonderful. But the technologies that changed the health of whole populations are mostly humble: a packet of salt and sugar, a clip on a finger, a one-page checklist, a fridge that keeps vaccines cold on a motorbike. This file explains what counts as health technology, how a new tool reaches a patient, and why technology makes us both healthier and poorer.

General education

This is an explanation of how health technology works as a system. It is not medical advice about any device, test or treatment. Decisions about your own care belong with a qualified clinician.

Question 1 · What is it?

Far more than machines: anything organised knowledge puts to work for health

The official definition is broad on purpose. A medicine, a vaccine, a test, a surgical procedure and a records system are all health technologies, because each is knowledge turned into something you can use.

Chapter 01

A rolled-up sheet of paper

In 1816 a French doctor, René Laennec, needed to listen to the heart of a young woman. The usual method was to press an ear directly against the patient's chest, which he found awkward. He rolled a few sheets of paper into a tube, put one end on her chest and the other to his ear, and heard the heartbeat more clearly than ever before. He went on to build a wooden version. That was the first stethoscope.

The story is worth telling because it shows the three parts of almost every health technology. There is an object (the tube). There is knowledge about how to use it (which sound means fluid in the lungs). And there is a practice that has to change (doctors now had to learn to listen, and they did, slowly, over decades). A technology is not only the gadget. It is the gadget plus the skill plus the routine that makes it useful. Remove any of the three and the object is just a tube.

The World Health Organization (WHO) uses a definition in this spirit. It describes health technology as the application of organised knowledge and skills in the form of devices, medicines, vaccines, procedures and systems, developed to solve a health problem and improve quality of life. That is why a vaccination schedule, a hand-washing protocol or a hospital's appointment software all count, alongside the robot.

Chapter 02

The five families

It helps to sort the field into five families. They are approved, paid for and used in different ways, which explains a lot of confusion in news stories.

The last column carries a lesson. Procedures and simple practices are often the best value in the whole table, and they are the ones with no company paid to promote them. This imbalance shapes which technologies people hear about, and it is one reason why public health agencies exist.

Chapter 03

Halfway technology and the real thing

In the 1970s the American physician and essayist Lewis Thomas proposed a useful way to rank medical technology. At the bottom is what he called non-technology: caring for a patient, comforting them and managing symptoms when nothing can be done about the disease itself. In the middle is halfway technology: expensive, complex tools that make up for a disease without curing it. At the top is decisive technology: something that prevents or cures the disease, usually because we finally understand how it works.

Polio shows the difference. In the 1940s and early 1950s, people whose breathing muscles were paralysed were kept alive in "iron lungs", large metal tanks that pumped air in and out by changing the pressure around the body. They were heroic, costly and labour-intensive. Hospitals had wards full of them. Then the polio vaccines arrived in the 1950s and early 1960s, and the wards emptied. The vaccine cost a tiny fraction of an iron lung. The decisive technology was both better and cheaper than the halfway one. Much of today's spending on chronic disease, from dialysis to many heart procedures, is closer to the iron lung than to the vaccine.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Invent it, prove it, approve it, pay for it, and get people to use it

A new tool has to pass through several gates before it helps a single patient. Each gate asks a different question, and many good inventions stop at the gate nobody thinks about: adoption.

Chapter 04

Gate one: is it safe, and does it do what it claims?

Medicines are tested in clinical trials, explained in Volume 44 (What makes evidence good). Devices follow a path that depends on risk. The United States sorts them into three classes: Class I for low-risk items such as bandages, Class II for moderate-risk items such as many infusion pumps, and Class III for devices that keep people alive, such as implanted heart valves.

Most moderate-risk devices reach the US market through 510(k) clearance, named after a section of the law. The maker shows the device is "substantially equivalent" to one already sold. Critics note that a chain of "equivalent to the previous one" can drift far from anything ever tested in patients. Class III devices need a stricter approval, usually with clinical data.

Europe tightened its rules after a scandal. In 2010 it emerged that a French company, Poly Implant Prothèse (PIP), had filled breast implants with cheap industrial-grade silicone. The EU responded with the Medical Device Regulation, adopted in 2017 and applied from 2021, which demands more clinical evidence. Device rules tend to be written after a failure, which is worth remembering when someone calls regulation red tape.

Chapter 05

Gate two: is it worth the money?

A device can be safe and effective and still be a poor use of money. Every health budget is limited, so buying one thing means not buying another. To make that choice openly, many countries use health technology assessment (HTA): a formal review of what a technology costs and what it delivers compared with the current best option.

The best-known body is England's NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence), set up in 1999. It measures benefit in quality-adjusted life years, or QALYs. One QALY is one year of life in full health; two extra years at three-quarters of full health count as 1.5 QALYs. For most of its history, NICE has usually recommended technologies costing less than about £20,000 to £30,000 per QALY gained, with exceptions and adjustments over time.

How a cost-effectiveness question is framed
                     extra cost of new option
  cost per QALY  =  -----------------------------
                     extra QALYs it delivers

  New device:  costs 12,000 more per patient
               adds 0.8 QALY per patient
               => 15,000 per QALY   -> likely "worth it"

  New device:  costs 60,000 more per patient
               adds 0.5 QALY per patient
               => 120,000 per QALY  -> likely "not at this price"

Pricing a year of life sounds cold. Defenders reply that the choice is made anyway. Every health system rations; the only question is whether it does so openly, with rules, or quietly, by queue and by luck. Germany, France, Canada and Australia run their own versions. The United States has no single national body doing this for all payers.

Chapter 06

Gate three: the records that have to talk to each other

The technology with the widest reach in a modern hospital is not a scanner. It is the electronic health record (EHR): the digital file holding your diagnoses, medicines, allergies and results. In the United States, a 2009 law known as the HITECH Act paid hospitals and doctors to adopt EHRs, and within about a decade almost all hospitals had one.

Adoption was not success. Many systems were built around billing, so doctors spend hours a day clicking boxes, and studies have linked heavy EHR workload to burnout. Many systems also could not exchange data, so a file at one hospital could be invisible to the hospital across the road. The fix is interoperability: shared standards, the most important today being FHIR (pronounced "fire"), which lets software request small standard pieces of health data over the internet. Estonia and Denmark are the usual examples of countries that have gone furthest. A record that cannot travel with the patient is a filing cabinet with a screen.

Chapter 07

Gate four: the technology that works in the lab and fails in the ward

The last gate is the one most often forgotten. A tool has to fit into real work done by tired people. Hospitals in poorer countries have long received donated equipment, such as incubators for newborns and hospital monitors, that soon sat broken. The machine was rarely the problem. There was no spare part nearby, the power was unstable, or nobody had been trained to repair it. WHO has warned for years that a large share of medical equipment in low-resource settings is out of service for these reasons.

The response is called appropriate technology: designing for the place where the tool will be used. Examples include solar-powered vaccine fridges, oxygen concentrators that tolerate power cuts, and rapid diagnostic tests that need no laboratory, no electricity and only a drop of blood. The best device for a district hospital is often not the most advanced one. It is the one that is still working in three years. Topic 19 returns to this in Healthcare access in developing countries.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Technology explains both why we live longer and why care costs more

The same force pushes in two directions at once. Understanding both is the key to almost every argument about health budgets.

Chapter 08

The humble tools that saved the most lives

Ask which health technologies saved the most lives in the last century and the honest answer rarely includes a machine. Vaccines prevent millions of deaths every year. Oral rehydration solution, a precise mix of clean water, salt and sugar, treats the dehydration that makes diarrhoea deadly for small children. It works because sugar helps the gut absorb salt, and water follows the salt. In 1978 the medical journal The Lancet called it potentially the most important medical advance of the century. Before it, the main treatment was a drip into a vein, which needs a clinic.

Another is the pulse oximeter, the clip on your finger that shines red and infrared light through the skin and estimates how much oxygen your blood is carrying. Oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood absorb these two colours differently. It was developed in Japan in the 1970s, notably by the engineer Takuo Aoyagi, and it became standard in operating rooms in the 1980s.

Then there is the checklist. In 2008 WHO launched a one-page Surgical Safety Checklist: confirm the patient, the operation and the site; check the anaesthetic; count the instruments before closing. A study published in 2009, covering eight hospitals in eight countries, found that deaths after surgery fell from about 1.5% to 0.8% after the checklist was introduced, and complications fell by about a third. A sheet of paper did what few new machines have done.

Chapter 09

The engine of cost

Rich countries spend a larger share of national income on health than fifty years ago. Why? Ageing populations explain some of it, but less than people assume. The factor most economists put near the top of the list is new technology: new things to do for patients that were impossible before.

The reason is simple. In most industries, technology makes things cheaper: a computer today does more than one costing a hundred times as much in 1990. In medicine, technology mostly makes things possible. A hip replacement does not replace a cheaper hip replacement. It replaces a life in a wheelchair. Medical technology usually adds a new service rather than making an old one cheaper, so it raises spending even when it is excellent value.

There is a second mechanism. A new technology often spreads beyond the patients it was first tested on. Keyhole surgery made gallbladder removal less painful and quicker to recover from, and the number of gallbladder operations rose, because surgeons were now willing to operate on milder cases. Each operation was cheaper and safer; the total bill went up. This is called indication creep: the group of people for whom a treatment is "indicated" quietly widens.

Chapter 10

When the tool is built for some bodies and not others

Technology also matters because it can carry hidden bias. During the COVID-19 pandemic, doctors relied heavily on pulse oximeters to decide who needed oxygen or admission. A study published in 2020 in the New England Journal of Medicine compared finger readings with direct blood tests in thousands of patients. Black patients were about three times as likely as white patients to have dangerously low oxygen that the oximeter had missed. The light passing through the skin is affected by skin pigment, and the devices had been calibrated mostly on lighter-skinned volunteers.

The effect had been reported in smaller studies decades earlier and largely ignored. Regulators have since moved to require testing on people with a wider range of skin tones. A device that is accurate "on average" can be systematically wrong for a particular group, and the group it fails is usually the one that was missing from the testing. The same lesson applies with even more force to software, as AI in healthcare explains.

Chapter 11

A hospital is now a computer network with beds

In May 2017 a piece of ransomware called WannaCry spread across the world, locking files and demanding payment. It hit parts of England's National Health Service especially hard. Computers in many hospitals and GP practices stopped working, thousands of appointments were cancelled, and some ambulances were diverted. The NHS was not specifically targeted; it was vulnerable because many machines ran old, unpatched software.

Since then, attacks on hospitals have become a regular feature of cybercrime, because hospitals cannot afford downtime and are therefore more likely to pay. Volume 14 explains how ransomware works (Ransomware). The health-system lesson is that every digital tool that makes care faster also creates a new way for care to stop. Paper backups and offline drills are now part of patient safety.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous gains, real costs, and a distribution problem

Few people would choose to be ill in 1950 rather than today. But the benefits of health technology come with costs that are financial, human and political.

Chapter 13

Why "newer" is not the same as "better"

Surgical robots, the best known being the da Vinci system first cleared in the United States in 2000, let a surgeon at a console control fine instruments through small cuts. For some operations they offer real advantages. For others, comparisons with ordinary keyhole surgery have found similar results at a higher cost.

Metal-on-metal hip replacements, promoted in the 2000s as more durable, are the darker case. Many failed early, shed metal particles into surrounding tissue and had to be replaced, leading to large recalls. A new technology should be judged against the best existing option, in ordinary hospitals, over years, not against no treatment in a showcase centre over months. National registries that track every implant, as the UK and several Nordic countries keep for joints, are how such problems get caught.

Chapter 15

The bottleneck is adoption, not invention

Almost every expert account of the future of health technology lands on the same point. The world already has more good technology than it uses. Proven tools such as oral rehydration solution, basic vaccines, checklists and oxygen still fail to reach millions of people who need them.

So the most important health technologies of the next twenty years may not be inventions at all. They may be better ways of choosing, buying, maintaining and spreading the tools we already have. That is less exciting than a robot surgeon. It is also where most of the lives are. The wider picture of where health systems are heading is in Future healthcare systems, and the medical science side is in The future of medicine.

The core idea

Health technology is organised knowledge put to work: medicines, devices, tests, procedures and information systems. A new tool must pass four gates: safety, value for money, fit with other systems, and real-world adoption. Technology is the main reason we live longer, and also a main reason health spending rises, because it usually adds new services rather than making old ones cheaper.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The WHO surgical checklist study (2009): deaths after surgery fell from about 1.5% to 0.8%.
  • NICE has long used a range of about £20,000–30,000 per QALY as its rough guide to value.
  • Pulse oximeters missed dangerously low oxygen about three times as often in Black patients as in white patients (2020 study).
The common mistake

Assuming the newest, most expensive tool is the most important. The cheapest technologies, from oral rehydration to checklists, have saved the most lives.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a technology that is excellent value per patient can still make a health budget grow? If you can, you have understood indication creep and the difference between "possible" and "cheaper".

The words, in plain words
Health technology
Any use of organised knowledge to solve a health problem: medicines, devices, tests, procedures or systems.
Medical device
A physical product used in care, from a plaster to a pacemaker.
510(k) clearance
The common US route to market for moderate-risk devices, based on similarity to an existing device.
Medical Device Regulation
The EU's stricter device rules, adopted in 2017 and applied from 2021.
Health technology assessment
A formal review of what a technology costs and what it delivers compared with current care.
QALY
Quality-adjusted life year: one year of life in full health, used to compare very different treatments.
Electronic health record
The digital file holding a patient's medical history, medicines and results.
Interoperability
The ability of different systems to exchange and use each other's data.
FHIR
A modern standard for packaging health data so software can request it over the internet.
Halfway technology
Lewis Thomas's term for costly tools that compensate for a disease without curing it.
Appropriate technology
Technology designed for the conditions where it will actually be used.
Indication creep
The gradual widening of the group of patients a treatment is used for.
Overdiagnosis
Finding a real abnormality that would never have caused harm, and then treating it.
