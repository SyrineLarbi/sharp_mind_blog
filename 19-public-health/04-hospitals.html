Sharp Mind · Public Health & Society · Topic 04 of 20

Hospitals. The most complex organisations most of us will ever enter, usually on our worst day.

A large hospital runs around the clock, employs thousands of people with hundreds of different skills, and handles everything from a newborn's first breath to an old person's last. It is a hotel, a laboratory, a factory and a school at once. It is also the most expensive part of any health system, and, surprisingly, a place where people can be harmed by the care meant to help them. Understanding how hospitals work explains waiting times in emergency departments, debates about closing local units, and why a simple checklist can save lives.

Question 1 · What is it?

From a shelter for the poor to an engine of cure

For most of history, hospitals were places of charity and often of death. Only in the last hundred and fifty years did they become places where people went to get better.

Chapter 01

A short history of a strange institution

Early hospitals were mostly religious. In medieval Europe, institutions such as the Hôtel-Dieu in Paris, one of the oldest still operating, cared for the poor, pilgrims and the dying. The care was food, shelter and prayer more than medicine. In the medieval Islamic world, large hospitals known as bimaristans, in cities such as Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo, were more medical. They had separate wards for different illnesses, trained physicians, and in some cases treated patients regardless of religion or wealth.

Until the late nineteenth century, though, anyone who could afford it was treated at home. Hospital wards were crowded and infections spread from bed to bed. Two changes transformed them. The first was hygiene and nursing. Florence Nightingale, working in British military hospitals during the Crimean War in the 1850s, used careful statistics to show that soldiers were dying far more from disease than from wounds, and campaigned for cleanliness, ventilation and trained nurses. The second was the germ theory of disease and antiseptic surgery, which Joseph Lister began in the 1860s, followed by anaesthesia, X-rays and, in the twentieth century, antibiotics.

Only once hospitals could cure more than they harmed did the rich start choosing to go there. That happened in many countries only in the early twentieth century.

Chapter 02

What a modern hospital is

Today a hospital is a place that provides care needing beds, specialists, equipment or round-the- clock nursing that cannot be given at home or in a clinic. Hospitals come in several kinds.

Hospitals also differ by ownership. Some are owned by the state, as in much of Britain and the Nordic countries. Some are non-profit, often with religious or charitable roots, which are common in Germany, the Netherlands and the United States. Some are for-profit companies. In Germany, public, non-profit and private hospitals all treat patients from the same insurance funds. Who owns a hospital and who pays for its patients are separate questions, as How healthcare systems work explains.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A system of queues, beds and handovers

From outside, a hospital looks like a building full of doctors. From inside, it looks more like an airport: everything depends on how smoothly people flow from one stage to the next.

Chapter 03

The patient's path

Patients arrive by two main doors. Elective patients are planned: a hip replacement booked months ahead, a scheduled cancer treatment. Emergency patients arrive unplanned, through the emergency department, often by ambulance. A third group, outpatients, come for a consultation or a test and go home the same day.

The first step, triage, comes from the French word for sorting. A nurse quickly decides who needs care first, based on how sick people are, not when they arrived. That is why someone with chest pain is seen before someone who has waited longer with a sprained ankle.

Chapter 04

Why a full hospital stops working

A common surprise is that the emergency department's long waits are often not caused by the emergency department. Patients who need admission wait there because no ward bed is free. The ward bed is not free because a patient in it is ready to leave but cannot, perhaps because a care home place or support at home has not been arranged. The block is at the back door, and the queue appears at the front door.

The mathematics of queues explains why this gets worse so quickly. When a system runs close to full capacity, small random rises in demand cause waiting times to shoot up, not gently but steeply. A hospital running at very high bed occupancy has no slack for a bad flu week. Many planners in England have used around 85 percent occupancy as a rough safe level, although the right figure varies with the size of the hospital. Many hospitals run well above it for long periods.

Hospital flow is a whole-system problem: the emergency department is where the queue shows, not usually where it starts. This is why social care for older people, which looks like a separate service, is one of the strongest levers on hospital performance. England's target, set in 2004, that 95 percent of emergency patients should be seen and admitted or discharged within four hours made this visible, because it is regularly missed when the rest of the system is blocked.

Chapter 05

The people inside

Doctors are the visible face of a hospital, but nurses are its backbone. They are the largest professional group and the ones at the bedside around the clock. Around them work pharmacists, physiotherapists, radiographers, laboratory scientists, porters, cleaners, cooks, IT staff and managers. Clinical work is organised into departments by specialty, each led by senior doctors, with nursing led separately.

Nurse staffing has some of the clearest evidence in hospital research. A well-known 2002 study by Linda Aiken and colleagues, looking at surgical patients in Pennsylvania hospitals, found that each extra patient added to a nurse's workload was linked to a rise of about 7 percent in the odds of a patient dying within 30 days. Later studies in Europe found similar patterns, and also linked a higher share of degree-educated nurses to better survival. How many nurses there are, and how well trained, is a safety matter, not just a cost.

Chapter 06

How hospitals are paid

Hospitals are paid in a few standard ways, covered in detail in Healthcare financing. Many countries now pay a fixed price per case, using diagnosis-related groups (DRGs): one price for a typical appendix removal, another for a typical stroke. Others give each hospital a global budget for the year. Some still pay per day or per service.

Each method changes behaviour. Per-day payment encourages long stays. Per-case payment encourages short, efficient stays and more cases, and may tempt hospitals to record patients as sicker than they are to get a higher price, a practice called upcoding. Fixed budgets control costs but give little reason to treat more patients, so waiting lists can grow. Most countries mix these methods to balance the incentives.

Chapter 07

Practice makes safer: volume and outcome

For many complex operations, such as pancreatic cancer surgery, oesophageal surgery or certain heart procedures, patients do better on average in hospitals and with surgeons that perform them often. This volume–outcome relationship has been found again and again. Teams that do something frequently develop routines, spot complications earlier and handle them better.

This is the main argument for concentrating specialist services in fewer, larger centres. England, for example, reorganised stroke care in London around a smaller number of specialist units, and studies found lower death rates afterwards. The cost is longer travel for some patients. It is a genuine trade-off, explored in Question 4.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where the money goes, and where harm can happen

Hospitals absorb the largest share of health budgets, and they are where the most serious mistakes are made. Both facts shape health policy everywhere.

Chapter 08

The biggest bill in the system

In most countries, hospital care is the largest single item of health spending. Hospitals are staffed around the clock, use costly equipment, and treat the sickest patients. One day in intensive care can cost more than many visits to a family doctor.

Countries vary hugely in how many hospital beds they keep. In the early 2020s, Japan and South Korea had more than twelve beds per thousand people, Germany around eight, and the United Kingdom, the United States and Sweden fewer than three. Fewer beds do not necessarily mean worse care. Some countries treat more patients as day cases or at home. But when beds are very few, the system has little room for surges, as COVID-19 showed. A bed count tells you less about quality than about how a country chooses to deliver care.

Chapter 09

When care itself causes harm

In 1999 the US Institute of Medicine published a report called To Err Is Human. It estimated that between 44,000 and 98,000 people died each year in American hospitals because of medical errors. The numbers were debated, but the report changed the conversation. It argued that most errors come from bad systems, not bad people: confusing drug labels, poor handovers, tired staff, missing information.

The most common harm is infection. Patients are often weak, have wounds, tubes and catheters, and share space. The World Health Organization estimates that out of every 100 patients in acute-care hospitals, about 7 in high-income countries and about 15 in low- and middle-income countries will catch at least one infection during their stay. Many of those infections are now resistant to common antibiotics (see Vol 13 · Antibiotic resistance).

The basic defence is the one Semmelweis found in the 1840s: clean hands. It remains hard to achieve consistently, because staff touch many patients and surfaces every hour. Hand-hygiene campaigns, alcohol gel at every bed, and checking compliance have reduced some infections significantly, though no hospital is free of them.

Chapter 10

What safety science learned from aviation

Hospitals have borrowed heavily from airlines, which made flying extremely safe by studying every error and standardising routines. The best-known example is the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, introduced in 2008. Before anaesthesia, before the first cut and before the patient leaves theatre, the team pauses to confirm basic things: the right patient, the right operation, the right side, allergies, expected blood loss, and that every instrument and swab is counted.

A study of eight hospitals in different countries, published in 2009, found that after the checklist was introduced, deaths after surgery fell from about 1.5 percent to 0.8 percent, and major complications from about 11 percent to 7 percent. Later studies found smaller or mixed effects in some settings, often because the checklist was ticked rather than used. A checklist works when it changes how a team talks, not when it becomes paperwork.

Speaking up

Part of the checklist's value is that every team member, including the most junior nurse, introduces themselves by name. Research on teams suggests that people who have spoken once are more likely to speak again when they notice a problem. In hierarchies where juniors fear seniors, errors go unchallenged.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Big and specialised, or small and close?

The great hospital debate in many countries is about where care should happen. Both sides have strong arguments.

The common compromise is a network: local hospitals handle common problems and stabilise emergencies, and specialist centres take the complex cases, with fast transfer between them.

Chapter 12

The hospital as an expensive default

Hospitals are excellent at acute crises: a car crash, a heart attack, a difficult birth. They are less suited to what most patients now have, which is several long-term conditions that flare up and settle. For a frail older person, a hospital stay carries its own risks: confusion (delirium), muscle loss from lying in bed, falls and infections. Clinicians often say that every day in bed costs an older patient strength that takes much longer to rebuild.

The best hospital stay for many patients is the one avoided, or the one kept short. That is not an argument against hospitals. It is an argument for strong primary care, community nursing and social care, so that hospitals can do what only they can do.

Chapter 14

The hospital as a hub, not a fortress

The direction of travel in most rich systems is clear even if the pace is not. The hospital is becoming less a building that patients enter and stay in, and more a hub at the centre of a network: specialist teams, intensive care and operating theatres in the middle, with diagnostics, follow-up and monitoring pushed out into clinics and homes.

In poorer countries the challenge is different. Many people have no access to safe surgery at all, and district hospitals lack staff, oxygen, blood and reliable electricity. For them, the priority is not fewer hospitals but working ones. Every system needs hospitals; the question is how to use them for what only they can do.

The core idea

Hospitals provide care that needs beds, specialists and round-the-clock staff. They work as systems of flow, where a blocked exit fills the front door. They are the biggest cost in health systems and a place where harm from care, especially infection and error, must be actively prevented.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 7 in 100 acute-care patients in rich countries, and 15 in 100 in poorer ones, catch an infection during their stay (WHO).
  • To Err Is Human (1999): 44,000–98,000 deaths a year from errors in US hospitals, by its estimate.
  • Surgical checklist study (2009): deaths fell from about 1.5% to 0.8%.
The common mistake

Blaming the emergency department for long waits. The queue usually starts at the discharge end, where patients wait for care at home or in care homes.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a hospital at 98 percent bed occupancy has far longer waits than one at 85 percent, even though it has only a few more patients? If so, you understand hospital flow.
