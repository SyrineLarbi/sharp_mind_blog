Sharp Mind · Public Health & Society · Topic 19 of 20

Healthcare access in developing countries. The medicine usually exists. The problem is the road to it.

Most of the deaths that still happen in poorer countries are from causes that medicine learned to prevent or treat decades ago: complications of childbirth, pneumonia, diarrhoea, malaria, tuberculosis, HIV. The knowledge exists, and often the medicine is cheap. What fails is the chain that should connect a sick person to it: the money to pay, the clinic within reach, the nurse on duty, the drug on the shelf. This file explains what access really means, why it breaks, which countries have done remarkably well with little money, and what is changing now that foreign aid is shrinking.

Question 1 · What is it?

Being able to get the care you need, when you need it, without being ruined by the bill

"Access" sounds like one thing. It is really a chain of conditions, and a single broken link is enough to stop care from happening.

Chapter 01

Three delays

Imagine a young woman in a rural village who goes into labour, and the labour goes wrong. She is bleeding heavily. In 1994 two public health researchers, Deborah Maine and Sereen Thaddeus, described why women in her situation die, and their framework is still used worldwide. They called it the three delays.

The first delay is in deciding to seek care. The family may not recognise the danger signs, may need permission from an elder, or may fear the cost. The second delay is in reaching care. There may be no road, no vehicle, no money for fuel, or a river in flood. The third delay is in receiving care once she arrives. The health centre may have no trained midwife on duty, no blood for a transfusion, or no drug to stop the bleeding.

Any one delay can kill. Heavy bleeding after birth can cause death within hours. That is why access is a chain, not a building. Building a clinic solves part of the second delay. It does nothing about the first or the third. The same pattern applies to a child with pneumonia or an adult with a fever that could be malaria. Maternal health has its own topic, Maternal health.

Chapter 02

The five links in the chain

Health researchers usually break access into several dimensions. A useful version has five.

LinkThe questionHow it breaks
AvailabilityDo the service, the staff and the medicine exist at all?No surgeon in the district; vaccine out of stock
Geographic reachCan people physically get there in time?Hours of walking; no transport at night
AffordabilityCan people pay, including travel and lost work?Fees at the door; a family sells its livestock to pay
AcceptabilityDo people trust it and feel respected?Disrespectful treatment; no female staff where that matters
QualityDoes the care actually work once received?Wrong diagnosis; poor hygiene; staff absent

The last row is often forgotten. Studies in many low- and middle-income countries have found that poor quality of care, not lack of access, now accounts for a large share of avoidable deaths. A clinic that is open but gives the wrong treatment is access on paper only. Getting people through the door is half the job. What happens inside decides the outcome.

Chapter 03

The scale of the gap

The global goal has a name: universal health coverage (UHC). It means that everyone can get the health services they need without suffering financial hardship. All UN member states signed up to it as part of the Sustainable Development Goals for 2030. Progress is measured by WHO and the World Bank. Their 2023 monitoring report estimated that about 4.5 billion people, more than half the world, were not fully covered by essential health services in 2021, and that about 2 billion faced financial hardship because of health costs.

"Developing countries" is a loose label, and the picture varies hugely within it. The World Bank sorts countries by income into low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high. Most of the gap is in low-income and lower-middle-income countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. But gaps also exist inside rich countries, and some poor countries outperform richer neighbours, as Question 3 shows. Income matters, but policy choices matter almost as much.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Money, people, medicines and the first point of contact

Every health system, rich or poor, has to solve the same four problems. In poorer countries, each is harder, and they reinforce each other.

Chapter 04

Money: who pays, and how

A rich country may spend several thousand dollars per person per year on health. Many low-income countries spend a few tens of dollars. That gap explains a lot, but the way money is raised matters as much as the amount. There are three main sources: government budgets (from taxes), insurance (where people or employers pay into a shared pool), and out-of-pocket payment, where the patient pays at the point of care.

Out-of-pocket payment is the worst way to fund health, for two reasons. It keeps the poor away, because they cannot pay at the moment they are sick. And it pushes families into poverty, because a serious illness can cost more than a year's income. In many low-income countries a large share of all health spending, often a third or more, still comes straight from patients' pockets. That is why the core idea of financing reform is pooling: collecting money from many people in advance, through tax or insurance, so the healthy pay for the sick. The details are in Healthcare financing.

Governments themselves often give health a small share of their budgets. In 2001, African Union countries meeting in Abuja pledged to spend at least 15% of their government budgets on health. Only a few have met that target consistently. Many face heavy debt repayments that compete with health and education for the same money.

Chapter 05

People: the missing health workers

Money cannot treat anyone without trained people. WHO's 2006 World Health Report drew attention to a stark imbalance: sub-Saharan Africa carried roughly a quarter of the world's burden of disease but had only about 3% of its health workers. WHO now projects a global shortfall of around ten million health workers by 2030, concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries.

Training more is only part of the answer, because trained staff leave. Rural postings are hard, pay is low, and supplies are unreliable. Many doctors and nurses move to cities, to the private sector, or abroad. Rich countries, including the UK, the US, Canada and those of the Gulf, recruit large numbers of nurses and doctors trained in countries such as the Philippines, India, Nigeria, Ghana and Zimbabwe. This is often called the brain drain.

The debate is genuinely two-sided. Migrants have the right to seek better lives, and the money they send home supports families and economies. But a country that trains a nurse at public expense and then loses her to a rich health system has, in effect, subsidised that system. A WHO code of practice adopted in 2010 asks rich countries to recruit ethically, and WHO keeps a list of countries with severe shortages where active recruitment should be avoided. Health workers are a global market, and poor countries are on the losing side of it.

Chapter 06

Medicines: from list to shelf

In 1977 WHO published its first Model List of Essential Medicines: the medicines that a basic health system needs, chosen for effectiveness, safety and cost. It has been updated regularly since and has been adopted, in adapted form, by most countries. Most drugs on it are old, off-patent and cheap. The list shows that a working system does not need the newest drugs. It needs the right ones, reliably available.

"Reliably" is the hard part. A medicine must be bought, stored, transported and tracked to the last clinic. Vaccines must stay cold the whole way, which is called the cold chain. Stock-outs, where a clinic runs out, are common. In the gaps, a market of poor-quality products grows: in 2017 WHO estimated that about one in ten medical products in low- and middle-income countries was substandard or falsified. A fake antimalarial does more than fail one patient. It can also help resistance spread. Delivering supplies is a logistics problem, and some of the most interesting innovations are logistic. In Rwanda, since 2016, the company Zipline has used small drones to drop blood and medical supplies to remote hospitals.

Chapter 07

The first point of contact: primary care and community health workers

In 1978, at a WHO and UNICEF conference in Alma-Ata (now Almaty, in Kazakhstan), the world's governments declared that primary healthcare, basic care close to where people live, was the key to "Health for All by the year 2000". The target date was missed by a wide margin. The idea was renewed in 2018 at a conference in Astana. It remains the core of most serious plans, as Primary healthcare explains.

One of its most effective tools is the community health worker: a local person, often a woman, trained for weeks or months rather than years, who visits households, gives vaccines, treats common childhood illnesses, distributes bed nets and refers serious cases. Ethiopia built a national Health Extension Programme from the mid-2000s, deploying tens of thousands of salaried health extension workers, mostly women, to rural health posts. It is widely credited with contributing to large falls in child deaths. The cheapest health worker who is present is worth more than the best one who is three hours away. The limits are real too: community workers need supervision, supplies and pay, and many programmes have collapsed when relying on volunteers.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Proof that poor countries can be healthy, and the cost of failing

The strongest reason to study access is that some countries have closed most of the gap with little money. Their stories show that the problem is solvable.

Chapter 08

Good health at low cost

In 1985 a well-known report from the Rockefeller Foundation asked why some poor places had life expectancies close to those of rich countries. It looked at Sri Lanka, the Indian state of Kerala, Costa Rica and China. The answers were remarkably similar. Each had invested early in free or cheap primary care, in education, especially of girls and women, and in basic nutrition and sanitation, and each had made public services reach rural areas, not only cities.

Education appears in almost every success story. A mother who can read is more likely to recognise danger signs, use clinics, vaccinate her children and space her pregnancies. Later work on other countries, such as Bangladesh's rapid fall in child deaths, has pointed to similar ingredients. Health outcomes depend on far more than health services, which is the central idea of Public health.

Chapter 09

The HIV turnaround

At the start of the 2000s, HIV was killing millions of people a year, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa. Effective combination drugs had existed since the mid-1990s, but they cost over ten thousand dollars per patient per year in rich countries, far beyond what poor countries could pay. Many experts said treatment in Africa was unaffordable and the focus should be prevention only.

Three things changed that. Generic manufacturers, notably the Indian company Cipla in 2001, offered combination treatment for around a dollar a day. Activists and governments fought patent battles over the right to use cheaper generics in emergencies. And new international funding arrived: the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria from 2002, and the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) from 2003. Within two decades, tens of millions of people were on treatment. What had been called "impossible" became routine once price, money and political will moved together.

Chapter 10

Covering everyone: Thailand and Rwanda

In 2002 Thailand, then a middle-income country, launched its Universal Coverage Scheme, popularly known at first as the "30 baht scheme" because a visit cost a flat 30 baht, less than a US dollar. It was funded mainly from taxes, built on a strong network of district hospitals and health centres, and paid providers a fixed amount per registered person, which kept costs under control. It is widely regarded as one of the most successful health reforms in a middle-income country.

Rwanda took a different route after the 1994 genocide had destroyed its health system. It built community-based health insurance, the mutuelles de santé, in which households pay a small annual premium according to their income level, with the poorest subsidised. Combined with community health workers and strong performance tracking, coverage rose to most of the population, and child deaths fell steeply. Two very different models, tax-funded and insurance-based, both worked because they pooled money and reached rural areas.

Mobile phones have helped in many places. Kenya's mobile money system, M-Pesa, launched in 2007, made it easier to save for, pay for and receive help with health costs, and text-message systems remind patients about appointments and medicines. Telemedicine extends this, as Telemedicine explains.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What has worked, what has backfired, and the arguments in between

Every approach to widening access has strengths and costs. Several of the biggest debates in global health are about which ones to choose.

Chapter 11

Approaches weighed

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
User feesRaise some money locally and may discourage trivial visits.Keep the poorest away; many countries that removed them saw clinic use rise sharply.
Donor-funded disease programmesFast, measurable results against HIV, malaria, polio and in vaccination.Can create parallel systems and pull staff from general care; vulnerable to aid cuts.
Community health workersCheap, close to people, effective for common illnesses and prevention.Fail without pay, supervision and supplies; cannot replace hospitals.
Tax-funded coveragePools risk widely; Thailand and Sri Lanka show it can work.Needs a working tax system; hard where most work is informal.
Private providersAlready where many people go first; can add capacity quickly.Quality varies widely; charges can be ruinous without regulation.

User fees deserve a note, because they show how thinking changed. In 1987 the Bamako Initiative, backed by UNICEF and WHO, encouraged African countries to let communities charge modest fees for medicines, to keep clinics supplied. It did improve supply in some places. But evidence built up that fees deterred the poor, and from around 2000 many countries, Uganda among the first in 2001, abolished fees at public clinics and saw use rise. Today most experts agree that charging at the point of care is the least fair way to fund it, though how to replace the money is still argued over.

Chapter 12

The long argument: vertical or horizontal?

Global health has argued for decades between two approaches. Vertical programmes attack one disease with their own money, staff, supplies and targets: a polio campaign, a malaria bed-net programme. Horizontal approaches strengthen the general health system: clinics, staff, records, supply chains, so that it can handle whatever comes.

Vertical programmes have a record of striking wins. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980 and wild polio has been pushed back to just two countries, Pakistan and Afghanistan. They are easy to measure and attractive to donors. Critics argue they can weaken the wider system, for instance by paying staff more to work on one disease. Horizontal investment is slower and harder to measure, but it is what keeps a mother alive when her labour goes wrong. The Ebola epidemic in West Africa in 2014–2016 made the case vividly: it spread fastest where general health systems were weakest. Most experts now favour a diagonal approach, using disease programmes deliberately to build shared infrastructure. See Pandemics and Vaccination programs.

Chapter 14

The question underneath: whose responsibility?

The aid cuts of the mid-2020s have reopened an old debate. One view says rich countries have a moral duty, and a self-interest in preventing pandemics, to keep funding health in poor ones. Another says decades of aid have created dependence and that countries must take ownership of their own systems, with aid as a short bridge, not a permanent support. Many people in recipient countries hold a version of both views: they want help, but on their own terms and priorities.

The evidence from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Rwanda and Ethiopia points to a shared conclusion. Lasting access comes from a country's own commitment to pooled funding, primary care and staff in rural areas, with outside money most useful when it builds those things rather than working around them. The next topic, Future healthcare systems, looks at what all health systems, rich and poor, will need to change.

The core idea

Access is a chain: available, reachable, affordable, acceptable and good enough to work. In poorer countries it breaks on money, staff, medicines and distance. Countries that succeeded with little money pooled funds, invested in primary care, community health workers and education, and reached rural areas.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 4.5 billion people were not fully covered by essential services in 2021 (WHO and World Bank).
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: roughly a quarter of the disease burden, about 3% of the world's health workers (WHO, 2006).
  • About 1 in 10 medical products in low- and middle-income countries was substandard or falsified (WHO estimate, 2017).
The common mistake

Thinking the answer is mainly more hospitals or new technology. Most avoidable deaths are from treatable conditions, and the fixes are pooled money, staff who stay, drugs on the shelf, and good quality care close to home.
