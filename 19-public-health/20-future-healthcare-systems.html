Future healthcare systems. Built to rescue the acutely ill, now asked to manage long lives.

Most health systems were designed in the twentieth century around a simple picture: a person falls ill or gets injured, goes to a doctor or a hospital, is treated, and goes home. That picture fitted a world of infections, accidents and shorter lives. Today the typical patient is older, has two or three long-term conditions at once, and needs care for years, not days. Costs rise, staff are short, and the money comes from a shrinking share of working-age people. This file explains the pressures, the main reforms being tried around the world, the trade-offs between them, and what is honestly likely to change.

Question 1 · What is it?

A system is more than its hospitals

To talk about the future of healthcare systems, you first need a clear picture of what a system is, and of the world it was built for.

Chapter 01

Designed for a different century

When Britain's National Health Service opened in 1948, the main threats to health were infections, injuries, childbirth and diseases of poverty. Life expectancy at birth for men was under seventy. The design reflected that: general practitioners as the first contact, and hospitals for serious episodes. Many other countries built similar structures, whether paid for by taxes or by insurance.

The patients have changed more than the design. Thanks largely to the successes of those systems, most people in rich countries now live into their eighties. WHO estimates that non-communicable diseases, long-term conditions such as heart disease, cancer, diabetes and chronic lung disease, cause about three-quarters of all deaths worldwide. Many older people live with several at once, a state called multimorbidity. A man of eighty with diabetes, heart failure, arthritis and early memory loss does not need one rescue. He needs coordinated care from several professionals, for years, mostly outside hospital.

Most systems are still organised around the episode, while most of the need is now continuous. That mismatch is the starting point for almost every reform discussed below.

Chapter 02

Chapter 03

Five pressures at once

Five forces push on every system, rich or poor, though with different strength. Ageing increases need. Cost growth from new technology and labour makes each unit of care more expensive. Workforce shortages limit how much care can be given at any price. Climate change adds new health threats while the sector itself is a polluter. And pandemics, as COVID-19 showed, can overwhelm a system that runs close to full capacity in normal times.

What makes the coming decades hard is that the five arrive together, and several feed each other. An ageing population both needs more care and supplies fewer workers. Rising costs collide with slower growth in tax revenue. The rest of this file looks at each pressure, then at the reforms.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The forces behind the pressure, and the main shift in response

Each pressure has a mechanism, and understanding it explains why simple solutions, such as "cut waste" or "build more hospitals", rarely work on their own.

Chapter 04

Ageing: more need, fewer hands

WHO projects that by 2030 about one person in six worldwide will be aged sixty or over, and that the number of people aged eighty or more will roughly triple between 2020 and 2050. Japan is the country furthest along. Close to three in ten Japanese people were aged sixty-five or over in the early 2020s. In 2000 Japan introduced compulsory long-term care insurance, paid by everyone from the age of forty, to fund care at home and in care homes. Germany had introduced a similar scheme in 1995.

Those dates point to a crucial fact. Much of the cost of ageing falls not on hospitals but on long-term care: help with washing, dressing, eating and getting around, often for years. In many countries this care is funded separately from health, means-tested, and delivered by low-paid workers or unpaid family members, mostly women. When it fails, older people end up in hospital beds they do not need, unable to go home, and hospitals clog up. The social side of ageing is in Aging populations.

Chapter 05

The cost engine: technology and the Baumol effect

Health spending in rich countries has risen faster than national income for most of the past half century. The United States spends roughly a sixth of its GDP on health, more than any other country; many European countries spend around a tenth. Two forces explain much of the rise.

The first is technology, which mostly adds new things to do rather than making old things cheaper, as Healthcare technology explains. The second was named by the economist William Baumol in the 1960s. In manufacturing, productivity rises fast: a factory makes far more cars per worker than it did fifty years ago. But a nurse dressing a wound, like a string quartet playing Beethoven, takes about as long as it always did. Wages in such services must still rise to keep up with the rest of the economy, or nobody would do the work. So their relative cost rises year after year. This is called Baumol's cost disease.

The lesson is counter-intuitive. Rising health spending is not proof of waste. Some of it is the natural result of a rich society buying a labour-intensive service. Waste exists, and estimates of it are large, but cutting it alone will not stop the trend.

Chapter 06

The workforce cliff

Health systems are among the largest employers in most economies. WHO projects a global shortfall of around ten million health workers by 2030, concentrated in poorer countries. Rich countries face their own version: ageing doctors and nurses retiring, burnout after the pandemic, and competition from better-paid jobs. They fill gaps partly by recruiting from abroad, with consequences for poorer countries, as Healthcare access in developing countries explains.

Responses fall into three groups. Train more, which takes years. Change who does what, called task shifting: nurses prescribing, pharmacists treating minor illnesses, physician associates doing routine work. And use technology to remove low-value tasks, such as the AI scribes described in AI in healthcare. In the coming decades, the binding limit on care may be people, not money.

Chapter 07

The great shift: from hospital-centred to integrated care

The most common response worldwide has a single name: integrated care. The idea is to organise services around the patient rather than around institutions, so that the GP, the hospital, the community nurse, the pharmacist and the social care worker share information, budgets and goals. In England, 42 Integrated Care Systems became legal bodies in 2022, each bringing together the NHS and local government in an area. In the United States, organisations such as Kaiser Permanente, which combine insurance, hospitals and doctors under one roof, are often cited as an integrated model.

Integration sounds obvious, and it is hard. Each organisation has its own budget, its own targets and its own culture. Evidence so far suggests that integrated care often improves patients' experience, but savings and fewer hospital admissions have been harder to demonstrate. Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Paying for health, not for activity

How a system pays its providers decides what they do. Several of the most important experiments of the coming decades are about changing that incentive.

Chapter 08

Volume versus value

There are three basic ways to pay doctors and hospitals. Fee-for-service pays for each test, visit or operation. It rewards activity, so it encourages more of it, needed or not. Salary or fixed budgets pay the same regardless of activity, which controls costs but can reward doing less and lengthen waiting lists. Capitation pays a fixed amount per registered person per year, which rewards keeping people well but can tempt providers to avoid sick patients.

Reformers have spent two decades trying to pay for value, a word popularised by the Harvard economist Michael Porter, who defined it as health outcomes achieved per dollar spent. In the United States, the 2010 Affordable Care Act encouraged accountable care organisations: groups of providers that share in the savings if they keep costs down while meeting quality targets. "Bundled payments" pay a single price for a whole episode, such as a hip replacement and the recovery that follows. The results have been real but modest. Every payment method has a perverse incentive; the art is choosing which one you can best police.

Chapter 09

Prevention, and the test case of the new weight-loss drugs

Almost everyone agrees that prevention should get more attention. Most health systems spend only a few per cent of their budgets on it. The difficulty is political as much as medical: prevention's benefits arrive years later and are invisible, because nobody sees the heart attack that did not happen, while its costs arrive now. Disease prevention covers the methods.

A new test case has arrived. The GLP-1 drugs, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, produce large weight loss and have shown benefits for heart and kidney disease, as Volume 13 explains (GLP-1 drugs). In principle they could prevent a great deal of later illness. But a large share of adults in many countries could be eligible, many may need to take the drugs long-term, and prices in the mid-2020s were high, though falling. Health systems have had to ration access. Whether a preventive drug saves money depends on its price, how many people take it and for how long, and these questions will shape budgets for years. See Obesity.

Chapter 10

A different model: Singapore

Singapore is often studied because it achieves high life expectancy while spending a smaller share of GDP on health than most rich countries. Its system mixes several tools. Since 1984, workers have been required to save part of their wages in personal medical accounts called MediSave. There is also a national insurance scheme for large bills and a government safety net for those who cannot pay. Public hospitals compete on price within tight state control. In 2023 Singapore launched a programme called Healthier SG, which enrols residents with a regular family doctor and focuses on prevention.

The model has critics. Personal savings accounts work less well for people with low incomes or chronic illness, and Singapore's wealth, small size and strong state make comparisons tricky. But it shows that systems are not simply "tax-funded" or "private". The future is likely to be hybrids, borrowing pieces from different models, as many countries already do. The main existing models are compared in How healthcare systems work and Healthcare financing.

Chapter 11

Two new jobs: climate and pandemic readiness

Healthcare is a significant polluter. Estimates suggest the sector produces somewhere around 4–5% of global greenhouse gas emissions, from energy-hungry hospitals, supply chains, medicines and anaesthetic gases. In 2020 England's NHS became the first national health system to commit to net zero, by 2040 for the emissions it controls directly and by 2045 for those it can influence. At the same time, climate change brings heatwaves, floods and shifting patterns of infectious disease, which increase demand for care.

The second job is readiness for the next pandemic. COVID-19 showed that systems running near full capacity have no room for a surge. In May 2025 the World Health Assembly adopted a Pandemic Agreement on prevention, preparedness and response, though key details, including how countries share pathogens and the benefits from them, were left for further negotiation. A system that is efficient in normal times and fragile in a crisis is not efficient. Keeping spare capacity costs money every year and only pays off in rare years. See Pandemics.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every reform helps something and costs something

No reform is free. The useful question is which trade-off a society is willing to accept.

Chapter 13

The hardest part is political

Most of these reforms are well known to experts. The obstacle is usually politics. Closing a small local hospital, even to fund better community care, is one of the most unpopular decisions a politician can make. Explicit rationing, saying openly that a treatment is not worth its price, produces painful headlines about named patients. Raising taxes or insurance contributions is unpopular. Accepting longer waits is unpopular too.

Different countries resolve this in different ways. Some rely on independent expert bodies, such as England's NICE or Germany's institute for quality and efficiency in healthcare (IQWiG), to make technical choices at arm's length from ministers. Some let markets and prices do more of the rationing. Some use waiting lists. None of these is neutral, and people disagree in good faith about which is fairest. Every system rations care; the real choice is by price, by queue, or by an open rule. Trust in the institutions making those choices may be the most important resource any system has.

Chapter 15

What to watch

Health systems change slowly. Most of the NHS of the 2040s, like most of the US or German system, will look familiar to today's patients. The changes will come at the edges and add up: a hospital visit replaced by a home monitor, a GP appointment by a nurse or a pharmacist, a referral letter drafted by software, a preventive drug paid for because it keeps people out of hospital.

Three signs are worth watching in any country. First, whether long-term care is properly funded, because when it fails, hospitals fail too. Second, whether the workforce grows and stays, because no technology replaces enough people. Third, whether money moves with the patient, from hospitals toward homes and communities. Countries that get those three right will cope with ageing. Countries that do not will face longer queues, rising private spending and growing inequality, the subject of Health inequality. The future of healthcare is less a story of invention than of organisation.

The core idea

Health systems were built for episodes of acute illness; most need is now continuous care for older people with several long-term conditions. Ageing, cost growth, staff shortages, climate and pandemics press at once. The main responses are integrated care, care closer to home, paying for value, task shifting and digital tools, and each has a trade-off.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Non-communicable diseases cause about three-quarters of deaths worldwide (WHO).
  • By 2030 about 1 in 6 people worldwide will be 60 or over (WHO).
  • Healthcare produces roughly 4–5% of global greenhouse emissions (estimates vary).
The common mistake

Assuming rising health spending is mostly waste. Much of it is the natural result of ageing, new technology and the Baumol effect; cutting waste helps but will not reverse the trend.
