Telemedicine. The doctor did not change. The distance did.

For most of history, getting medical care meant that the patient and the clinician had to be in the same room. Telemedicine breaks that rule. A consultation can happen by video, a scan can be read on another continent, and a heart can be watched from a hospital while its owner sleeps at home. The idea is over a century old. What changed is that, in 2020, a pandemic forced almost every health system to try it at once. This file explains what telemedicine is, how it works, what that giant experiment showed, and why "remote" is a tool rather than a replacement.

Question 1 · What is it?

Care delivered across a distance, in four main forms

Telemedicine is not one technology. It is a family of ways to move information, instead of moving people.

Chapter 01

A radio in the outback

In the 1920s, the interior of Australia was one of the emptiest inhabited places on Earth. A farmer injured on a remote station might be days from the nearest doctor. A Presbyterian minister, John Flynn, campaigned for a solution, and in 1928 what became the Royal Flying Doctor Service made its first flight. The aircraft was only half the idea. The other half was the radio. An engineer, Alfred Traeger, designed a radio set powered by pedals, so that a family with no electricity could call a base and describe the problem. A doctor could give advice over the air and decide whether the plane needed to come.

That combination still describes telemedicine today. Most of the value is in deciding, from a distance, who needs to travel and who does not. The pedal radio has become a smartphone, and the plane is often an ambulance, but the logic is the same. Telemedicine is at its best when distance is the main barrier between a person and the right decision.

The idea is even older. In the early 1900s the Dutch physiologist Willem Einthoven, inventor of the practical electrocardiogram (ECG, a recording of the heart's electrical activity), sent heart recordings along telephone wires from a hospital to his laboratory more than a kilometre away. He called the result a "telecardiogram".

Chapter 02

The four forms

Two words are often mixed up. Telemedicine usually means clinical care at a distance: diagnosing and treating. Telehealth is wider and also includes health education, training of staff and administration. Within telemedicine, four forms matter.

FormHow it worksExample
Live (synchronous)Patient and clinician talk in real time, by video or phoneA GP appointment by video; a therapy session
Store-and-forward (asynchronous)Information is captured, sent, and reviewed laterA photo of a rash sent to a dermatologist; an X-ray read overnight in another country
Remote patient monitoringDevices at home send measurements to a care teamA heart-failure patient's daily weight; a pacemaker reporting its own data
Clinician to clinicianA local doctor consults a distant specialist, with the patient present or notA rural emergency doctor calls a stroke neurologist

The public mostly thinks of the first row. In practice, the less visible rows often bring the biggest clinical gains, because they move scarce specialist knowledge to where it is needed without anyone travelling.

Chapter 03

A change of place, not a new kind of medicine

It is tempting to treat telemedicine as a new branch of medicine. It is more useful to see it as a new place where ordinary medicine happens. The clinical questions do not change. What is wrong? How sure are we? What should happen next? What changes is which information is available.

On a video call a doctor can see your face, your breathing, your skin and your home, and can hear how you describe things. They cannot press on your abdomen, listen to your lungs or feel your pulse. Some home devices, such as blood-pressure cuffs, digital stethoscopes and pulse oximeters, fill part of that gap. So the right question is never "is telemedicine good?". It is "for this problem, is the information we can get remotely enough to decide safely?" For a medication review, usually yes. For a child with a high fever and a stiff neck, no.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A chain of devices, networks, rules and people

A remote consultation looks like a simple video call. Behind it sit privacy rules, licensing laws, payment codes and a care team that must know what to do with the data.

Chapter 04

The technical chain

Every telemedicine service has the same links. There is a device at the patient's end: a phone, a laptop, a sensor. There is a connection: mobile data, broadband or, in some places, satellite. There is a platform: the software that books the visit, runs the video and stores the notes. And there is the health record, which should receive what was decided so that the next clinician can see it.

Each link has a weak point. Video needs a steady connection, which many rural and poor households lack. Platforms must protect privacy: in the United States, a law called HIPAA (the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) sets rules for health data, and the EU's data protection law, the GDPR, treats health data as a special category needing extra protection. And if the platform does not connect to the main record, remote care creates a second, disconnected file about the same patient, which is exactly how mistakes happen.

Chapter 05

Store-and-forward: the specialist who reads while you sleep

Some of the most successful telemedicine never involves a live conversation. In teleradiology, scans taken in one hospital are sent electronically to radiologists somewhere else. A hospital in the United States, for example, may send night-time scans to radiologists working in daylight in Australia or India, so that results come back within an hour rather than waiting for morning. The images are digital already, so moving them costs almost nothing.

Teledermatology works in a similar way. A GP photographs a mole or rash with a special lens and sends it to a skin specialist, who decides whether the patient needs to be seen urgently, routinely, or not at all. Eye screening for people with diabetes uses the same model: photographs taken in a high-street clinic are graded centrally. The pattern is consistent. Where a diagnosis depends mainly on an image, distance hardly matters.

Chapter 06

Telestroke: when minutes are brain

The strongest evidence for live telemedicine comes from stroke. Most strokes are caused by a clot blocking an artery in the brain. A clot-dissolving drug can limit the damage, but only if it is given within a few hours, and only after a brain scan has ruled out bleeding, because the same drug would make a bleed worse. The decision needs a specialist. Small hospitals often do not have one on duty at 3 a.m.

In a telestroke network, the local team scans the patient and connects by video to a stroke neurologist at a larger centre. The neurologist reviews the scan, examines the patient on camera, using a standard scoring checklist for things like arm weakness and speech, and advises whether to give the drug or transfer the patient for a procedure to pull the clot out. Studies in several countries have found that such networks get more patients treated, faster, in hospitals that would otherwise have no specialist. The video does not replace the neurologist. It lends one to a hospital that could never afford its own.

Chapter 07

Remote monitoring: a loop, not a gadget

Remote patient monitoring sounds like a device problem. It is mostly an organisation problem. Take heart failure, where the heart pumps weakly and fluid builds up in the body. A rise in body weight of a kilogram or two over a few days can be an early sign of trouble, before breathlessness starts. Weighing patients daily at home, and acting on the trend, can catch it early.

The monitoring loop, and where it breaks
  patient at home          care team                 action
  ---------------          ---------                 ------
  scale / cuff / sensor -> data arrives -> rule:  "weight up 2 kg in 3 days?"
         ^                                   |
         |                                   v
         +---- advice, new dose  <----  nurse reviews, calls patient

  breaks if:  patient stops measuring     (adherence)
              nobody is paid to review    (staffing)
              alerts flood the team       (alert fatigue)

Trials of remote monitoring in heart failure have had mixed results. Some found fewer hospital admissions, others found little difference. The difference usually lay in the loop, not the device: data that nobody reviews and acts on is just a more expensive way of being ignored. The same is true of continuous glucose monitors for diabetes, which send blood-sugar readings to a phone every few minutes. The device is only as good as the decisions it feeds.

Chapter 08

The rules: who may treat, who pays, what may be prescribed

Three kinds of rule decide whether telemedicine can happen at all. The first is licensing. A doctor is normally licensed to practise in a particular country, or in the United States in a particular state. In many places, a doctor may treat a patient only where the patient is physically located, so a New York doctor seeing a patient on holiday in Florida can be on shaky legal ground. Some US states have joined agreements that make multi-state licences easier, and EU rules allow some cross-border care, but the details vary widely.

The second is payment. If insurers or public systems pay less, or nothing, for a remote visit, clinics will not offer it. The third is prescribing. Many countries restrict the remote prescribing of controlled drugs, such as strong painkillers and some stimulants, to reduce abuse. Before 2020, rules like these, more than technology, were the main brake on telemedicine. That is why the pandemic, which loosened many of them overnight, changed everything.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The largest unplanned experiment in the history of healthcare

In a few weeks of 2020, health systems moved routine care onto screens. The results showed what remote care can do, and where it falls short.

Chapter 09

Spring 2020

When COVID-19 lockdowns began, clinics faced a stark choice: see patients remotely or not see them at all. Governments relaxed licensing, payment and privacy rules almost overnight. The scale of the switch was enormous. In the United States, according to a federal government analysis, telehealth visits in the Medicare programme for older people rose from under a million in 2019 to over fifty million in 2020. In England, many GP practices moved to "total triage", where every patient first contacts the practice remotely and is then offered a phone, video or in-person appointment.

After the first waves, use fell back but stayed well above its pre-pandemic level. Mental health care kept the highest share of remote visits; surgery and specialties that depend on physical examination returned mostly to in-person care. The pandemic did not prove that telemedicine could replace medicine. It proved which parts of medicine were never really about being in the same room.

Chapter 10

Who gains most: distance, time and stigma

The people who gain most are those for whom travel is the real barrier. That includes rural residents, people with disabilities, people who cannot take time off work, carers who cannot leave a relative, and older patients with chronic conditions who need frequent, brief check-ins. A fifteen-minute review that used to cost half a day of travel and waiting can now cost fifteen minutes.

Mental health is a special case. Talking therapies rely mostly on conversation, and many studies have found video therapy broadly comparable to face-to-face therapy for common conditions such as depression and anxiety. For some people, not having to walk into a clinic also reduces the stigma that stops them seeking help. Volume 43 explains what the therapies themselves do (Therapy, what the different kinds actually do).

Large middle-income countries show the population-scale version. India's government telemedicine service, eSanjeevani, launched in 2019 and expanded rapidly during the pandemic, connecting patients at local health centres with doctors elsewhere. By official counts it had delivered more than a hundred million consultations by 2023. Where doctors are scarce and unevenly spread, telemedicine is less a convenience than a way of redistributing them.

Chapter 11

What the evidence says, and what it does not

The research picture is more cautious than the marketing. Reviews have found that for many follow-up visits, medication reviews, mental health care and long-term condition management, remote care gives results broadly similar to in-person care, with high patient satisfaction. For telestroke and teleradiology, the case is strong.

The weaker points are also clear. A 2019 US study of children with respiratory infections found that those seen through direct-to-consumer telemedicine apps were more likely to be prescribed antibiotics than those seen in person, which is the wrong direction, because most such infections are viral. Some studies suggest that easier access adds visits rather than replacing them, so total costs can rise. And many studies are short, small or run by enthusiastic early adopters. "As good as in-person" has been shown for some uses, not for medicine in general.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Access and convenience, against touch, fragmentation and new divides

The same feature, removing the need to be in the room, creates most of the benefits and most of the risks.

Chapter 13

The convenience trap

The boom in telemedicine brought a wave of companies offering doctors on demand through an app. Some built useful services. Others discovered that a model built on quick, one-off consultations does not easily make money or deliver good care. The UK company Babylon Health, once valued at several billion dollars and running an NHS GP service in London, collapsed in 2023. Its story is a reminder that growth in users is not the same as a working model of care.

The deeper risk is to continuity of care: being looked after over years by people who know you. Research has long linked continuity with fewer hospital admissions and better outcomes. A patient who sees a different stranger each time, through different apps, gets fast answers but no one who notices the slow pattern. The best systems use remote care to strengthen the relationship with a regular team, not to replace it.

Chapter 15

The right question for every new service

The arguments of the 2020s will be less about whether telemedicine works and more about where it belongs. Health systems are writing rules on which conditions can be handled remotely, how remote visits are paid, and how to make sure people without good internet are not pushed to the back of the queue. The digital divide is the issue most likely to decide whether telemedicine reduces inequality or widens it, as Health inequality explains.

For a citizen, one test covers almost every case. When a remote service is offered, ask: would the decision be any different if I were in the room? If not, remote care saves time for everyone. If yes, insist on being seen. That simple question sums up the whole field.

The core idea

Telemedicine moves information instead of people, in four forms: live consultations, store-and-forward, remote monitoring and clinician-to-clinician advice. It works best where distance is the main barrier and the decision does not need touch. Its limits are physical examination, the digital divide and fragmented care.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1928: the Royal Flying Doctor Service starts in Australia, using pedal-powered radio.
  • US Medicare telehealth visits went from under 1 million in 2019 to over 50 million in 2020.
  • India's eSanjeevani passed 100 million consultations by 2023, by official counts.
The common mistake

Asking "is telemedicine as good as a real visit?" as if there were one answer. It depends entirely on the problem. For a medication review, usually yes. For a stiff neck and a fever, no.
