Sanitation. The least glamorous public health measure, and one of the most powerful.

Every person produces waste every day, and that waste carries the germs of some of the deadliest diseases in history. Sanitation is the work of keeping it away from people's hands, food and water, from the moment it leaves the body to the moment it is safely treated or contained. Rich countries solved this problem so thoroughly that their citizens rarely think about it. Billions of people elsewhere still live with it every day, and it still kills children in large numbers.

Question 1 · What is it?

Separating people from their waste, safely, all the way

Sanitation usually means the safe management of human excreta, faeces and urine. In a wider sense it also covers wastewater, solid rubbish and hygiene, especially handwashing.

Chapter 01

The summer London could not breathe

In the hot summer of 1858, the River Thames in London smelled so bad that the period became known as the Great Stink. For decades the growing city had flushed its sewage into the river, the same river from which much of its drinking water was drawn. Cholera had killed tens of thousands of Londoners in repeated epidemics. Most experts at the time believed disease spread through bad air, or "miasma". The smell, in their view, was the danger.

They were wrong about the cause, but the smell drove the right action. Parliament, whose building sits on the river, quickly approved a plan by the engineer Joseph Bazalgette. Over the next two decades his workers built a network of large intercepting sewers that carried London's waste east, away from the city, along with roughly 1,100 miles of smaller street sewers feeding into them. Cholera did not return to London on the old scale. Much of the system is still in use today.

The lesson has two parts. First, engineering can save more lives than medicine. Second, you do not always need to fully understand a disease to beat it. Germ theory was confirmed only in the following decades.

Chapter 02

The sanitation ladder

The global standard for measuring sanitation comes from the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP). It sorts household facilities into rungs on a ladder.

RungWhat it meansExample
Safely managedAn improved toilet, not shared, with waste safely treated on site or carried away and treatedA flush toilet linked to a sewer and treatment plant; a well-built pit that is safely emptied
BasicAn improved toilet, not shared, but waste not proven to be safely managedA good latrine whose contents are dumped untreated when emptied
LimitedAn improved toilet shared between two or more householdsA shared block in a crowded city settlement
UnimprovedA toilet that does not separate waste from human contactA pit with no slab, a hanging latrine over water
Open defecationNo toilet at allFields, bushes, railway lines, riverbanks

On this measure, about 3.5 billion people, more than four in ten of the world's population, lacked safely managed sanitation in the early 2020s. Around 400 million still practised open defecation, most of them in rural areas of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The UN Sustainable Development Goals aim for safely managed sanitation for all by 2030, a target the JMP says will be missed by a wide margin at current rates.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Breaking the routes from one gut to another

Most sanitation-related disease spreads by the faecal-oral route: germs from one person's faeces end up in another person's mouth. Sanitation works by blocking that route at every step.

Chapter 03

The F-diagram

Public health engineers use a simple diagram, first published in a WHO monograph in 1958 by Edmund Wagner and J. N. Lanoix, to show how faecal germs travel. It is known as the F-diagram because each route begins with F.

The F-diagram: how germs travel from faeces to a new host
                 FLUIDS (water)  ----------+
                /                          |
  FAECES ---- FINGERS  -------------> FOOD ---> MOUTH of a
                \                          |    new person
                 FLIES  ------------------+|
                  \                        |
                   FIELDS (soil, crops) ---+

  BARRIERS:
    toilets and sewers  -> stop faeces reaching any route   (primary)
    handwashing         -> blocks fingers
    clean water         -> blocks fluids
    food hygiene        -> blocks food
    covered waste       -> blocks flies

  the first barrier, the toilet, blocks all routes at once

The diagram makes the key point. Safe water, handwashing and food hygiene each block one route. A toilet that safely contains waste blocks all of them at the source. That is why experts call it the primary barrier.

Chapter 04

The sanitation chain

A toilet is only the first link. Waste has to go somewhere, and engineers describe a chain with several stages: containment (the toilet and what it drains into), emptying, transport, treatment, and finally safe disposal or reuse. A break anywhere puts people back at risk.

In rich cities with sewers, the chain is mostly invisible. Flush, and the water carries the waste through pipes to a treatment plant, as described in Volume 15. But sewers are expensive. They need pipes under every street, a reliable water supply to flush them, and a treatment plant at the end. Most people in poorer countries who have toilets are not connected to sewers. They use on-site sanitation: pit latrines or septic tanks.

On-site systems fill up. When they do, someone must empty them. In many cities this is done by hand, by workers who climb into pits with buckets, often without protective equipment. The contents are then frequently dumped into drains, rivers or fields. This part of the chain is called faecal sludge management, and it is one of the most neglected areas in global health. A city can have a toilet in every home and still have its waste in the streets.

Chapter 05

Technologies for places without sewers

Ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrine

Developed in Zimbabwe in the 1970s. A pit with a slab and a vent pipe, screened at the top. Air flows up the pipe, carrying smells away, and flies attracted to the light at the top are trapped by the screen. Cheap, simple and effective.

Pour-flush toilet

A toilet pan with a water seal, flushed with a bucket of water, draining to a pit or tank. Common in South Asia. The water seal blocks smell and flies.

Septic tank

A sealed underground tank where solids settle and liquids drain into the ground. Widely used in suburbs and rural areas of rich countries too. Must be emptied periodically.

Container-based sanitation

Toilets with sealed containers that a company collects and replaces regularly, taking the waste for treatment. Used in some dense settlements, for example in Haiti and Kenya.

In 2011 the Gates Foundation launched the Reinvent the Toilet Challenge, asking engineers to design toilets that work without sewers, water or power and kill pathogens on site. Several prototypes have been built. Cost and durability remain the obstacles to wide use.

Chapter 06

Getting people to use toilets

For decades, many sanitation programs built toilets that went unused, became storage sheds, or collapsed. Building is not the same as changing behaviour. In 2000, a development consultant called Kamal Kar, working in Bangladesh, developed an approach called Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS). Instead of providing subsidised toilets, facilitators visit a village and lead residents to map where people defecate, walk through those areas, and work out how much faeces they are eating through food and water. The aim is to trigger shared disgust and collective action. The village then builds its own toilets and declares itself "open defecation free".

CLTS spread to dozens of countries. Its results vary. In some places it changed norms quickly. In others, cheap toilets collapsed and people went back to old habits. Critics have also raised concerns about the use of shame, and about the poorest households being pressured into building toilets they cannot afford. Most programs now combine community mobilisation with support for the poorest and with local markets for toilet parts.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Health, dignity, safety and money

Sanitation prevents disease, but its effects reach far beyond health statistics.

Chapter 07

The greatest medical milestone

In 2007 the British Medical Journal asked its readers to vote for the most important medical milestone since 1840. They chose the sanitary revolution: clean water and sewage disposal. It beat antibiotics, anaesthesia and vaccines. The vote was not scientific, but it reflected a judgement many historians share. The fall in deaths from cholera, typhoid and dysentery in nineteenth-century cities came largely from engineering and law. In Britain, Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary conditions of the working population led to the Public Health Act of 1848, one of the first laws to make public health a duty of government.

Today, WHO estimates that unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene together cause more than a million deaths a year, mostly from diarrhoeal disease, with young children hit hardest. Poor sanitation also spreads intestinal worms, which infect around 1.5 billion people and can cause anaemia and stunted growth, and diseases such as trachoma, a bacterial eye infection that is a leading infectious cause of blindness. Most of these deaths are preventable with technology that is more than a century old.

Chapter 08

Women, girls and dignity

The burden of poor sanitation falls hardest on women and girls. Where there are no toilets, women often wait until dark to relieve themselves, which can cause health problems and exposes them to harassment and assault. Girls may miss school during menstruation if schools lack private, working toilets with water. These effects are well documented in surveys and qualitative studies, although their exact size is hard to measure.

Sanitation is therefore also about dignity. In 2010 the UN General Assembly recognised access to safe drinking water and sanitation as a human right, and in 2015 sanitation was recognised as a distinct right. The UN marks World Toilet Day each year on 19 November, a deliberate effort to talk about a subject most people avoid.

Chapter 09

India's toilet campaign

The largest sanitation campaign in history was India's Swachh Bharat Mission (Clean India Mission), launched in 2014. In 2014, India was home to a large share of the world's people practising open defecation. Over five years the government funded the building of more than 100 million household toilets in rural areas, alongside a major publicity campaign. In 2019 the government declared rural India open defecation free.

The achievement was real and large: access to toilets rose dramatically. But independent surveys found that the declaration overstated reality. Many people with toilets still sometimes defecated in the open, some toilets were incomplete or unusable, and the emptying and treatment of waste lagged behind. Researchers also pointed to social factors, including caste-linked attitudes towards cleaning latrines, that made people reluctant to use pits that would eventually need emptying. Toilets were the visible part; changing habits and managing waste took longer.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Big benefits, high costs, surprising evidence

Nobody disputes that sanitation saves lives at scale. The debates are about which approaches work, at what cost, and how much household toilets alone can achieve.

Economists still rate sanitation as very good value. A WHO-commissioned analysis estimated returns of several dollars for each dollar spent, through fewer deaths, lower medical costs and time saved. Those estimates depend on assumptions, but the direction is consistent.

Chapter 11

The trials that disappointed

In 2018 and 2019, three large, carefully designed trials, the WASH Benefits trials in Bangladesh and Kenya and the SHINE trial in Zimbabwe, tested whether improving household water, sanitation and handwashing would reduce child stunting. They found no effect on growth, and only mixed effects on diarrhoea. The results surprised many experts.

The researchers did not conclude that sanitation does not matter. They argued that the household measures tested were too modest. In a village where neighbours still defecate in the open and animals roam, a single family's latrine and handwashing station may not lower the overall load of germs much. Many experts now call for "transformative" sanitation: whole-community coverage, piped water and safe waste management, closer to what changed nineteenth-century cities. Sanitation is a shared good: your toilet protects your neighbours, and theirs protects you.

Chapter 13

Old sewers, new pressures

Rich countries face their own problems. Many older cities use combined sewers, in which rainwater and sewage share the same pipes. In heavy rain the system overflows into rivers by design. In England, public anger grew in the early 2020s over how often water companies released untreated sewage into rivers and the sea, and the issue became a major political argument about regulation and private ownership. London's Thames Tideway Tunnel, a new "super sewer" under the river, was completed in the mid-2020s to capture most of the capital's overflows. Climate change, bringing more intense rainfall, will put more pressure on systems like these. Pollution of rivers and seas is covered in Water pollution.

The other long-term direction is reuse. Human waste contains nitrogen, phosphorus and energy. Treated sludge can become fertiliser, and some plants produce biogas. Urine diversion toilets separate urine, which is rich in nutrients and mostly free of germs, for use on fields. Waste that is managed well becomes a resource, which may in time help pay for the systems that manage it.

The core idea

Sanitation is the safe management of human waste along a whole chain, from toilet to treatment. A safe toilet is the primary barrier against the faecal-oral spread of disease, blocking all routes at the source. Building toilets is not enough: people must use them, and the waste must be safely emptied and treated.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 3.5 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation in the early 2020s.
  • Around 400 million still had no toilet at all.
  • Unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene cause more than a million deaths a year (WHO estimate).
The common mistake

Counting toilets and calling the job done. A toilet whose waste ends up in the street has moved the problem, not solved it.
