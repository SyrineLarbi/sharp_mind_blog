Child health. The greatest change in human history that almost nobody noticed.

For most of human history, losing a child was normal. Parents expected it. Around 1800, by the best historical estimates, roughly four in ten children in the world died before their fifth birthday. Today it is under one in twenty-five. That change, more than any other, is why average life expectancy has risen so much. It happened mostly through simple, cheap tools: clean water, vaccines, a sugar-and-salt drink, a bed net, a mother's knowledge. And it is not finished. Millions of children still die each year from causes that are easy to prevent.

Volume 28 makes the point that the great fall in death rates, "particularly of children", happened largely before antibiotics, thanks to water, food and housing (Sewers, not surgeons). This file tells the rest of the story: how child deaths kept falling across the world after 1950, what still kills children, and what works against it.

General education

This file describes child health across populations. It is not medical advice. Worries about a particular child, especially danger signs such as fast breathing, inability to drink, or drowsiness, need a health worker straight away.

Question 1 · What is it?

Survival first, then growth and development

Child health covers everything from the first breath to adolescence. Public health measures it first by survival, and then by whether children grow, learn and thrive.

Chapter 01

A drink that saved millions

In 1971, during the war that led to the independence of Bangladesh, millions of refugees fled into camps in India. Cholera broke out. The standard treatment for the severe dehydration that cholera causes was a drip of fluid into a vein, which needed sterile equipment and trained staff. There was not nearly enough of either. A doctor called Dilip Mahalanabis and his team gave patients a simple solution to drink instead: clean water with salt and glucose. Death rates in the camps they ran were reported to be far lower than with the usual shortages.

The science had been worked out shortly before, by researchers in Dhaka and Kolkata. Glucose helps the gut absorb salt, and water follows salt. A child losing fluids through diarrhoea can be kept alive by drinking the right mixture, even while the illness continues. This is oral rehydration solution, or ORS. A 1978 editorial in The Lancet called it potentially the most important medical advance of the century.

ORS sums up child health. The tools that have saved the most children are cheap, simple, and usable by parents, not by specialists in hospitals.

Chapter 02

How it is measured

The headline measure is the under-five mortality rate: the number of children who die before their fifth birthday per 1,000 live births. It is used because most child deaths happen in these years, and because it reflects many things at once: nutrition, water, vaccines, health care and the mother's education. It is often used as a broad indicator of a society's development.

In absolute numbers, the UN estimated about 4.8 million deaths of children under five in 2023, compared with more than 12 million in 1990, even though there are more children today. About half the deaths were in sub-Saharan Africa, and about a quarter in South Asia.

Question 2 · How does it work?

What kills children, and what stops it

A small number of conditions cause most child deaths. For almost all of them, a cheap answer exists. The challenge is getting it to the child in time.

Behind many of these sits undernutrition. A child who is underfed has a weaker immune system, so a bout of diarrhoea or pneumonia is more likely to kill. A major review in The Lancet in 2013 estimated that undernutrition was an underlying factor in about 45% of deaths of children under five. Poor nutrition is rarely written on the death certificate, but it is often the reason the infection was fatal.

Chapter 04

The first month is the hardest

As deaths from infections in older children fell, deaths of newborns became a larger share of the total, now close to half. Newborn deaths have fallen more slowly because they depend on the quality of care around birth, which is harder to improve than handing out vaccines or bed nets. Mothers' and newborns' fates are closely linked, which is why Maternal health and newborn health are increasingly planned together.

One of the most effective tools for small babies came from a shortage. In Bogotá, Colombia, in the late 1970s, a hospital with too few incubators began keeping small babies in constant skin-to-skin contact on their mothers' chests. This became known as kangaroo mother care. The mother's body keeps the baby warm, breastfeeding is easier, and infections are fewer. Later trials found it reduces deaths among low-birthweight babies, and a large WHO-led trial published in 2021 found that starting it immediately after birth, rather than waiting until the baby was stable, saved more lives. WHO now recommends immediate kangaroo care for small and preterm babies.

Chapter 05

The child survival revolution

In the early 1980s James Grant, then head of UNICEF, launched what he called a child survival revolution. He promoted four cheap interventions under the acronym GOBI.

Grant was a relentless campaigner. He persuaded heads of state to hold national vaccination days and pushed coverage of basic vaccines from low levels to a large majority of the world's children by 1990. Critics argued that the approach was too narrow, picking a few "vertical" programs rather than building whole health systems. The debate between targeted programs and broad primary care has run through global health ever since. It is discussed in Primary healthcare.

Chapter 06

Breastfeeding, vitamin A and the community health worker

WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, then continued breastfeeding alongside other foods up to two years or beyond. Breast milk is clean, the right temperature, and contains antibodies that protect against infection. In places without safe water, formula mixed with dirty water is dangerous. In the 1970s, aggressive marketing of infant formula in poor countries led to a long international boycott of Nestlé and, in 1981, to an international code on the marketing of breast-milk substitutes.

Large trials in the 1980s and 1990s found that giving vitamin A capsules to children in deficient populations reduced deaths, especially from measles and diarrhoea. Many countries still run twice-yearly campaigns.

Then there is the person who delivers it all. In much of Africa and Asia, the backbone of child health is the community health worker, a local person with weeks or months of training, who visits homes, gives advice, and treats simple illnesses with a small kit. WHO and UNICEF promote a method called integrated community case management, in which these workers diagnose and treat pneumonia, diarrhoea and malaria at the doorstep. Ethiopia's Health Extension Program, which trained tens of thousands of such workers from 2003, is often cited as a model.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A healthy childhood shapes a whole life, and a whole society

The case for child health is not only that children's lives matter. It is that what happens early lasts.

Chapter 07

The first thousand days

The period from conception to a child's second birthday, roughly a thousand days, is when the brain and body grow fastest. Undernutrition in this window can cause stunting, which is about more than height. Stunted children tend to do worse at school and earn less as adults, and some of the damage cannot be fully reversed later. This is why nutrition programs focus heavily on pregnant women and children under two. The policies are covered in Nutrition policy.

Stimulation matters too. In a well-known study begun in Jamaica in the 1980s, researchers led by Sally Grantham-McGregor sent health workers to visit stunted toddlers weekly and show mothers how to play and talk with them. Twenty years later, the children who received the visits earned substantially more as adults than those who did not. Early childhood is where small investments buy the largest lifetime returns, a point the economist James Heckman has made widely.

Chapter 08

Fewer deaths, then fewer births

One of the most important effects of child survival is on family size. When parents expect most of their children to survive, they tend to have fewer children. Across the world, falling child mortality has been followed, usually within a generation or so, by falling birth rates. This is a core part of what demographers call the demographic transition. Smaller families can invest more in each child's education and health, and countries can benefit from a period in which many adults are of working age. The wider picture is in Population growth.

The fear that saving children would cause runaway population growth was therefore largely wrong in the long run. Child survival turned out to be one of the most reliable routes to smaller families.

Chapter 09

Child health in rich countries

In rich countries, child deaths are rare, and the main causes are different: problems around birth, congenital conditions, injuries, and cancer. The biggest success of recent decades was the fall in sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), the unexplained death of a sleeping baby. In the early 1990s, campaigns in the UK, the US and elsewhere told parents to put babies to sleep on their backs instead of their fronts. Rates of SIDS fell by roughly half or more within a few years. A single piece of advice, based on careful studies, saved thousands of babies.

The new concerns are chronic. Childhood obesity has risen in most countries since the 1980s. Rates of anxiety and depression among adolescents rose in many rich countries in the 2010s, though how much is caused by smartphones and social media is hotly debated. And exposure to pollution remains a quiet harm: in 2020 UNICEF estimated that around one in three children worldwide had raised levels of lead in their blood, which can damage brain development. The last country to stop selling leaded petrol did so in 2021.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Quick wins, slow systems

Child health has seen spectacular gains from targeted tools. The open question is whether that approach can finish the job.

Chapter 10

Two approaches, side by side

Targeted "vertical" programs

Strengths: fast, measurable, easy to fund and explain. Vaccines, bed nets and ORS reached hundreds of millions of children within a few decades.

Weaknesses: can create parallel systems that compete for staff; leave the hardest problems, such as newborn care, untouched; depend on donors who may move on.

Broad health-system building

Strengths: handles many problems at once, including those with no single fix; lasts after donors leave; reaches mothers and newborns.

Weaknesses: slow, expensive and hard to measure; results take years to show; easy for governments to under-fund.

Chapter 11

What went right, and what is stuck

The successes are well documented. Bangladesh, despite being poor and crowded, cut child deaths dramatically from the 1970s onward, through ORS taught door to door, family planning, vaccines, girls' education and community health workers. Rwanda rebuilt its health system after the 1994 genocide and cut child mortality steeply. Many countries in Asia and Latin America went from high to low child mortality within two generations.

What is stuck is just as clear. Progress slowed after about 2015. Newborn deaths fall slowly. Conflict zones, from the Sahel to Yemen and Sudan, have seen gains reversed. Malnutrition persists. And a child born in the country with the highest risk is dozens of times more likely to die before five than a child born in the country with the lowest.

Chapter 13

The longer view

Two long-term forces will shape the next generation. One is climate change. Children are more vulnerable to heat, to diseases carried by mosquitoes that spread to new areas, and to the floods and droughts that destroy crops and contaminate water. The other is urbanisation. More children will grow up in cities, often in informal settlements without sewers, where diarrhoeal disease and air pollution are major threats. That is why the next two files, on Sanitation and Clean water, are child health files too.

The deep lesson of the past seventy years is optimistic. Child deaths are not a fixed feature of poverty. Countries at the same income have very different child survival rates, depending on what their governments and communities choose to do. The tools are known, cheap and proven. The question is the will to deliver them.

The core idea

Child deaths have fallen dramatically, from roughly four in ten before age five around 1800 to under one in twenty-five today, mostly through cheap, simple tools: vaccines, ORS, breastfeeding, bed nets, clean water and better nutrition. Newborn deaths, malnutrition and conflict are the hardest remaining problems.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 4.8 million under-five deaths in 2023, down from more than 12 million in 1990.
  • Newborns (first 28 days) account for close to half of them.
  • Undernutrition is an underlying factor in about 45% of child deaths (Lancet, 2013).
The common mistake

Thinking saving children causes overpopulation. In practice, falling child deaths are followed by falling birth rates, as parents no longer need to plan for losses.
