Sharp Mind · Public Health & Society · Topic 05 of 20

Disease prevention. The best treatment is the one nobody ever needed.

A surgeon who saves a life gets thanks, a name and sometimes a film. A policy that quietly prevents a thousand strokes saves more lives and gets no thanks at all, because no one can point to the people it saved. That is the central problem of prevention. It works on numbers, not on faces. It often takes years to pay off. It can ask everyone to change a little so that a few are spared a lot. Yet prevention, much more than cure, explains why people today live decades longer than their great-grandparents.

Question 1 · What is it?

A fence at the top of the cliff

Prevention means acting before harm happens, or before it gets worse. It ranges from laws and sewers to a pill taken every morning.

Chapter 01

The ambulance and the fence

An old poem, often quoted in public health, tells of a town where people kept falling off a dangerous cliff. The townspeople argued about what to do. Most wanted to pay for an ambulance to wait in the valley below and carry the injured to hospital. A few suggested building a fence at the top. The ambulance won, because everyone could see it working.

The story captures something real. Treatment is visible: a patient, a doctor, a recovery. Prevention is invisible: a fall that did not happen, to a person who never knew they were at risk. Prevention is the fence, and fences are always harder to fund than ambulances. Yet the fence is usually cheaper, and it spares people the fall itself.

Chapter 02

The levels of prevention

Public health experts sort prevention by how early it acts. The categories help explain why the same disease can be attacked in very different ways.

LevelWhen it actsExample for heart diseaseExample for road deaths
PrimordialStops risk factors from appearing at allHealthy food environments, places to walk, no tobacco advertising to childrenSafer street design, speed limits
PrimaryStops the disease or injury from starting in people at riskQuitting smoking, treating high blood pressureSeat belts, drink-driving laws

TertiaryLimits damage once disease existsRehabilitation and medicines after a heart attackRehabilitation after injury

Some experts add a quaternary level: protecting patients from too much medicine, such as unnecessary tests and treatments. It is a reminder that prevention can cause harm too.

Chapter 03

Why prevention is hard to see

Suppose a country's blood-pressure programme prevents 2,000 strokes a year. No one can name the 2,000 people. Each of them simply goes on living, unaware that they would have had a stroke. Their success is a statistical fact, not a story. Politicians cannot visit them. Newspapers cannot interview them.

There is also a timing problem. Many preventive measures cost money now and pay off over decades. A government that bans smoking in public places pays the political cost this year. The fall in heart attacks and cancers comes over the following decades, often under a different government. Prevention asks people to pay today for benefits that are delayed, spread out and anonymous, which is exactly the kind of benefit human beings undervalue.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Find the cause, then change the world, the habit or the body

Prevention starts with knowing what causes a disease. Then there are three broad ways to act: change the environment, change behaviour, or intervene medically.

Chapter 04

First, find the cause

You cannot prevent what you do not understand. In 1950 two British researchers, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, published a study comparing patients with lung cancer to similar patients without it. Nearly all the lung cancer patients smoked. They then followed thousands of British doctors for decades, and found that smokers died much earlier, of lung cancer and many other diseases, and that those who stopped smoking gained back years of life.

This kind of work is epidemiology: the study of how diseases are distributed in populations and what causes them. It is the foundation of prevention, and the next topic, Epidemiology, explains its methods. The smoking studies matter here for one reason. Once a cause is proven, prevention becomes possible even before anyone understands the full biology. John Snow stopped a cholera outbreak decades before anyone saw the bacterium that caused it.

Chapter 05

Change the environment: prevention nobody has to remember

The most powerful prevention often requires no effort from individuals at all. It changes the world they live in so that the healthy choice is the default.

Food fortification

Adding iodine to salt prevents goitre and the brain damage that iodine deficiency causes in babies. Adding folic acid to flour, required in the United States from 1998 and in many other countries, reduced birth defects of the spine and brain (neural tube defects).

Road safety

Seat-belt laws, drink-driving limits, speed limits, safer car design and better road layout cut road deaths dramatically in many rich countries from the 1970s onward, even as traffic grew.

Water and sanitation

Clean water and sewers did more than any medicine to cut deaths from diarrhoeal disease, as Sanitation and Clean water explain.

Fluoridation

Adding small amounts of fluoride to drinking water reduces tooth decay. It is widely used in some countries and has been rejected or reversed in others, where the debate about optimal levels and consent continues.

Environmental prevention works for everyone at once, including the people least likely to follow health advice. That is why it tends to reduce inequality rather than widen it.

Chapter 06

Change behaviour: why information is not enough

The obvious way to prevent disease caused by behaviour is to tell people about the risk. It helps less than you would hope. Most smokers know smoking is dangerous. Most people know vegetables are good for them. Knowledge is necessary, but habits, prices, advertising, stress and the people around us usually matter more.

Tobacco control is the clearest lesson in what does work. Countries that cut smoking fastest used a package of measures together: high taxes, bans on advertising, smoke-free public places, graphic health warnings, and help for people trying to quit. Australia became the first country to require plain packaging, in 2012. In 2003 the WHO adopted the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, an international treaty that turned this package into a shared standard, and most countries have joined it.

The results can be large. In the United Kingdom, adult smoking fell from around 45 percent in the mid-1970s to about 12 percent by 2023. Smoking still kills, by WHO estimates, more than 8 million people a year worldwide, so the job is far from finished. But making the healthy choice cheaper, easier and more normal works far better than lecturing. The same logic now drives taxes on sugary drinks. In the UK, a levy on soft drinks introduced in 2018 led many manufacturers to cut the sugar in their products to avoid paying it.

Chapter 07

Medical prevention, and the number needed to treat

Some prevention happens through medicine. Vaccines are the most famous example (see Vaccination programs). Others are tablets: drugs that lower blood pressure or cholesterol, taken by people who feel perfectly well, to reduce their chance of a heart attack or stroke years later.

To judge such treatments, doctors use a simple measure: the number needed to treat (NNT). It answers the question, "How many people must take this for one of them to benefit?" If a drug taken for five years by 100 people prevents two heart attacks, the NNT is 50. The other 98 people took it without that particular benefit, either because they would never have had a heart attack anyway or because they had one despite the drug. All of them carried any side effects.

NNTs for preventive drugs are often in the tens or hundreds. That does not make them bad. For a cheap, safe drug and a serious outcome, treating a hundred to save one can be very worthwhile. But it explains why preventive medicine is a bet on averages, and why patients reasonably weigh it differently.

Chapter 08

Screening: when to look for trouble

Screening means testing healthy people to find hidden disease early. Volume 42 explains its traps: overdiagnosis, false alarms and the illusion of longer survival. Here, the question is how a health system decides whether a screening programme is worth running.

In 1968 the WHO published a set of principles by James Wilson and Gunnar Jungner that are still the basis of most decisions. In short: the condition should be an important health problem; there should be a recognisable early stage; there should be an acceptable, accurate test; early treatment should actually improve outcomes; and the whole programme should be worth its cost and its harms. Cervical screening, bowel cancer screening and newborn blood-spot tests meet these tests in many countries. Many tests that sound sensible fail them, which is why systems often decline to screen for conditions that worried patients ask about.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Most of the gains, most of the future burden

The diseases that kill most people today are largely preventable. And a single idea, the prevention paradox, explains why the best strategy is often surprising.

Chapter 09

The prevention paradox

In the 1980s the British epidemiologist Geoffrey Rose pointed out something counter-intuitive. Most heart attacks do not happen to the small group of people with very high blood pressure or cholesterol. They happen to the much larger group with slightly raised levels, simply because there are so many more of them.

That leads to a choice. The high-risk strategy finds the people at greatest risk and treats them intensively. It makes sense to each patient. The population strategy shifts the whole population a little, for example by lowering the salt in processed food so that everyone's blood pressure drops slightly. Each person gains very little. But across millions of people, the small shift prevents more disease than treating the high-risk few.

This is the prevention paradox: a measure that brings large benefits to a population offers little to each individual who takes part. It explains why people resist population measures ("why should I change, I'm fine?") even when they are the most effective option. Rose's ideas are still central to debates on salt, sugar, alcohol and air pollution.

Chapter 10

The size of the prize

The WHO estimates that non-communicable diseases, mainly heart disease, stroke, cancer, chronic lung disease and diabetes, cause around 41 million deaths a year, roughly three-quarters of all deaths worldwide. A large share of them are linked to a handful of risk factors: tobacco, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol use, and air pollution.

Not all of these deaths can be prevented, since everyone dies of something. The aim is to prevent early deaths and years lived with disability. Even so, the potential is huge. The decline in heart-disease death rates in many rich countries since the 1970s came partly from better treatment and partly from prevention, especially less smoking and better blood-pressure control. Studies that tried to divide the credit usually gave a large share, often around half, to changes in risk factors. Prevention is not a side project of medicine. It is where most of the remaining gains lie.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful, cheap, and politically difficult

Chapter 12

Two honest objections

"It is not always cheaper"

People often claim prevention saves money. Some measures do, such as many childhood vaccines and tobacco taxes. But reviews of cost studies find that many preventive measures cost more than they save, especially when large numbers must be treated to help a few, and because people who live longer use healthcare in later years. The fairer claim is that many are cost-effective: they buy health at a reasonable price, as treatments do.

"It limits freedom"

Critics argue that taxes on sugar or alcohol, bans and advertising rules interfere with adult choices and hit poorer people's budgets hardest. Supporters reply that industries already shape choices through price, marketing and product design, and that the burden of disease also falls hardest on the poor. Where a society draws the line is a question of values, and it differs between countries.

A third concern is commercial. Tobacco, alcohol, sugary-drink and ultra-processed food companies have strong interests in how prevention is designed. Public health researchers call these influences the commercial determinants of health. The tobacco industry's long campaign to cast doubt on the evidence about smoking, revealed in its own internal documents, is the warning case most often cited.

Chapter 14

The pill versus the policy

The coming debate in prevention is a tension between two visions. One is highly medical: find each person's risk with tests and genes, then give them the right drug. The other is environmental: change food, air, streets and prices so that everyone's risk falls without anyone needing a prescription.

Both will grow, and they are not really rivals. But the history of public health suggests caution about relying on the first alone. Medical prevention reaches those who come forward, and they tend to be the better-off. Environmental prevention reaches everyone. The fences that saved the most lives in history were built for whole populations, not prescribed one person at a time.

The core idea

Prevention acts at several levels: stopping risk factors appearing (primordial), stopping disease starting (primary), catching it early (secondary) and limiting damage (tertiary). Because most cases come from the moderate-risk majority, small shifts across a whole population often prevent more disease than intensive care for a few.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Non-communicable diseases cause about 41 million deaths a year, roughly three-quarters of all deaths (WHO).
  • Tobacco kills more than 8 million people a year (WHO).
  • UK adult smoking fell from about 45 percent (mid-1970s) to about 12 percent (2023).
The common mistake

Believing that telling people the facts is enough. Price, availability and defaults change behaviour far more than information alone.

Test yourself

Can you explain why cutting salt in all processed food could prevent more strokes than treating only people with very high blood pressure? If so, you understand the prevention paradox.

Stopping a disease or injury from starting.

Finding disease early, before symptoms, so treatment works better.
Tertiary prevention
Reducing the damage from a disease that already exists.
