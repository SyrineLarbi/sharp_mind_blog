Vaccination programs. The vaccine is the easy part. Reaching every child, every year, is the hard part.

A vaccine in a laboratory saves nobody. It has to be bought, kept cold across thousands of kilometres, given to the right child at the right age by a trained person, recorded, and given again for the next birth cohort, every year, forever. A vaccination program is that whole chain. When it works, it is invisible, because the diseases it prevents stop happening. That invisibility is its greatest achievement and its greatest weakness. People stop fearing diseases they have never seen, and they start fearing the vaccine instead.

General education

This is an explanation of how vaccination programs work, not medical advice. Questions about your own or your child's vaccines belong with a doctor, nurse or pharmacist who knows your history.

Question 1 · What is it?

A public service, not a product

A vaccination program is an organised, usually state-run effort to vaccinate a defined population on a planned schedule. Its goal is not only to protect each person, but to change how a disease behaves in the whole community.

Chapter 01

The last natural case of smallpox

In October 1977 a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, called Ali Maow Maalin fell ill with a rash. He had smallpox. He survived, and he became the last person on Earth known to catch the disease in the natural way. In 1980 the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated. A virus that had killed people for thousands of years, and by common estimates hundreds of millions in the twentieth century alone, was gone from nature.

The vaccine itself was old. The English doctor Edward Jenner had shown in 1796 that material from cowpox, a mild disease, protected people against smallpox. The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin vacca, cow. So why did eradication take until 1977? Because a vaccine that exists is not the same as a vaccine that reaches people. The final push, the WHO Intensified Smallpox Eradication Programme launched in 1967, was not a scientific breakthrough. It was a feat of organisation: money, logistics, record-keeping, and thousands of health workers searching villages for cases.

That is the subject of this file. Vaccines are a product of biology. Vaccination programs are a product of government, logistics and trust.

Two styles of delivery sit side by side. Routine immunisation is the steady, everyday work: each newborn gets the scheduled doses at clinics, year after year. Campaigns, sometimes called supplementary immunisation activities, are short bursts in which teams try to vaccinate every child in an area within days, whatever their past record. Routine work builds lasting protection. Campaigns patch the gaps routine work leaves.

Chapter 03

From one vaccine to a global system

In 1974, when only a small share of the world's children received basic vaccines, WHO launched the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI). It started with six diseases: tuberculosis, diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough (pertussis), polio and measles. Countries were helped to build national programs around a common schedule. Over the following decades the list grew to include hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b (a cause of childhood meningitis), pneumococcus and rotavirus (major causes of pneumonia and diarrhoea), and the human papillomavirus (HPV), which causes cervical cancer.

New vaccines were expensive, and poor countries could not afford them. In 2000 a partnership of governments, WHO, UNICEF, the World Bank and the Gates Foundation created Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance. Gavi pools demand from low-income countries, negotiates lower prices, and pays most of the cost at first. Countries pay a growing share as their incomes rise, until they "graduate" and pay the whole bill.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A chain that is only as strong as its weakest fridge

A program has to decide what to give, keep it cold, reach the people, and keep coverage high enough for long enough that the disease cannot spread.

Chapter 04

Herd immunity: the arithmetic of protection

Volume 28 explains the key number in an outbreak: how many people each case infects, on average. Epidemiologists call it the reproduction number, and in a population with no immunity, R0 ("R-nought"). Above one, cases multiply. Below one, the outbreak dies.

Vaccination lowers the effective number by removing people from the chain. If enough people are immune, each case finds too few people to infect, and the chain breaks. Even those who are not immune, such as newborns too young for a vaccine or people with weak immune systems, are then protected by the people around them. This is herd immunity, or community protection. A simple rule gives the threshold: the share that must be immune is roughly 1 minus 1/R0.

The herd immunity threshold, roughly
 disease      typical R0     share that must be immune
 -------      ----------     -------------------------
 flu-like       about 2      about 50%
 polio          5 to 7       about 80% to 85%
 measles       12 to 18      about 92% to 95%

 rule of thumb:  threshold = 1 - 1/R0

 measles is the warning light: it is so contagious that
 a small drop in coverage shows up as outbreaks first.

Two caveats matter. First, the threshold assumes immunity is spread evenly. In reality, unvaccinated people cluster in the same schools, neighbourhoods and religious communities, so a country can have 93% national coverage and still have towns at 60%, where outbreaks take off. Averages hide pockets, and diseases find pockets. Second, some vaccines prevent illness better than they prevent infection and onward spread. For those, "herd immunity" may never be fully reached, and vaccination mostly protects the vaccinated person.

Chapter 05

Who decides the schedule

Most countries have a committee of independent experts, called a National Immunization Technical Advisory Group (NITAG). In the US it is the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP); in the UK, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation (JCVI); in Germany, the Standing Committee on Vaccination (STIKO). WHO has its own global group, the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE). These bodies weigh the evidence and recommend. Governments decide and pay.

They ask the same questions each time. How much disease is there? How well does the vaccine work in this age group, and what are its side effects? At what age is a child most at risk? And, increasingly, is it good value compared with other uses of the same money? The UK, for example, formally weighs cost-effectiveness. This is why schedules differ between countries. Countries with little tuberculosis may not give BCG (the TB vaccine) to every baby. Countries where hepatitis A is rare may not include it.

Chapter 06

The cold chain and the last mile

Most vaccines must be stored between 2 and 8 °C. Too warm, and they slowly lose strength. Some, notably several freeze-sensitive ones, are ruined by freezing, which is a real risk in cheap fridges and cool boxes packed with ice. A spoiled vaccine looks exactly like a good one. The child gets the injection, the record says "vaccinated", and the child is not protected.

Programs fight this with simple tools. Many vials carry a vaccine vial monitor, a small square that darkens with accumulated heat, so a health worker can see at a glance whether the vial is still usable. Solar-powered fridges run clinics without reliable electricity. Volume 15 describes the wider cold chain for food and medicine (The cold chain).

The last mile, the final stretch from a district store to a child, is where most programs struggle. It means a nurse on a motorbike or a boat, on a day when the mother is free, with vaccine in stock and a working fridge at the other end.

Chapter 07

Measuring success: coverage, and the children with zero

The most watched number in global immunisation is DTP3 coverage: the share of infants who received all three doses of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine. It is used as a proxy because three visits show that a family has repeated contact with health services. Global DTP3 coverage rose from low levels in the 1970s to the mid-80s percent by the 2010s, fell during the COVID-19 pandemic, and had largely, but not fully, recovered by 2023.

Planners now focus on zero-dose children, those who never received even the first dose. Around 14 million infants a year were in this group in the early 2020s, concentrated in a small number of countries, in conflict zones, remote rural areas and urban slums. These children usually miss out on other services too: clean water, nutrition, a skilled birth attendant. Finding them is less a vaccine problem than a problem of reaching people the state does not yet reach. The pattern is explored further in Health inequality.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The cheapest lives ever saved, and the fragility of the result

Vaccination programs have saved more children than almost any other organised effort. They have also shown how quickly success can be lost.

Chapter 08

The scale of what was achieved

For the fiftieth anniversary of the EPI in 2024, a study led by WHO estimated that immunisation had saved at least 154 million lives since 1974, most of them infants. Measles vaccination alone accounted for a large majority of those. It is a model estimate, not a count, but its direction and rough size are not seriously disputed.

Economists also rate childhood vaccination among the best buys in health. The cost per child is small, the protection lasts years or a lifetime, and prevented illness saves medical bills and lost work. A program costs the price of a few visits per child. The disease it prevents can cost a life. That ratio is why vaccination appears near the top of almost every ranking of cost-effective health spending.

Chapter 09

Polio: the eradication that keeps nearly finishing

In 1988 the World Health Assembly launched the Global Polio Eradication Initiative. At that time polio paralysed an estimated 350,000 children a year in more than 120 countries. Since then, cases of wild polio have fallen by more than 99%. Two of the three types of wild poliovirus have been declared eradicated (type 2 in 2015, type 3 in 2019). As of 2025, wild type 1 still circulated only in Pakistan and Afghanistan.

The last steps have been the hardest, for reasons that are mostly human. In 2003 several states in northern Nigeria suspended polio vaccination for months after rumours that the vaccine was unsafe. In Pakistan, trust was damaged after it emerged in 2011 that the CIA had used a fake hepatitis vaccination drive to search for Osama bin Laden. Polio vaccinators there have been attacked and killed. The virus is not hard to stop. People's trust is hard to win back once it is lost.

There is also a technical twist. The oral polio vaccine contains a weakened live virus. It is cheap, easy to give as drops, and good at stopping spread. But in places where few children are vaccinated, the weakened virus can pass from child to child for a long time and slowly regain the ability to paralyse. These vaccine-derived outbreaks now cause more paralysis cases in some years than wild polio does. A newer oral vaccine (nOPV2), designed to be more genetically stable, has been used since 2021 to fight them. The final solution is to switch entirely to the injected, inactivated vaccine, which cannot cause this problem but costs more.

Chapter 10

Measles: what happens when coverage slips

Measles is the most contagious common human disease, which makes it the first to return when coverage drops. In 2019 the Pacific nation of Samoa, where routine measles coverage had fallen sharply, suffered an epidemic that killed more than 80 people, most of them young children. The government closed schools and ran an emergency mass campaign. The same year, several European countries lost their WHO "measles eliminated" status.

The US had declared measles eliminated in 2000, meaning no continuous spread for at least a year. In 2025 it recorded its largest annual number of cases in over three decades, centred on a large outbreak in West Texas, with deaths among unvaccinated children. Measles also does a hidden harm: it can wipe out part of the immune system's memory of other infections, a phenomenon researchers call immune amnesia. Children who recover may be more vulnerable to other diseases for months or years afterwards.

The trust problem in one story

In 1998 the British doctor Andrew Wakefield published a small study in The Lancet suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. Large studies in several countries found no such link. The paper was retracted in 2010 and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register for misconduct. But MMR coverage in Britain had already dropped, and measles came back. A single paper did lasting damage because fear spreads faster than reassurance.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous benefits, real costs, and hard choices

Nobody serious disputes that vaccination programs save lives on a vast scale. The honest debates are about side effects, compulsion, priorities and trust.

Chapter 12

Side effects, compensation and the mandate debate

Every vaccine has side effects. Most are trivial: a sore arm, a day of fever. Serious reactions are rare, but over millions of doses rare events happen to real people. History has real examples. In 1955, the "Cutter incident" in the US saw a faulty batch of polio vaccine containing live virus paralyse some children. In 2009-10 a pandemic flu vaccine, Pandemrix, was linked to an increase in narcolepsy in some European countries. In the Philippines in 2017, a dengue vaccine was found to raise the risk of severe dengue in children never previously infected, and the resulting scandal damaged confidence in vaccines generally.

These cases show both sides. The safety system worked: signals were spotted and acted on. And each event was used, often unfairly, to attack vaccines that had nothing to do with it. Many countries run no-fault compensation schemes, so people harmed by a recommended vaccine can be paid without proving negligence. The US set up its National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program in 1986.

On compulsion, countries differ. France expanded mandatory childhood vaccines from three to eleven in 2018. Italy made ten mandatory for school entry in 2017. Australia links some family benefits to vaccination ("No Jab, No Pay"). The UK and Scandinavian countries rely on voluntary programs with high trust. Supporters of mandates point to quick gains in coverage. Critics argue that mandates can deepen distrust in the groups that matter most, and that convenience, reminders and good conversations with health workers do more. The evidence suggests both tools can work, and that which one fits depends on why coverage is low: access problems need convenience, doubt needs trust, and complacency may respond to rules.

Chapter 14

The real future question: trust and money

Technology is not the main constraint. The two biggest open questions are political. The first is trust. WHO listed vaccine hesitancy, a delay or refusal despite availability, among its top ten threats to global health in 2019, before COVID-19 turned vaccines into a symbol in culture wars in many countries. In 2025 the new US health leadership replaced the members of the national vaccine advisory committee, which supporters described as overdue independent scrutiny and critics described as politicising science. However one judges such moves, they show that schedules are now contested in public in a way they rarely were.

The second is money. Gavi's budget depends on donor pledges, and several rich countries cut aid budgets in the mid-2020s. The COVID-19 pandemic showed the risk: COVAX, the scheme meant to share vaccines fairly, delivered a large number of doses in the end, but poor countries received them months after rich countries had bought most of the early supply. The next pandemic will test whether lessons were learned. See Pandemics and Disease prevention.

The deep lesson of fifty years is simple. Each new birth cohort starts with zero immunity. A program that succeeds for twenty years and then relaxes does not keep its gains. It hands the next generation the diseases its grandparents defeated.

The core idea

A vaccination program is the system that turns a vaccine into protection: a schedule chosen by experts, a cold chain, trained staff reaching every child, records, safety monitoring and trust. When coverage is high and even, herd immunity protects those who cannot be vaccinated. When it slips, the most contagious diseases return first.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Smallpox: last natural case 1977, declared eradicated 1980.
  • Immunisation saved an estimated 154 million lives in its first 50 years of the EPI (WHO-led study, 2024).
  • Measles needs about 95% immunity to stop spreading; polio about 80%.
  • Most vaccines must stay between 2 and 8 °C.
The common mistake

Looking at a national average and concluding all is well. Outbreaks start in pockets, among clusters of unvaccinated people, so local coverage matters more than the headline figure.

Test yourself

Can you explain why measles returns before other diseases when coverage drops, and why a country with 93% coverage can still have a deadly outbreak? If you can, you understand R0, the threshold and the pocket problem together.

The words, in plain words
Vaccination program
An organised, usually public, effort to vaccinate a population on a planned schedule.
Routine immunisation
The everyday delivery of scheduled vaccines to each new child, through clinics.
Campaign
A short, intense push to vaccinate everyone in a target group in an area, whatever their history.
Reproduction number (R0)
How many people one case infects, on average, in a population with no immunity.
Herd immunity
Protection of a whole community once enough people are immune that a disease cannot keep spreading.
Cold chain
The unbroken chain of refrigerated storage and transport from factory to patient.
Vaccine vial monitor
A heat-sensitive label on a vial that changes colour if the vaccine has been too warm for too long.
DTP3 coverage
The share of infants who got all three doses of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine, the standard measure of a program.
Zero-dose child
A child who has not received even the first routine vaccine.
NITAG
A national committee of independent experts that recommends which vaccines to use and when.
Vaccine-derived polio
Polio caused by the weakened virus in the oral vaccine after it spreads for a long time in an under-vaccinated community.
Vaccine hesitancy
Delay or refusal of vaccines even though they are available.
No-fault compensation
A scheme that pays people harmed by a recommended vaccine without them having to prove anyone was negligent.
