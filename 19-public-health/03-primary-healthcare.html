Primary healthcare. The least glamorous part of medicine, and the part that does the most.

Ask people to picture healthcare and they imagine a hospital: an operating theatre, a scanner, an ambulance. Yet most care, most of the time, happens somewhere quieter: a family doctor's room, a nurse's clinic, a pharmacy counter, a health worker visiting a village. This first level of care handles the great majority of health problems, catches serious illness early, and keeps long-term conditions under control. Countries with strong primary care tend to get better health for less money. Almost every country also underfunds it.

Question 1 · What is it?

The first door, and the one you keep coming back to

Primary care is where you go first, for almost anything, and where someone keeps track of you over years. Primary health care is a bigger idea that includes the community around it.

Chapter 01

A morning in a family practice

Picture a typical morning list for a family doctor in almost any country. A toddler with a fever. A man in his fifties whose blood pressure needs checking. A woman with low mood after a bereavement. A teenager asking about contraception. An elderly patient on eight different medicines who has started to fall. A builder with back pain. Someone with a cough that has lasted six weeks.

Most of these problems will be solved in the room. A few need tests. One or two might need a specialist. The cough is the one the doctor watches closely, because among a hundred ordinary coughs there is occasionally a lung cancer or tuberculosis. The skill of primary care is handling huge variety while spotting the rare serious case among the common harmless ones. It is a different skill from a specialist's, who knows one organ very deeply.

Chapter 02

Primary care and primary health care

Two phrases sound almost the same and mean different things. Primary care is the first level of clinical services: the general practitioner (GP), family doctor, practice nurse or local clinic. Primary health care (PHC) is a wider idea. It includes primary care, but also community action on health, such as clean water, nutrition, health education and involving people in decisions about their own health services.

The wider idea was set out in 1978 at a WHO and UNICEF conference in Alma-Ata, then in the Soviet Union and now Almaty in Kazakhstan. Its declaration called for "Health for All by the Year 2000" through primary health care. That target was missed by a long way. But the declaration changed how the world talks about health. It said health is a human right, that it depends on far more than doctors, and that care should be close to where people live and work. In 2018 countries renewed the commitment in the Declaration of Astana, at a conference held in the same country.

Chapter 03

The four Cs

The American researcher Barbara Starfield, who spent her career studying primary care, described four core features. They are now the standard way to judge whether primary care is really doing its job.

First contact

It is the place people go first for a new problem. It must be easy to reach: near, affordable, and open when needed.

Continuity

The same clinician or team knows the patient over time. They remember your history, so they notice when something changes.

Comprehensiveness

It deals with almost any problem, at any age, body or mind, rather than one disease or one organ.

Coordination

When a patient needs specialists, hospitals or social care, primary care organises it and pulls the pieces together.

A walk-in clinic that sees you once, for one problem, and never again may be convenient. But it offers only one of the four. The value of primary care lies in all four together, especially continuity.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Teams, lists, and health workers who walk

Primary care looks very different in a London suburb and in rural Ethiopia. The principles underneath are the same.

Chapter 04

Who does the work

The image of a single family doctor doing everything is out of date in most places. Modern primary care is a team. A typical team in a rich country might include GPs, practice nurses who run clinics for diabetes and asthma, nurse practitioners who can diagnose and prescribe, pharmacists who review medicines, physiotherapists, mental-health workers and administrative staff. In many countries, community pharmacies are also a first stop for minor illness.

Splitting work this way is called task-sharing, or task-shifting. Jobs that do not need a doctor's full training move to other trained professionals. A nurse can manage routine blood-pressure checks as well as a doctor, and often better, because she has more time. This frees doctors for the complex cases. The aim is that each person works at the top of their training, not below it.

Chapter 05

The list, the gate and the relationship

In countries such as the UK, the Netherlands, Denmark and Norway, every resident registers with one practice. The practice then has a list: a known population it is responsible for. Registration allows the practice to invite people for vaccinations and screening, track who has long-term conditions, and be paid per person (capitation, explained in Healthcare financing).

Registration usually comes with gatekeeping. Patients need a referral from their GP to see most specialists. This sends the right people up to expensive specialist care and keeps the wrong ones out. It also means the GP holds the full picture, so a cardiologist and a rheumatologist do not each prescribe without knowing about the other.

Where there is no registration, people often go straight to specialists or hospitals. That can feel faster. It tends to cost more, and nobody is in charge of the whole patient.

Chapter 06

Community health workers

In many low- and middle-income countries, primary care reaches villages not through doctors, who are scarce, but through community health workers. These are local people given months, not years, of training. They visit homes, treat common childhood illnesses, distribute bed nets and family-planning supplies, encourage vaccination and safe births, and refer serious cases to clinics.

The idea has deep roots. In China in the 1960s and 1970s, "barefoot doctors", farmers with basic medical training, brought simple care to the countryside. Later programmes became more structured. Brazil's Family Health Strategy, launched in 1994, assigns teams made up of a doctor, a nurse and several community health agents to defined neighbourhoods. It has grown to cover a large part of the population, and studies link its expansion to falls in infant mortality and avoidable hospital admissions. Ethiopia's Health Extension Program, begun in the early 2000s, trained tens of thousands of health extension workers, mostly women, and placed them in rural health posts. It is widely credited as one factor in the country's large fall in child deaths.

Why it works

A community health worker is trusted because she is from the village, speaks the language and knows the families. Simple, well-proven interventions, such as oral rehydration for diarrhoea or antibiotics for pneumonia in children, do not need a doctor. What they need is someone close by who is trained, supplied and supervised. Programmes fail when workers are unpaid, unsupplied or ignored by the clinics they refer to.

Chapter 07

The slow conditions: where primary care earns its keep

The biggest killers in most of the world today are long-term conditions: heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic lung disease and some cancers. They develop slowly and can often be controlled for decades. That control happens almost entirely in primary care.

Take high blood pressure. It usually causes no symptoms. Left alone, it damages arteries for years and then causes a stroke or a heart attack. Found early, it can be treated with cheap daily tablets. The work is undramatic: measure, prescribe, check again, adjust, encourage. Diabetes is similar: regular checks of blood sugar, feet, eyes and kidneys prevent blindness, amputations and kidney failure. Most of the benefit of modern medicine for chronic disease comes from these routine visits, not from heroic rescues. The prevention side is in Disease prevention.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

More health per unit of money

The evidence for strong primary care is unusually consistent. That makes its neglect all the more striking.

Chapter 08

What the evidence shows

Starfield and her colleagues compared countries and regions over many years. Their work found that places with stronger primary care tended to have lower death rates, better health on several measures, more equal outcomes between rich and poor, and lower total spending. These are associations, and countries differ in many ways, so the exact size of the effect is debated. But the direction has held up across many studies.

Continuity has its own evidence. A large Norwegian study published in 2022 followed patients over many years and found that those who had kept the same GP for longer were less likely to use out-of-hours services, less likely to be admitted to hospital, and less likely to die over the study period. Again, this is observational. Yet it fits what doctors and patients report: a clinician who knows you notices what a stranger would miss.

Health systems also track avoidable hospital admissions: stays for conditions, such as asthma, diabetes complications or heart failure, that good primary care should usually prevent. High rates are a warning sign that the first level is failing.

Chapter 09

The economics: why cheap care is not a second-best

A hospital stay for a stroke can cost more than many years of blood-pressure tablets and checks for the same patient. An emergency admission for an asthma attack costs more than many inhalers and a nurse's review. That is the simple economics of primary care: a small spend early often avoids a large one later, and it spares the patient the illness itself.

The WHO argues that most of the essential services needed for universal health coverage can be delivered through primary health care, and that investing in it is the most efficient route to coverage for poorer countries. A health budget that pours money into a few city hospitals while rural clinics lack nurses and medicines gets far less health for the same money.

Chapter 10

The inverse care law

In 1971 Julian Tudor Hart, a GP in a Welsh mining village, published a short paper in The Lancet that is still quoted today. His observation was blunt.

"The availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served."

Julian Tudor Hart · "The Inverse Care Law" · The Lancet, 1971

In plain words: the areas with the most illness tend to have the fewest doctors and the worst services. Poor neighbourhoods and remote regions find it hardest to attract and keep clinicians. Market forces make it worse, because doctors can earn more in wealthy areas. Half a century later, the pattern is still visible in many countries, rich and poor. Strong primary care, spread fairly, is one of the few tools that works against the inverse care law, which is why it features so heavily in Health inequality.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Strong foundations, constant strain

Primary care's strengths are real. So are the problems that keep it squeezed.

Chapter 12

The squeezed middle of medicine

In many rich countries, primary care is under strain. Patients are older and have more conditions at once. Hospitals discharge people sooner, sending more follow-up work back to the community. Guidelines add more checks to every visit. Meanwhile the number of family doctors has not kept up in many places, and surveys in several countries report high levels of burnout. A short appointment, often ten to fifteen minutes, must hold more and more.

Medical students can also see primary care as lower status and lower paid than specialties, so it can be hard to recruit. And because its benefits are slow and spread out, it struggles for money against hospitals, whose needs are visible and urgent. A crisis in a hospital makes the news. A slow decline in primary care shows up years later as a crisis in a hospital.

Chapter 14

Renewal, not replacement

Every few years a new idea promises to replace the family doctor: telephone hotlines, retail clinics, symptom checkers, now AI. Each has found a useful place. None has replaced the core functions Starfield described. The likeliest future is that the tools change and the job stays the same: a known team, easy to reach, that handles most problems, keeps track of you over time, and coordinates everything else.

The real question for the coming decades is political rather than technical: whether countries will move money and staff towards that first level, as nearly every expert report recommends, or keep spending most new money on hospitals. Where a country puts its next health dollar is the clearest sign of whether it believes in primary care.

The core idea

Primary care is the first, continuing, comprehensive and coordinating level of care. Primary health care adds community action on health. Strong primary care, especially with continuity, is linked to better health, fairer outcomes and lower costs.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1978: the Alma-Ata Declaration, "Health for All by the Year 2000".
  • 2018: the Declaration of Astana renewed the commitment.
  • 1971: Julian Tudor Hart's inverse care law.
The common mistake

Seeing primary care as a cheap filter in front of "real" medicine. It is where most illness is handled and most chronic disease is controlled, and it needs its own skills.

The words, in plain words
Primary care
The first level of clinical care: family doctors, practice nurses, local clinics.
Primary health care
Primary care plus community action on health, such as water, nutrition and education.
General practitioner (GP)
A doctor trained to handle all kinds of problems in patients of any age.
Secondary care
Specialist and general hospital care, usually reached by referral.
Gatekeeping
The rule that patients need a GP's referral to see most specialists.
Registration (list)
Each resident signing up with one practice, which then looks after them.
Continuity of care
Seeing the same clinician or team over time.
Task-sharing
Moving suitable tasks from doctors to other trained health workers.
Community health worker
A local person with short training who provides basic care and advice in the community.
Chronic condition
A long-lasting illness, such as diabetes or high blood pressure, that needs ongoing care.
Avoidable admission
A hospital stay that good primary care could usually have prevented.
Inverse care law
The tendency for good care to be least available where it is most needed.
