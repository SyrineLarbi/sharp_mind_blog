Healthcare financing. Follow the money and you will understand why doctors do what they do.

Health is a strange thing to pay for. You cannot predict when you will need it, the bill can be larger than a year's income, and the seller knows far more than the buyer. So almost no country leaves it to ordinary shopping. Instead, money is collected from many people, put into a shared pot, and used to buy care for whoever falls ill. How that is done, and above all how the people who provide care are paid, shapes waiting lists, prices, and even which operations are performed.

Question 1 · What is it?

The plumbing that moves money from the healthy to the sick

Healthcare financing is the set of arrangements that collect money for health and pay it out for care. It exists because health spending behaves unlike almost any other spending.

Chapter 01

Why you cannot just buy healthcare like shoes

When you buy shoes, you know roughly when you need them, you can compare prices, and you can judge the result yourself. Healthcare breaks all three rules. In 1963 the economist Kenneth Arrow, later a Nobel laureate, wrote a famous paper explaining why. Its title says it: "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care".

First, need is unpredictable. Most years you spend almost nothing. Then one year you have a heart attack or a premature baby, and the bill is enormous. Second, the costs are concentrated. In any population, a small group of very sick people accounts for most spending. In the United States, for example, survey data consistently show the costliest 5 percent of people account for around half of all health spending. Third, the patient cannot easily judge what they are buying. They depend on the doctor's advice, and the doctor may also be the one who is paid for the treatment.

Put these together and the answer is obvious. People need protection against the risk of large, sudden costs, not just a way to pay small ones. That protection is what financing systems provide, whether through a government, a social fund or a private insurer.

Chapter 02

Three jobs: raise, pool, buy

The World Health Organization breaks financing into three functions. Every system, rich or poor, does all three, well or badly.

1 · Raising revenue

Collecting money: taxes, compulsory contributions from wages, insurance premiums, patients' own payments, and in poorer countries foreign aid.

2 · Pooling

Putting that money into shared funds, so that the healthy pay for the sick and the costs of illness are spread across many people and across time.

3 · Purchasing

Paying providers: deciding which services to buy, from whom, at what price, and by which method of payment.

This simple frame clears up much confusion. When people argue about "public" versus "private" healthcare, they often mix the three jobs. A system can raise money through taxes (public), pool it in a single national fund (public), and buy care from private clinics (private). That is roughly Canada. Ask about each job separately and most debates become clearer.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From your pay slip to the surgeon's bank account

Each of the three jobs has a handful of standard methods. The choices made at each step create the incentives that everyone in the system then responds to.

The mix matters because each source shares the burden differently. Income taxes usually take a larger share from richer people. Wage contributions miss people who work informally, which in many countries is most workers. Flat premiums take the same amount from a cleaner and a banker. Out-of-pocket payment is the least fair source of all, because it charges people exactly when they are sick.

Chapter 04

Pooling, and the problem of who joins

Pooling is the heart of financing. If a thousand people each put in a modest sum, the pot can pay for the two or three of them who need a costly operation this year. Next year it may be someone else. The bigger and more varied the pool, the more predictable its costs, just as a large insurer can predict how many of its customers will crash their cars.

Pooling has a weakness. If joining is voluntary, the people most likely to join are those who expect to be ill. Healthy people stay out, so the average cost rises, premiums go up, more healthy people leave, and the pool can collapse. Economists call this adverse selection, and the spiral it causes is sometimes called a "death spiral". Insurers respond by refusing or overcharging people with existing illnesses, which leaves the sickest without cover.

The standard solution is to make membership compulsory. Tax-funded systems do this automatically, since everyone pays tax. Bismarck-style countries make insurance mandatory. Switzerland (since 1996) and the Netherlands (since 2006) require everyone to buy a basic private policy, forbid insurers from refusing anyone for that policy, and help poorer people with the cost. The US Affordable Care Act of 2010 tried a similar mix. Compulsion is not an accident of left-wing politics. It is the technical answer to adverse selection.

Risk adjustment

When several competing funds or insurers share a market, one may attract older, sicker members. Countries such as Germany and the Netherlands therefore move money between funds according to the age and health of their members. This stops insurers from competing by avoiding sick people, and pushes them to compete on service instead.

Chapter 05

How providers are paid, and why it changes what they do

This is the part most people never think about, and it may matter most. Doctors and hospitals are human. They respond to how they are paid, often without noticing. Here are the main methods.

MethodWhat is paidThe quiet incentive
Fee-for-serviceA price for each visit, test or procedureDo more: more tests, more visits, more operations
SalaryA fixed wage regardless of activityNeutral on volume, but little reward for extra effort
CapitationA fixed amount per registered patient per yearKeep patients healthy, but also avoid costly patients and refer them on
Case-based (DRG)One price per hospital stay, set by the diagnosisTreat efficiently and discharge quickly, sometimes too quickly
Global budgetA fixed yearly sum for the whole hospitalControl costs, but waiting lists can grow
Pay-for-performanceBonuses for meeting quality targetsImprove measured things, possibly neglect unmeasured ones

A DRG (diagnosis-related group) system sorts hospital stays into categories, such as "hip replacement without complications", and pays a fixed price for each. The US Medicare programme introduced this in 1983, and most European countries later adopted versions. Before it, a hospital paid per day had a reason to keep patients longer. After it, average stays fell.

No method is neutral. Each one tilts behaviour in some direction. That is why most systems blend them, for example paying family doctors partly by capitation and partly by fees for specific services they want more of, such as vaccinations.

Chapter 06

Co-payments and the great insurance experiment

Should patients pay something at the time of care? Supporters say a small charge stops people using care they do not really need. Economists call that unnecessary use moral hazard: when someone else pays, people consume more. The charges come in several forms. A deductible is an amount you pay each year before insurance starts. A co-payment is a fixed fee per visit or prescription. Co-insurance is a percentage of the bill.

The best evidence comes from the RAND Health Insurance Experiment in the United States, which ran from the 1970s into the early 1980s. Thousands of families were randomly given plans that ranged from free care to plans with heavy cost-sharing. Those who paid more used noticeably less care. The twist was that they cut useful care about as much as useless care. Patients are not good at telling which visit is necessary. For most participants, health outcomes looked similar. But poorer and sicker people, such as those with high blood pressure, did worse when they had to pay.

The lesson most systems draw is to keep charges low or zero for essential and preventive care, and for low-income and chronically ill patients, while using modest charges elsewhere.

Chapter 07

When the seller creates the demand

The other side of moral hazard sits with providers. Because the doctor both advises and sells, a doctor paid by the procedure may, consciously or not, recommend more procedures. This is called supplier-induced demand. It is hard to measure, and its size is debated, but the pattern appears in many places: rates of some elective operations vary widely between regions with similar populations, often tracking how many surgeons and beds each region has.

The fix is not to distrust doctors personally. It is to design payment so that doing the right thing and earning a living point the same way. Good financing makes it easy for honest professionals to behave well.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Budgets, bankruptcies and the price of a pill

Health is often the largest or second-largest item in a rich government's budget, and the way it is financed decides whether illness can push a family into poverty.

Chapter 08

Why the bill keeps rising

In most rich countries, health spending has grown faster than the economy for decades. Three forces drive it. New technology is the biggest: medicine can do far more than it could, and doing more costs more. Ageing adds to it, since older people have more long-term conditions. And rising incomes raise expectations: richer societies choose to spend more of their wealth on staying alive and comfortable.

A fourth force has a famous name. The economist William Baumol noticed that some services, such as playing a string quartet, cannot be made much more productive. It still takes four musicians. Yet their wages must rise along with wages elsewhere in the economy. So these services get steadily more expensive relative to manufactured goods. This is Baumol's cost disease, and much of healthcare, especially nursing and care for the elderly, is a labour-intensive service of exactly this kind.

Rising spending is not automatically bad. People value health highly. The problem is that most of it is paid from shared funds, so it competes with schools, pensions and roads.

Chapter 09

The out-of-pocket line

The WHO's World Health Report of 2010, on financing, made an influential observation. Financial catastrophe from health costs becomes rare only when out-of-pocket payments fall to around 15 to 20 percent of a country's total health spending. In many low- and lower-middle-income countries the share is far higher, sometimes more than a third or even half.

When people pay directly, two things happen. Some pay and are impoverished. Others simply do not go, which never shows up in a spending survey. A mother who does not take her feverish child to a clinic because of the fee is invisible in the statistics until the child dies. The cost of out-of-pocket financing is measured partly in money and partly in care that never happened.

Chapter 10

Finding the money in poorer countries

Low-income countries face a hard arithmetic. Most workers are informal, so payroll contributions reach few of them. Tax collection is weak. Health budgets may be a small sum per person per year. In 2001, African Union leaders signed the Abuja Declaration, pledging to spend at least 15 percent of government budgets on health. Only a few countries have reached it in any given year.

For decades, donors filled part of the gap, especially through programmes for HIV, tuberculosis, malaria and vaccines, such as the Global Fund and Gavi. Those programmes saved millions of lives. They also created dependence, and many countries are now being pushed to finance more themselves. That push became urgent in 2025, when major donors, above all the United States, cut global health aid sharply.

Common tools now discussed include "sin taxes" on tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks, which raise money and reduce harm at the same time, and better use of existing budgets. There is no shortcut. Sustainable coverage requires domestic, pooled, mostly public money. More on the access side is in Healthcare access in developing countries.

Chapter 12

Two arguments that never end

Competition or a single buyer?

Supporters of competing insurers, as in the Netherlands or Switzerland, argue that choice pushes insurers to serve patients better and to innovate. Supporters of a single payer, as in Taiwan or Canada, argue that one buyer has more power to hold down prices and spends far less on administration and marketing. Both systems can achieve universal coverage. Evidence suggests single-payer systems are cheaper to administer; whether competition improves quality is still contested.

Should the patient pay at all?

One side says a charge at the point of use teaches people that care has a cost and reduces waste. The other side points to the RAND results: patients cut good care along with bad, and the poor and sick suffer most. Most systems now settle on a compromise of small charges with exemptions for children, the poor, and people with chronic illnesses.

Chapter 14

The million-dollar cure problem

Some new treatments are one-off cures or near-cures with prices in the millions of dollars. Gene therapies for rare inherited diseases have been launched at around two million dollars or more per patient. New drugs for very common conditions create the opposite problem: modest prices per person, but so many potential patients that the total bill is huge. Weight-loss drugs such as semaglutide are the leading example (see Vol 13 · GLP-1 drugs).

Financing systems were built to pay for care year by year. They are not designed for a single huge payment today that saves costs over decades, or for millions of people wanting a new drug at once. Ideas include paying in instalments over years, linking payment to results, and pooling rare disease costs across countries. The coming test for financing is not just spending more but paying wisely for medicine that is suddenly far more powerful.

The core idea

Financing does three jobs: raise money, pool it so the healthy cover the sick, and purchase care. Compulsory pooling solves adverse selection. The way providers are paid (fee-for-service, salary, capitation, DRG, budgets) quietly shapes what they do.

The numbers worth knowing
  • In the US, the costliest 5 percent of people account for around half of health spending.
  • Financial catastrophe becomes rare when out-of-pocket payments fall below about 15–20 percent of health spending (WHO, 2010).
  • The Abuja target: 15 percent of government budgets for health (2001).
The common mistake

Thinking user charges only cut waste. Randomised evidence shows people cut useful care as readily as useless care, and the poor and sick are hurt most.
