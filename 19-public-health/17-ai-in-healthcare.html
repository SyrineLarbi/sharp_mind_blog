AI in healthcare. Brilliant at one narrow task, and only as fair as the data it learned from.

Few subjects attract more confident predictions than artificial intelligence in medicine. One camp says software will soon read every scan and replace half of all doctors. The other says it is hype that will hurt patients. Both are wrong in useful ways. Medical AI is already doing real work in a few areas, failing quietly in others, and changing the parts of healthcare nobody films, such as paperwork. This file explains what these systems are, how they are built and checked, where they help, where they cause harm, and how to judge the next headline.

General education

This explains how medical AI works and is governed. It is not medical advice. A chatbot's answer about your symptoms is not a diagnosis; check anything important with a qualified clinician.

Question 1 · What is it?

Software that learns patterns from past cases

Almost all medical AI in use today is narrow. Each system does one task, learned from thousands or millions of examples, and knows nothing outside it.

Chapter 01

A photograph of the back of the eye

Diabetes can damage the tiny blood vessels at the back of the eye, a condition called diabetic retinopathy. Caught early, it can be treated and blindness prevented. Caught late, the damage is permanent. The test is simple: a photograph of the retina. The problem is volume. Hundreds of millions of people worldwide have diabetes, each needs regular checks, and there are not enough eye specialists to look at all the pictures.

In 2018 the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) authorised a system called IDx-DR. A nurse or technician in a family doctor's office takes the photographs, and the software decides whether the patient has more than mild retinopathy and should be referred. No specialist looks at the image first. It was the first AI system cleared in the US to make a screening decision on its own.

The case shows what medical AI is good at. The task is narrow, repetitive and high-volume. There is a clear right answer that experts largely agree on. And the alternative is often not "a specialist looks instead", but "nobody looks at all". The strongest case for medical AI is not beating doctors. It is reaching patients that no doctor would have reached.

Chapter 02

Four kinds of medical AI

KindWhat it doesExamples
PerceptionFinds patterns in images, signals and samplesReading mammograms, retinal photos, ECGs, skin photos, pathology slides
PredictionEstimates the chance that something will happenSepsis alerts, risk of readmission, deterioration warnings on a ward
LanguageReads, writes and summarises text and speechDrafting clinical notes from a recorded consultation, answering patient messages
DiscoverySearches huge spaces of molecules or biological structuresPredicting protein shapes, suggesting candidate drugs

The four behave differently. Perception tools can be checked against a clear answer: the biopsy did or did not show cancer. Prediction tools are harder, because acting on a prediction changes what happens next. Language tools are the newest and the most general, and they can produce fluent text that is wrong. Discovery tools are far from the patient and are tested later, in ordinary clinical trials. Asking "does AI work in medicine?" is like asking "do drugs work?". It depends entirely on which one, for what.

Chapter 03

What "learning" means here

Traditional medical software followed rules written by people: "if the potassium level is above this number, show a warning". Most modern medical AI uses machine learning instead. Nobody writes the rules. The system is shown many examples, each labelled with the right answer, and adjusts millions of internal settings until its answers match the labels as closely as possible. The methods are explained in Machine learning and, for images, Computer vision.

The consequence is important. The system learns whatever pattern best predicts the label in the data it was given, whether or not that pattern is the disease. Volume 13 tells the well-known case of a chest X-ray model that partly learned to recognise portable scanners rather than illness. That is not a rare bug. It is the default behaviour of pattern-learning, and the main reason why testing matters so much.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Data in, model trained, tested elsewhere, approved, then watched

A medical AI tool's life has more stages than people expect, and most of the failures happen between the laboratory result and the hospital ward.

Chapter 04

How accuracy is measured

Two numbers describe any test, human or machine. Sensitivity is the share of people who have the disease that the test correctly flags. Specificity is the share of people without the disease that the test correctly clears. A screening tool for a dangerous cancer wants high sensitivity, so few cases are missed. But push sensitivity up and specificity usually falls, so more healthy people are called back. Every AI tool sets a threshold that trades one against the other.

Many papers also report a single summary called the AUC (area under the curve). It runs from 0.5, no better than a coin toss, to 1.0, perfect ranking. An AUC of 0.9 sounds impressive. On its own it says nothing about how many false alarms staff will face, how the tool behaves in a different population, or whether patients end up better off. Volume 42 explains why a test result is a probability, not a verdict (A result is a probability). A good score on a test set is the start of the evidence, not the end.

Chapter 05

The data: where it comes from and who agreed

Models need large amounts of patient data, and that data belongs, in a moral sense, to patients. In 2017 the UK's data protection regulator ruled that the Royal Free hospital trust in London had failed to comply with data protection law when it shared about 1.6 million patient records with Google's DeepMind to test a kidney-injury alert app. Patients had not been properly informed. The app itself aimed at something useful; the way the data moved did not respect the people it described.

Since then, many health systems have built trusted research environments: secure computers where approved researchers can analyse data without taking copies away. Another approach, federated learning, trains a model across several hospitals without moving patient data at all. Each site trains locally and only the learned settings are combined. Both try to answer the same question. How do you learn from millions of patients without betraying any of them?

A second data problem is representation. If a skin-cancer model is trained mostly on photos of light skin, it will be less reliable on dark skin. If a model is trained in one country's hospitals, it learns that country's patients, equipment and habits of recording.

Chapter 06

External validation: the sepsis alarm

Sepsis is the body's extreme response to an infection, and it can kill within hours. Early treatment saves lives, so an alert that spots it early is valuable. One of the largest US electronic health record companies, Epic, offered a sepsis prediction model that was used in hundreds of hospitals.

In 2021 researchers at the University of Michigan tested the model on their own patients, a step called external validation. They found that it missed about two-thirds of the sepsis cases, while still generating alerts for many patients who did not have sepsis. It performed much worse than had been suggested. The company disputed parts of the analysis and later revised its approach, but the episode became a standard example. A model must be tested on patients it has never seen, in hospitals that did not build it, before anyone relies on it. Every hospital is a slightly different world.

Chapter 07

Regulating software that might keep learning

Most countries regulate medical AI as a kind of medical device, under the label software as a medical device. The FDA keeps a public list of AI-enabled devices it has authorised. By 2025 it had passed a thousand, and roughly three-quarters were in radiology. Most were cleared through the "equivalent to an existing device" route described in Healthcare technology, and relatively few came with evidence from trials showing that patients did better.

A special problem is software that changes. A "locked" model behaves the same every day. An "adaptive" one keeps learning from new data, which could improve it or quietly make it worse. US regulators now let makers file a plan in advance describing how a model may be updated. The EU's AI Act, adopted in 2024, treats most medical AI as high-risk, adding requirements on data quality, human oversight and record-keeping to the existing device rules. See AI regulation. The general direction is clear: approval at launch is not enough; performance must be monitored in use, as a drug's side effects are.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Too few clinicians, too much data, and old biases that software can copy

Health systems everywhere face rising demand and staff shortages. AI promises to stretch scarce expertise, but it can also scale up old mistakes.

Chapter 08

Screening at scale: the mammogram trial

In many European screening programmes, each mammogram is read by two radiologists, a safeguard that needs a lot of specialist time. Sweden ran a large randomised trial, known as MASAI, in which roughly 80,000 women were split into two groups. One had standard double reading. In the other, AI sorted the images by risk: low-risk scans were read by one radiologist, high-risk scans by two, and the software highlighted suspicious areas.

Results published in 2023 found that the AI-supported group had about 20% more cancers detected, without a rise in false positives, while the radiologists' reading workload fell by about 44%. This is the kind of evidence that matters, because it compares doctors with AI against doctors without it, in a real programme. Longer follow-up is still needed to check whether the extra cancers found were ones that mattered, rather than slow cancers that would never have caused harm.

Chapter 09

The quiet revolution: paperwork

The use of AI that spread fastest in 2024 and 2025 was not diagnostic at all. It was the ambient scribe. With the patient's consent, a device records the consultation, and a language model drafts the clinical note, the referral letter or the summary for the patient. The clinician reviews and signs it. Doctors in many countries spend a large share of their day on documentation, and early users report that scribes give them back time and let them look at the patient instead of the screen.

The risks are those of language models in general. They can drop an important detail, or insert something that was never said, as Volume 49 explains (Why it invents things). In a medical note such an error can travel through the record for years. The safety of an AI scribe depends on whether the clinician genuinely checks the draft, and on whether a busy clinician will keep doing that after the hundredth correct one.

Chapter 10

Bias written into the data

In 2019 a team led by the economist and physician Ziad Obermeyer published a study in Science of an algorithm used across US health systems to pick patients for extra care programmes. It predicted future health costs as a stand-in for health needs. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same level of illness, the algorithm judged them healthier than they were. At the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker. Correcting the target would have greatly increased the share of Black patients selected for help.

Nobody had programmed race into the model. The bias came from the choice of what to predict. An algorithm that learns from an unequal system will learn the inequality, and then apply it faster and at larger scale. The authors worked with the maker to fix it, which shows the problem is solvable once it is looked for. The general mechanism is in Volume 49 (Bias, and where it comes from) and the social context in Health inequality.

Chapter 11

Far from the bedside: discovery

Proteins are the working machines of the body, and their function depends on their folded three-dimensional shape. Working out a single shape by experiment could take years. In 2020 a system from Google DeepMind called AlphaFold predicted protein shapes with accuracy close to experiments in an international competition. Its makers, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, together with David Baker for designing new proteins.

AI is also changing the search for medicines. In 2020 researchers at MIT used a deep-learning model to identify a molecule, which they named halicin, that killed many drug-resistant bacteria in laboratory tests. Several drug candidates designed largely with AI have since entered clinical trials. AI can shorten the search for a candidate; it cannot shorten the trials that prove a drug is safe in people, and that is where most drug costs and failures lie.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Scale and consistency, against hidden errors and unclear responsibility

The features that make AI attractive, speed, scale and tirelessness, are the same ones that let a single error reach millions of patients.

Chapter 13

Who is responsible when it is wrong?

Suppose an AI tool says a scan is clear, the doctor agrees, and the patient's cancer is missed. Is the doctor to blame for trusting the tool, the hospital for buying it, or the maker for building it? Most legal systems have not settled this. In practice, the clinician who signs off usually carries the responsibility. That pushes doctors to double-check everything, which removes much of the time the tool was meant to save, or to trust it, which is risky. This tension is known as the "human in the loop" problem: a human is formally in charge but may not be in a position to catch the machine's mistakes.

There is also deskilling. A clinician who always works with an assistant may lose some ability to work without one, as pilots can with autopilot. Early studies in areas such as colonoscopy have raised this concern, and it is still being researched. The safest systems are designed so that the human and the machine catch different kinds of error, rather than the human simply rubber-stamping the machine.

Chapter 15

Four questions for any medical AI headline

The flow of announcements will only grow. Four questions sort most of them. First, was it tested on new patients, in a different place? Second, did it compare clinicians with the tool against clinicians without it? Third, did patients end up better off, with fewer deaths, earlier treatment or fewer harms, or did only an accuracy score improve? Fourth, who was missing from the data?

A headline that cannot answer these questions is describing a promising experiment, not a change in care. The realistic future is neither a robot doctor nor a hype bubble. It is a long, uneven process of fitting narrow tools into real work, one tested use at a time. The wider shape of that future is in Future healthcare systems, and the broader debate on AI and jobs in How work may change because of AI.

The core idea

Medical AI is mostly narrow software that learns patterns from labelled past cases, for perception, prediction, language or discovery. It learns whatever predicts the label, whether or not that is the disease, so it must be validated externally, compared against clinicians without it, and monitored in use. It can scale expertise, and it can scale bias.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 2018: the first autonomous AI screening system (diabetic eye disease) authorised in the US.
  • The FDA's list of AI-enabled devices passed 1,000 by 2025, about three-quarters in radiology.
  • Sweden's MASAI trial (2023): about 20% more cancers found, with about 44% less reading work.
The common mistake

Treating "AI beat doctors on a test set" as "AI improves care". The question that counts is whether doctor-plus-AI beats doctor alone, for real patients.

Test yourself

Can you explain how an algorithm that never saw anyone's race could still treat Black patients unfairly? If you can, you have understood that the choice of target matters as much as the data.

The words, in plain words
Machine learning
Software that learns rules from labelled examples instead of being given them.
Narrow AI
A system built for one specific task, with no ability outside it.
Sensitivity
The share of people with a condition that a test correctly flags.
Specificity
The share of people without a condition that a test correctly clears.
AUC
A single score from 0.5 (chance) to 1.0 (perfect) for how well a model ranks cases.
External validation
Testing a model on patients from places that did not help build it.
Software as a medical device
Software regulated as a medical product because it informs diagnosis or treatment.
Federated learning
Training a model across several sites without moving patients' data.
Ambient scribe
A tool that listens to a consultation and drafts the clinical note.
Sepsis
The body's extreme, life-threatening reaction to an infection.
Automation bias
The tendency to accept a machine's answer, even when it is wrong.
Deskilling
The loss of skills that a person no longer practises because a tool does the task.
AlphaFold
An AI system that predicts the 3D shape of proteins from their sequence.
