Epidemiology. Detective work on whole populations, using counting as the magnifying glass.

A doctor looks at one patient and asks what is wrong with them. An epidemiologist looks at thousands of people and asks why some get ill and others do not. That simple change of view, from the person to the population, found the link between smoking and cancer, the risk factors for heart disease, and the cause of many outbreaks long before anyone saw the germ. It is also the science behind most health headlines, which is why knowing its basic tools is one of the best defences against being misled.

Question 1 · What is it?

Who gets ill, where, when, and why

Epidemiology is the study of how health and disease are distributed in populations, and of the factors that determine that distribution. Its first great case was solved with a map and a water bill.

Chapter 01

John Snow's second, better proof

Volume 28 tells the famous part: in 1854 the London doctor John Snow mapped cholera deaths around a water pump in Broad Street, Soho, and persuaded officials to remove its handle. But the map alone convinced few people. Most doctors then believed cholera spread through bad air, or "miasma", and a cluster around a pump could be explained in other ways.

Snow's stronger evidence came from a different investigation. In south London, two private companies supplied water to homes on the same streets. One, the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, took its water from the Thames downstream of the city's sewage outlets. The other, the Lambeth Company, had moved its intake upstream, to cleaner water. Neighbours breathed the same air, lived in similar houses and were often equally poor. The main difference was the pipe into their house.

Snow went door to door finding out which company supplied each home where someone had died of cholera. The death rate in homes supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall was many times higher than in homes supplied by Lambeth. Snow had found a "natural experiment": chance had divided people into two groups that differed mainly in the suspected cause. That design, comparing groups alike in everything except the thing you are studying, is still the heart of epidemiology.

Chapter 02

Person, place and time

Epidemiologists start any problem by describing it along three axes. Person: who is affected, by age, sex, job, income, habits? Place: where do cases cluster, by country, neighbourhood, school or water supply? Time: when did cases begin, is there a season, are they rising or falling?

These simple questions solve a surprising number of puzzles. If a food poisoning outbreak affects only people who attended one wedding, and cases began a day later, the search narrows quickly to the food served there. If a cancer is rising only among young adults in one region, investigators ask what changed there, and when. Description comes before explanation, and good description often points straight to the answer.

Chapter 03

Counting properly: rates and risks

Raw numbers mislead. A city of ten million will have more cancer cases than a town of ten thousand, simply because it has more people. So epidemiologists use rates: cases divided by the population at risk, over a period of time.

MeasureWhat it countsExample
IncidenceNew cases in a periodNumber of new diabetes diagnoses per 1,000 adults this year
PrevalenceAll existing cases at a point in timeShare of adults living with diabetes today
Mortality rateDeaths in a population over a periodDeaths from heart disease per 100,000 people per year
Case fatality rateShare of diagnosed cases who die of the diseaseDeaths among people confirmed to have a given infection
Relative riskRisk in an exposed group divided by risk in an unexposed groupSmokers' lung cancer risk compared with non-smokers'

Incidence and prevalence are easy to confuse. A disease that is quickly cured or quickly fatal can have high incidence but low prevalence. A disease people live with for decades, such as diabetes, has high prevalence even with modest incidence. Prevalence is like water in a bath; incidence is the tap, and recovery or death is the drain.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Comparing groups, and fighting the traps

Every epidemiological study compares groups. The art is in choosing the groups so that the comparison is fair, and in spotting the many ways it can be unfair.

Chapter 04

The main study designs

DesignHow it worksFamous example
Cross-sectionalA snapshot: measure exposure and disease in a population at one timeNational health surveys measuring obesity and blood pressure
Case-controlStart with people who have the disease (cases) and similar people who do not (controls); compare their past exposuresDoll and Hill's 1950 study of lung cancer patients and their smoking
CohortStart with healthy people, record exposures, follow them for years and see who gets illThe British Doctors Study (from 1951); the Framingham Heart Study (from 1948)
Randomised controlled trialAssign people to an exposure or treatment by chance, then compare outcomesVaccine and drug trials

Case-control studies are fast and cheap, and good for rare diseases: you need only find the cases. Cohort studies are slow and costly, but show what came first, and can study many diseases at once. The Framingham Heart Study, which began in 1948 in a town near Boston, followed thousands of residents and helped establish high blood pressure, high cholesterol and smoking as causes of heart disease. It also made the phrase "risk factor" common.

Randomised trials are the strongest test of cause, because chance, not choice, decides who is exposed. But you cannot randomly assign people to smoke for thirty years, or to live near a factory. Much of what we know about the causes of disease had to come from observational studies, which is why epidemiology's methods for handling their weaknesses matter so much.

Chapter 05

Confounding: the hidden third factor

Suppose a study finds that coffee drinkers have more lung cancer. Does coffee cause lung cancer? Probably not. In many past populations, coffee drinkers were also more likely to smoke. Smoking is linked to both coffee drinking and lung cancer, so it creates a false link between them. This is confounding, and the hidden factor is a confounder.

How a confounder creates a false link
                 SMOKING  (the confounder)
                /        \
      linked to          causes
              /            \
      COFFEE  - - - - - - -  LUNG CANCER
               apparent link,
               but no direct cause

Epidemiologists fight confounding in several ways. They measure likely confounders and adjust for them statistically. They match cases and controls on age, sex and other factors. They restrict a study, for example to non-smokers only. But they can only adjust for what they measured. An unmeasured confounder is the permanent ghost in every observational study.

Chapter 06

Bias, and a cautionary tale about hormones

Bias is a systematic error that pushes a result in one direction. Three kinds are common. Selection bias: the people studied differ from the population in a way that matters, like the returning aircraft in Volume 39. Recall bias: people with a disease search their memories harder for possible causes than healthy people do. Measurement bias: exposures or outcomes are recorded inaccurately, or differently in different groups.

A famous warning concerns hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for women after menopause. In the 1980s and 1990s, large observational studies found that women taking HRT had less heart disease. Many doctors recommended it partly for that reason. Then randomised trials, above all the Women's Health Initiative, whose main results were published in 2002, found no protection against heart disease in the groups studied, and some increased risks, including of stroke and blood clots.

What went wrong? Women who chose HRT tended to be wealthier, more health-conscious and in better general health. Researchers call this the healthy user effect. The observational studies had partly measured the kind of woman who takes HRT, not the effect of HRT itself. Later analyses suggested that age and timing of starting HRT also matter, and the debate about its overall balance of benefits and harms has continued. But the lesson for method was lasting: when people choose an exposure, their choosing can create the result.

Chapter 07

From association to cause: the Bradford Hill considerations

If trials are impossible, how can we ever say something causes a disease? In 1965 Austin Bradford Hill, Doll's partner on the smoking studies, set out nine considerations for judging whether an association is likely to be causal. They are not a checklist that proves cause, and Hill said so. They are a way to weigh evidence.

ConsiderationThe question it asksSmoking and lung cancer
StrengthIs the link large?Heavy smokers had many times the risk of non-smokers
ConsistencyIs it found by different studies in different places?Yes, in many countries and designs
TemporalityDoes the cause come before the effect?Smoking began years before the cancer
Dose–responseDoes more exposure mean more risk?More cigarettes, more risk
Plausibility and coherenceDoes it fit biology and other knowledge?Tobacco smoke contains cancer-causing chemicals
ExperimentDoes removing the exposure reduce the effect?Risk falls after quitting
Specificity, analogyIs the link specific; are there similar known causes?Weaker points; smoking causes many diseases

Temporality is the only one that is strictly required. A cause must come before its effect. The rest add weight. When many considerations point the same way, as they did for smoking, the case for cause becomes overwhelming even without a trial.

Chapter 08

Surveillance and outbreak work

A second branch of epidemiology works in real time. Surveillance is the ongoing collection of health data to spot problems early. Doctors and laboratories must report certain "notifiable" diseases, such as measles or tuberculosis, to public health authorities. Death certificates, hospital records and laboratory results feed national statistics.

When something unusual appears, outbreak investigators follow a standard sequence. They confirm that an outbreak really exists, agree a case definition (who counts as a case), find and count cases, draw an epidemic curve of cases over time, form a hypothesis about the source, test it, often with a quick case-control study, and act to stop it. The shape of the epidemic curve alone can be revealing: a sharp single peak suggests one shared source, such as a contaminated meal, while waves suggest person-to-person spread. That second pattern leads into the next topic, Pandemics.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The causes we know, and the headlines we read

Most of what we know about preventing disease came from epidemiology. Most health news is epidemiology too, often reported badly.

Chapter 09

What counting has saved

The list of epidemiology's discoveries is long. Smoking causes lung cancer, heart disease and much more. High blood pressure and cholesterol cause heart attacks and strokes. Folic acid before pregnancy prevents spinal birth defects. Asbestos causes a cancer of the lining of the lungs. The human papillomavirus causes cervical cancer.

One finding shows how quickly it can change behaviour. In the 1980s, studies in several countries found that babies put to sleep on their fronts were at higher risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Advice to parents had often been the opposite. When countries launched "back to sleep" campaigns in the late 1980s and early 1990s, SIDS rates fell sharply, by around half or more in many places. No new drug or machine was needed, only a pattern found in the numbers and a change in advice.

Chapter 10

Relative risk, absolute risk, and the headline trap

Imagine a headline: "Eating X raises your risk of bowel cancer by 50 percent." That sounds alarming. Now suppose the risk over ten years goes from 2 in 1,000 to 3 in 1,000. That is indeed a 50 percent rise in relative risk. But the absolute risk rises by just 1 in 1,000. A thousand people would have to change their diet for ten years to prevent one case, assuming the link is causal at all.

Both numbers are true. Each answers a different question. Relative risk tells researchers whether a factor matters. Absolute risk tells you how much it matters to you. Whenever you see a percentage change in risk, ask: from what, to what? Volume 44 adds more on reading a health headline.

Ask too what kind of study it was. A single observational study linking a food to a disease is a reason for curiosity, not for changing your life. Many such links fail to hold up when other teams look, or when trials are done.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful at scale, humble about cause

Each design has strengths and blind spots. Good epidemiologists combine them, and are honest about what each cannot show.

Chapter 12

Where the field has stumbled

Epidemiology's biggest weakness shows most in nutrition. Diet is hard to measure: people misremember what they ate and change their diets over time. Foods come in bundles: people who eat more vegetables also tend to exercise more and smoke less. As a result, studies have produced a long stream of conflicting headlines about eggs, coffee, red wine, fat and more. Some links have held up; many have not.

Another problem is the sheer number of analyses. If researchers test many possible links in one large data set, some will look significant by chance alone. Pre-registering study plans, sharing data and demanding replication help. The field's strength is self-correction: the HRT and diet reversals came from epidemiologists themselves. But a single study, however large, rarely settles a question.

Chapter 14

A science that depends on public trust

Epidemiology needs people to answer surveys, join cohort studies, allow use of their records, and report illness. During COVID-19, its findings and its uncertainties became public arguments, sometimes bitter ones. Estimates changed as data improved, which is how science should work, but to some people it looked like confusion or deceit.

The future of the field may depend as much on communication as on method: explaining what is known, what is uncertain, and why advice changes. Counting is only useful if people trust the counters.

The core idea

Epidemiology studies who gets ill, where, when and why, by comparing groups. Its designs range from snapshots to case-control, cohort and randomised studies. Its permanent enemies are confounding and bias, and the Bradford Hill considerations help judge when an association is probably a cause.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1854: John Snow's cholera investigations in London.
  • 1948: the Framingham Heart Study begins; 1950: Doll and Hill's smoking study.
  • 1965: Bradford Hill's nine considerations for causation.
The common mistake

Reading a relative risk as personal risk. "50 percent higher" can mean 2 in 1,000 becoming 3 in 1,000. Always ask: from what, to what?

Test yourself

Can you explain why observational studies thought HRT protected women's hearts, and why trials disagreed? If so, you understand confounding and the healthy user effect.

The words, in plain words
Epidemiology
The study of how health and disease are distributed in populations, and why.
Incidence
The number of new cases in a population over a period.
Prevalence
The number or share of people who have a condition at a given time.
Relative risk
How many times more likely the exposed group is to get a disease than the unexposed group.
Absolute risk
The actual chance of getting a disease over a period, such as 3 in 1,000.
Case-control study
Comparing people with a disease to similar people without it, looking back at exposures.
Cohort study
Following a group over time to see who develops disease.
Randomised controlled trial
A study that assigns treatments by chance to make groups comparable.
Confounder
A hidden factor linked to both the exposure and the outcome, creating a false link.
Bias
A systematic error that pushes results in one direction.
Healthy user effect
People who choose a health behaviour tend to be healthier for other reasons.
Natural experiment
A situation where chance or policy divides people into comparable groups.
Surveillance
Continuous collection of health data to spot problems early.
Epidemic curve
A chart of new cases over time during an outbreak.
