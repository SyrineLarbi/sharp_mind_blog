Medicine. The long, slow, unglamorous fight to stop people dying of things.

For almost all of history, medicine did more harm than good and the main reason to see a doctor was company. The change came within a few generations, and it came less from brilliant cures than from handwashing, clean water, and finding out what was actually causing things. This is what happens inside an illness, and what we learned to do about it.

Part I

Before it worked

Chapter 01

Two thousand years of confident nonsense

The dominant theory held that the body contained four fluids and illness was an imbalance between them. It was systematic, internally consistent, taught in universities, and almost entirely wrong.

Its main treatment was bleeding the patient, which weakens someone already ill, and it was applied to practically everything for roughly two thousand years. A great many people were bled to death by competent physicians following best practice.

It is worth pausing on why it lasted. Most illnesses resolve on their own. So a doctor bleeds you, you recover, and the treatment is credited — while the patients who die are explained by the severity of their disease. Without a comparison group there is no way to tell a working treatment from a useless one, and nobody was running comparisons.

The single most important idea in modern medicine is therefore not a drug. It is the controlled trial: give some patients the treatment, give matched patients nothing or something inert, and compare. It sounds obvious. It arrived astonishingly late, and it immediately began demolishing accepted treatments.

Chapter 02

The doctor nobody believed

In a Vienna hospital in the 1840s there were two maternity wards. In one, staffed by doctors, roughly one mother in ten died of fever. In the other, staffed by midwives, it was closer to one in twenty-five. Women begged to be admitted to the second.

Ignaz Semmelweis worked out why. The doctors also performed autopsies, and went from the dissection room to the delivery room without washing. He instituted handwashing in a chlorine solution and deaths in his ward fell to match the midwives'.

He was ignored and then resented. He had no theory to offer — germs had not been established — and his finding carried an unbearable implication: that respectable physicians were killing their patients with their own hands. He grew increasingly angry, denounced his colleagues, was committed to an asylum, and died there at forty-seven.

Within a few decades Pasteur and Lister had established that microorganisms cause infection and Semmelweis was entirely vindicated. He was right, he had the numbers, and he was rejected anyway — which is a permanently useful thing to know about how evidence actually travels.

Part II

What actually makes you ill

Chapter 03

Bacteria and viruses are not the same thing

This distinction matters practically, because one of them antibiotics work on and the other they do not.

A bacterium is a complete living cell. It eats, it grows, it divides. Most are harmless or useful; a minority cause illness by damaging tissue or releasing toxins. Because it is a living cell with its own machinery, you can attack that machinery — a wall it needs to build, a process it depends on — without harming your own cells, which do things differently. That is what an antibiotic does.

A virus is not alive in any ordinary sense. It is genetic instructions in a protein coat, with no machinery of its own. It cannot eat, grow or reproduce. What it does is get inside one of your cells and hijack that cell's machinery to manufacture copies of itself, usually destroying the cell in the process.

And that is exactly why antibiotics are useless against a virus: there is no separate machinery to poison. The machinery is yours. Attacking it means attacking you. This is why antibiotics for a cold do nothing except breed resistant bacteria, and why antiviral drugs are far harder to design.

Chapter 04

How an epidemic actually spreads

One number governs an outbreak: on average, how many people does each infected person infect?

Above one, and cases multiply — not steadily but explosively, because each generation is a multiple of the last. Below one, the outbreak shrinks and dies out. The entire object of every public health measure is to push that number under one.

Which is why interventions that seem modest can be decisive. You do not have to stop every transmission; you have to stop enough. Cutting contacts by a third can be the difference between exponential growth and exponential decline.

It is also why acting early feels excessively cautious and is not. Because growth is multiplicative, the visible case count always lags the infection count by the incubation period. By the time a problem looks serious, the true size of it is already several times larger — so a measure that looks like an overreaction on the day it is taken is frequently taken too late.

Vaccination works partly through the same number. Once enough people cannot pass it on, the chain breaks even for people who are not immune — which is how a population protects the few who genuinely cannot be vaccinated.

Chapter 05

What cancer is

Every cell carries instructions about when to divide and when to stop, and it has checking mechanisms, and it can be instructed to destroy itself if something is badly wrong.

Cancer begins when a cell accumulates damage to those particular controls — the ones governing division and self-destruction. It divides when it should not, ignores the signals to stop, evades the instruction to die, and passes all of that to its descendants.

Usually several separate faults must accumulate in the same cell, which is why the risk climbs so steeply with age: damage accumulates over decades. It is also why it is not one disease but hundreds — a cancer is defined by which cell type it started in and which controls failed, and those differences determine what treatment might work.

The difficulty of treating it follows from what it is. A cancer cell is your own cell, with the same machinery, and any poison that kills it will damage healthy cells too. That is why traditional chemotherapy hits fast-dividing cells generally — cancer, but also hair, gut lining and blood — and why the side effects are what they are.

The newer approaches are attempts to find a difference to exploit: drugs targeting a specific faulty protein a particular tumour depends on, or treatments that remove the disguise a tumour uses to hide from your immune system and let the immune system do the work. Both are genuine advances and neither is a general cure.

Chapter 06

The diseases of long life and plenty

Heart disease, stroke and type 2 diabetes are the big killers in wealthy countries, and they share a cause: bodies evolved for scarcity and exertion living in abundance and stillness.

Arteries silt up over decades as fatty deposits build in their walls. A heart attack happens when one of those deposits ruptures and a clot blocks an artery feeding the heart muscle, which then begins to die for lack of oxygen. A stroke is the same event in the brain — either a blockage or a burst vessel — which is why speed of treatment matters so enormously in both.

Type 2 diabetes is a different failure. The hormone that tells cells to take sugar out of the blood stops working properly, because the cells have been exposed to so much of it for so long that they stop responding. Sugar stays in the blood, where over years it damages small vessels — which is why the complications appear in eyes, kidneys, nerves and feet.

All three are substantially preventable, and everyone knows how: movement, diet, not smoking, sleep. The reason this knowledge does not translate into behaviour is not ignorance. It is that the costs are immediate and the benefits are invisible and decades away, which is a problem in psychology rather than medicine.

Part III

What actually saved the most lives

Chapter 07

Sewers, not surgeons

Life expectancy in industrial countries roughly doubled between about 1850 and 1950. It is tempting to credit doctors. Mostly you should credit engineers.

The largest single contribution was separating drinking water from sewage. Before that, cholera and typhoid were routine, and the mechanism was not understood — until John Snow mapped cholera deaths in London and found them clustered around one water pump, and had the handle removed.

After clean water came better nutrition, less crowded housing, milk pasteurisation, and vaccination. Antibiotics and modern surgery arrived later and mattered enormously for individuals, but the great collapse in death rates — particularly of children — happened largely before them.

The most effective medicine has generally been the least dramatic. A sewer saves more lives than a surgeon and nobody makes a film about it. That is worth remembering when judging where health spending should go.

Chapter 08

The mind, and why it took so long

Mental illness was understood as moral failure, possession, or weakness for most of history, and treated accordingly — confinement, restraint, and interventions that would now be recognised as torture.

What we can say now with reasonable confidence: these are conditions of an organ, the brain, involving chemistry, structure, genetics and circumstance together. They are common. They are treatable, though imperfectly. And the distinction between a physical and a mental illness is much blurrier than the separate hospital wings suggest.

What we cannot say is that we understand them well. The chemical explanation popularised in the 1990s — that depression is simply a shortage of one particular messenger — is at best a considerable oversimplification, and the drugs built on it work for many people by mechanisms not fully understood. Talking therapies have good evidence for several conditions. Both work better together than either alone.

The honest summary is that this is the area of medicine with the largest gap between how many people are affected and how well we understand it — and that saying so plainly is more useful than pretending otherwise.

Reference

What to carry forward

The idea that made medicine work

Not a drug — the controlled comparison. Most illnesses resolve anyway, so without a control group any treatment appears to work. Two thousand years of bleeding people survived precisely because nobody was comparing.

Bacteria versus viruses

A bacterium is a living cell with its own machinery you can poison. A virus has no machinery — it hijacks yours, which is exactly why antibiotics cannot touch it and why taking them for a cold only breeds resistance.

Epidemics and cancer

One number decides an outbreak: how many people each case infects. Push it below one and it dies out — and because growth is multiplicative, by the time it looks serious it is already much bigger. Cancer is your own cell with its division and self-destruct controls broken, which is precisely why it is so hard to poison selectively.

What actually saved the lives

Sewers, clean water, food and housing — most of the collapse in death rates happened before antibiotics. The most effective medicine is usually the least dramatic, and Semmelweis is the reminder that being right with the numbers in hand is not sufficient.
