Part I

In the kitchen

Chapter 01

Why browned food tastes better

When you sear a steak, toast bread, roast coffee or fry an onion, the same reaction is happening: proteins and sugars rearranging at high heat into hundreds of new compounds that did not exist in the raw ingredient.

That reaction is the entire reason browned food tastes of anything much. A boiled onion and a caramelised one are chemically different objects.

Two practical rules follow, and they explain most of what a chef will tell you.

It needs heat above boiling. Water boils and then stays at that temperature no matter how much heat you add — the extra energy goes into turning it to steam. So as long as the surface is wet, it cannot get hot enough to brown. That is why you pat meat dry before searing, why a crowded pan steams instead of browning, and why nothing ever browns in a stew.

It needs contact. Food sitting in its own released liquid is boiling. Space in the pan, high heat, and patience.

This also explains why searing does not seal in juices, which is a very durable myth. Weigh a steak before and after and it loses moisture either way. Searing is done for flavour, and flavour is sufficient reason.

Chapter 02

Salt, and the three things it does

Salt makes things taste of themselves rather than of salt, up to a point, because it suppresses bitterness and lets other flavours through. That is why a pinch improves chocolate and coffee.

It also pulls water out of things, by drawing moisture through cell walls. That is why salting aubergine draws out liquid, why salted meat keeps — bacteria need water and salt takes it away — and why salting a salad early makes it weep.

And it lowers the freezing point of water, which is why it goes on roads and why an ice-and-salt mixture gets colder than ice alone. The salt disrupts water's ability to lock into a solid structure, so it has to get colder before it can freeze.

The timing rule worth knowing: salt meat well in advance or immediately before cooking, not in between. Early, and the drawn-out moisture dissolves the salt and is reabsorbed, seasoning throughout. Immediately, and nothing has happened yet. In between is the worst case — a wet surface that will not brown.

Chapter 03

How a microwave actually works

It does not heat from the inside out, and it does not cook with radiation in the dangerous sense.

It emits waves tuned to make water molecules flip back and forth extremely rapidly. That jostling is heat — and it is why the food gets hot and the ceramic plate does not. The plate has no water in it. It warms only by contact with the food.

Several everyday puzzles resolve at once. Food heats unevenly because the waves form a pattern of strong and weak regions, which is what the turntable is fighting and why stirring halfway matters. Nothing browns, because the surface stays at the temperature of boiling water. Metal causes sparks because the waves push charge along it and it can arc. And a cup of water can superheat past boiling with no bubbles if the cup is very smooth, then erupt when you move it — which is why you put a wooden stick or spoon in.

The frequency is not special to water and it does not make food radioactive. When the door shuts, the emission stops; there is no residue.

Chapter 04

Never put water on a pan fire

This one is worth the whole volume if you did not already know it.

Burning oil is far hotter than water's boiling point. Pour water in and it does not put the fire out — it sinks beneath the oil, flashes instantly into steam, and expands enormously, throwing burning oil upward and outward in a fireball. Kitchen fires become house fires exactly this way.

What to do instead: turn off the heat and cover the pan. A lid, a baking tray, a fire blanket. Removing oxygen is the whole job. Then leave it covered and leave it alone — lifting the lid to check lets air back in and it can reignite.

The same logic applies generally. A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat, and you only have to remove one. Water works on paper and wood because it removes heat. It is the wrong tool for oil, and actively dangerous on anything electrical.

Part II

Around the house

Chapter 05

Why your fridge warms the room

A fridge does not make cold. There is no such thing as cold — only less heat. A fridge moves heat from inside to outside.

It does it by exploiting a simple fact: a liquid turning to gas absorbs heat, and a gas turning back to liquid releases it. So a fluid is allowed to evaporate inside the fridge, taking heat from your food, then compressed back into a liquid outside, dumping that heat into the room through the coils at the back.

Which is why the back of a fridge is warm, why a fridge with its door open will heat your kitchen rather than cool it — it is moving heat out plus adding the energy of the motor — and why the coils need space and dust removal to work efficiently.

An air conditioner is precisely the same machine with the hot end outside the window. A heat pump is the same machine again, run in reverse to bring heat in, which is why it can deliver more warmth than the electricity it uses — it is not creating heat, only relocating it.

Chapter 06

Why clothes dry on a cold day

Water does not need to boil to become vapour. At any temperature, some molecules at the surface are moving fast enough to escape — that is evaporation, and it happens on a washing line in January.

Three things speed it up, and they are the three things you can actually control. Warmth, because more molecules have enough energy. Moving air, because it carries away the escaped moisture before it can return. And dry air, because air already full of water cannot accept more.

That is why a windy cold day dries washing better than a still humid warm one, why a dehumidifier works, and why a bathroom fan matters more than opening a window in wet weather.

It also explains why you feel cold when wet even in warm air. Evaporation takes heat from whatever it is leaving — and that is your skin. Sweating works on exactly this principle, which is also why sweating in very humid air does not cool you: the sweat cannot evaporate, so it just sits there.

Chapter 07

Cleaning, and which products not to mix

Most cleaning is one of three jobs. Soap and detergent lift grease by wrapping it — the mechanism from the chemistry volume. Acids such as vinegar and lemon dissolve mineral deposits, which is limescale, and are good on kettles, taps and shower screens. Bleach destroys stains and organisms by breaking their molecules apart.

Matching the tool to the job saves effort: vinegar does nothing to grease and detergent does nothing to limescale.

The mixtures that are genuinely dangerous

Bleach and ammonia — found in many glass cleaners — produce a toxic gas that has killed people cleaning bathrooms. Bleach and acid, including vinegar and many descalers and toilet cleaners, release chlorine gas. And mixing different drain cleaners can react violently enough to erupt.

The safe rule is simple and worth following absolutely: use one product, rinse thoroughly, then use another if you must — and never combine them in the same container or the same surface.

Chapter 08

Electricity in the house

Three things are worth understanding because they keep you safe.

A fuse or breaker protects the wiring, not the appliance. It cuts the supply when too much current flows, before the cable heats enough to start a fire. A breaker that trips repeatedly is reporting a real fault; replacing it with a bigger one removes the protection rather than the problem.

Earthing gives stray current an easier route than you. If a live wire touches a metal casing, the earth wire carries it away and trips the breaker instead of waiting for someone to touch it.

A residual current device compares what goes out with what comes back. If some current is escaping — for instance through a person — it disconnects in a fraction of a second. This is the device that actually saves lives, and it is why outdoor sockets and bathrooms need one.

Practical consequences: water conducts, so wet hands and electricity do not mix. Daisy-chained extension leads draw everything through one cable rated for less. And a plug that is warm to the touch is telling you something.

Part III

Outside

Chapter 09

Reading the weather without an app

From the last volume: warm air rises, cools, and lets go of its water. Every sign below is that one process being visible.

High wispy ice clouds arriving and thickening often mean a weather system approaching — the classic sequence is wisps, then a lowering grey sheet, then rain within a day. Tall cauliflower-shaped clouds with hard edges mean strong rising air and possible thunderstorms; if the top spreads into an anvil, it has hit the ceiling of the lower atmosphere and is a mature storm.

A falling barometer means a low-pressure system, which means rising air, which means clouds and rain. Rising pressure means sinking air, which suppresses cloud — hence settled weather.

And the folk sign with real physics behind it: a red sky at sunset means you are looking west through a lot of dust-free dry air, and in places where weather arrives from the west, that is tomorrow's air. Red in the morning means the dry air has already passed and what is behind it may not be.

For thunderstorms, the useful number: count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by three for the distance in kilometres, or five for miles. If it is under thirty seconds, you should already be indoors, and stay there for thirty minutes after the last thunder.

Chapter 10

Why your car does what it does

Stopping distance is not proportional to speed — it grows much faster. Doubling your speed roughly quadruples the distance needed to stop, because the energy that must be got rid of grows with the square of speed. That is the single most useful fact about driving, and it is why a small increase in speed makes a large difference to whether you stop in time.

Wet roads lengthen it further, and at speed on standing water the tyre can ride up onto a film of water and lose contact entirely — which is why worn tread is dangerous. The grooves exist to give the water somewhere to go.

Cold mornings: batteries deliver less current when cold because the reactions slow, and tyre pressure drops with temperature, which is why the warning light appears on the first frost and often means nothing is wrong.

And the seat belt, in the language of the first physics volume: in a crash the car stops and you continue, because nothing has stopped you. A belt applies the stopping force over a longer time and across the strong parts of your skeleton. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why airbags work with belts rather than instead of them.

Reference

The ten that change what you do

Kitchen

Dry surface and space in the pan, or it steams instead of browning. Salt early or at the last moment, never in between. The microwave heats water, which is why the plate stays cool and nothing browns.

The safety ones

Never water on a pan fire — turn off the heat, cover it, leave it covered. Never mix bleach with ammonia or with any acid, including vinegar and descalers. A breaker that keeps tripping is reporting a fault, not being awkward.

Home

A fridge moves heat rather than making cold, which is why its back is warm and why an open door heats the room. Drying needs warm, moving, dry air — and evaporation takes heat from whatever it leaves, which is why wet means cold and why sweating fails in humidity.

Outside

Falling pressure means rising air means rain. Flash-to-thunder in seconds divided by three gives kilometres. And double your speed, quadruple your stopping distance — the most useful number in this volume.
