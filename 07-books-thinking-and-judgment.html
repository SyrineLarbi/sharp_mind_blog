Part I · Judgement under uncertainty

Four books on being wrong

How your mind misleads you, how to hold a view in probabilities, and how much of what happens is luck.

Book 01

Thinking, Fast and Slow

Daniel Kahneman · 2011 · ~500 pages

The one idea

You run two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic and always on; System 2 is slow, effortful and lazy. Most errors are not System 1 being stupid — they are System 1 confidently answering a question that System 2 never checked.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ WYSIATI — "WHAT YOU SEE IS ALL THERE IS"
  The most useful idea in the book and the least famous.
  Your mind builds the best possible story from the
  information AVAILABLE, and never registers what is
  missing. Coherence feels like truth. So a tidy story
  built on two facts feels MORE certain than a messy one
  built on ten.
  ⇒ the question that operationalises it:
    "What would I need to know that I don't, before this
     confidence is justified?"

⭐ SUBSTITUTION — answering an easier question
  Faced with a hard question, you silently swap it for an
  easy one and answer that.
    "Is this a good investment?"   → "Do I like the
                                      company?"
    "Is this candidate any good?"  → "Do they remind me
                                      of someone good?"
    "How happy am I with my life?" → "What mood am I in
                                      right now?"
  ⇒ the tell: your answer arrived instantly and you
    cannot say what it was based on.

ANCHORING
  Any number present moves your estimate, INCLUDING a
  number you know to be irrelevant. Negotiators, price
  tags and first offers all exploit this.
  ⇒ defence: write your own number down BEFORE hearing
    theirs. It is the only defence that reliably works.

AVAILABILITY
  You judge how common something is by how easily an
  example comes to mind — so vivid, recent and frightening
  things feel frequent. Plane crashes over car journeys.
  ⇒ defence: ask for the base rate, or at least "how
    would I know if this were rare?"

BASE-RATE NEGLECT / REPRESENTATIVENESS
  You judge by resemblance to a stereotype rather than by
  probability. The famous "Linda" problem: given a
  description of a thoughtful activist, people rate
  "Linda is a bank teller AND a feminist" as more likely
  than "Linda is a bank teller" — which is impossible,
  because a subset cannot be larger than its set.
  ⇒ resemblance is not probability. Ask "how many of
    these are there in the world?" first.

PROSPECT THEORY AND LOSS AVERSION
  Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains
  please. And the asymmetry has a direction people miss:
    facing GAINS  → risk-averse (take the sure thing)
    facing LOSSES → risk-SEEKING (gamble to get back to
                    even)
  ⇒ which is precisely why people double down on failing
    projects, bad hires and sunk investments. It is not
    stubbornness. It is the shape of the curve.

FRAMING
  Identical facts, different words, different decision.
  "90% survival" and "10% mortality" are the same number
  and produce different choices FROM DOCTORS.
  ⇒ before deciding, restate the option in the opposite
    frame and see if you still prefer it.

⭐ THE PLANNING FALLACY AND THE OUTSIDE VIEW
  Your estimate of how long your project will take is
  built from your plan. It should be built from what
  happened to SIMILAR PROJECTS.
    INSIDE VIEW  · "here's our plan, six weeks"
    OUTSIDE VIEW · "the last four things like this took
                    four months each"
  ⇒ the outside view is almost always right and almost
    never used, because it feels like it ignores what is
    special about your case. It does. That is the point.

REGRESSION TO THE MEAN — the one that changes how you manage
  Kahneman's flight instructors insisted that praising a
  good landing produced a worse next landing, and shouting
  at a bad one produced a better next landing. They had
  observed this for years and it was real.
  It was also pure regression: an exceptional performance
  is usually followed by a more average one whatever you
  say. The instructors had learned, from genuine evidence,
  that punishment works and praise does not.
  ⇒ EXTREME PERFORMANCE IS FOLLOWED BY LESS EXTREME
    PERFORMANCE FOR STATISTICAL REASONS. Do not build a
    theory of causation on top of it.

Use it tomorrow

Before any estimate, ask what happened to the last three similar things — the outside view, and it is the single highest-return habit in the book. Write your own number before hearing theirs. When an answer arrives instantly, ask which easier question you just answered.

Worth memorising

"What you see is all there is."

"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."

● Amber · extract only, or open Part I

Five hundred pages, and the extract above is most of the usable content. If you open it, read Part I (the two systems) and the chapter on the outside view; the final third, on the experiencing versus remembering self, is interesting but not actionable.

The honest caveat: the chapters on priming — where an unnoticed cue supposedly shifts your later behaviour — are the part of this book most damaged by the replication crisis. Kahneman himself wrote publicly in 2017 that he had placed too much faith in underpowered priming studies. The core work on heuristics, framing and prospect theory is not in question; the priming material should be read as a hypothesis, not a finding.

Already yours: System 1 and 2, twelve biases, prospect theory and the outside view are all in master-yourself/phase-1, file 01, and the evaluation subset is in phase-0, file 03.

Book 02

Poor Charlie's Almanack

Charlie Munger · collected talks, 1994–2005 · ~550 pages

The one idea

Carry a latticework of big models from many disciplines, and attack problems backwards. A person with one discipline's tools will force every problem into that discipline's shape.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE LATTICEWORK
  "To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks
   like a nail."
  Take the three or four BIG ideas from each big
  discipline and carry them permanently:
    physics      · critical mass, equilibrium
    biology      · evolution, incentives, selection
    psychology   · the misjudgments below
    maths        · compounding, probability, permutations
    economics    · scale, competition, opportunity cost
    engineering  · redundancy, margin of safety,
                   backup systems
  ⇒ the value is in the CROSSING. Most good insights are
    one field's standard idea applied where it is not
    standard.

⭐ INVERT, ALWAYS INVERT
  "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll
   never go there."
  Do not ask how to succeed. Ask how you would guarantee
  failure, then avoid that.
  ⇒ built out as a technique in sharp-mind file 02 §04.

⭐ THE 25 CAUSES OF HUMAN MISJUDGMENT
  From the 1995 Harvard talk, revised in the book. The
  ones that actually earn their place:

  ① INCENTIVE-CAUSED BIAS — the big one
     "Never, ever think about something else when you
      should be thinking about the power of incentives."
     People are not lying to you. They genuinely believe
     the thing they are paid to believe. This is far
     more common and far harder to detect than deceit.
     ⇒ the question: "how is this person paid?"

  ② THE LOLLAPALOOZA EFFECT — Munger's own contribution
     Several biases acting in the SAME DIRECTION at once
     produce results far beyond their sum. An auction,
     a cult, a bubble, a bad hire everybody liked.
     ⇒ any one bias you can usually catch. Four at once
       you will not.

  ③ DEPRIVAL SUPER-REACTION — losing hurts disproportionately,
     including ALMOST getting something and losing it
  ④ SOCIAL PROOF — strongest under uncertainty and stress,
     which is exactly when you most need to think alone
  ⑤ AUTHORITY MISINFLUENCE — people follow a confident
     senior person off a cliff, and afterwards cannot
     explain why
  ⑥ CONSISTENCY AND COMMITMENT — once said out loud,
     a position becomes yours to defend
     ⇒ so do NOT state a position publicly before you
       have thought, if you want to stay able to change
  ⑦ ENVY AND JEALOUSY — "it's not greed that drives the
     world, it's envy". Almost never admitted, and it
     explains a surprising share of workplace behaviour
  ⑧ BIAS FROM ASSOCIATION — liking the message because
     you like the messenger, and the reverse

⭐ CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE
  The value is not its size. It is knowing where the EDGE
  is. Inside it, act. Outside it, say so (file 04 §05).
  "You don't have to be brilliant, only a little wiser
   than the other guys, on average, for a long time."

TWO-TRACK ANALYSIS — the practical routine
  ① What are the rational factors here?
  ② What subconscious influences are pushing me — which
     of the 25 is operating right now, on me?
  ⇒ run both tracks explicitly on any large decision.

Use it tomorrow

On any recommendation you receive, ask how the recommender is paid. On your next decision, invert it. And when you notice you are about to state a position publicly that you have not thought through — don't, because commitment will make it yours to defend.

Worth memorising

"Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."

"Invert, always invert."

"It's not greed that drives the world, it's envy."

● Amber · open one talk only

Read "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" — the 1995 Harvard speech, revised version in the book, about forty pages. That talk is the entire value. The rest of the almanack is tributes, photographs, repeated speeches and hagiography, and the physical book is large and expensive for what it contains. The talk circulates freely as a transcript.

Already yours: inversion is built out in file 02, §04; Munger appears in one clause of master-yourself/phase-10, file 03.

Book 03

Thinking in Bets

Annie Duke · 2018 · ~250 pages

The one idea

Every decision is a bet on an uncertain future. So judge the quality of the decision, not the quality of the outcome — because luck sits in between them and breaks the link.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ "RESULTING" — the name is the tool
  Judging a decision by how it turned out. Once the
  behaviour has a name you catch yourself doing it, which
  is most of the benefit.

  THE GRID THAT MAKES IT CONCRETE

                    GOOD OUTCOME      BAD OUTCOME
                 ┌────────────────┬────────────────┐
  GOOD DECISION  │   DESERVED     │   BAD LUCK     │
                 │                │  ⚠ do not      │
                 │                │    change the  │
                 │                │    process     │
                 ├────────────────┼────────────────┤
  BAD DECISION   │  ⭐ DUMB LUCK  │  JUST DESERTS  │
                 │  THE DANGEROUS │                │
                 │  CELL — it     │                │
                 │  TEACHES YOU   │                │
                 │  THE WRONG     │                │
                 │  LESSON        │                │
                 └────────────────┴────────────────┘

  ⇒ OUTCOME = DECISION QUALITY + LUCK
  ⇒ the top-right cell is where good people abandon good
    processes. The bottom-left is where bad processes get
    promoted. Both are expensive; the second is worse
    because it is invisible and it compounds.

⭐ SAY A NUMBER OUT LOUD
  Not "I think it'll work" but "I'm about 60% on this."
  Three things happen:
    · you become calibratable — you can be scored later,
      and scoring is how calibration improves
    · disagreement gets cheaper — 60 vs 40 is a smaller
      fight than yes vs no
    · you stop having to defend certainty you never had
  ⇒ this is the single most portable habit in the book.

⭐ THE "WANNA BET?" TEST
  When someone states something confidently — including
  you — imagine being offered a real bet on it. Confidence
  collapses instantly and accurately.
  ⇒ you do not have to say it out loud. Running it
    silently on your own claims is the useful version.

MOTIVATED REASONING — why smart people are not immune
  We are not truth-seekers by default. We are
  "be-right"-seekers, and intelligence mostly buys a
  better ability to construct the defence.
  THE SELF-SERVING ASYMMETRY, stated plainly:
    my wins   → skill        your wins   → luck
    my losses → luck         your losses → skill
  ⇒ catch it by asking, after any outcome: "if the same
    thing had happened to a colleague, what would I say
    caused it?"

TRUTH-SEEKING GROUPS
  A group with an explicit norm of accuracy over comfort.
  The charter Duke proposes:
    · focus on accuracy over confirmation
    · accountability — you may be asked for your reasoning
    · openness to views you dislike
  ⇒ and one practical rule: WHEN ASKING FOR AN OPINION,
    DO NOT GIVE YOURS FIRST, and do not say the outcome
    before describing the decision. Both contaminate.

BACKCASTING AND THE PRE-MORTEM
  BACKCAST  · stand in a future where it WORKED and tell
              the story of how
  PRE-MORTEM· stand in a future where it FAILED and tell
              that story
  ⇒ do both. The second finds more.

Use it tomorrow

Attach a percentage to your next prediction, out loud. After the next bad outcome, ask whether the decision was wrong or whether it was variance — and answer honestly. When asking someone for their view, give yours last.

Worth memorising

"Life is poker, not chess."

"Resulting." — one word, and the most useful vocabulary item in this file.

● Green · worth reading fully

Short, operational, and it repeats itself less than almost anything else in this file. If you read one book from these three handbooks cover to cover, this is a defensible choice — it is written by a professional poker player turned decision researcher, and the poker framing does real work rather than being decoration.

That said, the extract above is genuinely sufficient. Read it fully because you enjoy it, not because you need to.

Already yours: resulting, the decision journal, probabilistic thinking and the pre-mortem are covered in full in master-yourself/phase-8, file 02.

Book 04

Fooled by Randomness · Antifragile

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2001 and 2012 · ~350 and ~500 pages

The one idea

You systematically underestimate how much of what happens is randomness, and you build things that are brittle to it. Stop trying to predict; change your exposure instead.

What the books actually give you
⭐ SURVIVORSHIP BIAS, PROPERLY UNDERSTOOD
  You only ever see the winners. The people who did the
  same things and failed are not writing books, not being
  interviewed, and not in your sample.
  "The cemetery is silent."
  ⇒ before copying anyone's strategy: how many people ran
    this strategy, and where are they now?

⭐ ALTERNATIVE HISTORIES
  Judge a decision by the DISTRIBUTION OF WORLDS it could
  have produced, not the one that happened to occur.
  Someone who bet everything on one outcome and won made
  a bad decision with a good result.
  ⇒ this is the same conclusion as Duke's "resulting",
    reached from a completely different direction — which
    is itself a reason to take it seriously.

NARRATIVE FALLACY
  We convert noise into stories, compulsively and
  retrospectively. Market commentary is the purest form:
  the same 0.3% move is explained by whichever headline
  is nearest to hand.
  ⇒ when an explanation arrives AFTER the event and
    fits perfectly, suspect it.

⭐ FRAGILE · ROBUST · ANTIFRAGILE — the triad
  FRAGILE      breaks under stress and volatility
  ROBUST       resists it, unchanged
  ANTIFRAGILE  IMPROVES because of it
  Muscles, immune systems, careers with varied experience
  and reputations that survived a scandal are antifragile.
  A single-client business, a body that never trains, and
  a CV with one employer are fragile.
  ⇒ ask of anything you depend on: WHICH OF THE THREE IS
    THIS? It is a better question than "is this risky?"

BARBELL STRATEGY
  Extreme safety on most of it, extreme risk on a small
  part, NOTHING in the middle — because the middle is
  where you take real risk for unremarkable reward.
    money   · mostly very safe, a little very speculative
    career  · a stable base plus small wild bets
    reading · ancient books and brand-new ideas, little
              of this year's bestsellers
  ⇒ the shape caps your downside while leaving the
    upside open.

OPTIONALITY
  Prefer things with capped downside and uncapped upside.
  With that shape you do not need to be right often —
  you need to be exposed when you are.
  ⇒ "is my downside capped?" is a better pre-decision
    question than "will this work?"

VIA NEGATIVA — improvement by removal
  What to STOP doing usually beats what to start. We know
  far more about what harms than about what helps, and
  the knowledge of harm is more robust.
  ⇒ the strongest single application: remove one thing
    from a process rather than adding a check to it.

SKIN IN THE GAME
  Do not take advice from someone who bears no cost if
  they are wrong. Symmetry of exposure is the only
  reliable filter on advice, and it is cheap to check.

Use it tomorrow

Ask of your next commitment whether the downside is capped. Ask, of anyone giving you confident advice, what happens to them if they are wrong. And classify one thing you depend on as fragile, robust or antifragile — the answer is usually uncomfortable.

Worth memorising

"The cemetery is silent."

"Don't tell me what you think. Tell me what you have in your portfolio."

● Amber · extract only, and be selective

Taleb is right about several important things and the ratio of insight to page count is poor. The prose is combative, the digressions are long, and the same point returns many times in different costume. Fooled by Randomness is the best-argued of the set; Antifragile has the best single idea — the triad — and the most padding around it.

Read the extract. If one of the ideas above grips you, read the twenty pages where he develops it and stop.

Four books on getting good

Book 05

The Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto · 1987 · ~180 pages

The one idea

Put the answer first and group the support beneath it, because the reader assembles understanding top-down whether you wrote that way or not. Writing in the order you thought of things makes the reader do your work.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE PYRAMID AND ITS THREE RULES
  One governing thought at the top, supported by a group
  beneath it, each of which may be supported in turn.

  ① IDEAS AT ANY LEVEL SUMMARISE THE IDEAS BELOW
     if the three supports do not add up to the claim,
     the claim is wrong or a support is missing
  ② IDEAS IN A GROUP ARE THE SAME KIND OF THING
     three reasons, or three steps, or three options.
     Never two reasons and a step — the most common
     structural error people make and the hardest to
     see in your own writing
  ③ IDEAS IN A GROUP ARE LOGICALLY ORDERED
     by importance, by time, by structure, or by
     comparison. Never "the order they occurred to me"

⭐ SCQA — the introduction, in four sentences
  SITUATION     something the reader already agrees with
                ⚠ MUST CONTAIN NO NEWS. Its only job is
                  shared ground for the next part to
                  disturb
  COMPLICATION  what changed, or what is now wrong
  QUESTION      the question now live in the reader's head
  ANSWER        your governing thought
  ⇒ the point: you MANUFACTURE the question your answer
    answers. An answer to a question the reader is
    actually holding is received completely differently.

DEDUCTIVE VS INDUCTIVE ORDER — and which to use
  DEDUCTIVE  all managers need X · this team lacks X ·
             therefore this team needs X
             ⚠ forces the reader to hold a chain, and if
               they lose one link the conclusion is gone
  INDUCTIVE  three parallel reasons supporting one claim
             ⇒ USE THIS IN BUSINESS WRITING ALMOST ALWAYS.
               Each support stands alone, so an
               interruption costs you one reason instead
               of the argument.

MECE — mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive
  no overlaps, no gaps. In practice you rarely need
  perfect exhaustiveness; you need to not CLAIM more than
  you covered. One word fixes it: "the three MAIN
  reasons".

HEADINGS AS ASSERTIONS
  ✗ "Background" · "Analysis" · "Next steps"
  ✓ "Damage has tripled and has not recovered"
  ⇒ THE TEST: read only the headings. If they form an
    argument, the document works. If they form a table
    of contents, you have wasted the one part of the
    document that is certain to be read.

THE "SO WHAT?" TEST
  Apply to every group: if these three things are true,
  SO WHAT? The answer is the box above them — and if
  there is no clean answer, the group is not a group.

Use it tomorrow

Put your conclusion in the email subject line. Rewrite every heading in your next document as a claim rather than a topic. Before sending anything over two lines, check the conclusion is in the first sentence.

Worth memorising

"The reader can only absorb one idea at a time."

And the heading test — if the headings do not make the argument, rewrite them.

● Green · worth reading fully — with one condition

This is the most useful book in this file by a distance, and the ideas in it will change your writing permanently. It is also dry, oddly expensive, and written as a corporate training manual rather than as a book to enjoy.

So: read file 03 of this library first — it is a compressed, worked version of the same material with twelve rewrites. Buy Minto only if you write documents for senior audiences regularly and want the full treatment of grouping and logical order.

Already yours: file 03 is built on this book. Nothing in master-yourself covers it.

Book 06

Sources of Power

Gary Klein · 1998 · ~330 pages

The one idea

Experts under time pressure do not compare options. They recognise the situation, one workable option surfaces, they run it forward in their head, and they act. All the expertise is in the recognition.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE RECOGNITION-PRIMED DECISION MODEL
  Klein set out to document how fireground commanders
  compare options under pressure. He found they did not.
  They reported "just knowing" what to do.

    ① SIZE UP     "this is a ____ kind of situation"
    ② ONE OPTION  surfaces, typical for that situation
       SURFACES
    ③ SIMULATE    run it forward: "and then what?"
    ④ ACT         if it holds. If not, modify it, or
                  take the next option that comes

  THREE VARIATIONS HE DOCUMENTS
    SIMPLE MATCH      recognise, act
    DIAGNOSE          the situation is unclear; work out
                      which type it is first
    EVALUATE A COURSE the option is clear; the simulation
                      is where the work happens

  ⇒ A NOVICE RUNS THE SAME LOOP. The difference is that
    the option surfacing for them is a bad one, because
    the size-up was wrong. So "consider more options" is
    the wrong training. BETTER SIZE-UP is the training.

MENTAL SIMULATION, AND HOW IT FAILS
  The quality check that separates fast from reckless —
  and it has a specific failure mode. When an anomaly
  appears that contradicts the simulation, people EXPLAIN
  IT AWAY to preserve the story, one small excuse at a
  time, until the situation is unrecognisable.
  ⇒ the defence: when you find yourself explaining away
    the SECOND anomaly, stop and re-run the size-up from
    scratch. Two explained anomalies is the threshold.

CUES — expertise is different perception, not more data
  Experienced NICU nurses could identify babies becoming
  septic before the tests showed it, and could not say
  how. Klein's interviews recovered the cues: subtle
  changes in colour, tone, feeding, alertness — a pattern
  nobody had written down.
  ⇒ EXPERTS DO NOT HAVE MORE INFORMATION. THEY SEE
    DIFFERENT INFORMATION. Which is why asking an expert
    for their RULES gets you little, and asking for their
    STORIES gets you a lot.

STORIES AS THE STORAGE FORMAT OF EXPERTISE
  Expertise is held and transmitted as cases, not
  principles. The practical consequence for you:
  ⇒ when learning from someone experienced, ask "tell me
    about a time this went wrong" — never "what are the
    principles?" The first gets you cues; the second gets
    you platitudes they half-believe.

WHY DECISION-ANALYSIS TRAINING CAN MAKE EXPERTS WORSE
  Teaching an experienced person to enumerate and score
  options replaces a fast working process with a slow
  one. Useful for novices and for genuinely novel
  decisions; harmful applied to routine expert judgement.

Use it tomorrow

Name the situation type before answering. Run one step of simulation — "and then what?" — before committing. And when learning from someone experienced, ask for a story about a failure rather than for their principles.

Worth memorising

"Experts see the world differently. They see things the rest of us cannot."

And the two-anomaly rule: explaining away the second one is the signal to re-run the size-up.

● Amber · extract only

The model is in the first few chapters and you now have it. The rest is case studies — genuinely interesting ones, from firegrounds, intensive care units and military command — but they illustrate rather than extend. The writing is careful and slightly academic.

One honest limit worth carrying: this is field observation, not controlled experiment. It is a well-supported description of how experienced people report deciding. It is not a demonstration that recognition beats deliberation, and Klein and Kahneman later wrote a joint paper agreeing that expert intuition is trustworthy only in domains with regular patterns and fast, accurate feedback — which leads directly to the next two books.

Already yours: RPD is in file 01, §03; the pre-mortem, which is Klein's, is in master-yourself/phase-8, file 02.

Book 07

Peak

Anders Ericsson & Robert Pool · 2016 · ~330 pages

The one idea

Expertise comes from deliberate practice, which is a narrow and demanding thing that almost nothing people call "practice" actually is. Experience alone produces almost no improvement.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE DEFINITION, IN FULL — all six, not three
  ① A SPECIFIC GOAL aimed at a WEAKNESS
     not "get better at presenting" but "stop trailing
     off at the end of sentences"
  ② FULL ATTENTION — not background repetition
  ③ IMMEDIATE FEEDBACK — the binding constraint. Without
     it, hours produce nothing at all
  ④ REPETITION WITH REFINEMENT — same thing again,
     adjusted, not the same thing again
  ⑤ AT THE EDGE OF CURRENT ABILITY — uncomfortable by
     construction. If it is comfortable it is not this
  ⑥ USUALLY A TEACHER, or at minimum a clear model of
     what good looks like

  PURPOSEFUL vs DELIBERATE — Ericsson's own distinction
    PURPOSEFUL  has ① to ⑤. Available to anyone
    DELIBERATE  adds an established field with known
                benchmarks and real teachers — music,
                chess, surgery
  ⇒ most of what you want to improve has no such field,
    so PURPOSEFUL practice is your realistic target. Say
    so honestly rather than pretending otherwise.

⭐ MENTAL REPRESENTATIONS — the actual currency
  Practice does not build muscle memory so much as it
  builds better internal models of the domain. Better
  models let you NOTICE more, PLAN further ahead, and
  SELF-CORRECT without a coach.
  ⇒ this is the same finding as the chess chunking work
    in sharp-mind file 01 §02, from the other side: the
    expert's advantage is representational, and it is
    domain-specific.

⭐ THE OK PLATEAU
  You reach "good enough", the skill becomes automatic,
  and automatic means no further improvement — forever.
  Twenty years of driving does not make you a better
  driver.
  ⇒ improvement requires DELIBERATELY LEAVING
    AUTOMATICITY: a harder target, a new constraint, a
    feedback source you did not have.

⭐ THE 10,000-HOUR RULE IS NOT ERICSSON'S, AND HE DISOWNS IT
  The number came from Gladwell's reading of Ericsson's
  violinist study. In the study it was an AVERAGE for the
  best students by age twenty — not a threshold, not a
  guarantee, and not the finding. Ericsson wrote a chapter
  objecting to the popularisation.
  ⇒ THE REAL CLAIM IS ABOUT QUALITY, NOT QUANTITY. Ten
    thousand hours of ④-less repetition produces a
    twenty-year plateau, which is the common case.

EXPERIENCE IS NOT EXPERTISE
  Doctors often perform WORSE the more years since
  training, on measures where feedback is absent or
  delayed. Years accumulate; skill does not, unless the
  loop is closed.
  ⇒ so the question about any activity is not "how long
    have I done this?" but "WHERE IS MY FEEDBACK COMING
    FROM?" If the answer is nowhere, the years are not
    accruing anything.

Use it tomorrow

Pick one specific weakness, not a general skill. Then find the feedback loop — that is the part people skip, and without it the rest is decoration. The weekly recording drill in file 04 is exactly this applied to how you speak.

Worth memorising

"The right sort of practice carried out over a sufficient period of time leads to improvement. Nothing else."

And: where is my feedback coming from?

● Amber · extract only

The six-part definition, mental representations and the OK plateau are the book, and they are above. The remainder is case studies and an extended argument against innate talent that you probably do not need to be persuaded of — and which is, in fairness, the most contested part of the book. Later meta-analyses found deliberate practice explains a substantial but far from complete share of performance variance, and less in some domains than others.

Which is exactly the limitation the next book is about.

Already yours: covered in full, including the 10,000-hour correction, in master-yourself/phase-8, file 01.

Book 08

Range

David Epstein · 2019 · ~350 pages

The one idea

Early specialisation and deliberate practice win in kind learning environments. Most of real life is wicked, and there breadth, late specialisation and analogical thinking win instead.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ KIND vs WICKED LEARNING ENVIRONMENTS
  Robin Hogarth's distinction, and the most valuable idea
  in the book by a wide margin.

  KIND                          WICKED
  ────                          ──────
  rules are fixed and known     rules change, or are hidden
  feedback is immediate and     feedback is delayed,
    accurate                      noisy, or misleading
  the same situation recurs     situations rarely repeat
  ⇒ chess, golf, firefighting   ⇒ hiring, strategy, most
    music, surgery                 of medicine, careers,
                                   investing, management

  ⇒ DELIBERATE PRACTICE AND EXPERT INTUITION WORK IN KIND
    ENVIRONMENTS. IN WICKED ONES THEY CAN MAKE YOU
    CONFIDENTLY WRONG — because you are learning patterns
    from feedback that is not telling you the truth.

  ⭐ SO THE FIRST QUESTION ABOUT ANY DOMAIN IS: WHICH IS
    THIS? And the honest answer for most professional
    work is "wicked", which means your intuition there
    deserves less trust than it feels like it deserves.
    This is the necessary qualifier on Klein and Ericsson,
    and all three authors broadly agree on it.

ANALOGICAL THINKING
  Solving a new problem by mapping it onto a distant one.
  Kepler worked out planetary motion largely through
  analogies — to light, to magnets, to boats — because he
  had no precedent to imitate.
  ⭐ AND THE KEY FINDING: MULTIPLE ANALOGIES BEAT ONE.
    A single analogy anchors you to its source. Two or
    three from unrelated domains force you to extract
    what is actually structural.
  ⇒ practical: "what else works like this?" and do not
    stop at the first answer.

⭐ DESIRABLE DIFFICULTIES — practice that feels worse and works
  SPACING       leave gaps between sessions
  INTERLEAVING  mix problem types rather than blocking
                them ("massed" practice)
  TESTING       retrieve rather than review — self-testing
                beats rereading by a wide margin
  GENERATION    attempt the answer BEFORE being told it,
                even when you will get it wrong

  ⭐ ALL FOUR MAKE PRACTICE FEEL LESS EFFECTIVE AND MAKE
    LEARNING MORE DURABLE. Blocked, immediate, re-read
    practice produces fast visible progress and poor
    retention — which is why almost everyone chooses it.
  ⇒ if your studying feels smooth and satisfying, that
    is evidence against it working.

MATCH QUALITY
  Switching fields is frequently not a failure but an
  improvement in fit, and late specialisers often
  outperform early ones over a career. The early
  specialiser wins the first decade; the "sampler" tends
  to end up better matched.

THE OUTSIDER ADVANTAGE
  On the InnoCentive platform, problems companies could
  not solve internally were disproportionately solved by
  people from OUTSIDE the relevant field — which is the
  analogical point, institutionally.

Use it tomorrow

Before trusting a gut read, ask whether the domain is kind or wicked. Interleave your practice instead of blocking it. And on your next hard problem, deliberately find two analogies from unrelated fields rather than one.

Worth memorising

Kind or wicked? — four words, and it tells you how much to trust your own intuition.

And: if practice feels smooth, that is evidence against it.

● Amber · extract only

Kind versus wicked, desirable difficulties and multiple analogies are the whole book, and all three are above. The rest is a very readable procession of anecdotes — Federer versus Tiger Woods, the Challenger disaster, jazz musicians — assembled to support the thesis. Enjoyable; not additionally informative.

One caution in the book's own spirit: it is built largely from selected cases, which is a method that can support almost any thesis. The kind/wicked distinction stands on its own research footing (Hogarth, and the Kahneman–Klein agreement); the broader "generalists win" claim is more loosely supported than the narrative suggests.

Already yours: nothing in master-yourself covers kind versus wicked, which is precisely why it earns its place here.

Part III · The catalogues

Two reference books, and how to use them

Both are lists. Lists are useful to look things up in and useless to read, and treating them as books is why most people get nothing from either.

Book 09

The Art of Thinking Clearly

Rolf Dobelli · 2011 · 99 chapters of two pages each

The one idea

Ninety-nine cognitive errors, one per chapter, each with an example and an instruction. The format is the contribution — it is a checklist, not an argument.

The entries actually worth having
⭐ THE SWIMMER'S BODY ILLUSION — the best entry in the book
  Professional swimmers do not have those bodies because
  they swim. They swim at that level BECAUSE they have
  those bodies. Selection, mistaken for treatment.
  ⇒ the general form: whenever you see a trait in a
    successful group, ask whether the activity produced
    the trait or SELECTED for it. This one question
    invalidates a large share of self-improvement advice,
    including some in books on this very list.

⭐ CHAUFFEUR KNOWLEDGE — Munger's distinction, well put
  Two kinds of knowledge: real understanding, and the
  ability to talk fluently about something. Planck's
  chauffeur could deliver the lecture; he could not
  answer a question.
  ⇒ the test is a follow-up question. It is also why
    sharp-mind file 06 §05 insists on stating your model
    rather than performing fluency.

ACTION BIAS
  Doing something when doing nothing is better. Football
  goalkeepers dive on penalties far more often than
  staying centre would justify — because standing still
  and conceding FEELS worse than diving and conceding.
  ⇒ in a crisis, "what happens if we wait a day?" is an
    underused question.

OMISSION BIAS — the mirror of the above
  We judge harm caused by action more harshly than
  identical harm caused by inaction. Which is why
  organisations tolerate ongoing damage they would never
  actively choose.

STORY BIAS, HINDSIGHT, OVERCONFIDENCE, SUNK COST,
SOCIAL PROOF, AUTHORITY, CONTRAST EFFECT, AVAILABILITY,
SELF-SELECTION, FORECAST ILLUSION, NEWS ILLUSION
  — all present, all correct, all two pages, and all
  covered more carefully elsewhere in this library.
● Red · skip as a book · keep as a lookup

Do not read this front to back. Ninety-nine chapters of identical shape is numbing, and by chapter thirty the effect is that every bias feels equally important, which is the opposite of useful.

It is also, substantially, other people's work — Kahneman, Cialdini, Taleb — and Dobelli was criticised on publication for how lightly he attributed it. Take the swimmer's body illusion and chauffeur knowledge, which are genuinely well-framed here, and use the contents page as a checklist when you suspect you are fooling yourself.

Read instead: master-yourself/phase-1, file 01 for the twelve biases that matter with real explanation, and phase-0, file 03 for the evaluation subset.

Book 10

Super Thinking

Gabriel Weinberg & Lauren McCann · 2019 · ~350 pages, ~300 models

The one idea

A survey of roughly three hundred mental models from across the disciplines, grouped into themed chapters. Munger's latticework, assembled for you.

The models worth taking out of it
⭐ GOODHART'S LAW — the most useful single model here
  "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a
   good measure."
  Because people optimise the number rather than the
  thing the number was standing in for. Call-centre
  handle times, story points, lines of code, exam
  results, response-time SLAs — all of them.
  ⇒ when proposing any metric, ask: "how would someone
    hit this number without doing the actual work?"
    Then assume someone will.

⭐ CHESTERTON'S FENCE
  Do not remove a fence until you know why it was put
  there. The obviously-pointless process usually has an
  incident behind it that nobody wrote down.
  ⇒ "what was this for?" before "let's kill this". It is
    also the single best first question when you inherit
    anything.

SECOND-ORDER THINKING
  "And then what?" — one more step than everyone else
  takes. Most bad plans are first-order correct.

HANLON'S RAZOR
  Never attribute to malice what is adequately explained
  by carelessness, overload, or not knowing. In
  organisations this is right the overwhelming majority
  of the time, and assuming it is right also happens to
  be the better strategy when it is not.

THE MAP IS NOT THE TERRITORY
  Your model is a simplification and it will fail at the
  edges. The dashboard is not the business.

THE MOST RESPECTFUL INTERPRETATION
  Respond to the most generous reading of what someone
  said. Cheap, and it defuses an enormous amount.

LOCAL vs GLOBAL OPTIMUM
  Climbing the nearest hill can prevent you reaching the
  mountain. Applies to careers as much as to algorithms.

PARKINSON'S LAW      work expands to fill the time given
PATH DEPENDENCE      today's options are constrained by
                     decisions made long ago, often by
                     people who are gone
LINDY EFFECT         for non-perishable things, the
                     longer it has survived, the longer
                     it is likely to last. A good filter
                     for what to read
LEVERAGE             find the small input with the
                     disproportionate output — and note
                     it is almost never the thing you
                     enjoy doing most
● Amber · lookup only, never front to back

A model catalogue read linearly produces nothing. Models only stick when you attach one to a live problem — read three hundred in a week and you will retain none, which is the standard experience with this book and not your fault.

How to actually use it: when you are stuck on something real, open the index, find two or three models that might apply, and try them on the actual problem. That is what the book is for. The Great Mental Models series by Shane Parrish is the same idea with fewer models and more depth per model, and is the better choice if you want to read rather than reference.

Reference

Don't bother — and what to read instead

● Red · the thinking-and-judgment shelf
  • Outliers — Malcolm Gladwell. The source of the 10,000-hour rule, which is a misreading of Ericsson's violinist study; Ericsson said so himself. Enjoyable storytelling with causal claims the evidence does not carry. Instead: the Peak extract above, which contains the correction.
  • Blink — Malcolm Gladwell. Argues that snap judgements are often excellent — roughly the opposite of what Kahneman shows — and leans heavily on priming and thin-slicing research that is among the worst affected by replication failures. Instead: Klein's RPD above, which is the defensible version of the same intuition, with the conditions attached.
  • The 48 Laws of Power — Robert Greene. Historically loose, entertaining, and a genuinely poor operating manual: a substantial share of the advice would, applied literally, get you fired or divorced. Instead: Johnstone's Impro in file 09 for how status actually works, and file 05 for the version that does not require you to be unpleasant.
  • Any book whose entire thesis fits in its title. Grit, Deep Work, Essentialism, The One Thing — the ideas are real and each is one good essay. If a colleague can tell you the thesis in a sentence and nothing is lost, that was the book.
  • Rich Dad Poor Dad — the specific financial claims range from unverifiable to actively bad, and the central biography has never been substantiated. Instead: master-yourself/phase-10, file 01.
  • The Secret, and the manifestation shelf generally — the central claim is false, and the corollary that people attract their own misfortune is harmful. Instead: master-yourself/phase-5, file 03, which covers what expectation effects genuinely do and do not do.
A note on the replication crisis, since it comes up three times above

A large number of famous psychology findings from roughly 1995 to 2012 have failed to replicate in larger, pre-registered studies. The hardest-hit areas are social priming (unnoticed cues changing behaviour), ego depletion (willpower as a finite fuel), and power posing (postures changing hormones) — which matters because all three are popular in exactly this genre.

What has held up well: the heuristics and biases programme, prospect theory and framing, loss aversion, and the basic structure of social judgement. Where a claim in this library rests on shaky ground, the entry says so. If a book you are reading rests its argument on one striking study, treat that as a hypothesis.

On these extractions.
