The age of revolutions. When people decided the world they were born into was only one option.

For most of history, the arrangement you were born into was simply how things were — a king ruled because kings ruled, and you farmed because your father farmed. Within about a century that assumption broke, in America, in France, in Haiti, and in the cotton mills of northern England. Some of those revolutions were political and loud. The one that changed daily life most had no battles at all.

Part I

The idea underneath

Chapter 01

Where authority is supposed to come from

The old answer was God. A king ruled because God had arranged the world that way, and questioning it was both treason and blasphemy — which is a very effective combination, because it makes disagreement not merely dangerous but wicked.

Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries a set of writers, working in the aftermath of Europe's religious wars and with the new scientific method in the air, proposed something else. Government, they argued, is not divinely ordained but a kind of bargain: people give up some freedom in exchange for protection and order. If the government stops keeping its side — if it becomes a threat rather than a protection — then the bargain is void and people may replace it.

Alongside it came a second claim that we now find obvious and that was then genuinely radical: that rights belong to people because they are people, not because a ruler has granted them, and therefore cannot be taken away by the ruler who did not give them.

Both ideas were in books. Books were now cheap. Literacy was rising. And a merchant or a lawyer who had read them and who paid taxes to a king he had never met might reasonably begin to wonder what, precisely, he was getting for the money.

Part II

Three political revolutions

Chapter 02

America — a tax argument that escalated

It did not begin as a demand for independence. It began as a complaint about procedure.

Britain had just fought an enormously expensive war and decided, not unreasonably, that the American colonies should contribute to the cost of their own defence. The colonists objected — not primarily to the amount, but to the principle that a parliament in which they had no representative could tax them at all. No taxation without representation is a constitutional argument, not a nationalist one.

It escalated the way these things do: a protest, a heavy-handed response, a crowd, some deaths, a commemoration of the deaths, a harsher law, a larger protest. Tea went into Boston harbour. The punishment for the tea closed the port. By the time anyone fired, positions had hardened past retreat.

The war was won with substantial French help, by an army that mostly avoided battle and concentrated on not being destroyed — a strategy that works when your opponent is three thousand miles from home and you only have to outlast him.

What was genuinely new came afterwards. The victors wrote down a form of government in a single document, with power deliberately split between parts that could obstruct each other, on the explicit theory that concentrated power is dangerous regardless of who holds it. And they made it amendable, which is the quiet masterstroke: a constitution that cannot change gets broken instead.

The founding document also declared that all men are created equal while a fifth of the population was enslaved, and several of the men who signed it owned people. That contradiction was noticed at the time, argued about at the time, and deferred — and it would take a civil war of unprecedented bloodiness to resolve.

Chapter 03

France — the one that ate itself

France in 1789 was the richest and most populous country in western Europe and its government was bankrupt. It had spent enormously on wars, including helping the Americans, and its tax system exempted precisely the people with the most money.

The king summoned an assembly of the three estates — clergy, nobility, everyone else — for the first time in a hundred and seventy-five years, to authorise new taxes. The third estate, representing about ninety-seven per cent of the country, promptly asked why the other two per cent should have two thirds of the votes, declared itself the National Assembly, and swore not to disperse until France had a constitution.

Then, in July, a Paris crowd stormed a fortress-prison looking for gunpowder. The Bastille held seven inmates and was militarily insignificant, and it did not matter in the slightest: it was a symbol, and its fall told everyone in France that the government could be defied and nothing would happen.

What followed is the reason this revolution is remembered differently from the American one. Within four years the king had been executed, France was at war with most of Europe, and a Committee of Public Safety was executing thousands of citizens in the name of protecting the republic. Robespierre, who drove the Terror, argued that virtue without terror is powerless — and was himself guillotined within a year, to general relief.

Out of the exhaustion came a general who restored order, crowned himself emperor, conquered most of Europe, and was eventually defeated by the same combination that defeats most conquerors: distance, winter and everybody else uniting against him.

The uncomfortable comparison

Both revolutions were made by people reading the same books. One produced a durable constitution; the other produced the Terror and then an emperor. The difference is not the quality of the ideas. It is that the Americans were building a government where there had not really been one, and the French were tearing down a functioning state in the middle of a war, with a hungry capital and foreign armies on the border. Revolutions are shaped less by their principles than by their circumstances.

Chapter 04

Haiti — the revolution nobody wanted to talk about

The richest colony in the world, by some measures, was a French sugar island called Saint-Domingue. Its wealth rested on the labour of around half a million enslaved people, worked under conditions so lethal that the population had to be constantly replenished by new arrivals.

In 1791, they rose. The revolt was organised, it was sustained, and over thirteen years it defeated the French colonial forces, then a Spanish invasion, then a British expedition, then an enormous army sent by Napoleon specifically to restore slavery. Its greatest leader, Toussaint Louverture — himself formerly enslaved, a brilliant commander and politician — was lured into negotiations, seized, and died in a French prison. The revolution continued without him and won.

In 1804 Haiti declared independence: the first state in the Americas to abolish slavery outright and the only country ever created by a successful revolt of enslaved people.

And the response of the rest of the world tells you exactly what was at stake. France demanded an enormous indemnity — compensation to slaveholders for the loss of their property, meaning the people themselves — and Haiti, blockaded and diplomatically isolated, agreed to pay it. The country spent more than a century paying reparations to the nation that had enslaved it. The United States, itself a slaveholding republic, refused to recognise Haiti for nearly sixty years.

This revolution is missing from a great many general histories. Its absence is not an oversight. It was embarrassing to everyone with an interest in arguing that the rights announced in the other two revolutions had natural limits.

Part III

The revolution without a battle

Chapter 05

Coal, water and the end of muscle

Every economy before this one ran on muscle and weather. Things were made by hand, moved by animal, and powered by wind or falling water — which meant production happened where the river was and stopped when the river froze.

The change was learning to get useful work out of heat. Burn coal, boil water, use the steam to push a piston. Early engines were desperately inefficient and it did not matter, because their first job was pumping water out of coal mines — an engine sitting on top of its own fuel supply can afford to be wasteful.

Then somebody put the engine in a building full of machines, and later on wheels, and the two constraints that had bounded human production since farming began were gone at once. Production no longer had to be near moving water, and it no longer had to stop.

Britain went first, and the reasons are unromantic: coal and iron close together and close to the sea, a banking system that would lend, a legal system that protected an invention long enough to profit from it, colonies supplying cotton and buying cloth, and — as the last volume described — capital accumulated from the Atlantic trade.

Chapter 06

What it was like to live through

We should resist telling this as progress, because for the first two generations it was mostly misery.

People left villages for cities that had no sewers, no clean water and no building standards. Families lived in single cellar rooms. Cholera arrived and killed by the thousand. Work that had been governed by daylight and season became governed by a clock and a bell, for twelve or fourteen hours, six days a week, in air full of cotton dust, tending machines that took fingers and arms. Children of six worked because a family needed every wage. Life expectancy in the worst industrial cities fell below what it had been in the countryside.

The wealth was real and so was the squalor, and they were produced by the same process. What eventually changed it was not the market correcting itself but people organising: factory acts, limits on children's hours, inspectors, unions — every one of them fought bitterly, every one of them predicted to ruin the economy, and none of them did.

The honest summary

Industrialisation made humanity vastly richer and made the first generations who experienced it measurably worse off. Both are true, and the gap between them is filled with politics — which is another way of saying that how a technology's benefits get distributed is a decision, not a law of nature.

Part IV

Who counts as a person

Chapter 07

Abolition

The campaign to end the slave trade is one of the first recognisably modern political movements, and its methods are worth knowing because they are still the methods.

Campaigners gathered evidence and published it — testimony from sailors, the diagram of a slave ship packed with human beings that became one of the most effective images ever printed. They organised petitions on a scale nobody had seen. They ran a consumer boycott of slave-grown sugar involving perhaps three hundred thousand households. They put a symbol on cheap pottery so ordinary people could wear the cause. And they kept going for decades against an interest with enormous money and parliamentary influence.

Britain abolished the trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1833 — compensating the owners, not the enslaved, with a sum so large that the British government finished repaying the loan in 2015. The United States required a civil war that killed more Americans than any other conflict in its history. Brazil, the largest importer of all, did not abolish until 1888.

Formal abolition also did not end forced labour; it changed its legal form, into indenture, sharecropping, convict leasing and colonial compulsion. The line between a labour contract you cannot refuse and slavery is thinner than the law suggests.

Chapter 08

Votes for women

The argument against was that women were represented by their husbands and fathers, which was an odd thing to say to a widow who paid the same taxes as her neighbour.

The campaign ran for decades in the polite register — petitions, meetings, bills that were talked out of parliament year after year. When it became clear that politeness was being used as a way of never deciding, a section of the movement turned to confrontation: smashing windows, chaining themselves to railings, arson. They were imprisoned, went on hunger strike, and were force-fed, which is torture and was recognised as such at the time. One woman stepped in front of the king's horse at the Derby and died.

The First World War then did something the campaign could not: with men at the front, women ran the factories, the transport and the farms, and the argument that they were constitutionally unsuited to public affairs became difficult to make with a straight face. Britain gave a partial vote in 1918 and an equal one in 1928.

Whether the militancy helped or hindered is still argued. What is not in doubt is the pattern — a long polite phase that achieves sympathy and nothing else, a confrontational phase that forces the question onto the agenda, and an external shock that finally makes the old position untenable.

Reference

What to carry forward

The idea that did the work

Authority stopped being divine and became a bargain — and rights stopped being granted by rulers and became something people have by being people. Once that is in cheap print and literacy is rising, every existing arrangement has to justify itself.

Why the same idea produced different outcomes

America built where there was little to tear down and wrote an amendable constitution. France demolished a functioning state during a war with a starving capital, and got the Terror and then an emperor. Circumstances shape revolutions more than principles do.

The one that gets left out

Haiti — the only country created by a successful revolt of enslaved people — then paid its former enslavers reparations for over a century. Its omission from general histories is not an accident of record-keeping.

The revolution without a battle

Steam ended the two constraints on production: you no longer had to be near falling water and you no longer had to stop. It made humanity far richer and the first generations measurably worse off — and what closed that gap was organised politics, not the market.
