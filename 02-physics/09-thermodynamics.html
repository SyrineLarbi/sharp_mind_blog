Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 09 of 20

Thermodynamics. The rules every engine, fridge and living thing must obey, with no exceptions ever found.

Thermodynamics began as a practical question from the age of steam: how much work can you get out of a lump of coal? The answer turned out to reach far beyond engines. The same few rules decide how efficient a power station can be, why your phone gets warm, why a heat pump can deliver more heat than the electricity it uses, and why your body needs food at all. They are some of the most trusted statements in science. They also say clearly what is impossible, which makes them the best defence against anyone selling a machine that runs for free.

Question 1 · What is it?

The science of heat, work, and what you can turn into what

Thermodynamics is about energy in bulk: how it moves as heat, how it becomes work, and the limits on that exchange. It does not need to know what matter is made of to give the right answer.

Chapter 01

A science born in the engine room

Thermodynamics started with money. In the early 1800s, steam engines pumped mines and drove Britain's factories. They worked, but they were terribly wasteful, and nobody knew whether a better design could do much more with the same coal. Engineers improved them by trial and error.

In 1824 a young French engineer called Sadi Carnot published a short book, Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire. He asked the question in its most general form. Forget pistons and valves. What is the best that any heat engine could ever do? His answer was surprising. The limit does not depend on the machine or the fuel. It depends only on how hot the hot side is and how cold the cold side is. Carnot died of cholera in 1832, aged 36, and his book was mostly ignored for years. Today it is seen as the founding document of the field.

Carnot believed heat was a fluid, called "caloric". That was wrong, yet his main result survived the correction. Thermodynamics keeps being right even when the picture underneath it changes.

Chapter 02

Four words that people mix up

Everyday language uses "heat" and "temperature" as if they meant the same thing. In physics they are different, and most confusion about the subject starts here.

WordWhat it means in physicsEveryday example
EnergyThe capacity to cause change. It comes in many forms (motion, chemical, electrical, nuclear) and is measured in joules.The energy stored in a litre of petrol
TemperatureHow vigorously the particles in a thing are moving, on average. It says which way heat will flow, not how much is stored.A spark from a sparkler is very hot but holds very little energy
HeatEnergy on the move because of a temperature difference. Things do not "contain heat"; they contain energy, and heat is a transfer.A hot pan warming your hand
WorkEnergy transferred by a force pushing something through a distance. It is the useful, organised kind of transfer.Steam pushing a piston, a motor turning a wheel

A sparkler's sparks are over 1,000 °C, yet each is so tiny that it holds almost no energy. A bath at 50 °C is far cooler and would scald you. Temperature is intensity; energy is quantity.

Chapter 03

The four laws on one page

The whole subject rests on four laws. They are numbered strangely, from zero to three, because the most basic one was only recognised as a law after the other three already had their numbers.

LawFormal ideaPlain version
ZerothIf A is in balance with C, and B is in balance with C, then A and B are in balance with each other.Thermometers work. Temperature is a real, shared property.
FirstEnergy is conserved. The change in a system's energy equals heat added minus work done.You cannot get something for nothing.
SecondHeat flows by itself only from hot to cold. No engine can turn heat completely into work. Entropy of an isolated system never decreases.You cannot even break even.
ThirdAs temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a minimum, and absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.You cannot get out of the game.

The plain versions are an old student joke, and an accurate one. The first law forbids machines that create energy. The second forbids machines that turn all their heat into work. The third says the coldest possible temperature, absolute zero, −273.15 °C, is a limit you can approach but never touch. Scientists have cooled atoms to within a billionth of a degree of it, and never all the way.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Energy is kept, but its quality is always spent

The first law says the amount of energy never changes. The second law says its usefulness always declines. Engines, fridges and kettles all live inside that pair of rules.

Chapter 04

The first law: heat is a form of energy

The caloric theory died slowly. In the 1790s Count Rumford, supervising the boring of cannon in Munich, noticed that drilling produced heat endlessly for as long as the drill turned. A stored fluid should have run out. Heat seemed to come from the motion itself.

The decisive measurements came in the 1840s from James Prescott Joule, the son of a brewer in Manchester. In his best-known experiment, a falling weight turned a paddle wheel inside an insulated tank of water. The paddles stirred the water, and the water warmed very slightly. Joule measured exactly how much a given fall raised the temperature. The same amount of mechanical work always produced the same amount of heat. Heat and work were two forms of one thing. The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him.

This is the first law of thermodynamics, and it is the law of conservation of energy applied to heat. Energy can change form, from chemical to heat, from heat to motion, from motion to electricity, but the total stays the same. It is why every claimed "free energy" machine fails. The wider story of conservation has its own file: Energy and conservation.

Chapter 05

The second law and the engine limit

If energy is never lost, why do engines waste so much? Because the first law counts quantity, and the second law cares about quality. A heat engine works by letting heat flow from a hot place (burning fuel, or steam) to a cold place (the air, or a river). On the way, it diverts part of that flow into work. The rest must be dumped into the cold side. An engine cannot run on a hot side alone; it needs somewhere colder to throw its waste heat.

Every heat engine, drawn the same way

   Best possible efficiency (Carnot):   1 - Tc / Th    (temperatures in kelvin)

Carnot's limit uses temperatures measured from absolute zero, on the kelvin scale (0 K is −273.15 °C, and 0 °C is about 273 K). Take a modern steam power station with steam at about 550 °C (about 823 K) and cooling water at about 30 °C (about 303 K). The best any engine could possibly do between those temperatures is 1 − 303/823, or about 63 per cent. Real plants lose more to friction, leaks and imperfect heat exchange, and a typical coal plant converts somewhere around a third to two-fifths of its fuel energy into electricity.

The formula gives engineers one clear lever: make the hot side hotter. That is why the history of engines is a history of materials that survive higher temperatures. It is also why the best gas power stations use a "combined cycle". A gas turbine burns fuel at very high temperature, and its hot exhaust then boils water for a second, steam turbine. Together they reach about 60 per cent, among the highest of any heat engine in regular use.

Chapter 06

Running the engine backwards: fridges and heat pumps

Heat never flows from cold to hot by itself. But it can be pushed uphill, if you pay for it with work. That is what a fridge does. A fluid called a refrigerant circulates in a closed loop. It evaporates inside the fridge, and evaporating absorbs heat, the same reason wet skin feels cold in a breeze. A compressor then squeezes the vapour, which makes it hot, and it releases that heat through the coils at the back. The inside gets colder, the kitchen gets slightly warmer, and the electricity pays for the trip.

A heat pump is the same machine pointed the other way. It pulls heat out of cold outdoor air, or the ground, and delivers it inside a house. Here is the part that surprises people. Because the electricity is used to move heat rather than to make it, a heat pump typically delivers about three to four units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses. Engineers call that ratio the coefficient of performance. An electric heater, by comparison, turns one unit of electricity into exactly one unit of heat.

Not a free lunch

A heat pump does not break the first law. The extra heat was already outside, in the cold air. The electricity only carries it in. The second law sets the price: the bigger the gap between outside and inside, the more work each unit of heat costs, so efficiency drops on the coldest days.

Chapter 07

How heat travels: three routes

Heat moves from place to place in three ways, and almost every practical problem, from cooking to building design to cooling a computer chip, is about controlling them.

Conduction

Direct contact. Fast particles knock into slower neighbours and pass on their energy. Metals conduct well because they have free electrons that carry energy quickly. Still air conducts badly, which is why wool, down and double glazing all work by trapping air.

Convection

Heat carried by a moving fluid. Warm air or water expands, becomes lighter, and rises, and cooler fluid sinks to take its place. Convection drives sea breezes, boiling pans, and the great circulation of the atmosphere and oceans.

Radiation

Heat carried by light, mostly infrared light that our eyes cannot see. It needs no material at all, which is how the Sun warms Earth across empty space. A shiny surface reflects it, which is why emergency blankets and vacuum flasks are silvered.

A vacuum flask blocks all three: the vacuum stops conduction and convection, and the silvered walls stop most radiation. You can block the routes, but you can never make heat stop wanting to flow.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Power stations, bodies, and the planet

Thermodynamics sets the ceiling on how well we can turn fuel into useful work. That ceiling shapes energy policy, the climate debate and even biology.

Chapter 08

Why most of the world's fuel becomes waste heat

Every power station that burns fuel, or uses nuclear fission, is a heat engine. So is every petrol or diesel car. The second law guarantees that a large share of their fuel energy ends up as low temperature heat in the air or a river. That is not bad engineering. It is the price of turning heat into work.

MachineRough share of energy turned into useful workWhy
Petrol car engineTypically about 20–30% in real driving; the best designs reach about 40% at their sweet spotA heat engine, often run at part load in traffic
Coal or nuclear steam plantAbout 33–40%Limited steam temperature
Combined-cycle gas plantAbout 60%Two engines in series, a very hot top temperature
Large electric motorAbove 90%Not a heat engine, so Carnot's limit does not apply

This explains a fact that confuses many energy debates. When a country replaces fuel-burning machines with electric ones powered by wind, solar or hydro, its total energy use can fall sharply without anyone doing less. The heat-engine losses simply disappear, because wind and solar electricity never had to pass through a heat engine. The same logic is why an electric car needs far less energy per kilometre than a petrol one.

Chapter 09

You are a thermodynamic system

A resting adult gives off roughly 100 watts of heat, about as much as an old-fashioned light bulb. That is why a crowded room warms up so quickly. The energy comes from food, through chemical reactions that are a slow, controlled version of burning. Muscles turn chemical energy into motion directly, but well under half of it; the rest becomes heat. That is why exercise makes you hot and why you shiver when you are cold: shivering is your muscles deliberately wasting energy to warm you.

Life also obeys the second law. A living body is a highly ordered structure that keeps itself in shape by taking in high-quality energy (food, or sunlight for plants) and releasing low-quality energy (heat). You stay alive by constantly spending order you take in from outside. The Entropy file explains this in full.

Chapter 10

The climate is a heat-balance problem

Earth's temperature is set by a thermodynamic budget. Sunlight arrives, mostly as visible light. The ground and oceans absorb it and warm up, and then they radiate energy back toward space as infrared light. When incoming and outgoing energy are equal, the temperature is steady.

Certain gases in the atmosphere, mainly water vapour and carbon dioxide, let visible sunlight pass but absorb and re-emit part of the outgoing infrared. That slows the escape of heat, and the surface has to warm until the outflow matches the inflow again. This is the greenhouse effect, and its basic physics has been understood since the nineteenth century. Without any greenhouse gases, Earth's average surface temperature would be far below freezing. Adding more carbon dioxide tips the balance further toward warming. Volume 30 explains how that warming is measured (What is happening, and how it is known).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A theory of enormous reach, with clear blind spots

Thermodynamics is powerful because it ignores the details. That same habit is what it cannot do.

Chapter 11

Strengths and weaknesses of the theory

It is the only physical theory of universal content which I am convinced will never be overthrown, within the framework of applicability of its basic concepts.

Albert Einstein · on classical thermodynamics, Autobiographical Notes (1949)
FeatureStrengthWeakness
Ignores what matter is made ofGives correct answers for steam, batteries, stars and cells alike. It survived the discovery of atoms and of quantum physics unchanged.It cannot explain why its laws hold. That needs statistical physics, which counts the particles.
Works with states, not pathsYou can calculate the energy available from a reaction without knowing every step in between.It says nothing about speed. It says diamond should slowly turn to graphite, but not that this would take far longer than the age of the universe.
Built for balance (equilibrium)Very precise for systems that have settled down.Weather, living cells and flowing fluids are far from balance, where the classical theory gives only partial answers.
States limits clearlyInstantly rules out perpetual motion and inflated efficiency claims.A limit is not a design. Knowing the ceiling does not tell you how to reach it.
Treats heat as energy in bulkSimple, reliable tools for engineers.At the scale of single molecules, the laws hold only on average, with brief, measurable fluctuations.

The last row is subtle and important. For a handful of molecules, heat can briefly flow the "wrong" way, and experiments have measured it. The second law is a law of large numbers. For anything you can see, failure is too unlikely ever to happen.

Chapter 13

The open frontier: systems that never settle down

The classical theory is a science of systems at rest. Much of the interesting world is not at rest. A cell, a hurricane and a city all keep a structure only because energy is flowing through them. Since the 1990s, "non-equilibrium" thermodynamics has found exact results, called fluctuation theorems, that say how often tiny systems break the second law and by how much.

The most suggestive link is with information. In 1961 Rolf Landauer at IBM argued that erasing one bit of information must release a minimum, tiny amount of heat. Experiments in the 2010s supported his limit. Today's computers waste vastly more than that minimum, but it says something deep: information is physical, and handling it has a thermodynamic cost. As computing becomes a larger share of the world's electricity use, that old engine-room question, how much useful work can we get from our energy, is returning in a new form.

The core idea

Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. The first law says energy is never created or destroyed. The second law says heat flows by itself only from hot to cold, so no engine can turn all its heat into work. The best possible efficiency depends only on the hot and cold temperatures.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Absolute zero is −273.15 °C (0 kelvin), and cannot be reached.
  • Carnot limit: 1 − Tcold/Thot, in kelvin.
  • Steam power plants: about a third to two-fifths efficient; combined-cycle gas about 60%.
  • A heat pump delivers about 3–4 units of heat per unit of electricity.
  • A resting person gives off about 100 watts.
The common mistake

Thinking engines are wasteful only because engineers are careless. Most of the waste is required by the second law, not by poor design. Better engineering can only close the gap to Carnot's limit, never pass it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a heat pump can give out three times more heat than the electricity it uses, without breaking the first law? If you can, you have understood the difference between making heat and moving it.

The words, in plain words
Thermodynamics
The physics of heat, work and energy, and the limits on turning one into another.
Temperature
A measure of how fast a thing's particles are moving on average. It decides which way heat flows.
Heat
Energy moving from a hotter thing to a colder one.
Work
Energy transferred by a force moving something, the organised and useful kind of transfer.
Joule
The unit of energy. One watt is one joule per second.
Kelvin
A temperature scale starting at absolute zero. Its degrees are the same size as Celsius degrees.
Absolute zero
The lowest possible temperature, −273.15 °C, where particle motion is at its minimum.
Heat engine
Any machine that makes work from heat flowing from a hot place to a cold one.
Carnot efficiency
The best efficiency any heat engine could have between two given temperatures.
Heat pump
A machine that uses work to move heat from a colder place to a warmer one.
Coefficient of performance
Heat delivered by a heat pump divided by the energy used to run it.
Latent heat
Energy absorbed or released when a substance melts, boils, freezes or condenses, without changing temperature.
Equilibrium
A state where nothing is changing overall, because all flows are balanced.
