Black holes. A prediction even its discoverers doubted, now photographed twice.

For most of the twentieth century, black holes were a strange mathematical result that many physicists hoped nature would avoid. Today they are among the best-studied objects in astronomy. We have weighed them using the stars that orbit them, heard them collide, and taken pictures of the shadows they cast. They also remain the place where our two best theories of nature, gravity and quantum physics, openly contradict each other. That makes black holes both a settled fact and one of the great unsolved problems at the same time.

Question 1 · What is it?

A region where gravity wins completely

A black hole is a region of space where matter is packed so densely that nothing, not even light, can climb back out. It is not an object with a surface, but a place with an edge.

Chapter 01

From "dark stars" to a reluctant prediction

The basic idea is older than most people think. In 1783 the English clergyman and scientist John Michell reasoned with Newton's laws. If a star were heavy enough, the speed needed to escape its surface would exceed the speed of light, and its light could not get out. He called such objects "dark stars". The French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace reached a similar idea a few years later. Then the notion faded, because nobody knew how light and gravity really interacted.

Einstein's general relativity, published in 1915, described gravity as the curving of space and time by mass. Within weeks, the German astronomer Karl Schwarzschild found an exact solution of Einstein's equations for a single round mass, working it out while serving in the army on the Russian front. He died the following year. His solution contained a strange boundary: a radius at which, mathematically, time seemed to stop. For decades most physicists, Einstein among them, assumed real stars would never be squeezed that small.

In 1939 Robert Oppenheimer and Hartland Snyder showed that a heavy enough star, once it ran out of fuel, would collapse past that boundary with nothing to stop it. In the 1960s Roger Penrose proved that such collapse was not a quirk of perfect symmetry but a general result. The American physicist John Wheeler popularised the name black hole in 1967. The idea went from mathematical embarrassment to physical prediction in about fifty years.

The event horizon is the boundary of no return. Anything inside it, even light, must move further in. Its size is set by the mass, a distance called the Schwarzschild radius. For a black hole with the mass of the Sun it would be about 3 kilometres. If Earth were crushed into a black hole, it would be about the size of a large marble, under 2 centimetres across.

Just outside the horizon, gravity is so strong that light can travel in circles, forming a photon ring. Further out, many black holes are surrounded by an accretion disc, gas spiralling in and heated by friction to millions of degrees. At the centre, relativity predicts a singularity, a point of infinite density. Most physicists read that infinity as a sign that the theory has reached its limit, not as a literal description.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Collapse, feeding, spinning and, perhaps, evaporating

A black hole is born when nothing can resist gravity. After that, it grows by eating, can spin, can power the brightest objects in the universe, and in theory slowly leaks away.

Chapter 04

How a star becomes a black hole

A star is a long balancing act. Gravity pulls its gas inward; the heat and pressure from nuclear fusion in its core push outward. When a very massive star, roughly twenty or more times the Sun's mass, runs out of fuel it can fuse, that support fails. Its core collapses in less than a second.

For smaller cores, the collapse stops when matter is packed into a ball of neutrons, as dense as an atomic nucleus: a neutron star. The rebound can blow the outer star apart in a supernova. But if the remaining core is more than about two to three times the Sun's mass, even neutrons cannot hold it up. No known force can stop the collapse, and the core falls inside its own event horizon. Some very massive stars may skip the explosion and collapse almost directly.

Chapter 05

Falling in: why size changes everything

What would happen to someone falling into a black hole depends surprisingly on its size. The danger is not gravity itself but the difference in gravity between your head and your feet. Close to a small, stellar-mass black hole, that difference becomes enormous well before the horizon. Your feet would be pulled much harder than your head, stretching you into a thin stream. Physicists, with dark humour, call this spaghettification.

For a supermassive black hole the horizon is so large that the stretching at the edge is mild. You could cross the horizon of a black hole of a billion Suns without feeling anything unusual at that moment. The bigger the black hole, the gentler its edge. Your fate inside would be the same, though; you would just reach it later.

Chapter 06

Spinning black holes and cosmic jets

Real stars rotate, so real black holes should spin. In 1963 the New Zealand mathematician Roy Kerr found the solution of Einstein's equations for a spinning black hole. Near a spinning one, space itself is dragged around, so nothing can stay still there, even outside the horizon.

That spin matters for astronomy. Gas falling into a black hole heats up and glows, and accretion is the most efficient way known to turn matter into light. It can release a sizeable fraction of the infalling mass as energy, several times more per kilogram than nuclear fusion in stars, which converts under one per cent. Twisted magnetic fields near a spinning black hole can also launch narrow jets of particles at nearly the speed of light, stretching far beyond the host galaxy.

The brightest of these feeding black holes are quasars, first recognised in 1963, when Maarten Schmidt showed that a faint star-like point was in fact enormously far away and therefore extraordinarily bright. A single quasar can outshine all the stars of its galaxy combined.

Chapter 07

Hawking radiation: black holes are not quite black

In 1974 Stephen Hawking combined relativity with quantum theory near the horizon and found something nobody expected. Black holes should glow faintly, as if they had a temperature, and so slowly lose mass. The smaller the black hole, the hotter it glows and the faster it shrinks. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would have a temperature far below that of the cosmic background, so in today's universe it actually absorbs more than it emits.

Its evaporation time would be vastly longer than the current age of the universe, by a factor with dozens of zeros. Hawking radiation has never been observed, because it is far too faint for any real black hole. But it is widely accepted as theory, and it led directly to the information puzzle in Chapter 14.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

How we proved they exist, and what they do to galaxies

Something that emits no light is hard to find. Astronomers found black holes three different ways, and each piece of evidence is independent of the others.

Chapter 08

Evidence one: X-rays from a dark companion

The first strong case came from X-ray astronomy, which needs telescopes in space because the atmosphere blocks X-rays. In the early 1970s, astronomers found that a bright X-ray source called Cygnus X-1 was orbiting a giant blue star. From the orbit they could estimate the mass of the unseen partner. It was far too heavy to be a neutron star, and it was invisible. Gas pulled off the blue star was heating up as it fell toward something small and very dense.

Stephen Hawking famously bet the physicist Kip Thorne in 1974 that Cygnus X-1 was not a black hole, as an insurance policy in case his life's work was wrong. He conceded the bet in 1990. Dozens of similar systems are now known in our galaxy. In 2024 the Gaia space mission identified a quiet black hole of about 33 solar masses, called Gaia BH3, simply from the wobble it causes in a companion star.

Chapter 09

Evidence two: stars racing around the centre of our galaxy

The centre of the Milky Way is about 26,000 light-years away, hidden behind dust. Infrared light passes through the dust. From the 1990s, two teams, one led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany and one by Andrea Ghez in the United States, tracked individual stars there for decades, using techniques that correct for the blurring of Earth's atmosphere.

The stars move in tight elliptical orbits around an invisible point. One star, known as S2, completes an orbit in about 16 years and passes within about 120 times the Earth–Sun distance of the centre, moving at several thousand kilometres per second. Its orbit reveals a mass of about four million Suns packed into a region smaller than our Solar System. Nothing except a black hole fits. Genzel and Ghez shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics with Roger Penrose. The object is called Sagittarius A*, pronounced "A-star". More on our galaxy is in The Milky Way.

Chapter 10

Evidence three: the pictures, and the sounds

A single radio dish cannot see anything as small as a black hole's shadow from Earth. So the Event Horizon Telescope linked radio observatories across the world, from Hawaii to Spain to the South Pole, into one virtual telescope roughly the size of the planet. Each site recorded data on hard drives, which were flown to central computers and combined.

In April 2019 the collaboration released the first image of a black hole: the one in the galaxy M87, about 55 million light-years away, with a mass of about six and a half billion Suns. It showed a glowing ring of hot gas around a dark centre, the shadow of the horizon, with the size and shape that Einstein's theory predicted. In May 2022 they released an image of Sagittarius A*, our own galaxy's black hole, which looks strikingly similar despite being about a thousand times less massive.

A fourth line of evidence is sound, in a sense. Since 2015, detectors have recorded gravitational waves from pairs of black holes spiralling together and merging. The shape of each signal matches relativity's predictions for black holes and nothing else that is known.

Chapter 11

Why galaxies care about their black holes

A supermassive black hole holds only a tiny fraction of its galaxy's mass. Yet astronomers have found that larger galaxies tend to have proportionally larger central black holes. That suggests they grow together. The leading explanation is feedback: when the black hole feeds, the energy it releases heats and blows away gas, which slows star formation across the galaxy and starves the black hole in turn.

Black holes are also the toughest test of general relativity. Every image and every merger signal so far has matched the theory. Recent observations with the James Webb Space Telescope have found surprisingly large black holes already present less than a billion years after the Big Bang, which is forcing astronomers to rethink how fast they can grow. Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What we know firmly, and where knowledge stops

For a subject like this, the honest trade-off is between the strength of the evidence and the limits of the theory.

What black holes give science

A natural laboratory for gravity at its most extreme, a key to how galaxies evolve, and the brightest beacons in the early universe. They turned general relativity from a subtle correction into a theory with dramatic, testable predictions.

What they cost science

A deep contradiction. Relativity and quantum physics give conflicting answers about what happens to information, and black holes are where that conflict cannot be avoided. They are also easy to sensationalise, which spreads misconceptions.

Chapter 14

The information paradox

Quantum physics has a firm rule: information about a system can be scrambled but never truly lost. In principle, you could reconstruct a burnt book from its ashes, smoke and heat. Hawking's calculation said that a black hole's radiation is featureless, depending only on its mass, charge and spin. If a black hole evaporates completely, the information about everything that fell in seems to vanish. In 1976 Hawking argued that it really does, which would break quantum theory.

Most physicists now believe information survives, and Hawking himself changed his view in 2004. Work since 2019 has shown, within certain theoretical models, how information could find its way out in the radiation. How it actually happens in a real black hole is still unknown. The answer is expected to say something deep about the quantum nature of space and time, which is why a question about objects nobody can visit is one of the most actively studied problems in physics. The link to entropy is explained in Entropy.

The core idea

A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape past the event horizon. They form when massive stars collapse, and supermassive ones sit at the centres of galaxies. We know they exist from X-ray systems, star orbits, direct images and gravitational waves.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A black hole of one Sun's mass: horizon about 3 km in radius.
  • Sagittarius A*: about 4 million Suns, about 26,000 light-years away.
  • M87's black hole: about 6.5 billion Suns; first image released 2019.
  • Our own galaxy's black hole imaged in 2022.
The common mistake

Imagining the event horizon as a solid surface. It is a boundary in space, not a thing, and for a big black hole you could cross it without noticing.

The words, in plain words
Event horizon
The boundary around a black hole beyond which nothing can escape.
Schwarzschild radius
The radius of the event horizon, set by the black hole's mass.
Singularity
The point at the centre where relativity predicts infinite density, a sign the theory breaks down.
Accretion disc
A flattened, glowing disc of gas spiralling into a black hole.
Photon ring
A thin ring where light can orbit just outside the horizon.
Stellar-mass black hole
One formed from a collapsed star, a few to tens of times the Sun's mass.
Supermassive black hole
One of millions to billions of solar masses, found at galaxy centres.
Spaghettification
The stretching of an object by the difference in gravity across it.
Quasar
An extremely bright galaxy centre powered by a feeding supermassive black hole.
Jet
A narrow beam of fast particles launched from near a spinning black hole.
Hawking radiation
A predicted faint glow by which black holes slowly lose mass.
Event Horizon Telescope
A worldwide network of radio dishes working as one Earth-sized telescope.
Information paradox
The conflict over whether information that falls into a black hole is lost forever.
