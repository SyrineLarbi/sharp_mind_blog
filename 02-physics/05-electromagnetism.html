Electromagnetism. Two forces that turned out to be one, and the modern world built on the join.

In the early 1800s electricity and magnetism were separate curiosities. Within about fifty years, three discoveries had joined them into one force. A current makes a magnet. A moving magnet makes a current. And the two, chasing each other through space, make light. Those three facts explain the electric motor, the power station, the transformer, radio, Wi-Fi and the colours you see. They also hold every atom in your body together. This file follows the story from a twitching compass needle to the equations that, by most physicists' account, began modern physics.

Question 1 · What is it?

One force with two faces

Electromagnetism is the force between electric charges. When charges sit still we call its effects electric. When they move, we also see magnetic effects. They are one thing seen from different angles.

Chapter 01

The compass that twitched

In April 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted was demonstrating electric current from a battery. According to the usual account, he noticed during a lecture that a compass needle nearby jumped each time he connected the circuit. Whatever the exact setting, he followed it up carefully over the next months and published in July 1820. A current in a wire pushed a compass needle sideways, at right angles to the wire.

That right angle was the strange part. Every force known until then pushed or pulled along the line joining two things. Gravity pulls the apple straight towards the Earth. This new force curled around the wire. Electricity in motion produced magnetism, and the magnetism circled the current. News spread across Europe within weeks, and in France André-Marie Ampère quickly showed that two wires carrying currents attract or repel each other, just like magnets. The unit of current, the amp, is named after him.

Chapter 03

The force behind almost everything you touch

Physics recognises four fundamental forces: gravity, electromagnetism, and two forces that act only inside atomic nuclei, called the strong and weak nuclear forces. Of these, electromagnetism is the one that shapes daily life most, though people rarely notice it.

When you push a door, nothing actually touches in the way it feels. The electrons in your hand repel the electrons in the door. When a chair holds you up, it is electromagnetic repulsion between atoms that stops you sinking through it. Friction, the stretch of a rubber band, the strength of steel, every chemical reaction, and the signals in your nerves are all electromagnetic. Apart from gravity and the inside of the nucleus, every force you experience is electromagnetism.

It is also enormously strong. The electric repulsion between two protons is about 10³⁶ times stronger than the gravitational pull between them, a 1 followed by 36 zeros. We do not notice this because positive and negative charges almost always balance. Gravity only wins at the scale of planets because it never cancels.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Motors, generators, transformers, and a wave

Each of the four effects became a machine. The last became a theory that changed physics forever.

Chapter 04

Electromagnets: magnetism with an on-off switch

A single wire makes a weak magnetic field. Wind the wire into a coil and the fields from each loop add together. Put an iron core inside the coil and the iron's domains line up with the field, multiplying it many times. The result is an electromagnet: a magnet that is strong when current flows and almost nothing when it stops.

That switchability is the whole point. Scrapyard cranes pick up cars and drop them by cutting the current. A doorbell's electromagnet pulls a striker against a bell. A relay uses a small current to work an electromagnet that closes a switch in a big circuit, which is how early telephone exchanges and the first computers worked. Loudspeakers push a cone back and forth with an electromagnet driven by the music signal. The magnets in MRI scanners and particle accelerators are electromagnets made from superconducting wire.

Chapter 05

Faraday and the discovery of induction

Michael Faraday is one of the great stories of science. He was the son of a blacksmith, left school early and became a bookbinder's apprentice in London. He read the science books he bound, attended public lectures by the chemist Humphry Davy, and talked his way into a job as Davy's assistant at the Royal Institution in 1813. He had almost no mathematics. He thought in pictures, and it was Faraday who imagined "lines of force" filling the space around magnets, the idea that became the modern field.

If electricity can make magnetism, Faraday asked, can magnetism make electricity? In August 1831 he found the answer. A steady magnet beside a wire does nothing. But a changing magnetic field creates a voltage in a nearby wire. Push a magnet into a coil and a current flows. Hold it still and the current stops. Pull it out and the current flows the other way. This is electromagnetic induction, and it is probably the single most economically important discovery in physics.

Chapter 06

How an electric motor works

A wire carrying current inside a magnetic field feels a sideways push. Bend the wire into a loop on an axle, and the push on one side of the loop is up while the push on the other is down. The loop turns. If you reverse the current every half turn, using a sliding contact or, in modern motors, electronic switching, the loop keeps turning in the same direction.

The motor and the generator are the same machine run in opposite directions

Faraday built the first device that turned electricity into continuous motion in 1821, a wire that circled endlessly round a magnet in a dish of mercury. Practical motors followed over the century. Today electric motors use a large share of all the electricity generated worldwide, often estimated at around 40 to 45 percent, running fans, pumps, compressors, conveyor belts, lifts, trains and cars. Every motor is a controlled use of the push between current and field.

Chapter 07

Generators and transformers

Run a motor backwards and it becomes a generator. Turn the coil in the magnetic field, and induction drives a current through it. Almost all the world's electricity is made this way. Steam from burning coal or gas, or heated by a nuclear reactor, spins a turbine; so does falling water, and so does the wind. The turbine turns a generator. Solar panels are the main exception, making electricity directly from light without any moving magnet.

A transformer uses induction without any movement at all. Two coils are wound on one iron core. Alternating current in the first coil makes a constantly changing magnetic field in the core, and that changing field induces a current in the second coil. If the second coil has more turns, the voltage goes up; fewer turns, and it goes down. This is how grids raise voltage for long-distance transmission and lower it again for homes, as explained in Electricity. It only works with alternating current, because a steady current makes no change for induction to use.

Induction is everywhere once you look. An induction hob makes a rapidly changing field that induces currents directly in the bottom of an iron pan, heating the pan and not the glass. Wireless phone chargers pass energy between two flat coils. Contactless bank cards are powered for a moment by the reader's field. Metal detectors, guitar pickups and the sensors in traffic lights that detect waiting cars all rely on it.

Chapter 08

Maxwell: four equations and a prediction

In the 1860s the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell did for electricity and magnetism what Newton had done for motion. He took Faraday's pictures of fields and turned them into mathematics. In a paper published in 1865, and in his book of 1873, he set out a complete theory. Later physicists, notably Oliver Heaviside, condensed it into the four short equations now known as Maxwell's equations.

In plain words they say: electric charges produce electric fields; there are no lone magnetic poles; a changing magnetic field produces an electric field (Faraday's induction); and an electric current, or a changing electric field, produces a magnetic field. That last phrase was Maxwell's own addition, and it changed everything.

With it, the equations predicted that a changing electric field makes a changing magnetic field, which makes a changing electric field, and so on. The disturbance should travel outward on its own as a wave. Maxwell calculated its speed from laboratory measurements of electricity and magnetism. It came out at the measured speed of light. Maxwell concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. Electricity, magnetism and optics, three separate subjects, had become one.

Chapter 09

Hertz, radio, and the electromagnetic spectrum

Maxwell died in 1879, before anyone had made his waves deliberately. In experiments around 1887 and 1888, the German physicist Heinrich Hertz used sparks to produce electromagnetic waves and detected them across his laboratory. He showed they reflect, bend and travel at the speed of light. The unit of frequency, the hertz, is named after him. Within about fifteen years, Guglielmo Marconi and others had turned the waves into wireless telegraphy, and in 1901 Marconi reported receiving a signal sent across the Atlantic.

We now know light is only a thin slice of a whole family. All of it is the same wave, differing only in wavelength and frequency.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Power, communication, and the road to relativity

Electromagnetism gave us the electrified world, the wireless world, and the clue that led Einstein to rethink space and time.

Chapter 10

From a coil on a bench to a world on the grid

Faraday's induction coil of 1831 took about fifty years to grow into public power. In the early 1880s the first central power stations opened in London and New York. By the middle of the twentieth century electricity reached most homes in rich countries. Today the large majority of people on Earth have access, though as of the mid-2020s several hundred million people, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, still do not.

The change was not just more light. Electric motors let factories put a small motor on every machine instead of driving everything from one steam engine through belts and shafts. Economic historians argue that this reorganisation of factory floors, which took decades, was where most of the productivity gains from electricity came from. The technology arrived quickly; learning to use it well took a generation. Many observers draw the same lesson about computers and AI today.

Chapter 11

A world connected by invisible waves

Every wireless device is an application of Maxwell's equations. A phone's antenna drives electrons back and forth, making waves that spread out at the speed of light. Another antenna, perhaps kilometres away, feels those waves push its own electrons, which creates a tiny current that carries the signal. Radio, television, mobile networks, satellite links, GPS (Volume 12), Bluetooth and Wi-Fi all work this way. Radar sends waves out and listens for echoes, which is how air traffic control tracks planes and weather services track rain.

Because all these services share the same family of waves, they must not interfere. Governments divide the radio spectrum into bands and license them, and international agreements coordinate the bands across borders. Spectrum auctions for mobile networks have raised very large sums. The next generations of mobile technology are covered in 5G and 6G.

Chapter 12

The puzzle that led to relativity

Maxwell's equations held a hidden problem. They give one speed for light, with no mention of who is measuring it. But Newton's mechanics said speeds should add: if you run towards a light beam, it should seem to come at you faster. Experiments, most famously by Albert Michelson and Edward Morley in 1887, found no such difference.

In 1905 Albert Einstein published a paper with a revealing title, "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies". He took Maxwell seriously: the speed of light is the same for everyone. To make that work, time and space themselves had to stretch and shrink depending on how fast you move. That was special relativity, summarised in Volume 25, Why nothing goes faster than light.

One of its consequences is beautiful. Magnetism can be understood as a side effect of electricity plus relativity. A current in a wire looks electrically neutral to you. Move alongside the electrons and, because of relativity, the spacing of the charges changes slightly from your point of view, and you feel an electric force. What one observer calls magnetic, another calls electric. They really are one force.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The most successful theory in physics, and its practical costs

As science, electromagnetism has almost no weaknesses at everyday scales. As technology, it brings dependence, crowded airwaves and a long-running health debate.

The health question deserves a fair statement because it is often argued badly. Radio waves, including those from phones and masts, are non-ionising: each bit of energy they carry is far too small to damage DNA directly. Their known effect, at high intensity, is gentle heating, which international exposure limits are designed to keep well below. In 2011 the World Health Organization's cancer agency classified radio-frequency fields as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B), a category that signals limited evidence, not demonstrated harm. Large studies since then have not shown a clear rise in brain tumours as phone use spread. Most health agencies conclude that normal exposure is not a known health risk, while continuing to monitor.

Chapter 14

The most precisely tested idea we have

As a theory, electromagnetism has one limit: Maxwell's version treats fields as smooth and continuous. At the scale of atoms, light comes in lumps called photons, and the theory needed a quantum upgrade. That upgrade, quantum electrodynamics (QED), was completed in the late 1940s by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger, Sin-Itiro Tomonaga and others.

QED's predictions for the magnetic strength of the electron agree with experiment to better than one part in a billion, making it one of the most precisely tested theories in all of science. Maxwell's equations remain exactly right whenever there are enough photons to treat light as a smooth wave, which covers radio, power engineering and almost all optics.

Whatever happens, the equations will not change. The future of electromagnetism is engineering, not new laws: better materials for magnets and conductors, smarter control of motors and grids, and more efficient use of a radio spectrum that every new device wants a share of.

The core idea

Electricity and magnetism are one force. A current makes a magnetic field; a magnetic field pushes on a current; a changing magnetic field makes a current; and changing fields together travel as electromagnetic waves, of which light is one kind.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1820 Ørsted, 1831 Faraday, 1865 Maxwell, 1887–88 Hertz.
  • Electric motors use roughly 40–45 percent of the world's electricity, by common estimates.
  • Electric repulsion between two protons is about 10³⁶ times their gravitational attraction.
The common mistake

Thinking a steady magnet makes electricity. Only a changing magnetic field induces a current, which is why generators spin and why transformers need alternating current.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a motor and a generator are really the same machine, and how an electric car uses that fact when it brakes? If you can, you have understood the core of the subject.

The words, in plain words
Electromagnetism
The single force behind both electric and magnetic effects.
Field
A description of the force a charge or magnet would feel at every point in space.
Electromagnet
A coil of wire, usually around iron, that becomes a magnet when current flows.
Relay
A switch worked by an electromagnet, so a small current can control a large one.
Electromagnetic induction
The creation of a voltage by a changing magnetic field.
Motor
A machine that turns electrical energy into motion using the push between current and field.
Generator
A machine that turns motion into electrical energy by induction.
Transformer
Two coils on one core that change the voltage of alternating current.
Maxwell's equations
Four equations that describe all of classical electricity, magnetism and light.
Electromagnetic wave
Linked electric and magnetic fields travelling through space at the speed of light.
Electromagnetic spectrum
The full range of these waves, from radio to gamma rays.
Non-ionising radiation
Radiation, such as radio and visible light, too weak per photon to break chemical bonds.
Quantum electrodynamics
The quantum version of electromagnetism, in which light comes as photons.
