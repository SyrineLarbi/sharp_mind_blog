Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 18 of 20

Dark matter. Five times more of it than of everything we can see, and nobody knows what it is.

Weigh a galaxy by the light of its stars, then weigh it again by how its stars move, and the two numbers do not agree. The second is far larger. The same gap appears in clusters of galaxies, in the bending of light, and in the afterglow of the Big Bang. Most astronomers conclude that the universe contains a large amount of matter that does not shine, absorb or reflect light, and that feels only gravity. It has been searched for underground, in space and in colliders for decades. This file explains why the evidence is so strong, why the searches have so far come up empty, and why a minority of physicists think the answer lies in gravity instead.

Question 1 · What is it?

Mass that we detect only by its pull

"Dark matter" is the name for whatever supplies the extra gravity that visible matter cannot. The name describes what it does, not what it is.

Chapter 01

A cluster that weighed too much

In 1933 the Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky, working at Caltech, studied the Coma Cluster, a swarm of more than a thousand galaxies held together by gravity. He measured how fast the galaxies were moving. They were moving so fast that, given the mass of the visible stars, the cluster should have flown apart long ago. To hold it together there had to be much more mass than could be seen. He called it dunkle Materie, German for "dark matter".

Zwicky's estimate of the gap was too large, because the measurements of his day were rough. And astronomers had other explanations available, such as dim stars or gas they had not yet detected. The idea stayed on the fringe for about forty years. It took a second, independent line of evidence, from a completely different kind of measurement, to make it mainstream.

Chapter 02

Combining all the evidence, astronomers can describe dark matter's behaviour quite precisely, even without knowing what it is made of.

Searches in the 1990s also tested whether the missing mass could be dark compact objects, such as dead stars or stray planets, in our galaxy's outskirts. Such objects would occasionally pass in front of background stars and briefly brighten them by bending their light. Large surveys found far too few of these events. The leading view is that dark matter is a new kind of particle, one that is not in the Standard Model.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four independent ways to weigh the invisible

The case for dark matter does not rest on one observation. It rests on several, made with different methods, at different scales, that all point to roughly the same amount.

Chapter 03

Rotation curves: galaxies that spin too fast at the edge

In our Solar System, distant planets orbit more slowly than close ones. Neptune crawls; Mercury races. That is because almost all the mass is in the Sun at the centre. You would expect the same in a galaxy: most of the stars are near the middle, so stars far out should orbit more slowly.

In the 1970s the American astronomer Vera Rubin, working with the instrument-maker Kent Ford, measured how fast stars and gas orbit at different distances from the centres of spiral galaxies, starting with our neighbour Andromeda. Radio astronomers measuring the hydrogen gas far beyond the visible disc found the same thing. The orbital speeds did not fall off. They stayed roughly flat, far beyond where the stars thin out.

A flat curve means that the mass inside each orbit keeps growing with distance, even where there is almost no light. Each galaxy appears to sit inside a much larger, roughly spherical cloud of unseen mass, called a dark matter halo. Rubin's measurements across many galaxies in the 1970s and 1980s convinced most astronomers that the problem was real and universal.

Chapter 04

Gravitational lensing and the Bullet Cluster

Einstein's relativity predicts that mass bends the path of light. A massive galaxy cluster acts like a crude lens, distorting and magnifying the galaxies behind it into arcs. By measuring the distortion, astronomers can map where the mass is, whether or not it shines. This gravitational lensing consistently finds far more mass in clusters than the stars and hot gas account for.

The most famous example is the Bullet Cluster, two galaxy clusters that collided and passed through each other. Most of the ordinary matter in a cluster is hot gas, which glows in X-rays. In the collision, the two gas clouds slammed into each other and slowed down, staying near the middle. A 2006 study combined X-ray maps of that gas with lensing maps of the mass. Most of the mass was not where the gas was. It had sailed on ahead with the galaxies, just as particles that barely interact would. Many astronomers see it as the clearest single picture of dark matter, because it is hard to explain by changing the law of gravity alone.

Chapter 05

The fingerprint in the oldest light

The strongest evidence of all comes from the cosmic microwave background, the leftover glow from about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. It is almost perfectly even, but contains tiny ripples of temperature, about one part in 100,000. Those ripples record sound waves that were travelling through the early universe's hot gas.

The pattern of those waves depends on how much ordinary matter and how much dark matter there was. Ordinary matter was pushed around by light; dark matter was not, and only pulled. Satellites, most precisely the European Planck mission, measured the pattern in detail. The answer is that ordinary matter makes up about 5% of the universe's contents and dark matter about 27%, with the remaining roughly 68% being dark energy. A completely separate measurement, of the light elements made in the first few minutes after the Big Bang, gives the same small share for ordinary matter.

Chapter 06

The scaffolding of the universe

Dark matter also explains why there are galaxies at all. The early universe was extremely smooth. Ordinary matter, still interacting with light, could not start clumping until about 380,000 years in. Starting from such tiny ripples, it would not have had time to form the galaxies we see.

Dark matter could start clumping earlier, because light did not push it apart. It formed a web of dense knots and filaments, and ordinary matter later fell into those wells and formed stars. Computer simulations that include cold dark matter reproduce the large-scale "cosmic web" of galaxies seen in surveys, with its filaments and empty voids. Simulations without it do not. The details of how galaxies grew are in Cosmic evolution.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The hunt for most of the matter in the universe

If dark matter is a particle, finding it would be the biggest discovery in physics for decades. If it is not, then our theory of gravity is wrong somewhere. Either way, the stakes are high.

Chapter 07

Searching underground

Our galaxy's halo should be full of dark matter streaming through the Solar System, and through you, all the time. Near the Sun its density is estimated at very roughly the mass of one proton per few cubic centimetres. Very occasionally, a dark matter particle might bump into an atomic nucleus and make it recoil. Direct detection experiments try to catch that tiny kick.

The leading experiments use tonnes of ultra-pure liquid xenon, a heavy element, watched by light sensors that would see the faint flash of a recoil. To shield them from cosmic rays, they sit deep underground: LUX-ZEPLIN in a former gold mine in South Dakota, XENONnT under a mountain at Gran Sasso in Italy, and PandaX in China. They have become thousands of times more sensitive over two decades. As of 2025, none has found a convincing signal.

A claim that did not hold up

For over twenty years the DAMA experiment in Italy has reported a yearly rise and fall in its signal, which it attributes to Earth moving through the dark matter halo. Other experiments using the same detector material have not confirmed it, and most physicists now think it has another cause.

These detectors are now approaching a natural floor sometimes called the neutrino fog. At that sensitivity, neutrinos from the Sun and the atmosphere start to produce identical recoils, which makes any dark matter signal harder to pick out.

Chapter 08

The main candidates

WIMPs

Weakly interacting massive particles, perhaps tens to thousands of times heavier than a proton. For decades the favourite, because a particle with roughly the strength of the weak force would naturally leave about the right amount behind after the Big Bang. The underground searches and the LHC have ruled out much of the simplest range.

Axions

Extremely light hypothetical particles, first proposed in the 1970s to solve an unrelated puzzle about the strong force. Experiments such as ADMX in the United States use strong magnets to try to turn them into faint radio signals. Interest has grown as WIMPs have failed to appear.

Primordial black holes

Black holes formed in the first instant after the Big Bang. Many mass ranges have been ruled out by observations, but not all, so they remain a possibility for part of the dark matter.

Something else

"Sterile" neutrinos, whole hidden sectors of particles with their own forces, and many other ideas. The honest summary is that the possible masses span an enormous range, and the searches have covered only parts of it.

Chapter 09

Searching in the sky and in colliders

Two other strategies run alongside the underground ones. The first is indirect detection: if dark matter particles occasionally destroy each other, they might produce gamma rays or other particles where dark matter is dense, such as the centre of our galaxy. The Fermi gamma-ray telescope found an unexplained excess of gamma rays from the galactic centre. Some read it as a dark matter signal; many others think it comes from a population of fast-spinning pulsars. It is still contested.

The second is to make dark matter in a collider. If LHC collisions produced dark matter particles, they would escape the detectors unseen, showing up only as missing energy. No such excess has been found. Together, the three strategies have narrowed the possibilities a great deal, which is real progress, even if it does not feel like it.

Chapter 10

The rival idea: change gravity instead

In 1983 the Israeli physicist Mordehai Milgrom proposed a different explanation. Perhaps there is no missing matter. Perhaps Newton's law of gravity changes at the very low accelerations found at the edges of galaxies. His idea, called MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics), predicts galaxy rotation curves surprisingly well from the visible matter alone, often better than dark matter models do for individual galaxies. It also explains a tight relationship between a galaxy's visible mass and its rotation speed.

But MOND struggles on larger scales. It does not fully explain galaxy clusters, it has great difficulty with the Bullet Cluster, and versions compatible with relativity have trouble matching the cosmic microwave background pattern. Most cosmologists therefore favour dark matter, while a vocal minority argue that the success of MOND in galaxies is a clue that dark matter models have not properly explained. The debate is scientific and ongoing, and both sides agree that the galaxies are behaving strangely.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A hypothesis that explains a great deal and has never been seen

For a scientific idea, the trade-off is explanatory power against direct proof.

It helps to remember a historical parallel. In the 1840s, irregularities in the orbit of Uranus led astronomers to predict an unseen planet, and Neptune was found. In the nineteenth century, a similar problem with Mercury's orbit led to the prediction of another unseen planet, "Vulcan", which never appeared; the real answer was Einstein's new theory of gravity. Dark matter is today's version of that choice: new matter, or new gravity. So far, the evidence favours new matter.

Chapter 13

What if it is never found?

It is possible that dark matter interacts with ordinary matter only through gravity. In that case, no underground detector or collider would ever see it, and its nature could stay unknown even as maps of it became extremely precise. Some physicists consider this a real possibility; others think it unlikely, given how many candidate theories predict some other interaction.

Even then, the subject would not be a dead end. Astronomy could still learn a lot from how dark matter behaves: how it clumps in the smallest galaxies, whether it interacts with itself at all, and how it shapes the cosmic web. Studying the shadow can tell you a great deal about the object casting it. And every negative result removes possibilities, which is how science narrows toward an answer.

The core idea

Galaxies and clusters contain far more mass than we can see. The extra mass shows up in rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the Bullet Cluster and the cosmic microwave background. Most scientists think it is a new, invisible kind of particle, but it has never been detected directly.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Ordinary matter about 5%, dark matter about 27%, dark energy about 68%.
  • Dark matter outweighs ordinary matter about five to one.
  • Zwicky named it in 1933; Rubin's rotation curves came in the 1970s.
  • The Bullet Cluster study that separated mass from gas was published in 2006.
The common mistake

Confusing dark matter with dark energy. Dark matter pulls things together; dark energy drives the expansion apart. They are two separate puzzles with similar names.

Can you explain why the Bullet Cluster is hard to explain with modified gravity alone? If you can, you understand why most astronomers think dark matter is a real substance.

The words, in plain words
Dark matter
Matter that gives off and absorbs no light, detected only by its gravity.
Ordinary matter
Matter made of atoms: stars, gas, planets and people. Also called baryonic matter.
Rotation curve
A graph of how fast stars orbit at different distances from a galaxy's centre.
Dark matter halo
The large, roughly spherical cloud of dark matter around a galaxy.
Galaxy cluster
Hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound together by gravity.
Gravitational lensing
The bending of light by mass, used to map mass whether it shines or not.
Cosmic microwave background
The faint glow left over from the hot early universe.
Cold dark matter
Dark matter made of slow-moving particles, which can clump early.
WIMP
A hypothetical heavy particle that interacts only weakly with ordinary matter.
Axion
A hypothetical very light particle, another dark matter candidate.
Direct detection
Trying to catch a dark matter particle hitting an atom in a detector.
Neutrino fog
The background of neutrino signals that limits how sensitive detectors can usefully become.
MOND
Modified Newtonian Dynamics, a proposal to change gravity instead of adding dark matter.
