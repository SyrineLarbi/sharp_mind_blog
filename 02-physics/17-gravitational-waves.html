Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 17 of 20

Gravitational waves. Space itself stretching and squeezing, felt by the most sensitive instruments ever built.

For all of history, astronomy meant collecting light: visible light, then radio waves, X-rays and the rest. In 2015 humanity gained a completely different way to study the universe. A pair of instruments in the United States felt a tiny ripple in the fabric of space, sent out by two black holes colliding more than a billion years ago. Gravitational waves let us observe things that give off no light at all, test Einstein's theory where gravity is most extreme, and even trace where the gold in a wedding ring came from.

Question 1 · What is it?

Ripples in the shape of space

A gravitational wave is a disturbance in spacetime itself, travelling outward at the speed of light. As it passes, it stretches distances in one direction and squeezes them in the other.

Chapter 01

If space can bend, it can ripple

Einstein's general relativity says that mass curves space and time, and that this curvature is what we feel as gravity. A common picture is a heavy ball on a stretched rubber sheet. The picture has limits, but it helps with one point. If you move the ball suddenly, the dent in the sheet cannot update everywhere at once. The change spreads outward as a wave.

That is a gravitational wave. Any mass that accelerates makes them, in principle, even you waving your arm. But gravity is by far the weakest of the forces, and the waves are unimaginably faint unless the masses are enormous and moving very fast. Only cosmic catastrophes, such as two black holes merging, make waves strong enough to detect.

What a wave does as it passes is unusual. It does not push you; it changes the distances between things. A ring of floating particles would be stretched into an oval one way, then the other way, back and forth, as the wave went by. Your own body would stretch and squeeze too, by an amount far too small to notice.

Chapter 02

A hundred-year wait

Einstein predicted gravitational waves in 1916. He also calculated how weak they would be and doubted anyone would ever measure them. In the 1930s he even briefly argued that they might not exist at all, before the error in that argument was found. For decades physicists disagreed about whether the waves carried real energy or were just a quirk of the mathematics.

In the 1960s the American physicist Joseph Weber built large aluminium cylinders designed to "ring" when a wave passed, and claimed detections. Other groups built similar instruments and found nothing, and his claims are not accepted today. The first convincing evidence came indirectly. In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor discovered a pair of dense dead stars, one of them a pulsar, a neutron star that sends out regular radio pulses like a lighthouse. Over the following years, the two stars' orbit shrank at exactly the rate expected if they were losing energy as gravitational waves. It was proof the waves were real, without detecting a single one. Hulse and Taylor received the 1993 Nobel Prize.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Measuring a change a thousand times smaller than a proton

The detectors are giant rulers made of laser light. The engineering challenge is not sensing the wave; it is ignoring everything else.

Chapter 03

Why the waves are so faint

Physicists describe the size of a gravitational wave by its strain: how much a length changes, divided by the length. The strongest waves reaching Earth have a strain of around one part in 1021, a thousandth of a millionth of a millionth of a millionth. Over the distance from Earth to the nearest star, about four light-years, that would be a change roughly the width of a human hair.

The sources are far away, and the waves weaken as they spread. The source must also be lopsided and changing. A perfectly round, steadily spinning star makes no waves at all. Two heavy objects whirling around each other are ideal. As they lose energy, they spiral closer, orbit faster, and the waves rise in both strength and pitch until the objects merge. Physicists call that rising signal a chirp, after the sound it makes when converted into audio.

Chapter 04

The instrument: two arms and a laser

The detectors that succeeded are laser interferometers. The idea dates back to the nineteenth century, and the version used for gravitational waves was developed from the 1970s, notably by Rainer Weiss at MIT and by Kip Thorne and Ronald Drever at Caltech.

A laser beam is split in two and sent down two long tubes at right angles. Mirrors at the ends reflect the beams back, and they are recombined. The setup is tuned so that the returning light waves exactly cancel. When a gravitational wave passes, one arm gets very slightly longer while the other gets shorter. The light no longer cancels perfectly, and a little reaches the sensor.

The American observatory, LIGO (the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory), has two such detectors, one in Hanford, Washington, and one in Livingston, Louisiana, about 3,000 kilometres apart. Each arm is 4 kilometres long, and the light bounces back and forth many times to make the effective path much longer. The arms change length by about a thousandth of the width of a proton.

Chapter 05

Silencing the world

At that sensitivity, everything is noise: earthquakes on the other side of the planet, ocean waves hitting the coast, passing lorries, falling trees, even the random jiggling of atoms in the mirrors themselves. The engineering answers are extreme.

Isolation

The 40-kilogram mirrors hang from layered pendulums and sit on platforms that actively cancel ground shaking, like noise-cancelling headphones for vibration.

Vacuum

The beam tubes hold one of the largest high vacuums on Earth, so that stray air molecules do not disturb the light.

Two sites

A real wave hits both detectors within about a hundredth of a second. Local noise appears at only one. Requiring a matching signal at both removes most false alarms.

Europe's Virgo detector near Pisa in Italy, with 3-kilometre arms, and Japan's underground KAGRA detector now work with LIGO as one network. With three or more detectors, the difference in arrival times lets astronomers estimate where in the sky the wave came from, much as your two ears tell you the direction of a sound.

Chapter 06

Reading the chirp

The shape of a signal holds a lot of information. Relativity predicts exactly how the chirp should rise and end for any pair of masses and spins. Physicists compare each signal with large banks of computer-simulated waveforms, called templates, to find the best match.

From that match they learn the masses of the two objects, how fast they were spinning, the mass of the final black hole, and how much energy was radiated. Because the true strength of the wave is fixed by the masses, comparing it with the strength measured on Earth gives the distance. A gravitational-wave source tells you how far away it is, which most light sources do not. Astronomers call such sources "standard sirens".

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A new sense for astronomy

Gravitational waves reveal events that are dark to every telescope, and confirm physics that could not be tested any other way.

Chapter 07

14 September 2015

Just days after an upgraded LIGO began operating, both detectors recorded the same chirp, the Louisiana one about 7 thousandths of a second before the Washington one. It lasted around a fifth of a second. The team spent months checking that it was not a test signal or a glitch before announcing it in February 2016.

The signal matched two black holes of roughly 36 and 29 times the Sun's mass, about 1.3 billion light-years away, spiralling together and merging into one of about 62 solar masses. The missing three Suns' worth of mass had been converted into gravitational-wave energy in a fraction of a second. For that brief moment, the collision released more power than all the stars in the observable universe shining together, yet none of it was light.

It was also the first direct evidence that pairs of black holes exist and merge. Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for the work, which involved over a thousand scientists.

Chapter 08

17 August 2017: the origin of gold

Two years later the network caught a longer, quieter chirp lasting about a minute and a half: two neutron stars merging about 130 million light-years away. About 1.7 seconds after the merger, a space telescope recorded a short burst of gamma rays from the same region of sky. Within hours, telescopes around the world found a new glowing point in a galaxy called NGC 4993, and around seventy observatories studied it across the spectrum.

The glow, called a kilonova, showed the signature of heavy elements being made in the debris. Physicists had long suspected such mergers could forge much of the universe's gold, platinum and other heavy elements; this was the first direct confirmation that they do. The near simultaneous arrival of gravitational waves and gamma rays also showed that gravity travels at the speed of light, to about one part in a thousand million million, ruling out several alternative theories of gravity at a stroke.

Multi-messenger astronomy

This event began what astronomers call multi-messenger astronomy: studying the same event through different kinds of signal, here gravitational waves and light. Each messenger shows something the other cannot.

Chapter 09

What the growing catalogue teaches

By 2025 the LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA network had reported hundreds of candidate events. Most are merging black hole pairs, with a few involving neutron stars. That census is starting to answer old questions: how heavy black holes can be, how fast they spin, and whether pairs formed together from two stars or met later in crowded star clusters.

The signals also test relativity in its most violent regime. In 2025 the collaboration reported its clearest signal yet, loud enough to check a prediction Stephen Hawking made in 1971: that the total horizon area of black holes should never shrink when they merge. It did not. And standard sirens give an independent way to measure how fast the universe is expanding, which matters because other methods currently disagree, as the Dark energy file explains.

A different technique listens at much lower frequencies. Pulsar timing arrays watch dozens of very regular pulsars across the galaxy for tiny shared shifts in their timing. In June 2023 several teams, including the North American NANOGrav collaboration, reported evidence of a background hum of very long gravitational waves, most likely from pairs of supermassive black holes across the universe. The evidence is strong but not yet at the level of a definitive detection.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful new window with a narrow view

Gravitational waves see what light cannot. They also see only a few kinds of event, and only very roughly where they happen.

Chapter 11

A long bet that paid off

LIGO was a risk. Funded by the US National Science Foundation, it took decades and over a billion dollars in total. Its first version, operating in the 2000s, detected nothing, as its designers had warned might happen. Only the upgraded "Advanced LIGO" was sensitive enough. Critics in the early years questioned spending so much on an instrument that might never see anything.

The lesson is mixed and worth stating honestly. The payoff came because funders accepted a long, uncertain timeline and a staged design. The same patience applied elsewhere does not always pay. The BICEP2 telescope announced in 2014 that it had found traces of primordial gravitational waves from the birth of the universe; within a year, joint analysis with the Planck satellite showed the signal could be explained by dust in our own galaxy. Extraordinary claims need cross-checks.

Chapter 13

Hearing the whole sky

Astronomy has repeatedly been transformed by new windows. Radio astronomy found quasars and pulsars, which nobody had predicted. X-ray astronomy found black holes in binary systems. Each time, the biggest discoveries were the ones nobody expected. Gravitational-wave astronomy is barely ten years old as of 2025.

Its long-term promise is a full spectrum of gravitational waves, from the fast chirps heard on the ground, through the slower tones LISA will hear, to the deep hum of pulsar timing. Together they would cover black holes from a few Suns to billions of Suns, and perhaps test what happens near the horizon itself. The most interesting signal may be one nobody has yet imagined.

The core idea

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime made by accelerating masses and travelling at the speed of light. Only extreme events, like merging black holes and neutron stars, make detectable waves. Laser interferometers detect them by measuring tiny changes in the length of their arms.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Predicted by Einstein in 1916; first detected 14 September 2015.
  • LIGO's arms are 4 km long and change by about a thousandth of a proton's width.
  • The 2017 neutron-star merger showed gravity travels at the speed of light.
  • By 2025, hundreds of candidate events had been reported.
The common mistake

Thinking of gravitational waves as something like sound or pressure that pushes. They change distances themselves; everything in their path, including the detector, stretches and squeezes.

Can you explain why LIGO needs two detectors thousands of kilometres apart? If you can, you have understood both how small the signal is and how the noise is beaten.
