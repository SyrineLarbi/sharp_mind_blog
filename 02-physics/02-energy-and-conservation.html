Energy and conservation. The one account in nature that always balances.

You cannot see energy, hold it or point at it. Yet it is the most useful idea in all of science, because of one strict rule: the total amount never changes. It moves from place to place and from form to form, from sunlight to sugar to muscle to heat, but the account always balances. That rule lets an engineer check a design without building it, lets a doctor estimate what you eat, and lets anyone spot a fraudulent "free energy" machine at a glance. It also hides a trap. If energy is never lost, why do we talk about running out of it? This file answers both questions.

Question 1 · What is it?

A number that stays the same while everything else changes

Energy is not a substance. It is a quantity you can calculate for any situation, and its total stays fixed no matter what happens.

Chapter 01

The blocks that never go missing

The physicist Richard Feynman explained energy with a story. A child has 28 toy blocks that cannot be broken or joined. Every evening the mother counts them and finds 28. One day she finds only 27, and discovers the last one under the rug. Another day she finds 30, because a friend visited and left some. Some days blocks are hidden in a box, and she works out how many by weighing it. Each time, with enough cleverness, the count comes back to the same number.

Energy is like that, with one difference: there are no blocks. There is only the number. You calculate a bit for motion, a bit for height, a bit for heat, a bit stored in chemical bonds, and the total stays constant. Feynman put it bluntly in his lectures:

"It is important to realize that in physics today, we have no knowledge of what energy is."

Richard Feynman · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I (1963)

That sounds alarming, but it is honest. Energy is defined by the rule it obeys, not by what it is made of. The common school definition, "the capacity to do work", is a useful start. Here work has a precise meaning: a force pushing something through a distance. Lifting a suitcase onto a shelf is work. Holding it still, however tiring, is not work in the physics sense, because nothing moves.

Chapter 03

Joules, calories and kilowatt-hours

The scientific unit of energy is the joule, named after James Prescott Joule. One joule is small: roughly the energy needed to lift a small apple one metre. Because it is so small, everyday life uses bigger units, which confuses people. They are all the same quantity.

  • A food Calorie (a kilocalorie) is about 4,200 joules. A typical adult diet of around 2,000 kcal a day is about 8.4 million joules.
  • A kilowatt-hour (kWh), the unit on electricity bills, is 3.6 million joules. That means a day's food holds roughly 2.3 kWh, less than a single long hot shower uses.

Energy is often confused with power. Power is the rate at which energy is used or delivered, measured in watts: one watt is one joule per second. A 2,000-watt kettle uses energy ten times faster than a 200-watt fridge, but the fridge runs all day. Energy is the amount; power is the speed. A human body, averaged over a day, runs at about 100 watts, roughly the same as an old incandescent light bulb.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Energy changes form, and the books always balance

The law of conservation took decades to establish, because the energy that seems to disappear almost always turns into heat, which is easy to miss.

Chapter 04

Kinetic energy, and the tyranny of the square

The energy of a moving object is half its mass times its speed squared, written KE = ½mv². The mass part is intuitive: a lorry carries more energy than a bicycle at the same speed. The squared speed is not intuitive, and it matters more than anything else in the formula. Double the speed and the energy goes up four times. Triple it and the energy goes up nine times.

That square explains a surprising amount. A car at 60 km/h carries about 44 percent more energy than one at 50 km/h, not 20 percent more. In a crash, that extra energy has to go somewhere, mostly into bending metal and bodies. A meteorite the size of a car can release the energy of a large bomb because it arrives at tens of kilometres per second. And it is why wind turbines are so sensitive to wind speed: the energy carried by moving air grows even faster than the square, because faster wind also brings more air through the blades each second.

Chapter 05

Potential energy: stored for later

Lift something and you do work against gravity. That work is not lost. It is stored as gravitational potential energy, equal to mass times gravity's strength times height. Let the object fall and the stored energy turns back into motion.

A roller coaster shows the whole exchange. A motor hauls the train to the top of the first hill, giving it potential energy. On the way down, potential energy becomes kinetic energy and the train speeds up. On the next hill, kinetic becomes potential again and it slows. Every later hill must be lower than the first, because some energy turns into heat and sound at every metre, through friction and air resistance. A coaster designer is, at heart, an energy accountant.

The same idea stores electricity for whole countries. In pumped-storage hydroelectricity, spare power at night pumps water uphill into a reservoir. When demand peaks, the water runs back down through turbines. About three-quarters or more of the energy comes back. As of the mid-2020s this is still by far the largest form of grid energy storage in the world, although batteries are growing quickly.

Chapter 06

How heat was caught in the accounts

For a long time, heat was thought to be a fluid called "caloric" that flowed from hot things to cold. In the 1840s several people, working separately, showed instead that heat is a form of energy and that it can be exchanged for motion at a fixed rate. The German physician Julius Robert Mayer argued for it in 1842. The English brewer and physicist James Joule measured it. The German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz stated the general principle in 1847.

Joule's most famous experiment used a falling weight to turn a paddle wheel in a container of water. The paddles stirred the water, and the water got very slightly warmer. By measuring how far the weight fell and how much the temperature rose, he showed that a given amount of mechanical work always produces the same amount of heat. The temperature changes were tiny, fractions of a degree, and his care in measuring them convinced the sceptics.

The result is the law of conservation of energy: in a closed system, energy is neither created nor destroyed, only converted from one form to another. In the study of heat it is called the first law of thermodynamics, covered further in Thermodynamics.

Chapter 07

Where the "lost" energy goes: efficiency

No machine turns all of its input into the output you want. The rest is not destroyed. It leaves as heat, sound or vibration. Efficiency is the useful output divided by the total input.

Where 100 units of energy go, roughly

 LED bulb               [#####...................]  several times more light per watt

These figures are typical ranges, not exact values, and real machines vary. But the pattern is clear and important. Burning fuel to make motion wastes most of the energy as heat, while electric motors waste very little. That is a major reason electric cars use far less energy per kilometre than petrol cars, even when the electricity comes partly from burning fuel in a power station.

There is a deeper limit behind the waste from engines. Any engine that turns heat into work must dump some heat to a colder place. This is not a matter of better engineering. It is a law of nature, the second law of thermodynamics, explained in Entropy.

Chapter 08

Why energy is conserved at all

Most laws in physics are simply found to be true. Conservation of energy has a deeper reason, discovered by the German mathematician Emmy Noether in a theorem published in 1918. She proved that whenever the laws of physics have a symmetry, something is conserved.

For energy, the symmetry is time. The laws of physics are the same today as yesterday and tomorrow. An experiment done on Monday gives the same result if done on Friday. Noether showed that this sameness through time mathematically requires that energy be conserved. Similarly, the laws being the same everywhere in space requires that momentum be conserved. Many physicists rank her theorem among the most beautiful results in their subject, because it turns a list of rules into a consequence of the shape of the universe.

Chapter 09

Mass is energy too

In 1905 Albert Einstein added a new entry to the accounts. His equation E = mc² says that mass itself is a form of energy, and that a tiny amount of mass holds an enormous amount of it, because c, the speed of light, is a very big number and it is squared.

In chemical reactions the mass change is far too small to measure. In nuclear reactions it is not. The Sun turns hydrogen into helium in its core, and the helium weighs slightly less than the hydrogen it came from. The missing mass leaves as energy. The Sun converts about four million tonnes of mass into energy every second, and it has done so for about 4.6 billion years. That is the ultimate source of almost all the energy in food and fossil fuels on Earth. Nuclear power stations use the same principle with uranium, as described in Volume 11, Nuclear power.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A tool for checking claims, and the basis of every economy

Conservation of energy is the quickest truth test in science. It also explains why energy shapes wealth, climate and daily life.

Chapter 10

The free-energy machine that never works

For centuries, inventors have proposed perpetual motion machines: wheels with shifting weights, magnets arranged to push forever, water that lifts itself. Each one, if it worked, would produce more energy than it takes in. Each one has failed, because each one breaks the law of conservation. Scientific academies stopped examining such claims long ago; the French Academy of Sciences resolved in 1775 not to consider them any more.

Modern versions arrive as devices that "run on water", gadgets that cut your electricity bill by 70 percent by plugging into a socket, or cars that make their own fuel. The quick test is always the same: where does the energy come from? If the answer is "nowhere" or "the device itself", the claim is false. You do not need to find the hidden flaw. The accounts tell you there must be one.

Chapter 11

Energy as the master resource

Almost everything a modern economy does is a way of converting energy. Farming turns sunlight into food, and fertiliser production uses natural gas (Volume 12, Fertiliser). Steel and cement need intense heat. Transport turns fuel into motion. Computing turns electricity into calculation, and then into heat that data centres must remove.

The scale is hard to picture. As of the early 2020s, humanity used roughly 600 exajoules of primary energy a year, about 19 terawatts on average, or around 2,400 watts for every person on Earth. That is more than twenty times what a human body runs on. Roughly four-fifths of it still came from coal, oil and gas. When historians explain why some countries grew rich first, access to cheap, concentrated energy is always part of the story.

Chapter 12

Thinking in energy in daily life

The habit of asking "where does the energy go?" pays off at home.

Heat is where the energy is

In most homes in cold climates, space heating and hot water use more energy than everything else combined. Lights and phone chargers are tiny by comparison. Insulation and heat pumps (Volume 11) beat switching off chargers by a wide margin.

Exercise and food

The body is roughly a quarter efficient at turning food energy into mechanical work, and the rest becomes heat, which is why you get hot running. An hour's brisk walk uses a few hundred kilocalories, which a single pastry can replace. That imbalance is pure energy accounting.

The same reasoning explains why braking wastes fuel. Every time a car brakes, its kinetic energy, paid for with fuel, turns into heat in the brakes. Electric and hybrid cars use regenerative braking: the motor runs as a generator and puts some of that energy back into the battery.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A perfect law with an imperfect everyday meaning

The law is never broken. But "energy" in physics and "energy" in politics mean different things, and confusing them causes real mistakes.

Chapter 14

If energy is never lost, why can we run out?

This is the most important puzzle in the topic. The answer is that what we run out of is not energy but concentrated, usable energy. A litre of petrol holds its energy packed into chemical bonds. After it burns in an engine, every joule still exists, but it is spread out as slightly warm air and road, impossible to collect again.

Physicists have a word for the usable part, exergy, and the law of entropy says it always decreases in any real process. So the energy crisis is really an exergy crisis. That is also why "saving energy" makes sense even though energy cannot be destroyed: you are saving the concentrated sources and leaving them for later. Every power system is a search for high-quality energy (sunlight, wind, falling water, fuel, nuclear binding) and a way to degrade it as usefully as possible on its way to becoming waste heat.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Electrify, store, and maybe fuse

The law will not change. How humanity sources and converts energy is changing fast. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

One more trend deserves mention. Demand for electricity from data centres, driven partly by AI, has become a significant planning question for grids in several countries since about 2023 (Volume 14, Data centres). Every joule a computer uses ends as heat. Some projects already pipe that heat into district heating networks. That is energy accounting turned into policy: if the heat must appear somewhere, put it where it is useful.

The core idea

Energy is never created or destroyed, only converted. It comes in many forms, mostly reducible to motion and position. In every real process some of it turns into spread-out heat, which still counts in the total but can no longer do much useful work.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed: twice as fast, four times the energy.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 million joules; a day's food is roughly 2–3 kWh.
  • A petrol engine turns roughly a quarter to a third of its fuel into motion; an electric motor about 90 percent.
The common mistake

Confusing energy with power, or with usefulness. Energy is the amount, power is the rate, and running out means running out of concentrated energy, not energy itself.

Test yourself

Can you explain where the energy of a moving car goes when it brakes to a stop, and why an electric car can recover some of it? If you can, you have understood conversion, conservation and efficiency together.

The words, in plain words
Energy
A quantity that can change form but whose total never changes; roughly, the capacity to do work.
Work
A force moving something through a distance.
Kinetic energy
The energy of motion, equal to half the mass times the speed squared.
Potential energy
Energy stored by position or arrangement, such as height or a stretched spring.
Joule
The scientific unit of energy; about the energy to lift a small apple one metre.
Kilowatt-hour
The energy used by a 1,000-watt device for one hour; 3.6 million joules.
Power
The rate of using or delivering energy, measured in watts (joules per second).
Efficiency
The share of input energy that ends up as the output you want.
First law of thermodynamics
The law of conservation of energy, applied to heat and work.
Exergy
The part of energy that can still do useful work; it is used up even though energy is not.
Noether's theorem
The proof that every symmetry in the laws of physics comes with a conserved quantity.
Perpetual motion machine
An imagined device that produces energy from nothing. None can exist.
Regenerative braking
Braking that turns motion back into stored electricity instead of heat.
