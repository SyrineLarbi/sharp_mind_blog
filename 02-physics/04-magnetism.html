Magnetism. An invisible force that turns out to be electricity in motion.

A fridge magnet holds up a postcard against the pull of the entire Earth. A compass needle swings to point north without touching anything. A hospital scanner sees inside your body using a magnet tens of thousands of times stronger than the Earth's. For most of history magnetism looked like a separate, almost magical force. The modern answer is simpler and stranger. Every magnet, from a lodestone to the Earth itself, is made of moving or spinning electric charge. This file explains what magnetism is, where it comes from, why the Earth has it, and why so much of modern technology depends on it.

Question 1 · What is it?

A force between moving charges, felt as push and pull between poles

Magnetism is a force that acts at a distance, like gravity, but it can push as well as pull, and it comes in pairs of poles that can never be separated.

Chapter 01

Lodestones and the first compasses

Some rocks are naturally magnetic. The most common is a form of iron oxide called magnetite, and pieces of it strong enough to pick up iron were known as lodestones, from an old English word meaning "leading stone". Ancient Greek and Chinese writers described them. The word "magnet" probably comes from Magnesia, a region of Greece where such stones were found.

Chinese scholars were the first to turn this into an instrument. They noticed that a freely hanging lodestone or a magnetised needle settles in a north-south line. The scholar Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle compass in a book completed around 1088, and it was being used to navigate at sea within a few decades. European writers describe the compass from the late 1100s. A small piece of magnetised iron became one of the tools that made long ocean voyages possible, as Volume 19 describes.

Chapter 02

Poles, fields and the magnet you cannot split

Every magnet has two ends, called poles. The end that turns towards the Earth's north is called the north pole of the magnet; the other is the south pole. The rule echoes the one for electric charge: like poles repel, unlike poles attract. Push two north poles together and you feel them shove apart.

There is one big difference from electric charge. You can have a lone positive or negative charge, but nobody has ever found a lone magnetic pole. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not get a separate north and south. You get two smaller magnets, each with its own north and south. Cut again and the same happens, all the way down to single atoms.

The region around a magnet where its force acts is its magnetic field. Sprinkle iron filings on paper over a magnet and they line up along curved paths running from one pole round to the other. These are field lines. Where they crowd together, the field is strong; where they spread out, it is weak. A field is not a substance. It is a way of describing, at every point in space, how strong the magnetic force would be and in which direction it would act.

Chapter 03

The Earth is a magnet

Why does a compass point north at all? In 1600 the English physician William Gilbert published a book called De Magnete ("On the Magnet"). He carved a sphere out of lodestone and showed that a small compass needle moved over its surface behaved just as compasses do at different places on the Earth. His conclusion was that the Earth itself is a giant magnet. It was one of the first books built on careful experiment, and it influenced Galileo.

There is a twist in the names. A compass's north pole is attracted towards the Earth's geographic north. Since opposite poles attract, the Earth's magnetic pole in the Arctic is, in physics terms, a magnetic south pole. It is called the north magnetic pole for geographic reasons. That trips up students every year and changes nothing in practice.

The magnetic pole is also not in the same place as the geographic pole, the point the Earth spins around. So a compass does not point exactly to true north. The difference, called magnetic declination, varies from place to place and changes over the years. Navigators correct for it with charts that are updated regularly.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From spinning electrons to the molten core of the Earth

All magnetism comes from electric charge in motion. The differences between materials come down to how the tiny magnets inside their atoms line up.

Chapter 04

Where magnetism comes from

In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a compass needle moved when he switched on a current in a nearby wire. It was the first proof that electric current creates magnetism. Within months, scientists across Europe were experimenting with it, and the story continues in Electromagnetism.

But a fridge magnet has no current flowing through it. Where does its magnetism come from? The answer lies inside atoms. Each electron behaves like a tiny magnet. Part of this comes from its motion around the nucleus, and most of it, in magnetic materials, comes from a property physicists call spin. Spin is a quantum property with no perfect everyday equivalent, but the useful picture is that each electron is a minute bar magnet.

In most atoms, electrons come in pairs whose tiny magnets point in opposite directions and cancel. In a few elements, such as iron, some electrons are unpaired, so each atom is itself a small magnet. Whether the material as a whole is magnetic depends on whether those atomic magnets line up together.

Chapter 05

Domains: why iron can be magnetised and unmagnetised

In iron, nickel and cobalt, neighbouring atoms push each other's tiny magnets to point the same way. This effect, called ferromagnetism, is quantum mechanical in origin. Large groups of atoms all line up, forming regions called magnetic domains, each a fraction of a millimetre across or smaller.

In an ordinary iron nail the domains point in random directions and cancel out, so the nail is not a magnet. Stroke it with a magnet, or place it near one, and the domains that point the same way as the outside field grow at the expense of the others. The nail becomes a magnet. That is why a magnet can pick up a paperclip: it magnetises the clip first and then attracts it.

Materials differ in how easily their domains move. Soft magnetic materials, like the iron in a transformer, magnetise and demagnetise easily. Hard magnetic materials resist change and so make permanent magnets. Heat ruins the order. Above a certain temperature, called the Curie temperature after Pierre Curie, the jiggling of atoms destroys the alignment. For iron that is about 770 °C. A heated magnet cooled in the Earth's field takes on the direction of that field, which is how rocks record the field's history.

Chapter 06

Everything is a little magnetic, even a frog

Most materials are not ferromagnetic, but all of them respond to a magnetic field a little. Paramagnetic materials, such as aluminium and oxygen, are weakly pulled towards a magnet. Diamagnetic materials, which include water, carbon and most living tissue, are weakly pushed away. The effects are so small that you never notice them in daily life.

With a strong enough field, though, diamagnetism becomes visible. In the late 1990s the physicist Andre Geim and colleagues in the Netherlands floated a live frog in the field of a powerful magnet, around 16 tesla. The water in its body was repelled just enough to balance gravity. The frog was unharmed. The experiment won an Ig Nobel Prize, a prize for research that first makes people laugh, in 2000. Ten years later Geim won a real Nobel Prize in Physics, for work on graphene. He is, as far as is known, the only person to have won both.

Chapter 07

How strong is strong? The tesla

The strength of a magnetic field is measured in tesla (T), named after Nikola Tesla. One tesla is a strong field. Here is a sense of scale.

The modern revolution in everyday magnets came in the 1980s with neodymium-iron-boron magnets, the strongest permanent magnets widely available. They are why earphones, phone speakers and electric car motors can be so small and powerful. They also explain why rare-earth metals became a strategic resource.

Chapter 08

The geodynamo: the Earth's moving magnet

The Earth cannot be a simple permanent magnet. Its interior is far hotter than the Curie temperature of iron. Instead, its field is made by a natural generator called the geodynamo. About 2,900 kilometres below your feet lies the outer core, a layer of liquid iron and nickel. Heat escaping from the inner core keeps this liquid metal churning. The Earth's rotation twists the flow into spirals. Moving metal in a magnetic field generates electric currents, and those currents produce the magnetic field. The field sustains itself, like a generator that powers its own magnet.

Because the flow is turbulent, the field is always changing. The north magnetic pole has drifted from northern Canada towards Siberia. In the early 2000s it moved at about 50 kilometres a year, then slowed. Every few years scientists publish an updated World Magnetic Model, used by phones, aircraft and militaries; the latest edition was released at the end of 2024 for use from 2025.

Over geological time the field does something even more dramatic. It reverses: north and south swap. This has happened hundreds of times. The last full reversal was about 780,000 years ago. Reversals are recorded in volcanic rocks and in the striped sea floor, the evidence that helped confirm plate tectonics (Volume 30). The timing is irregular, and nobody can predict when the next will happen.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A planetary shield, a data store, and the hidden engine of technology

Magnetism protects life on Earth, guides animals across oceans, and sits inside nearly every machine that moves.

Chapter 09

The shield around the planet

The Sun constantly blows out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind. The Earth's magnetic field deflects most of it, creating a protective bubble called the magnetosphere. Some particles are funnelled down near the poles, where they collide with gases in the upper atmosphere and make them glow. That glow is the aurora, the northern and southern lights.

Mars shows what can happen without such protection. It lost its global magnetic field billions of years ago. Many scientists think this let the solar wind strip away much of its atmosphere over time, although how much the missing field mattered is still debated. Either way, a strong field is one of the features that may make a planet habitable.

When the Sun throws out an unusually large burst of particles, the result is a geomagnetic storm. In 1859 the largest one on record, the Carrington Event, made auroras visible in the tropics and gave telegraph operators electric shocks. In March 1989 a storm caused a blackout across Quebec in Canada for about nine hours. In May 2024 a strong storm produced auroras seen across much of Europe and North America. A Carrington-sized storm today could damage satellites and large power transformers, which is why space weather is now monitored and forecast.

Chapter 10

Animals that read the field

Many animals use the Earth's magnetic field to navigate. Experiments with migrating birds, sea turtles and salmon show that they change direction when the field around them is changed artificially. Young sea turtles appear to use the field's strength and angle as a kind of map to find their way around an ocean basin.

How they sense it is one of the open questions of biology. One leading idea is that light triggers a chemical reaction in a protein in birds' eyes whose outcome depends on the magnetic field, so the bird may literally see the field as a pattern. Another is that tiny crystals of magnetite in some animals act like compass needles. Both may be true in different species. The evidence is still being argued over, which makes it a good example of a scientific question that is genuinely unsettled.

Chapter 11

Magnets in technology

Motion

Every electric motor uses magnets, from a phone's vibration buzzer to a high-speed train. An electric car motor may contain one or two kilograms of rare-earth magnets. Maglev trains, such as the Shanghai airport line opened in 2004, float above the track on magnetic forces.

Memory

Hard disk drives store data by magnetising tiny regions of a spinning disk one way or the other, for 1 or 0. Most of the world's stored data in large data centres still sits on magnetic disks and tape, because they are cheap per byte.

Medicine and science

MRI scanners use strong fields to image soft tissue without X-rays (Volume 13). Particle accelerators use magnets to steer beams, and fusion experiments use them to hold plasma hotter than the Sun's core.

Smaller uses are everywhere: loudspeakers and microphones, credit-card stripes, security tags, induction hobs, door catches and the sensor in your phone that tells a map app which way you are facing. Magnetism is the quiet partner in almost every device that moves, stores or senses.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A clean, durable force with a supply problem and a myth problem

Magnets do their work without fuel and without wearing out. The costs sit in their materials, their hazards, and in the claims made about them.

Chapter 13

Magnetic healing: the long-running myth

Because magnetism is invisible and mysterious, it has attracted health claims for centuries. In the late 1700s the physician Franz Anton Mesmer claimed to cure patients with "animal magnetism". A French royal commission in 1784, which included Benjamin Franklin, tested his methods and concluded that the effects came from the patients' imagination.

Today the same idea is sold as magnetic bracelets, insoles and mattress pads. Careful trials have found no reliable benefit beyond the placebo effect. The physics explains why. The fields from such products are weak and fall off within a centimetre or two, and blood is not attracted to magnets in any useful way, because the iron in it is not ferromagnetic. If a magnet could pull your blood around, an MRI scanner, many thousands of times stronger, would do it dramatically. It does not.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. People with pacemakers, implanted defibrillators or other implants should follow their doctor's guidance on magnets and MRI scans.

The most likely change for ordinary people is invisible: more efficient motors, quieter wind turbines and cheaper medical scanners, all shaped by who controls the magnet supply chain. The most dramatic possibilities, like a field reversal or a monopole, are either unpredictable or may not exist at all. That contrast is typical of honest forecasting.

The core idea

All magnetism comes from moving or spinning electric charge. In iron and a few other materials, the tiny magnets of electrons line up in domains, making permanent magnets. The Earth's field is made by churning liquid metal in its core, and it shields the planet from the solar wind.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Earth's surface field: about 25–65 millionths of a tesla; an MRI scanner: 1.5–3 tesla.
  • Iron loses its magnetism above about 770 °C (its Curie temperature).
  • The last reversal of Earth's field was about 780,000 years ago.
The common mistake

Thinking magnetism and electricity are separate forces, or that everyday magnets act on blood and health. They are one force, and the fields of consumer magnets are far too weak to affect the body.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a magnet picks up a paperclip but not a coin made of aluminium, using the idea of domains? If you can, you understand ferromagnetism.

The words, in plain words
Magnetic pole
One of the two ends of a magnet, north or south. They always come in pairs.
Magnetic field
The region around a magnet or current where magnetic force acts, with a strength and direction at every point.
Field lines
Imaginary lines showing the direction of a magnetic field; closer together means stronger.
Tesla
The unit of magnetic field strength.
Spin
A quantum property of electrons that makes each one behave like a tiny magnet.
Ferromagnetism
Strong magnetism in iron, nickel and cobalt, where atomic magnets line up together.
Domain
A small region in which all the atomic magnets point the same way.
Curie temperature
The temperature above which a material loses its permanent magnetism.
Diamagnetism
A weak repulsion from magnetic fields shown by water and most materials.
Magnetic declination
The angle between where a compass points and true north.
Geodynamo
The process in the Earth's liquid outer core that generates its magnetic field.
Magnetosphere
The region around the Earth controlled by its magnetic field, which deflects the solar wind.
Geomagnetic storm
A disturbance of Earth's field caused by a burst of particles from the Sun.
