Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 19 of 20

Dark energy. The universe is not just expanding; it is speeding up, and nobody knows why.

In 1998 two rival teams of astronomers set out to measure how fast the expansion of the universe was slowing down. Both found that it was not slowing at all. It was accelerating. The cause was given a name, dark energy, and it turned out to make up about two-thirds of everything. More than a quarter of a century later, the measurements have been confirmed many times over, but the cause is still a mystery. Dark energy is the largest single ingredient of the universe, and the one physics understands least.

Question 1 · What is it?

A name for an acceleration nobody expected

Dark energy is whatever is making the expansion of space speed up. It seems to fill all of space evenly, and unlike matter, it does not thin out as space grows.

Chapter 01

Einstein's extra term

When Einstein applied general relativity to the whole universe in 1917, he hit a problem. Gravity pulls everything together, so a universe full of matter should not stay still; it should collapse. At the time, everyone assumed the universe was static. To hold it steady, Einstein added an extra term to his equations, a kind of built-in repulsion of empty space. It became known as the cosmological constant, usually written with the Greek letter lambda (Λ).

A decade later, Georges Lemaître and then Edwin Hubble showed that galaxies are rushing apart: the universe is expanding. A static universe was no longer needed, and Einstein abandoned the term. The physicist George Gamow later reported that Einstein called it his "biggest blunder", though there is no record of Einstein writing that himself. The irony is that the term he dropped may be exactly what the universe uses.

Chapter 02

1998: the expansion that would not slow down

For most of the twentieth century, cosmologists assumed one thing about the expansion: gravity should be slowing it down. The open question was by how much. Enough to stop it one day and pull everything back? Or only enough to make it gradually coast?

Two teams set out to measure the slowing. One, the Supernova Cosmology Project, was led by Saul Perlmutter in California. The other, the High-Z Supernova Search Team, was led by Brian Schmidt in Australia, with Adam Riess playing a central role in its analysis. They were competitors. Both used the same kind of exploding star to measure distances to very remote galaxies, and both reached the same conclusion in 1998. Distant supernovae were fainter, and so farther away, than they should have been if the expansion were slowing. It was speeding up.

The result was so surprising that both teams spent months looking for mistakes. Finding the same answer independently was a large part of why others accepted it quickly. Perlmutter, Schmidt and Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Chapter 03

How much, and how strange

Today's best estimates put the universe's contents at about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter and 5% ordinary matter. That makes dark energy the largest component by far. Yet its density is tiny. Spread evenly, it amounts to an energy equivalent to the mass of only a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. It dominates only because there is so much empty space.

Its strangest property is that it does not dilute. When space expands, matter spreads out and its density falls. Dark energy, if it is a cosmological constant, keeps the same density in every cubic metre, however much new space is created. So as the universe grows, dark energy becomes an ever larger share of it. In the early universe, matter dominated and the expansion slowed. Roughly five to six billion years ago, dark energy took over, and the expansion began to speed up.

What expansion does not do

The acceleration does not pull apart atoms, people, planets or galaxies. Within those, the other forces and local gravity are far stronger. Expansion acts across the vast gaps between groups of galaxies.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Measuring the history of the expansion

To detect acceleration you need two things for many distant objects: how far away they are, and how much the universe has expanded since their light left them.

Chapter 04

Exploding stars as measuring sticks

Distance is the hardest measurement in astronomy. A dim light could be a weak source nearby or a bright one far away. The answer is a standard candle: a kind of object whose true brightness is known. Compare how bright it looks with how bright it really is, and you get its distance, just as you could judge the distance of a 100-watt bulb at night.

The candles used in 1998 were Type Ia supernovae. These happen when a white dwarf, the dense leftover core of a star like the Sun, takes on too much mass, usually from a companion, and explodes. Because they explode under similar conditions, they reach similar peak brightness, and the small differences can be corrected for by how quickly each one fades. For a few weeks, a single one can outshine its whole galaxy, so they can be seen across billions of light-years. More on these explosions is in Supernovae.

Chapter 05

Redshift: how much space has stretched

As light travels through expanding space, its waves are stretched to longer, redder wavelengths. This redshift tells astronomers how much the universe has expanded since the light left its source. Combine redshift with distance for many supernovae, and you can reconstruct the expansion's history like a film played backwards.

If the expansion had been slowing, distant supernovae would be somewhat closer than a steady expansion predicts, and look a little brighter. They looked slightly dimmer than even a steady expansion allows. That small dimming, measured carefully over dozens of supernovae, was the whole discovery.

Chapter 06

Independent cross-checks

One method alone could have been fooled, for instance if distant supernovae were dimmed by dust or were slightly different from nearby ones. So cosmologists looked for completely different evidence. They found it.

The cosmic background

The pattern of ripples in the Big Bang's leftover glow shows that space is, on large scales, almost perfectly flat. That requires a specific total density. Matter supplies only about a third of it. Something else must provide the rest.

A ruler in the galaxies

Sound waves in the early universe left a preferred spacing between galaxies, about 500 million light-years today. Maps of millions of galaxies use this baryon acoustic oscillation scale as a standard ruler at different epochs. It was first detected in 2005.

The growth of structure

Dark energy slows the clumping of matter into galaxy clusters. Counts of clusters and measurements of weak lensing across the sky match a universe with dark energy.

Three or four independent methods, using different physics, agree on roughly the same amount of dark energy. That is why almost all cosmologists accept the acceleration as real, even though nobody can explain it.

The cosmological constant is the simplest option and fits most data. It is the "Λ" in the standard model of cosmology, called ΛCDM ("lambda cold dark matter"). The distinction between it and quintessence is exactly what the newest surveys, in Question 5, are trying to settle.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The fate of the universe and a crisis in theory

Dark energy decides how the universe ends. It also exposes the largest mismatch between theory and measurement in all of physics.

Chapter 08

With a cosmological constant, the far future is the Big Freeze. In about a hundred billion years, astronomers in our own merged galaxy would see no other galaxies at all; the rest would have receded beyond view. They would have no direct way to learn that the universe is expanding. We live at a time when the evidence is still visible.

Chapter 09

The worst prediction in physics

Quantum physics says empty space is not truly empty. It seethes with fields that can never be completely still. That restless vacuum should have energy, and energy in empty space would behave exactly like a cosmological constant. So quantum theory seems to offer a natural explanation for dark energy.

The problem is the size. When physicists estimate the vacuum's energy from known particle physics, the crudest estimates come out larger than the measured dark energy by a factor of around 10120, a one followed by 120 zeros. More careful estimates reduce the gap but still leave it enormous. It is often described as the worst prediction in the history of physics. Either something cancels almost all the vacuum energy with incredible precision, or we misunderstand how the vacuum creates gravity.

One controversial response is the anthropic argument. If there are many universes with different values of dark energy, only those with a small value would form galaxies and observers, so we should not be surprised to find ourselves in one. Steven Weinberg used reasoning of this kind in 1987 to estimate a value close to what was later measured. Many physicists find the argument unsatisfying or untestable. It remains contested.

Chapter 10

The Hubble tension

The current expansion rate is called the Hubble constant. It can be measured two main ways. Working outward from nearby stars and supernovae gives about 73 kilometres per second per megaparsec (a megaparsec is about 3.3 million light-years). Working forward from the cosmic background, assuming ΛCDM, gives about 67.

The gap is small in everyday terms but larger than the measurement errors, and it has persisted for about a decade as both methods improved. Observations with the James Webb Space Telescope have largely confirmed the nearby measurements, though some teams using other types of stars find values in between. As of 2025, the tension is unresolved. It might be a subtle error, or a sign that the standard model, perhaps the dark energy part, is incomplete.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A model that fits remarkably well and explains remarkably little

The standard cosmological model, with dark energy built in, is one of science's great successes. It is also an admission of ignorance.

A fair summary is that "dark energy" is a well-measured effect with a placeholder for a cause. That is not a weakness of the science so much as an honest description of where it stands.

Is dark energy changing?

A new generation of surveys is measuring the expansion history more precisely than ever, and early results have raised a real question. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 13

What to watch for

If dark energy is truly a cosmological constant, every new survey should keep finding the same value, at every epoch. If DESI's hint strengthens, and Euclid and Rubin independently confirm it, the simplest model of the universe would have to be replaced. That would be the biggest change in cosmology since 1998, and it would reopen the question of how the universe ends.

Cosmologists are deliberately cautious. Earlier hints in the field, at similar statistical strength, have faded with more data, and the analysis choices matter. The sensible position is interest, not belief: the next few years of data should be enough to tell whether the hint grows or disappears. Either way, the century-old question Einstein faced in 1917, what empty space contributes to the shape of the universe, is once again the central question of cosmology.

The core idea

Since about five to six billion years ago, the expansion of the universe has been speeding up. Dark energy is the name for the unknown cause. It seems to fill space evenly and does not dilute as space expands, so it increasingly dominates. Its simplest explanation is Einstein's cosmological constant.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Dark energy is about 68% of the universe's contents.
  • Acceleration discovered in 1998 using Type Ia supernovae; Nobel Prize 2011.
  • Hubble constant: about 67 from the early universe, about 73 from nearby measurements.
  • Vacuum energy estimates miss by up to a factor of about 10120.
The common mistake

Thinking dark energy is a kind of matter or a force pushing on galaxies. It is a property of space itself, or something that behaves like one, and it acts only on the largest scales.
