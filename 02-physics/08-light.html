Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 08 of 20

Light. The fastest thing there is, and the way almost everything is known.

Nearly everything you know about the world beyond arm's length reached you as light. It brings the words on this page to your eyes, the warmth of the Sun to your skin, and the signals of the internet under the oceans. It sets the speed limit of the universe. It carries, in its colours, the chemistry of stars no one will ever visit. And for three centuries the best minds in physics argued about whether it is a wave or a stream of particles, until the answer turned out to be "both, and neither". This file explains what light is, how it bends, reflects and splits into colours, how lasers and LEDs make it, and why it matters for life, technology and the night sky.

Question 1 · What is it?

An electromagnetic wave that arrives in lumps

Light is the narrow band of electromagnetic radiation your eyes can detect. It travels as a wave and is absorbed as particles called photons.

Chapter 01

Newton's prism: white is every colour at once

For most of history, people assumed white light was pure and that a prism somehow added colours to it. In the mid-1660s Isaac Newton tested this. He let a narrow beam of sunlight through a hole in a shutter, passed it through a glass prism, and saw it spread into a band of colours from red to violet. Then came the clever step. He picked out one colour, say green, and sent it through a second prism. It bent again, but stayed green. The prism was not creating colour; it was separating colours that were already mixed in white light. He also recombined the colours with a lens and got white again.

Newton published his full account in his book Opticks in 1704. The band of colours he named the spectrum. We now know that what our eyes call "visible light" is a narrow range of wavelengths, from about 400 billionths of a metre (violet) to about 700 billionths (red). Beyond each end the waves continue, invisible to us, as ultraviolet and infrared.

Chapter 02

How fast is light?

For a long time people believed light was instantaneous. The first good evidence otherwise came in 1676 from the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer. He was timing the eclipses of Io, a moon of Jupiter, and noticed they came a few minutes early when Earth was nearer Jupiter and late when it was farther away. The difference was the time light took to cross the extra distance.

Today the speed of light in empty space is known exactly: 299,792,458 metres per second, about 300,000 kilometres per second. It is exact because since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in a specific fraction of a second. A few everyday consequences:

  • Light takes about 1.3 seconds to come from the Moon and about 8 minutes 20 seconds from the Sun. If the Sun vanished, you would not know for eight minutes.
  • Light could circle the Earth about seven and a half times in one second.
  • Light slows down in materials: to about 225,000 km/s in water and roughly 200,000 km/s in glass. That slowing is what makes lenses work.

The speed of light in a vacuum is also the universe's speed limit for anything carrying energy or information, for reasons explained in Volume 25.

Chapter 03

Wave or particle? The three-hundred-year argument

WhenWhoClaimEvidence

Early 1800sThomas Young, Augustin FresnelWaveInterference stripes and diffraction patterns, which only waves produce

1905Albert EinsteinLight also comes in lumps of energy, later called photonsThe photoelectric effect: light knocks electrons out of metals in a way waves cannot explain

The photoelectric effect was the turning point. Shine dim blue light on certain metals and electrons fly out at once. Shine very bright red light and none come out at all. If light were only a wave, a bright enough beam of any colour should eventually shake electrons loose. Einstein explained that light delivers energy in packets, and each packet's energy depends on its colour: blue packets carry more than red. One blue photon can knock an electron out; no number of red ones can. Einstein's Nobel Prize of 1921 was awarded for this work, not for relativity.

The modern view is that light travels like a wave and is detected like a particle. It spreads, bends and interferes as a wave. But when it is absorbed, by your eye, a camera or a solar panel, it gives up its energy one photon at a time. This double nature is a central idea of quantum physics.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Bouncing, bending, splitting, and being made

Most of optics comes from two behaviours, reflection and refraction, plus one fact: different colours bend by different amounts.

Chapter 04

Reflection: mirrors, matte surfaces and polarised glasses

When light hits a smooth surface, it bounces off at the same angle it arrived, like a ball off a wall. That rule, angle in equals angle out, makes mirrors work. A mirror shows a clear image because its surface is smooth at the scale of light's wavelength, so all the rays bounce together in an orderly way.

Most surfaces are rough at that scale. Paper, wood and skin scatter light in all directions, which is called diffuse reflection. That is why you can see a page from any angle but cannot see your face in it. Almost everything you see, you see by diffuse reflection.

Light waves also vibrate in a direction across their path, called their polarisation. Light reflected off water or a road tends to vibrate horizontally. Polarised sunglasses block horizontal vibrations, which is why they cut glare from lakes and wet roads so effectively while letting most other light through. Phone and computer screens also use polarising layers, which is why some screens go dark when seen through polarised glasses at certain angles.

Chapter 05

Refraction, lenses and the light pipe

When light passes at an angle from air into glass or water, it slows down and changes direction, as described in Waves. How much a material slows light is measured by its refractive index: water is about 1.33, ordinary glass about 1.5, diamond about 2.4. The higher the index, the more the light bends, which is part of why diamonds sparkle.

A lens is a piece of glass or plastic shaped so that refraction bends rays in a useful way. A lens that is thicker in the middle (a converging lens) brings rays together to a point, and can form an image. The eye's own lens focuses an image on the retina. Cameras, microscopes, telescopes, projectors and glasses all work by refraction. Short-sighted eyes focus too strongly, so their glasses use lenses thinner in the middle to spread the rays slightly first.

Refraction has a striking special case. When light inside glass hits the surface at a shallow enough angle, it cannot get out at all, and reflects back inside completely. This is total internal reflection. An optical fibre, a glass thread thinner than a hair, traps light this way and guides it along for many kilometres. The physicist Charles Kao showed in the 1960s that very pure glass could carry light far enough for communication, and won a share of the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics. Fibres now carry the great majority of the world's internet traffic (Volume 12, Undersea cables).

Chapter 06

Rainbows: refraction sorted by colour

Newton's prism worked because each colour bends by a slightly different amount in glass. Violet bends most, red least. This spreading is called dispersion. Raindrops do the same.

  (from behind     /     \     light enters, bends,
   you)           |  drop |    reflects off the back,
                   \     /     bends again on the way out
                    '---'
                   /    \
              red /      \ violet     each colour leaves at its own angle,
                 v        v           about 42 degrees for red, 40 for violet,
              your eye sees red from higher drops, violet from lower ones

Sunlight enters a raindrop, bends, reflects off the back of the drop, and bends again as it leaves. The colours come out at slightly different angles, close to 42 degrees from the direction opposite the Sun. So a rainbow is always centred on the point directly opposite the Sun from you, which is why you see one only with the Sun behind you, and why it is a circle, usually cut off by the ground. Everyone sees their own rainbow, made by different drops. A fainter second bow, with its colours reversed, comes from light that reflects twice inside the drops.

Chapter 07

Colour: in the light, in the object, and in the brain

A red apple is red because its skin absorbs most colours of white light and reflects mainly red ones. Under a pure blue light, the same apple looks nearly black, because there is no red light for it to reflect. Colour is not a fixed property of an object; it depends on the light falling on it.

Your eye has three types of colour-sensitive cells, called cones, most sensitive to long, medium and short wavelengths, roughly red, green and blue. Every colour you see is your brain comparing the signals from those three. That is why screens can fool you with only three colours of tiny light: red, green and blue dots mixed in different strengths make every colour on the display. This is additive mixing, and red plus green light looks yellow.

Paints and inks work the other way. Each pigment absorbs some colours, so mixing pigments subtracts more and more light. This is subtractive mixing, which is why printers use cyan, magenta and yellow (plus black), and why mixing many paints gives a muddy brown. How the brain builds colour from the cones, and why colour vision differs between people, is explored in Vision.

Chapter 08

How light is made: glowing, atoms and LEDs

There are two basic ways to make light. The first is heat. Everything glows with radiation depending on its temperature. Your body glows in infrared, which is what thermal cameras see. A stove ring glows dull red at several hundred degrees. The Sun's surface, at about 5,500 °C, glows white. Old filament bulbs worked this way, which is why most of their energy came out as heat, not light.

The second way is atoms changing energy. Electrons in an atom can only sit at certain energy levels. When one drops to a lower level, it gives off a photon of one exact colour. Each element has its own set of colours: neon glows red-orange, sodium yellow-orange, as in old street lamps. This is the same fingerprint Volume 29 uses to read the chemistry of stars.

The light-emitting diode (LED) uses the same principle inside a semiconductor. Red and green LEDs existed from the 1960s and 1970s, but white lighting needed a bright, efficient blue. That arrived in the early 1990s, and Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. A white LED is usually a blue LED coated with a material that turns some blue light into yellow, which together look white. LEDs use a fraction of the energy of filament bulbs for the same light and last far longer.

Chapter 09

Lasers: light in perfect step

In 1917 Einstein worked out that a photon passing an excited atom can trigger it to release an identical photon, same colour, same direction, in step with the first. This is stimulated emission. Arrange many excited atoms between two mirrors, and photons bouncing back and forth trigger more and more identical photons. The result is a beam that is a single colour, very narrow and all in step. The word laser comes from "light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation". Theodore Maiman built the first working one, using a ruby crystal, in 1960.

At first it was called "a solution looking for a problem". Today lasers read barcodes, carry internet data through fibres, cut steel, correct eyesight, remove tattoos, measure distances, print chips, and guide some weapons. The detectors that first sensed gravitational waves in 2015 are, at heart, giant laser rulers that measured a change far smaller than an atomic nucleus (see Gravitational waves).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Life, time, knowledge, and the vanishing dark

Light feeds nearly all life, sets the body's clock, carries almost all our knowledge of the universe, and, in excess, erases the night.

Chapter 10

Light feeds the living world and sets its clocks

Plants, algae and some bacteria capture photons of sunlight and use their energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. That is photosynthesis, and almost every food chain on Earth begins with it. The oxygen in the air is its by-product. Fossil fuels are, in effect, ancient sunlight stored in plant and plankton remains.

Light also tells bodies what time it is. Besides the rods and cones used for seeing, the eye contains a small set of cells with a pigment called melanopsin that respond most to blue-ish light. They send signals to the brain's master clock, which keeps the body's daily cycle, its circadian rhythm, in line with day and night. Bright morning light helps keep the clock on time. Bright light late at night can delay it. How much evening phone screens disturb sleep is debated: the effect of the light itself seems modest for most people, and what you are doing on the screen may matter as much. Sleep itself is covered in Volume 33, What sleep is for.

Chapter 11

Light as messenger, from atoms to galaxies

Because light takes time to travel, looking far away means looking back in time. You see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago, nearby stars as they were years ago, and distant galaxies as they were billions of years ago (Volume 29, Looking out is looking back). Telescopes are time machines for light. Nearly all we know about stars, planets and the history of the universe has been learned by collecting light, and the invisible kinds beyond it, and reading its colours, brightness and shifts (Space telescopes).

Light is also how we see the very small. Microscopes opened the world of cells in the 1600s. Today, light techniques can track single molecules inside living cells. In 2023 the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz and Anne L'Huillier for flashes of light lasting attoseconds, billionths of a billionth of a second, short enough to follow electrons as they move.

Chapter 12

Light pollution: the night we have lost

Artificial light at night has spread far beyond what anyone needs to see by. A widely cited world atlas of night-sky brightness, published in 2016, estimated that more than 80 percent of the world's population lives under light-polluted skies, and that about a third of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way from home. Studies based on thousands of observations by members of the public suggest the night sky has kept brightening by several percent a year since then, faster than satellites alone recorded, partly because satellites miss much of the blue light from white LEDs.

The costs are real if hard to measure exactly. Migrating birds are drawn to and confused by lit buildings. Newly hatched sea turtles head towards street lights instead of the sea. Insects die in huge numbers around lamps. For people, the loss is partly cultural: a sky full of stars, which every human generation knew, is now something most children have never seen. Solutions are simple and cheap: shield lights so they point down, use warmer colours, dim or switch off when nobody is there.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A gift with a few sharp edges

Light is essential and mostly harmless, but some of its forms and uses carry real risks.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Never look directly at the Sun, including during a partial eclipse, without proper eclipse glasses: it can burn the retina without pain. Never point a laser at anyone's eyes or at an aircraft. Sun protection matters most in the middle of the day and for children.

Light will also keep opening new windows in science. Each time we have built a new way to collect it, a bigger telescope, a sharper microscope, a shorter flash, we have seen something nobody expected. The history of light is the history of seeing more.

The core idea

Light is an electromagnetic wave that is absorbed in lumps called photons. It reflects off surfaces, bends when it changes speed, and splits into colours because each colour bends differently. It is made by hot objects and by electrons changing energy in atoms, LEDs and lasers.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Speed in a vacuum: 299,792,458 m/s, about 300,000 km/s.
  • Sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth.
  • Visible light spans roughly 400 to 700 nanometres (billionths of a metre).
The common mistake

Thinking colour belongs to the object. An object's colour depends on which light it reflects, and on the light falling on it; your brain builds the colour from three kinds of cone.

Test yourself

Can you explain why you only see a rainbow with the Sun behind you? If you can, you understand refraction, reflection inside a drop, and dispersion together.

The words, in plain words
Photon
A single packet of light energy; bluer light has more energy per photon.
Spectrum
The range of colours, or wavelengths, that make up light.
Wavelength
The distance between crests of the light wave; it sets the colour.
Reflection
Light bouncing off a surface, at the same angle it arrived.
Refraction
Light bending as it changes speed passing into another material.
Refractive index
A number saying how much a material slows and bends light.
Lens
A shaped piece of transparent material that bends light to form an image.
Total internal reflection
Light trapped inside glass or water because it meets the surface at a shallow angle.
Dispersion
The spreading of white light into colours because each bends differently.
Polarisation
The direction in which a light wave vibrates across its path.
Photoelectric effect
Light knocking electrons out of a material, which showed that light comes in photons.
LED
A light-emitting diode, a semiconductor that glows efficiently when current passes.
Laser
A source of narrow, single-colour light whose waves are all in step.
Light pollution
Excess artificial light at night that brightens the sky and disturbs living things.
