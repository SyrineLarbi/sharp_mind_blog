Particle physics. Smashing things together very hard to see what the world is made of.

Particle physics asks the oldest question in science, what is everything made of, and answers it with some of the largest machines ever built. Its method sounds crude: accelerate tiny particles close to the speed of light, crash them together, and photograph the debris. Out of that debris came the electron, the quark, the neutrino and the Higgs boson. Along the way came medical scanners, cancer treatments and the World Wide Web. This file is about the field and its tools. The theory those tools confirmed has its own file, The Standard Model.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of the smallest known things

Particle physics studies the basic building blocks of matter and the forces between them. It is also called high-energy physics, for a reason that turns out to be central.

Chapter 01

A century of opening boxes inside boxes

In 1897 J. J. Thomson in Cambridge showed that the glowing "rays" inside a vacuum tube were made of tiny charged particles, far lighter than any atom. He had found the electron, and with it the first proof that atoms have parts. A few years later, in Ernest Rutherford's laboratory in Manchester, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired particles at a thin sheet of gold. Most went straight through. A very few bounced almost straight back. Rutherford compared it to firing a large shell at tissue paper and having it come back and hit you. In 1911 he explained it: nearly all of an atom's mass sits in a tiny, dense nucleus.

The nucleus was then found to contain protons, and in 1932 James Chadwick found its other ingredient, the neutron. For a moment the picture looked complete. Three particles, electron, proton and neutron, built every element. It did not last. Over the next thirty years physicists found dozens of new particles, so many that the collection was nicknamed the "particle zoo". The way out of the zoo, in the 1960s, was to see that protons, neutrons and most of the zoo were themselves made of smaller things: quarks.

Chapter 02

The cast list, in brief

Today's picture sorts the known fundamental particles, the ones with no known parts, into a few families. The full table, and the theory behind it, are in The Standard Model. Here is the short version.

FamilyMembersWhat they do
QuarksSix kinds, called up, down, charm, strange, top and bottomBuild protons (two up, one down) and neutrons (one up, two down)
LeptonsThe electron, its heavier cousins the muon and tau, and three neutrinosElectrons fill atoms; neutrinos appear in radioactive decay
Force carriersThe photon, the gluon, the W and Z bosonsCarry the electromagnetic, strong and weak forces
The Higgs bosonOne particleThe visible sign of the field that gives many particles their mass

Every matter particle also has an antiparticle, with the same mass and opposite charge. The electron's antiparticle is the positron. When a particle meets its antiparticle, both can vanish in a flash of energy. Antimatter is not science fiction. Hospitals use it every day, as Chapter 10 explains.

Chapter 03

Why "small" means "high energy"

Two facts from twentieth-century physics explain why the field needs giant machines. The first is about seeing. To see a small thing, you need a probe with a wavelength smaller than the thing. Quantum physics says every particle behaves like a wave, and the more energy it has, the shorter its wavelength. Seeing inside a proton requires particles with very high energy. Accelerators are, among other things, the world's most powerful microscopes.

The second fact is Einstein's E = mc2: mass and energy can turn into each other. When two particles collide with enough energy, that energy can turn into brand-new particles that were not there before, including heavy ones that exist only for a tiny fraction of a second. The collision is not breaking things open to see what was inside. It is creating new matter out of motion. To make a heavier particle, you need a more energetic collision. That is why each generation of discoveries has needed a bigger machine.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Accelerate, collide, detect, count

The method has four steps. Each one is an engineering feat, and the last one, statistics, is where most discoveries are actually made.

Chapter 04

Before machines: the sky's own accelerator

In 1912 the Austrian physicist Victor Hess carried radiation detectors up in a balloon. Radiation increased with height, which meant it was coming from space. These cosmic rays, mostly protons travelling at enormous speeds, hit the upper atmosphere and create showers of particles that rain down on us all the time.

For two decades cosmic rays were the best particle source available. In 1932 Carl Anderson, studying their tracks in a cloud chamber, a box of vapour in which charged particles leave trails like aircraft contrails, saw a particle that curved the wrong way in his magnet. It was the positron. The British physicist Paul Dirac had predicted antimatter from his equations a few years earlier. In 1936 Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer found the muon, a heavier copy of the electron that nobody had expected. The physicist Isidor Rabi is said to have asked, "Who ordered that?" Nobody has a full answer yet.

Chapter 05

Building the machines

Cosmic rays are free but unreliable. You cannot aim them or choose their energy. From the 1930s, physicists built their own accelerators. The principle is simple. Charged particles are pushed forward by electric fields and steered by magnets. In a circular machine, they go round and round, receiving a push on every lap.

The largest today is CERN's Large Hadron Collider (LHC), near Geneva on the border of France and Switzerland. It is a ring 27 kilometres around, about 100 metres underground. Two beams of protons travel in opposite directions inside pipes emptier than outer space, and are bent by more than a thousand superconducting magnets, cooled to 1.9 degrees above absolute zero. That is colder than the space between the stars. The protons reach 99.999999% of the speed of light and go round the ring about 11,000 times a second. At four points the beams cross, and the protons collide.

A word: hadron

A hadron is any particle made of quarks held together by the strong force. Protons and neutrons are hadrons. The LHC collides protons, and sometimes heavy atomic nuclei such as lead, which is why it is a "hadron" collider.

Chapter 06

Detectors: photographing what cannot be seen

The new particles made in a collision usually decay into lighter ones within a tiny fraction of a second. Nobody ever sees a Higgs boson directly. Physicists see its products, and work backwards. A modern detector is built like an onion, with each layer catching a different kind of particle.

The two largest LHC detectors, ATLAS and CMS, are each the size of a large building and weigh thousands of tonnes. When running, the LHC produces up to around a billion collisions a second in each. No computer system could store them all. Fast electronics called triggers decide in millionths of a second which few collisions look interesting enough to keep, and discard the rest. The stored data still runs to many petabytes a year and is analysed on a worldwide computing grid.

Chapter 07

Discovery is a statistical claim

A single collision almost never proves anything. A Higgs boson decaying into two photons looks just like many ordinary events that also produce two photons. What reveals the Higgs is a small excess: slightly more two-photon events at one particular energy than the background would give. Finding it means collecting billions of collisions and counting.

Particle physicists use a strict standard called five sigma. It means that if the new particle did not exist, random chance would produce a bump this large only about once in 3.5 million tries. The standard exists because smaller bumps often vanish with more data. Several excited claims in the field's history, at "three sigma", later disappeared. The rule forces patience. A discovery is not a picture; it is a probability.

Chapter 08

Neutrinos: the ghost particles

In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli faced a crisis. In one kind of radioactive decay, energy seemed to vanish. Rather than give up conservation of energy, he proposed a tiny, neutral, invisible particle that carried the missing energy away. He worried he had predicted something that could never be detected. It took until 1956 for Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines to catch it, using the huge flood of neutrinos from a nuclear reactor.

Neutrinos barely interact with anything. Tens of billions of them from the Sun pass through every square centimetre of your body each second, day and night, since they go straight through the Earth. To catch a few, physicists build giant tanks of water or ice deep underground, shielded from cosmic rays. In 1998 the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan showed that neutrinos change type as they travel, which is only possible if they have mass. The standard theory had said their mass was zero, so this was one of the first clear cracks in it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Knowledge first, spin-offs second, cooperation third

Particle physics rarely produces products. It produces understanding, tools and a model of how nations can work together.

Chapter 09

4 July 2012, and what it settled

In the 1960s several physicists, including Peter Higgs, François Englert and Robert Brout, proposed that an invisible field fills all of space and gives particles their mass as they move through it. If the field was real, collisions should be able to shake it hard enough to produce a particle: the Higgs boson. Searching for it took almost fifty years.

On 4 July 2012, the ATLAS and CMS teams at CERN announced, independently, that each had found a new particle with the expected properties. Peter Higgs, then 83, was in the audience, and was seen wiping his eyes. He and Englert shared the Nobel Prize in 2013; Brout had died in 2011. The discovery completed the Standard Model's list of particles. It also deepened a puzzle, because the Higgs boson's mass is hard to explain without new physics, and none has yet appeared.

The field also tells us about the past. Collisions at the LHC briefly recreate conditions like those a tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang. Lead-ion collisions there produce a hot "soup" of free quarks and gluons that is thought to have filled the early universe.

Chapter 10

What the rest of us got out of it

Medicine

PET scans inject a substance that releases positrons. Each positron meets an electron and both turn into two flashes of light, which the scanner detects to map activity in the body. Proton therapy uses accelerated protons to kill tumours while sparing more of the tissue around them. Most of the world's accelerators are not in physics labs at all. They are in hospitals and factories.

The World Wide Web

In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist at CERN, proposed a system to help physicists share documents across different computers. It became the World Wide Web. CERN released the technology free for anyone to use in 1993, a decision that shaped the open internet.

Superconducting magnets

Accelerators drove the development of strong, reliable superconducting magnets, the same kind used in hospital MRI scanners.

Detectors and computing

Fast radiation detectors and grid computing developed for particle physics are used in medical imaging, security scanning and large-scale data analysis.

The honest point is that none of these were the goal. They came from solving very hard problems at the edge of what was possible, which is a common pattern for basic research.

Chapter 11

Science across borders

CERN was founded in 1954, less than ten years after the Second World War, partly to rebuild European science and stop its best physicists leaving for America. It now has more than twenty member states, and thousands of scientists from around the world use its facilities. Large experiments involve collaborations of several thousand people from dozens of countries, who must agree on every analysis before a result is published.

That way of working has influenced other big projects, from astronomy to fusion research. It is not free of politics: after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, CERN's council decided to end its cooperation agreements with Russia and Belarus, which took effect in 2024. But as a long-running model of peaceful scientific cooperation, it is unusual.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Deep answers at a high price

The field's achievements are real. So is the question of whether the next step is worth what it costs.

Chapter 12

The case for and the case against

QuestionThe case forThe case against
KnowledgeFound the basic constituents of matter and confirmed a theory of three of the four forces to extraordinary precision.Since the Higgs, the LHC has found no new fundamental particle, and the theory gives no guaranteed target for the next machine.
CostSpread across many countries and decades, the cost per citizen is small.The LHC cost several billion Swiss francs to build. A successor could cost much more, money that could fund many smaller experiments.
Spin-offsMedical imaging, cancer therapy, the web, magnets and computing.Spin-offs are unpredictable and could arguably be achieved more cheaply by targeted research.
Way of workingVery high statistical standards, open data policies, cooperation across rival nations.Huge teams make individual credit hard, and careers can depend on one experiment for decades.
TalentTrains many people in data analysis, software and engineering who later move into industry.Critics argue the field absorbs talent that might solve more urgent problems.

Physicists themselves disagree about the next step. Some, such as the German physicist and writer Sabine Hossenfelder, have argued publicly that a bigger collider has no strong scientific case. Supporters reply that studying the Higgs boson in detail is itself a guaranteed scientific return, and that every past energy frontier eventually revealed something. This is a genuine, unsettled debate, not a fight between experts and cranks.

Chapter 14

Where the next surprise might hide

The field is in an unusual position. Its main theory works almost perfectly in the lab, yet it cannot be the whole story. It does not include gravity, it does not explain dark matter, and it cannot explain why there is more matter than antimatter. That gives particle physicists clear questions without clear directions.

So the field is spreading its bets. Alongside giant colliders, there are small, extremely precise experiments that measure known particles to many decimal places, looking for tiny deviations. There are deep underground searches for dark matter particles, and telescopes that use cosmic rays and neutrinos from space. The next discovery may come from a quiet tabletop experiment rather than a 90-kilometre ring. History offers both kinds of precedent, which is part of what keeps the debate alive.

The core idea

Particle physics finds the smallest building blocks of matter by colliding particles at high energy. Higher energy gives sharper vision and, through E = mc2, creates new heavy particles. Detectors record the debris, and discoveries are made by statistics, not single pictures.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The LHC ring is 27 km around, with magnets at 1.9 K.
  • The Higgs boson was announced on 4 July 2012.
  • A discovery needs five sigma, a fluke chance of about 1 in 3.5 million.
  • Tens of billions of solar neutrinos pass through each square centimetre of you every second.
The common mistake

Thinking colliders break particles open to see the pieces inside. Mostly, they turn the energy of the collision into new particles that did not exist before.

Test yourself

Can you explain why finding a heavier particle needs a bigger accelerator? If you can connect wavelength, E = mc2 and collision energy, you have understood the logic of the whole field.

The words, in plain words
Fundamental particle
A particle with no known smaller parts, such as an electron or a quark.
Quark
A fundamental particle that combines in groups to form protons, neutrons and other hadrons.
Lepton
A family of fundamental particles including the electron, muon, tau and the neutrinos.
Hadron
Any particle made of quarks bound by the strong force.
Antiparticle
A twin of a particle with the same mass and opposite charge.
Neutrino
A very light, neutral particle that hardly interacts with anything.
Cosmic rays
Fast particles from space that hit the atmosphere and create showers of other particles.
Accelerator
A machine that speeds up charged particles using electric fields and steers them with magnets.
Collider
An accelerator that smashes two beams into each other head-on.
Detector
Layers of instruments around a collision point that record the particles produced.
Trigger
Fast electronics that choose which collisions are worth recording.
Five sigma
The statistical standard for claiming a discovery in particle physics.
Higgs boson
The particle linked to the field that gives many fundamental particles their mass.
