Sharp Mind · Physics · Topic 03 of 20

Electricity. A slow crowd of electrons that delivers energy at almost the speed of light.

Flip a switch and a lamp lights instantly, although the electrons in the wire move slower than a snail. Touch a doorknob after walking on carpet and you feel a spark of several thousand volts that does you no harm, while the 230 volts in a wall socket can kill. Electricity is full of facts like these, which sound like contradictions until you understand four ideas: charge, current, voltage and resistance. This file builds those four ideas from the ground up, then follows them into batteries, the human heart, lightning and the power grid that runs modern life.

Question 1 · What is it?

Charge, and what happens when it moves

Electricity is the name for everything that happens because of electric charge: charges sitting still, charges pushing on each other, and charges flowing.

Chapter 01

From rubbed amber to two kinds of charge

The ancient Greeks knew that a piece of amber, rubbed with fur, would attract bits of straw. The Greek word for amber was elektron, and that is where the word "electricity" comes from. For about two thousand years this was a curiosity. In the 1700s experimenters found that there are two kinds of the effect. Two rubbed glass rods push each other away. A rubbed glass rod and a rubbed piece of resin pull together.

The American writer and scientist Benjamin Franklin called the two kinds positive and negative, names we still use. The rule is simple: like charges repel, opposite charges attract. Franklin also argued that lightning is the same thing as the spark from a rubbed rod, on a vast scale. His famous kite experiment of 1752 is usually told as a dramatic moment in a storm. Historians have questioned some details of the story, but lightning rods based on his idea spread quickly and worked.

Chapter 02

What carries the charge

Today we know that charge belongs to particles inside atoms. Each atom has a small, positive nucleus surrounded by negative electrons. Normally the two balance, so the atom is neutral. Rubbing two materials together can move some electrons from one to the other. The one that gains electrons becomes negative. The one that loses them becomes positive. That is static electricity: charge that has been separated and is sitting still.

Whether charge can flow depends on the material.

TypeWhat happens insideExamplesUsed for
ConductorsSome electrons are loose and can drift freely between atomsCopper, aluminium, silver, salty waterWires, cables
InsulatorsElectrons are held tightly; almost no flowRubber, plastic, glass, dry wood, airCable coatings, plugs, power-line supports
SemiconductorsFlow is weak but can be switched on and off by small changesSilicon, germaniumTransistors, computer chips, solar cells
SuperconductorsBelow a very low temperature, resistance falls to exactly zeroCertain metals and ceramics, deeply cooledMRI magnets, particle accelerators

Pure water is actually a poor conductor. The water in your taps, your body and the sea conducts well because of the dissolved salts in it. That is why wet skin and electricity are a dangerous mix.

Chapter 03

The four words: charge, current, voltage, resistance

The most useful picture is water in pipes. It is not perfect, but it gets the relationships right.

QuantityWhat it meansUnitWater picture
ChargeThe amount of electricityCoulomb (C)Amount of water

The carpet spark is now easy to explain. Its voltage is high, several thousand volts, but the amount of charge is tiny and it flows for a millionth of a second. Voltage is the push; what hurts you is current flowing through your body for long enough. A wall socket has a far lower voltage but can keep driving a large current indefinitely.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Circuits, Ohm's law, and the slowest fast thing in the world

Current only flows in a complete loop. Once you see the loop, batteries, switches, heaters and power lines all follow from a few simple rules.

Chapter 04

Ohm's law

In 1827 the German physicist Georg Ohm published the relationship that bears his name. Voltage equals current times resistance, V = I × R. Read it as: the push you need depends on how much flow you want and how hard the path is.

A worked example makes it real. A kettle in Europe runs on 230 volts and is rated at about 2,300 watts. That means it draws about 10 amps, so its heating element has a resistance of about 23 ohms. A phone charger cable carries only a fraction of an amp at 5 to 20 volts. The same law covers both.

Resistance is not just an obstacle. It is often the point. When current is forced through a resistance, electrons collide with atoms and pass energy to them as heat. Kettles, toasters, hair dryers and electric heaters are all deliberately chosen resistances. The same effect is a nuisance in cables, which is why they are made of low-resistance copper and why an overloaded extension lead gets warm.

Chapter 05

Slow electrons, fast signals

Here is the surprise promised at the top. In a typical household wire carrying a few amps, the electrons drift along at well under a millimetre per second. An electron leaving the switch might take hours to reach the lamp. Yet the lamp lights at once.

The reason is that the wire is already full of electrons. Closing the switch does not send electrons down the wire like water from a hose. It sends a push, an electric field, along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light. Every electron in the circuit starts drifting almost at the same moment. Think of a tube packed with marbles. Push one in at one end and one pops out at the other immediately, although each marble has moved only a little.

In mains electricity the electrons do not even travel. The current is alternating current (AC): the push reverses direction 50 times a second in Europe, Africa and most of Asia, and 60 times a second in North America and some other countries. The electrons just jiggle back and forth. Energy flows from the power station to your lamp; the electrons stay roughly where they are.

Chapter 06

Power, heat, and why power lines use huge voltages

Electrical power is voltage times current, P = V × I. So the same power can be delivered as high voltage with low current, or low voltage with high current. That choice matters enormously, because the heat wasted in a cable grows with the square of the current. Halve the current and you cut the losses to a quarter.

That is why long-distance transmission lines run at hundreds of thousands of volts. At very high voltage the current needed is small, so little energy is lost as heat along hundreds of kilometres. Near homes, transformers step the voltage back down to safe levels. Transformers only work with alternating current, a fact that decided a famous business battle.

Chapter 07

The war of the currents

In the 1880s Thomas Edison's companies built electricity systems using direct current (DC), which flows steadily in one direction. DC could not easily be changed in voltage, so power stations had to be close to customers. George Westinghouse, using alternating current systems based partly on patents from Nikola Tesla, could transmit power over long distances and transform it down locally. Edison's side campaigned that AC was dangerous. AC won most of the market by the 1890s, and a hydroelectric plant at Niagara Falls in the mid-1890s became a showcase for it.

The story has a modern twist. Today's electronics turn the AC from the wall back into DC inside their chargers, because chips need DC. Solar panels and batteries produce DC. And for very long distances or undersea cables, high-voltage direct current (HVDC) lines are now often the better choice, thanks to modern semiconductor converters. Both sides of the old war turned out to be partly right.

Chapter 08

Batteries and circuits

In 1800 the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by cloth soaked in salt water, and produced the first steady electric current. This "voltaic pile" was the first battery, and the volt is named after him. A battery is a chemical pump. Reactions inside it push electrons out of one terminal and take them back in at the other, keeping a voltage across the two. The chemistry is explained in Electrochemistry.

Components can be connected in two basic ways.

In series (one after another)

Current has only one path. If one part breaks, everything stops, like old Christmas lights where one dead bulb darkened the whole string. Batteries in series add their voltages: two 1.5 V cells give 3 V.

In parallel (side by side)

Each part has its own path across the same voltage. One can be switched off without affecting the others. House wiring is in parallel, which is why turning off the kitchen light does not turn off the fridge.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Your nerves, your heart, the sky and the grid

Electricity is not only something we produce. It is how bodies send signals and how storms release energy, and it is the backbone of modern civilisation.

Chapter 09

The body runs on electricity

Every nerve signal in your body is electrical. A nerve cell pumps charged atoms, called ions, across its membrane so that the inside is slightly negative. When it fires, gates in the membrane open and the voltage flips for about a millisecond, and that flip travels along the cell. The idea goes back to the 1780s, when Luigi Galvani made dead frogs' legs twitch with electricity. The details are in Neurons.

Your heart also beats to an electrical rhythm, started by a small cluster of cells and spread across the muscle. An electrocardiogram (ECG) records that rhythm from the skin. This is why electric shock is so dangerous. A current through the chest of only tens of thousandths of an amp can disrupt the heart's rhythm into a useless quiver called fibrillation. A defibrillator does the reverse: a strong, brief shock stops the chaotic activity so the heart's own pacemaker cells can restart a normal beat.

Safety

This is general education, not safety or medical training. Mains electricity can kill. Never work on wiring that is live, and if someone is being electrocuted, cut the power before touching them. Public defibrillators are designed to be used by untrained people and give spoken instructions.

Chapter 10

Lightning: static electricity at storm scale

Inside a thunderstorm, ice crystals and soft hail collide in strong updrafts. The collisions separate charge, leaving the top of the cloud mostly positive and the lower part mostly negative. When the voltage becomes large enough, the air itself breaks down and becomes a conductor for a moment. That is lightning.

A typical strike carries a current of tens of thousands of amps for a tiny fraction of a second, and heats the air in its channel to several times the temperature of the Sun's surface. The air expands so violently that it makes a shock wave, which we hear as thunder. Satellite counts suggest that lightning strikes somewhere on Earth around 40 to 50 times every second. A lightning rod does not attract lightning so much as give it a safe path to the ground, which is what Franklin proposed.

Chapter 11

The grid: the largest machine ever built

An electricity grid connects power stations to millions of users across a country or a whole continent. Its hardest job is one most people never think about. Supply and demand must match at every moment, because a grid stores almost nothing by itself. When millions of kettles switch on at the end of a popular television programme, power stations must increase their output within seconds.

Operators watch one number above all: the grid's frequency. On a European grid it should be 50 cycles per second. If demand exceeds supply, the spinning generators slow slightly and the frequency drops. If supply exceeds demand, it rises. Automatic systems respond to tiny changes. If the imbalance becomes too large, protective equipment disconnects parts of the grid to stop damage, and that can cascade into a blackout. On 28 April 2025 most of mainland Spain and Portugal lost power for many hours. Official investigations into the causes were reported in the months that followed, and grid stability with a high share of solar power became a major topic of debate.

Modern grids also face a newer challenge. Large spinning generators naturally resist sudden changes in frequency, a property called inertia. Solar panels and many wind turbines connect through electronics and do not provide it automatically. Engineers are developing electronics that imitate it. This is the subject of Renewable-energy engineering.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The perfect energy carrier, almost

Electricity is not an energy source. It is a way of moving energy. As a carrier it is superb, with a few stubborn weaknesses.

Chapter 12

Strengths and weaknesses

FeatureAdvantageDisadvantage
Speed and controlDelivered instantly, switched in microseconds, controlled precisely enough to run a computer chip.Needs constant, second-by-second balancing on the grid.
Clean at the point of useNo smoke or fumes in your home or street.Only as clean as the power stations that produce it; much is still generated from fossil fuels.
ConversionTurns into motion, heat, light or computation with high efficiency.Generating it from heat in a power station loses much of the fuel's energy.
TransportMoved hundreds of kilometres through wires with small losses at high voltage.Needs expensive lines, often opposed locally, and takes years to permit and build.
StorageBatteries and pumped hydro work for hours.Storing electricity for weeks or seasons remains costly.
SafetyModern protection (breakers, earthing, residual current devices) makes it very safe in well-wired buildings.Shock and fire remain real risks, especially with old or improvised wiring.

The deepest weakness is dependence. A modern society without electricity for a few days stops working: water pumping, payments, phones, hospitals, refrigeration and fuel pumps all need it. That makes grids critical infrastructure, and it is why their resilience to storms, heat waves and cyberattacks is a national security issue in many countries.

Chapter 14

From one-way delivery to a two-way network

The grid of the twentieth century was a one-way system: large power stations pushed energy out to passive customers. That is changing. Homes with rooftop solar panels sell power back. Electric cars plugged in overnight are, together, a huge battery. Smart meters let prices change by the hour, so a washing machine or car charger can wait for cheap, plentiful power.

The grid is becoming a network in which millions of devices both use and supply energy, coordinated by software. That brings flexibility and new risks, including cybersecurity. The physics underneath does not change at all. It is still charge, current, voltage and resistance, obeying rules worked out in the nineteenth century. What changes is the scale and the cleverness with which we use them. The engineering side continues in Electrical engineering.

The core idea

Electricity is the behaviour of electric charge. Voltage pushes charge, current is the flow, and resistance opposes it, related by V = I × R. Current needs a complete loop. Energy travels through a circuit almost instantly even though the electrons themselves barely move.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Mains power is about 230 V at 50 Hz in Europe and 120 V at 60 Hz in North America.
  • Electrons in a household wire drift at under a millimetre per second.
  • Currents of only tens of milliamps through the chest can stop the heart beating normally.
The common mistake

Thinking high voltage alone is what kills. Current through the body, for long enough, is what does harm; voltage matters because it decides how much current can be pushed through you.

Test yourself

Can you explain why power lines use very high voltages, using only P = V × I and the fact that cable losses grow with the square of the current? If you can, you understand transmission.

The words, in plain words
Charge
A basic property of particles; it comes in positive and negative, and like charges repel.
Electron
A tiny negative particle in every atom; the usual carrier of current in metals.
Current
The rate at which charge flows, measured in amps.
Voltage
The push that drives charge, or the energy per unit of charge, measured in volts.
Resistance
How strongly something opposes current, measured in ohms.
Ohm's law
Voltage equals current times resistance.
Conductor
A material that lets charge flow easily, such as copper.
Insulator
A material that blocks the flow of charge, such as rubber or plastic.
Semiconductor
A material whose ability to conduct can be switched, the basis of chips.
Direct current (DC)
Current that flows steadily one way, as from a battery.
Alternating current (AC)
Current that reverses direction many times a second, as in mains power.
Transformer
A device that raises or lowers AC voltage.
Grid frequency
How many times a second mains current reverses; it drifts when supply and demand do not match.
