Sound. Tiny ripples in air pressure that your ear turns into music, speech and warning.

When someone speaks to you, the air between you is barely disturbed. The pressure changes in normal conversation are around a ten-millionth of the air pressure around you. Your ear detects them, sorts them by frequency, and hands your brain a stream it recognises as a friend's voice, a car behind you, or a song you have not heard in years. Sound is the most intimate wave in daily life. This file explains what it is physically, how pitch, loudness and tone work, how the ear does its job, why loud noise causes damage that never heals, and how doctors, ships and animals use sound to see.

Question 1 · What is it?

A pressure wave, and an experience

The word "sound" means two things: a physical wave in a material, and the sensation it produces in a listener. Keeping them apart clears up most confusion.

Chapter 01

If a tree falls and no one hears it

The old puzzle asks whether a falling tree makes a sound when nobody is there to hear it. Physics answers by splitting the word. The falling tree certainly makes pressure waves in the air; a microphone would record them. Whether there is "sound" in the sense of something heard depends on a listener. The puzzle is about language, not physics.

This split matters because the physical wave and the experience do not always match. Two sounds with the same physical energy can seem very different in loudness if their frequencies differ. A dog hears whistles that are silent to you. As you age, high notes fade from your hearing even though they are still in the air. Physics measures the wave; the ear and brain decide what we hear.

Chapter 02

Squeezes and stretches in the air

Imagine a loudspeaker cone. When it moves forward, it pushes the air molecules in front of it closer together, making a region of slightly higher pressure called a compression. When it moves back, it leaves a region of slightly lower pressure, a rarefaction. Those molecules push on their neighbours, which push on theirs. A pattern of compressions and rarefactions travels outward. That is a sound wave. It is a longitudinal wave: the air moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels.

Because sound needs molecules to pass the push along, it cannot travel through empty space. In 1660 Robert Boyle put a ticking watch in a glass jar and pumped out the air. The ticking faded. In space, an explosion is silent. The roaring spaceships of films are poetic licence.

Chapter 03

The range of hearing, and beyond

A young person with healthy ears can hear frequencies from about 20 hertz to 20,000 hertz (20 kHz): from 20 to 20,000 pressure wobbles per second. The upper limit falls with age and noise exposure, and many adults hear little above 12 to 15 kHz.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Pitch, loudness, tone, speed and the ear

Three properties describe any sound you hear: how high, how loud, and what it is like. Each maps onto a physical feature of the wave.

Chapter 04

Pitch is frequency

The pitch of a note, how high or low it sounds, is set by its frequency. A faster vibration gives a higher note. A bass guitar's lowest string vibrates about 41 times a second. A piccolo's highest notes are over 4,000.

Music is built on a remarkable fact about pitch. Doubling the frequency gives the "same" note an octave higher. Two notes an octave apart sound so alike that singers call them by the same letter. The standard tuning note for orchestras, the A above middle C, is set at 440 Hz by international agreement; the A an octave above it is 880 Hz. The intervals that sound pleasant together, like the fifth, tend to have frequencies in simple ratios such as 3 to 2. The Greek thinker Pythagoras is traditionally credited with noticing these ratios on strings, though how much of that tradition is history and how much legend is uncertain.

Chapter 05

Loudness and the decibel

Loudness depends on the amplitude of the pressure wave: how big the squeezes and stretches are. The ear handles an enormous range. The loudest sound you can bear without pain carries about a million million times more energy per second than the faintest you can hear. To cope with that range, sound levels are measured on a logarithmic scale called the decibel (dB). On a logarithmic scale, each step multiplies instead of adds.

The key rule: every extra 10 decibels means ten times the sound energy, and the ear hears it as roughly twice as loud. So 80 dB is not a little more than 70 dB. It is ten times the energy. And just 3 dB more means double the energy.

Because the scale multiplies, risk rises fast. Workplace guidelines in many countries treat about 85 dB averaged over an eight-hour day as the point where protection is needed, and each 3 dB above it halves the safe time. At 100 dB, that is well under half an hour.

Chapter 06

Timbre: why a violin and a flute sound different

Play the same note, at the same loudness, on a violin and a flute. You can tell them apart at once. The difference is called timbre (pronounced "tam-ber"), or tone colour.

A real instrument never produces a single pure frequency. A string vibrating at 220 Hz also vibrates at 440, 660, 880 Hz and so on, all at once. These extra frequencies, whole-number multiples of the main one, are called harmonics or overtones. Each instrument produces a different mix of them, with different strengths, and with its own way of starting and fading. Your brain recognises the recipe of harmonics as the instrument's voice. It does the same with people. You recognise a friend on the phone because the shape of their throat, mouth and nose gives their voice a recognisable mix of frequencies.

Chapter 07

How the ear turns pressure into nerve signals

The ear is a three-stage machine.

Outer ear

The visible ear funnels sound into the ear canal. Its folds slightly change sounds depending on their direction, which helps you tell whether a sound is in front, behind, above or below.

Middle ear

The eardrum vibrates with the sound. Three tiny bones, including the stirrup (stapes), the smallest bone in the body, pass the vibration on and concentrate it onto a much smaller membrane, boosting the pressure.

Inner ear

The cochlea, a fluid-filled spiral about the size of a pea, holds thousands of hair cells. Each bends when the fluid moves, and fires a signal to the brain.

The cochlea also sorts sound by frequency. A membrane running along its spiral is stiff and narrow at one end and floppy and wide at the other. High frequencies make the stiff end vibrate most; low frequencies, the far end. So each position along the cochlea reports one range of pitch, like the keys of a piano laid out along a coil. The Hungarian-American scientist Georg von Békésy won the Nobel Prize in 1961 for working this out.

Hearing works with both ears together. A sound from your left reaches your left ear a fraction of a millisecond before your right, and slightly louder. Your brain uses those tiny differences to locate the source, which is why someone with hearing in one ear finds it hard to tell where sounds come from.

Chapter 08

The speed of sound, thunder and the sonic boom

Sound travels through air at about 343 metres per second at room temperature, faster in warm air and slower in cold. That is about a kilometre every three seconds. Light from lightning arrives almost instantly, so count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by three to get the distance in kilometres (or by five for miles).

When an aircraft flies faster than sound, the pressure waves it makes cannot get out ahead of it. They pile up into a cone-shaped shock wave trailing behind the plane. When that cone sweeps over you on the ground, you hear a sharp double bang called a sonic boom. The first piloted aircraft to fly faster than sound in level flight was the Bell X-1, flown by Chuck Yeager in 1947. A cracking whip makes a tiny sonic boom: its tip moves faster than sound.

Speed is measured against sound with the Mach number: Mach 1 is the speed of sound, Mach 2 twice that. Concorde cruised at about Mach 2 from 1976 to 2003, but booms were so disturbing that most countries banned supersonic flight over land, which limited its routes mostly to oceans.

Chapter 09

Rooms that sound good, and rooms that don't

In a closed room, sound bounces off walls, floor and ceiling many times before it fades. That lingering is reverberation. Some is good: it makes music sound full and warm. Too much and speech turns to mush, as in a big tiled hall or a busy restaurant with hard surfaces.

Room acoustics became a science in the 1890s when the American physicist Wallace Sabine was asked to fix a lecture hall at Harvard where nobody could understand the speaker. He measured how long sound took to fade and how different materials absorbed it, and found a formula linking the two. He then helped design Boston's Symphony Hall, opened in 1900 and still praised as one of the world's best concert halls. Today restaurants, open-plan offices and classrooms are judged partly by reverberation time, and soft materials such as curtains, carpet and acoustic panels are the cure.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Hearing you can lose, and sound that can see

Sound connects people, and it can quietly harm them. It also lets us look inside the body and the deep ocean.

Chapter 10

Noise, and the damage that does not heal

The hair cells in the cochlea are delicate. Very loud sound, or moderately loud sound for long periods, bends and breaks them. In birds and fish such cells can regrow. In humans and other mammals, hair cells that die are not replaced. The hearing they provided is gone for good. Damage often begins at the high frequencies, so the first sign is trouble following conversation in noisy places, when consonants like "s", "f" and "th" blur together.

Another common sign is tinnitus, a ringing, hissing or buzzing heard when there is no outside sound. A short spell after a concert is a warning that the ear has been overloaded. Persistent tinnitus is common, and for some people distressing.

The scale is large. The World Health Organization's 2021 World Report on Hearing estimated that about 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss, and projected nearly 2.5 billion by 2050. It has also warned that over a billion young people are at risk from unsafe listening habits, such as loud music through earphones. Hearing loss in middle age is also associated with a higher risk of dementia later; whether treating it lowers that risk is still being studied, and a large trial published in 2023 found a possible benefit only in a higher-risk group.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Practical rules that follow from the physics: if you must raise your voice to be heard at arm's length, the noise is probably loud enough to damage hearing over time. Earplugs at concerts and on noisy jobs work. Keep personal music at a level where you can still hear people around you. See a doctor for sudden hearing loss, which can be an emergency.

Chapter 11

Seeing with sound: ultrasound, sonar and echolocation

Sound reflects whenever it crosses a boundary between materials, so its echoes carry a picture of what it met. Bats were shown in the 1930s and 1940s to navigate and hunt by echolocation, sending out ultrasonic calls and reading the echoes. Dolphins and some whales do the same underwater.

Humans copied the trick. Sonar, developed largely during and after the First World War to find submarines, sends sound pulses through water and times the echoes. It now maps the sea floor, finds fish and guides ships. Medical ultrasound uses frequencies of a few million hertz. A probe sends pulses into the body and listens to echoes from the boundaries between tissues, building an image in real time. It uses no ionising radiation, which is why it is the standard way to check a pregnancy, as Volume 42 describes. Focused ultrasound can also be strong enough to break kidney stones or heat small regions of tissue, as a form of treatment.

Sound travels very well underwater, far better than light. In the oceans, a layer at around a kilometre depth traps sound so it can travel thousands of kilometres. Whales may use it to call over huge distances, and it is used to monitor for undersea earthquakes and nuclear tests.

Chapter 12

Noise as a public-health problem

Noise is not only a matter for ears. Long-term exposure to traffic, rail and aircraft noise, especially at night, disturbs sleep. In 2018 the WHO's European office published noise guidelines that recommended limits for road traffic, rail, aircraft and wind-turbine noise, linking high exposure to sleep disturbance, annoyance and, with less certainty, heart disease. The strength of the heart-disease evidence varies between studies and is still debated.

Noise is also unequal. Homes next to busy roads, flight paths and industry are often cheaper, so poorer households tend to get more of it. That makes noise a concern for city planning and fairness, not only for physics. Quiet road surfaces, sound barriers, better windows and electric vehicles at low speeds all help. Sustainable cities returns to the theme.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A wave that informs, heals and harms

Sound is cheap, safe and everywhere, which makes it a superb tool and a constant hazard.

One more trade-off is worth knowing. Hearing aids work by amplifying sound, but they cannot replace dead hair cells, so they make sound louder rather than clearer, and many users struggle in noisy rooms. For people with profound deafness, a cochlear implant skips the hair cells and stimulates the hearing nerve directly with electrodes. It is one of the most successful neural implants ever developed, used by over a million people worldwide by most estimates, though the sound it gives takes time to learn and is not the same as natural hearing.

The likeliest change for most people is the simplest: better habits, quieter cities and earlier treatment. The physics will not change, and neither will the biology of hair cells for some time. The cheapest way to have good hearing at seventy is still to protect it at twenty.

The core idea

Sound is a pressure wave of compressions and rarefactions travelling through a material. Pitch is frequency, loudness is amplitude, and timbre is the mix of harmonics. The cochlea sorts frequencies along its length, and its hair cells, once lost, do not regrow.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Human hearing: about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, shrinking with age.
  • Speed in air: about 343 m/s, a kilometre every three seconds.
  • +10 dB = ten times the energy; damage risk starts around 85 dB over a long day.
The common mistake

Thinking decibels add like ordinary numbers. The scale multiplies, so a small rise in decibels is a big rise in energy, and in risk to your ears.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a violin and a flute playing the same note at the same loudness still sound different? If you can, you understand pitch, loudness and timbre as separate things.
