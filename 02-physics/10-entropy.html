Entropy. Not a force and not a mess, but a count of possibilities that only goes up.

Entropy has a reputation for being mysterious, and the usual one-word definition, "disorder", makes it worse. The real idea is simpler and stranger. Entropy counts how many ways the tiny parts of a system can be arranged while it looks the same from outside. Because big counts beat small counts, systems drift toward states with more arrangements. From that single piece of arithmetic come the one-way flow of heat, the limits on every engine, the cost of erasing information, and possibly the reason the past is different from the future.

Question 1 · What is it?

One quantity, discovered twice

Entropy was first defined by engineers measuring heat, then explained by a physicist counting atoms. The two definitions turned out to be the same thing.

Chapter 01

A word invented to sound like energy

In the 1850s and 1860s the German physicist Rudolf Clausius was trying to make sense of Carnot's engine limit. He noticed that one quantity behaved oddly. When heat moved into a body, he divided the amount of heat by the body's temperature. Added up around any real engine cycle, that number never went down. It stayed the same in a perfect, reversible engine and rose in every real one.

In 1865 he gave it a name. He chose entropy, from a Greek word for "transformation", and said he deliberately made it sound like "energy" because the two ideas were so closely related. He then summed up thermodynamics in two sentences: the energy of the universe is constant, and the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.

Clausius's definition was practical. You could measure entropy changes with thermometers and calorimeters. But it said nothing about what entropy was. He had found a quantity that always grows without knowing why it grows.

Chapter 02

Boltzmann's answer: count the ways

The explanation came from the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann in the 1870s, at a time when many scientists still doubted that atoms existed. Boltzmann separated two levels of description. A macrostate is what you can observe: a gas with a certain temperature, pressure and volume. A microstate is the exact position and speed of every single molecule. A huge number of different microstates all look like the same macrostate.

Boltzmann's insight was that entropy measures how many microstates fit a given macrostate. A macrostate that can be produced in many ways has high entropy. One that can be produced in only a few ways has low entropy. His formula, S = k log W, links the entropy S to the number of ways W, through a tiny constant k now called Boltzmann's constant. The logarithm turns astronomically large counts into manageable numbers.

Boltzmann's ideas were fiercely attacked during his life, and he suffered from severe depression. He took his own life in 1906, shortly before experiments on the jittery motion of small particles in water convinced the remaining sceptics that atoms were real. His formula is carved on his gravestone in Vienna.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Arithmetic that only runs one way

There is no force pushing things toward higher entropy. There is only the fact that high-entropy states vastly outnumber low-entropy ones, and random motion explores them all.

Chapter 03

A hundred coins on a table

Throw 100 coins onto a table. The macrostate is just the count of heads. The microstate is which particular coins landed heads.

MacrostateNumber of microstates (ways)How likely
All 100 headsExactly 1About 1 throw in a million million million million million
90 heads, 10 tailsAbout 17 million millionStill essentially never
Exactly 50 headsAbout 100 thousand million million million million (1029)About 8% of throws, the single most likely result
Between 40 and 60 headsThe vast majority of all arrangementsAbout 96% of throws

Now shake the table gently, so a few coins flip at random each time. Start from all heads. After a while you will be near 50–50, and you will stay there. Nothing pushes the coins toward balance. It is just that almost every arrangement is a balanced one, so almost every random step leads into that enormous crowd and almost none lead out of it.

A real gas is the same game played with something like 1023 molecules instead of 100 coins. At that scale, "very unlikely" becomes "would not happen once in many lifetimes of the universe". The second law is not a rule that the parts obey. It is a statistical certainty that emerges from enormous numbers.

Chapter 04

Why heat flows from hot to cold, in entropy terms

Clausius's definition, heat divided by temperature, explains the direction of heat flow neatly. Suppose 1,000 joules of heat leave a hot block at 400 kelvin and enter a cooler block at 300 kelvin. The hot block loses 1,000 ÷ 400 = 2.5 units of entropy. The cool block gains 1,000 ÷ 300, about 3.3 units. The total has gone up by about 0.8.

Run the same transfer backwards, from cold to hot, and the total would go down. That is the forbidden direction. The same heat adds more entropy to a cold body than it removes from a hot one, so flowing downhill always raises the total. In Boltzmann's language, a given amount of energy opens up proportionally more new arrangements in a cold body, where the molecules had fewer options, than it closes in a hot one.

This also explains why engines must dump waste heat. Turning heat into work removes entropy from the hot source but adds none anywhere else, because organised motion has very few arrangements. To keep the total from falling, some heat must be passed to the cold side, where it raises entropy enough to pay the bill.

Chapter 05

Why "disorder" is a misleading word

Many textbooks call entropy "disorder". It is a helpful first picture, but it fails in cases you can see in your own kitchen. Shake oil and water together and leave them. They separate into two neat layers, which looks more ordered. Yet entropy rose. Water molecules forced to sit next to oil have to line up in restricted patterns; once the oil gathers into one layer, far more water molecules are free to move however they like. The visible tidiness hides an invisible gain in freedom.

Crystals growing in a cooling solution, or snowflakes forming in a cloud, look like order arising by itself. In each case heat is released into the surroundings, and the entropy gained there is larger than the entropy lost by the crystal. Entropy is about the number of hidden possibilities, not about how tidy something looks to us. A better everyday word is "spreading": energy and matter spread out into more ways of being arranged.

Chapter 06

Maxwell's demon and the price of information

In 1867 James Clerk Maxwell imagined a tiny being guarding a door between two boxes of gas. It watches each molecule. It opens the door to let fast ones pass to the right and slow ones to the left. Soon the right box is hot and the left box cold, with no work done. The demon seems to beat the second law using nothing but knowledge.

Solving the puzzle took about a century. In 1929 Leo Szilard argued that gaining information must cost something. The final answer came from Rolf Landauer in 1961 and Charles Bennett in 1982. Measuring can in principle be free, but the demon must store what it learns. Its memory eventually fills, and erasing a memory necessarily produces heat, at least a tiny fixed amount per bit. That heat pays back all the entropy the demon removed. An experiment in France in 2012 measured heat release close to Landauer's minimum when a single bit was erased.

Separately, in 1948 the American engineer Claude Shannon defined the "entropy" of a message as a measure of its unpredictability, with a formula that has the same mathematical shape as the physics one. A story often told, but not documented in writing, says John von Neumann advised him to use the word because nobody really knows what entropy is, which would give him the advantage in any argument. Whether or not that happened, the link is real. Physical entropy can be read as the information you are missing about the exact microstate.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Time, life, and every bit you store

Entropy is the only part of basic physics that tells the past from the future. It also explains what living things actually take from their food and from the Sun.

Chapter 07

The arrow of time needs a special beginning

The basic laws of motion work equally well forwards and backwards. A film of two billiard balls colliding looks fine in reverse. Yet the world as a whole clearly runs one way. The standard explanation is entropy: the future is the direction in which entropy increases.

But that raises a harder question. If high entropy is overwhelmingly likely, why was the entropy ever low? Statistics alone would predict that the past had higher entropy too. The answer most physicists accept is that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, and everything since has been the long slide away from it. The philosopher David Albert calls this assumption the past hypothesis. The early universe was hot and smooth, but for gravity a smooth spread of matter is the low-entropy, unusual state; clumping into stars and galaxies raises it.

Roger Penrose has estimated that a beginning this special is fantastically improbable, by a margin he puts at one chance in a number with more digits than there are particles in the observable universe. Nobody knows why the universe started that way. Every memory you have, every fossil and every footprint, exists because the universe started with low entropy and is still using it up. The Big Bang file covers the beginning itself.

Chapter 08

What the Sun really gives us

It is common to say that Earth runs on energy from the Sun. That is only half right. Earth radiates roughly as much energy back to space as it receives; otherwise it would keep heating up. What it keeps is not energy but low entropy.

Sunlight arrives from a surface at about 5,500 °C, as relatively few high-energy particles of light, or photons. Earth sends the same energy back out as infrared light from a much colder body, which takes roughly twenty times as many lower-energy photons. Many photons can be arranged in far more ways than few, so the outgoing light carries much more entropy. In between, plants, weather and every animal use that difference to build and maintain structure.

In his 1944 book What Is Life?, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger put it memorably: an organism feeds on "negative entropy". Food is valuable not for its calories alone but because its energy is concentrated and organised. Your body breaks it down into carbon dioxide, water and heat, which have far more entropy, and uses the difference to keep itself repaired.

Chapter 09

Entropy at work in everyday technology

Compression

Shannon's entropy sets the limit on how small a file can be squeezed without losing anything. Repetitive text has low entropy and compresses well. A file that is already random cannot be compressed at all.

Passwords

Security experts measure password strength in "bits of entropy": how many guesses an attacker would need. A long string of random words can beat a short password full of symbols, because what counts is the number of possibilities.

Chemistry

Whether a reaction happens by itself depends on both energy and entropy. Some reactions absorb heat and still run, such as instant cold packs dissolving, because the entropy gain is large enough.

The common thread is that entropy measures possibilities, whether they are molecular arrangements, messages or guesses. The chemical side is explored in Thermochemistry.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A supreme law, and a much-abused word

As a scientific idea, entropy is one of the most reliable ever found. As a metaphor, it is often used to say things it does not mean.

Chapter 10

Chapter 11

Three common misuses

"Evolution breaks the second law." This argument says that complex life could not arise from simpler forms because entropy must increase. It ignores the word "isolated". Earth is not isolated: it receives low-entropy sunlight and sends out high-entropy heat, as Chapter 08 showed. Local order can grow, and does, every time water freezes or a seed grows, as long as the total rises.

"Everything is falling apart, so effort is pointless." Entropy is sometimes used as a gloomy metaphor for society or relationships. As poetry that is fine, but it is not physics. The second law describes systems with no energy flowing in. A business, a garden or a friendship is kept in shape by effort, which is exactly what an open system with an energy supply can do.

"Entropy is a substance or a force." It is neither. It is a property of a state, like temperature or pressure, defined by counting. Nothing pushes systems toward high entropy; they simply wander into the region where nearly all the possibilities are.

Chapter 13

The information puzzle at the edge of physics

Black hole entropy created one of the most famous puzzles in modern physics. If a black hole evaporates into featureless heat, what happens to the information about everything that fell in? Quantum theory says information can never be truly destroyed. Gravity seemed to say it could. This is the black hole information paradox, explored further in Black holes.

Work since the 2010s suggests information does escape, encoded in the radiation, though how exactly is still debated. The larger lesson is that entropy, a quantity invented to improve steam engines, now sits at the centre of attempts to unite gravity and quantum theory. Some physicists suspect that space and time themselves may emerge from information and entanglement, with entropy as the bridge. That is a research programme, not an established result, but it shows how far Clausius's simple ratio has travelled.

The core idea

Entropy counts the microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. High-entropy states vastly outnumber low-entropy ones, so random change almost always increases entropy. That is why heat flows from hot to cold, why engines must waste heat, and why time seems to have a direction.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Boltzmann's formula: S = k log W, entropy from the number of ways.
  • Clausius named entropy in 1865.
  • Earth sends back about 20 infrared photons for each photon of sunlight it absorbs.
  • 100 tossed coins land between 40 and 60 heads about 96% of the time.
The common mistake

Equating entropy with visible mess. Entropy is about hidden possibilities, and things that look neater, like separated oil and water, can have higher entropy.

Test yourself

Can you explain why life does not break the second law, using the Sun's light and Earth's infrared glow? If you can, you have understood that entropy only has to rise in total, not everywhere.

The words, in plain words
Entropy
A measure of how many microscopic arrangements match what you see. More ways means more entropy.
Macrostate
The overall condition you can observe, such as temperature and pressure.
Microstate
The exact arrangement of every particle.
Second law
The total entropy of an isolated system never goes down.
Isolated system
One that exchanges neither energy nor matter with anything outside it.
Reversible process
An ideal change that creates no new entropy and could run backwards. Real processes only approach it.
Boltzmann's constant
The tiny number that converts a count of arrangements into units of entropy.
Photon
A single particle of light.
Maxwell's demon
An imagined being that sorts molecules, used to show the link between information and entropy.
Landauer's principle
Erasing information must release at least a minimum amount of heat.
Shannon entropy
A measure of how unpredictable a message is, and so how far it can be compressed.
Arrow of time
The difference between past and future, explained physically by rising entropy.
Heat death
A possible far-future state in which no usable energy differences remain.
