Waves. How energy and information travel without anything making the journey.

A ripple crosses a pond, but the water stays where it was. A voice crosses a room, but no air flies from the speaker's mouth to your ear. A wave is a pattern that moves while the material it moves through stays roughly in place. That one idea covers ripples, sound, light, radio, earthquakes, ocean swells and even, in quantum physics, matter itself. Better still, all these waves follow the same small set of rules. Learn how one kind reflects, bends, spreads and adds up, and you understand all of them. This file teaches those rules with everyday examples, then shows what they reveal about the Earth's core, tsunamis and the expanding universe.

Question 1 · What is it?

A travelling disturbance

A wave is a disturbance that moves from place to place, carrying energy, while the thing it disturbs only moves back and forth.

Chapter 01

The stadium wave

At a football match, the crowd sometimes makes a "Mexican wave". Each person stands up, raises their arms and sits down, just after the person beside them. Nobody walks around the stadium. Yet a clear shape races around the stands, often fast enough to circle the ground in under a minute.

That is a wave in its purest form. The pattern travels; the people do not. Each person only moves up and down, and passes the motion to a neighbour. A ripple on a pond works the same way. Drop a leaf on the water and watch it as a ripple passes. The leaf bobs up and down and stays roughly where it was. The water under it is doing the same. What moves outward is the disturbance, and with it, energy.

This explains how a wave can carry great power without carrying anything heavy. An ocean swell can travel thousands of kilometres across the Pacific and still break hard on a distant beach. The water that hits the beach did not come from the storm that made the swell. Only the energy did.

Chapter 02

The main kinds of wave

Waves can be sorted two ways: by what they travel through, and by the direction of the wobble.

KindWhat it meansExamples
Mechanical wavesNeed a material (a medium) to travel throughSound, water waves, earthquake waves, a wave on a rope
Electromagnetic wavesNeed no material; they cross empty spaceLight, radio, X-rays, microwaves
Transverse wavesThe wobble is at right angles to the direction of travelA shaken rope, light, the S waves of an earthquake
Longitudinal wavesThe wobble is along the direction of travel, as squeezes and stretchesSound in air, a pushed Slinky spring, P waves of an earthquake

Water waves are a mix. Each bit of water near the surface moves in a small circle, partly up and down and partly back and forth. That is why a swimmer beyond the breaking waves feels lifted and carried slightly forward and back, then returns to the same place.

Chapter 03

The words that describe every wave

   frequency = how many crests pass a point each second (hertz)

  • Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next.
  • Frequency is how many crests pass a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz). One hertz is one wave per second.
  • Period is the time between one crest and the next. It is one divided by the frequency.
  • Amplitude is the height of the wobble from the rest level. For sound it decides loudness; for light, brightness. The energy a wave carries grows with the square of its amplitude.

One equation connects them, and it holds for every wave: speed equals frequency times wavelength. If the speed is fixed by the material, then higher frequency must mean shorter wavelength. That is why a high-pitched sound has short waves and a low rumble has long ones, and why blue light has shorter waves than red.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Six behaviours shared by every wave

Waves of every kind reflect, bend, spread, combine, resonate and shift in pitch when their source moves. Once you know these six behaviours, a long list of everyday puzzles solves itself.

Chapter 04

What sets a wave's speed

The speed of a mechanical wave depends on the material, not on how hard you shake it. Two properties matter: how strongly the material springs back when disturbed (its stiffness or tension), and how heavy it is (its density). Stiff and light means fast. Floppy and heavy means slow.

Wave and mediumApproximate speed
Sound in air, at about 20 °C343 metres per second (about 1,235 km/h)
Sound in waterAbout 1,500 metres per second
Sound in steelAbout 5,000 to 6,000 metres per second
A tsunami in the deep open oceanRoughly 700 to 800 km/h, as fast as a jet airliner
Light in empty spaceAbout 300,000 kilometres per second

Water waves are an interesting case. Their speed depends on the depth of the water when the wavelength is long compared with the depth. That is why a tsunami, whose waves can be hundreds of kilometres long, races across a deep ocean and then slows sharply as the sea floor rises near a coast. More on that in Chapter 11.

Chapter 05

Reflection and refraction: bouncing and bending

When a wave hits a boundary, some of it bounces back. That is reflection. An echo is reflected sound; a mirror reflects light; a sea wall reflects waves back out to sea. Bats, dolphins, ships' sonar and medical ultrasound all send out waves and listen for the reflections to build a picture of what is there.

When a wave passes from one material into another where it travels at a different speed, it changes direction. That is refraction. The usual explanation is a line of soldiers marching from a road onto mud at an angle. The soldiers who reach the mud first slow down first, so the whole line swings round. A straw in a glass of water looks bent for this reason: light slows down in water.

Refraction explains a beach puzzle too. Waves far out at sea may approach the coast at an angle, yet they usually arrive almost parallel to the beach. The part of each wave in shallower water slows down first, and the wave swings round to face the shore. Refraction is just a change of speed seen from the side.

Chapter 06

Diffraction: why you can hear round corners but not see round them

When a wave passes through a gap or round an obstacle, it spreads out. That is diffraction. The effect is strongest when the gap or obstacle is about the same size as the wavelength.

That rule explains a familiar fact. You can hear someone talking in the next room through an open door, but you cannot see them. Sound waves in speech are tens of centimetres to a few metres long, similar to the size of a doorway, so they spread round it widely. Light waves are less than a thousandth of a millimetre long, far smaller than the door, so light goes almost straight through and casts sharp shadows.

Diffraction also sets a limit on how sharp any image can be. A telescope or microscope cannot see detail much smaller than the wavelength of the light it uses. That is why electron microscopes, which use electrons with much shorter wavelengths, can see far finer detail than light microscopes, and why chip factories use extreme ultraviolet light to print their tiniest features (Volume 12).

Chapter 07

Interference: waves that add and cancel

When two waves meet, they simply add together at every point. This rule is called superposition. Where two crests meet, they make a bigger crest. That is constructive interference. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel. That is destructive interference. Afterwards each wave carries on unchanged, as if the other had never been there.

Noise-cancelling headphones use destructive interference deliberately. A microphone listens to the noise outside, and a chip produces the exact opposite wave, crest for trough, so that the two cancel in your ear. It works best on steady low sounds such as engine hum, which are easy to predict.

Interference also settled a famous argument. Newton thought light was made of particles. In the early 1800s the English scientist Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw a pattern of bright and dark stripes on a screen behind them. Only waves do that: the stripes are where the waves from the two slits add and cancel. The rainbow colours on a soap bubble or an oil film on a puddle are also interference, between light reflected from the top and bottom of a very thin layer.

Chapter 08

Standing waves and resonance

Pluck a guitar string. Waves race to each end, reflect, and interfere with themselves. Only certain wavelengths fit neatly between the two fixed ends, and these build up into a standing wave that seems to vibrate in place without travelling. The longest one that fits gives the note you hear. Press the string against a fret and you shorten it, so a shorter wave fits and the note goes up. Every musical instrument works on this principle, as Sound explains.

Everything has natural frequencies at which it likes to vibrate. Push a child on a swing at just the right moment each time and the swing goes higher and higher with small pushes. That is resonance: a small, well-timed input building a large response. A singer who shatters a wine glass with a note is using the same effect, though it needs a very loud, very precise note and is rarely done.

Resonance can be a danger in engineering. When London's Millennium Bridge opened in 2000, it began to sway sideways as crowds crossed. Walkers instinctively fell into step with the sway, which pushed it further. The bridge closed for nearly two years while dampers were fitted. The famous collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the United States in 1940 is often described as simple resonance, but engineers now explain it as a related effect, aeroelastic flutter, in which the wind and the twisting deck fed energy into each other.

Chapter 09

The Doppler effect: when the source moves

Stand by a road as an ambulance passes. Its siren sounds higher as it approaches and drops the moment it passes. The siren has not changed. As the ambulance comes towards you, each new wave starts a little closer than the last, so the crests arrive bunched up: higher frequency, higher pitch. As it moves away, the crests are stretched out: lower pitch. This was explained by the Austrian physicist Christian Doppler in 1842, and it is called the Doppler effect.

It turns out to be one of the most useful effects in science. Police speed guns and weather radar measure the shift in reflected microwaves to find how fast a car or a raincloud is moving. Doctors use Doppler ultrasound to measure blood flow. Astronomers measure the shift in light from stars and galaxies, which shows that distant galaxies are moving away from us, the evidence that the universe is expanding (Volume 29, The Big Bang).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Seeing inside the Earth, surviving the sea, and carrying every message

Waves are how we learned what is under our feet, how disasters spread, and how all information travels.

Chapter 10

Earthquake waves and the hidden core

An earthquake sends out several kinds of wave. The fastest are P waves ("primary"), which are longitudinal, squeezing and stretching the rock. Next come S waves ("secondary"), which are transverse, shaking the rock sideways. Last, and usually most damaging, come surface waves that roll along the ground.

The difference between P and S waves revealed the inside of the Earth. S waves cannot travel through liquids, because a liquid does not spring back when you try to shear it sideways. Seismologists in the early twentieth century noticed that S waves never arrive on the far side of the Earth from a big earthquake. There is a shadow. The only explanation was a liquid layer deep inside: the Earth's outer core. In 1936 the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann used faint P-wave reflections to show that inside the liquid outer core there is a solid inner core. No one has ever been anywhere near it; we know it is there because of the way waves bend and bounce.

The speed difference also saves lives. P waves are fast and fairly gentle. Earthquake early warning systems, such as Japan's, detect them near the source and send alerts before the slower, damaging waves arrive. The warning is only seconds to tens of seconds, but it is enough to slow trains, stop surgery and get under a table.

Chapter 11

Tsunamis: a wave you barely notice until it arrives

A tsunami is usually started by an undersea earthquake that suddenly lifts or drops a large area of the sea floor. The whole column of water above it moves, creating a wave with a wavelength of up to hundreds of kilometres. In the deep ocean its height may be under a metre. A ship at sea might not notice it pass.

As it nears land, the water gets shallower, the wave slows, and the energy that was spread through kilometres of depth is squeezed into a much smaller space. The wave grows taller, sometimes to ten metres or more. Because the wavelength is so long, it does not break and retreat like a normal wave. It arrives as a rising flood that keeps coming for many minutes. Often the sea first draws back from the shore, exposing the sea floor; that is a warning sign to move to high ground immediately. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami killed around 230,000 people in fourteen countries, a story told in Volume 23.

Chapter 12

Every message is a wave

Information travels as waves almost everywhere. Speech is sound waves. Radio, mobile phones and Wi-Fi use electromagnetic waves. The internet's undersea cables carry pulses of light (Volume 12). To carry a message, a sender changes, or modulates, a steady wave: its strength (amplitude modulation, AM), its frequency (frequency modulation, FM), or, in modern digital systems, combinations of amplitude and timing that encode many bits at once.

Waves go deeper still. In 1924 the French physicist Louis de Broglie proposed that particles such as electrons also behave like waves. Experiments soon confirmed it: electrons fired at a crystal produce interference patterns. This is a foundation of quantum mechanics, introduced in Volume 25, Why the quantum world is strange. At the smallest scales, everything that exists has a wave nature.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What waves make possible, and what they cost

Waves are a gift to technology and a hazard in nature. The same behaviours that make them useful make them dangerous.

Rogue waves deserve a word because they were once dismissed as sailors' tales. On 1 January 1995 instruments on the Draupner oil platform in the North Sea measured a single wave about 26 metres high, far taller than the waves around it. It was the first such measurement widely accepted, and it changed how ships and offshore structures are designed.

Chapter 14

The strength and limit of wave thinking

The great advantage of the wave idea is that it is universal. The same mathematics describes a guitar string, a radio antenna and an electron in an atom. An engineer who understands one kind of wave can transfer that understanding to others, and many discoveries came from exactly that kind of analogy.

The limit is that waves are not the whole story. Light also arrives in lumps, called photons, and electrons also hit a detector at single points. Neither "wave" nor "particle" fully describes the small world; each picture works in its own situations. Learning which picture to use, and when, is part of becoming fluent in physics.

The core idea

A wave is a travelling disturbance that carries energy, not matter. Every wave has a wavelength, frequency and amplitude, with speed = frequency × wavelength. All waves reflect, refract, diffract, interfere, resonate and show the Doppler effect.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Sound in air: about 343 m/s; light: about 300,000 km/s, nearly a million times faster.
  • A tsunami crosses the deep ocean at about 700–800 km/h.
  • A wave's energy grows with the square of its amplitude.
The common mistake

Thinking a wave carries the water, air or rope along with it. The medium wobbles in place; only the pattern and the energy travel.

Test yourself

Can you explain why you can hear a person through an open doorway but not see them, using wavelength and diffraction? If you can, you understand the most important rule of how waves spread.
