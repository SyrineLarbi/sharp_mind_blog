The Standard Model. The most precisely tested theory in science, and physicists know it is incomplete.

The Standard Model is the name for the theory that describes every known fundamental particle and three of the four forces of nature. It was assembled in the 1960s and 1970s, and since then it has passed essentially every test thrown at it, sometimes to ten decimal places. It predicted particles before anyone found them, including the Higgs boson. It explains why the Sun shines and why atoms hold together. Yet it has nothing to say about gravity, dark matter or dark energy, and it depends on many numbers that have to be measured rather than explained. It is a triumph and an unfinished draft at once.

Question 1 · What is it?

Seventeen particles and three forces

The Standard Model is a single theoretical framework that lists the basic particles, describes how they interact, and predicts the results of experiments in great detail.

Chapter 01

A modest name for a huge achievement

The name sounds like an industrial specification, and that is partly how it came about. By the mid 1970s, several separate theories had been joined into one consistent framework that matched the experiments. Physicists started calling it "the standard model" to mean "the model everyone now works with". The name stuck long after it had become the most successful theory in the history of physics.

It was the work of many people over about twenty-five years, not one genius. Its pieces earned a long list of Nobel Prizes: for the theory of light and electrons in 1965, for joining the electromagnetic and weak forces in 1979, for the theory of the strong force in 2004, and for the Higgs mechanism in 2013. It is less a single discovery than a cathedral built by a whole generation.

Its scope is precise. It describes the fundamental particles and the electromagnetic, strong and weak forces. It does not include gravity, which is described by Einstein's general relativity. Since gravity is by far the weakest force between individual particles, the Standard Model can ignore it in almost every laboratory experiment.

Chapter 02

The particle table

The Standard Model's particles fall into two broad kinds. Fermions are the particles of matter. They obey a rule that no two identical ones can be in the same state, which is why matter takes up space and atoms have structure. Bosons carry forces, and many identical ones can pile into the same state, which is what makes a laser beam possible.

Counting the twelve fermions, the photon, the gluon, the W, the Z and the Higgs gives the seventeen particles usually quoted. Each fermion also has an antiparticle. The numbers are conventions, but the content is not: everything you have ever touched is built from just three of them, the up quark, the down quark and the electron.

Chapter 03

Three copies of everything

The table has a puzzling pattern. The second and third columns are copies of the first. The muon is an electron about 200 times heavier. The tau is heavier still. The top quark is a heavier version of the up quark, weighing about as much as an entire atom of gold. These heavier copies decay quickly into the lighter ones, which is why ordinary matter contains only the first generation.

Experiments at CERN in 1989 showed there are exactly three generations of the light neutrinos, and probably exactly three generations in all. Why nature made three copies, and why their masses are so different, the Standard Model does not say. It simply takes the masses as measured inputs. That is one of the clearest signs it is not the final theory.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Fields, exchanges and symmetry

The Standard Model is a quantum field theory. Its central ideas are that every particle is a ripple in a field, and that forces arise from symmetries those fields must respect.

Chapter 04

Everything is a field, and particles are its ripples

The deepest shift in the Standard Model is to stop thinking of particles as tiny balls. Instead, all of space is filled with fields, one for each kind of particle: an electron field, an up-quark field, a photon field and so on. A field is something that has a value at every point, like the temperature across a room. A particle is a small, localised ripple in its field, carrying a fixed amount of energy. This is why every electron in the universe is perfectly identical: they are all ripples in the same field.

Forces are then interactions between fields. When two electrons repel, the theory describes it as the electron field and the photon field exchanging energy. In the popular picture, the electrons "throw" a photon between them. Richard Feynman invented simple diagrams to calculate such exchanges, now used by every particle physicist.

The first part of the Standard Model to be completed was quantum electrodynamics (QED), the theory of light and charged particles, developed in the late 1940s by Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, building on work by Freeman Dyson and others. Its predictions for the magnetism of the electron agree with experiment to better than one part in a billion.

Chapter 05

Symmetry: the rule that writes the forces

Physicists use "symmetry" to mean something you can change without changing the outcome. A round plate looks the same however you turn it. In 1918 the German mathematician Emmy Noether proved a remarkable theorem: every continuous symmetry of the laws of physics comes with a conserved quantity. Laws that are the same today as tomorrow give conservation of energy. Laws that are the same here as over there give conservation of momentum.

The Standard Model uses a more powerful kind, called gauge symmetry. Roughly, it says that certain hidden features of a field can be adjusted differently at every point in space without changing anything physical. That turns out to be impossible unless another field exists to "compensate" for the local adjustments. That compensating field is a force-carrier. Demand one kind of gauge symmetry and you get the photon and electromagnetism. Demand others and you get the gluons and the W and Z.

This is the elegant core of the theory. The forces are not added by hand. They follow from the symmetries. The physicist's shorthand for the whole model is a list of three symmetry groups, written SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), corresponding to the strong force and the two parts of the electroweak force.

Chapter 06

The strong force: why quarks are never alone

The strong force binds quarks into protons and neutrons, and its leftover effect binds protons and neutrons into nuclei. Its theory is called quantum chromodynamics (QCD), from the Greek for "colour". Quarks carry a kind of charge called "colour", which has three values nicknamed red, green and blue. They have nothing to do with real colours; the name reflects the rule that quarks combine into "colourless" groups, as red, green and blue light combine into white.

The strong force behaves unlike any other. Between two quarks very close together, it is weak. Pull them apart and it grows stronger, like a rubber band, rather than weaker. This was explained in 1973 by David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer, and is called asymptotic freedom. If you pull hard enough, the energy in the "band" creates a new quark–antiquark pair, and you end up with two bound groups instead of a free quark. No one has ever isolated a single quark, and the theory says no one ever will. This is called confinement.

One consequence surprises almost everyone. The three quarks in a proton account for only about one per cent of its mass. The rest is the energy of the strong force field binding them, converted into mass through E = mc2. Most of your own weight is, in this sense, the energy of gluons.

Chapter 07

The weak force: the force that changes identity

The weak force does something the other forces cannot: it changes one kind of particle into another. It can turn a down quark into an up quark, which turns a neutron into a proton, releasing an electron and an antineutrino. That is beta decay, a common type of radioactivity. It is weak not because its basic strength is small, but because its carriers, the W and Z, are so heavy that they can only be exchanged over tiny distances.

In the 1960s Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg and Abdus Salam showed that the electromagnetic and weak forces are two faces of a single electroweak force. At the very high temperatures of the early universe they would have been the same. The theory predicted the W and Z particles, with specific masses. In 1983, experiments at CERN found them, at the predicted masses. Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer received the Nobel Prize the following year.

Chapter 08

The Higgs field: where the masses come from

The electroweak theory had a serious problem. Its symmetry required the W and Z to be massless, like the photon, but they are heavy. The same symmetry also forbade electrons and quarks from having mass directly. In 1964 several theorists, including Peter Higgs, François Englert and Robert Brout, found a way out. Suppose a new field fills all of space and, unlike other fields, has a non-zero value everywhere, even in empty space.

Particles that interact with this Higgs field behave as if they have mass; the stronger the interaction, the heavier the particle. The photon does not interact with it and stays massless. The underlying equations keep their symmetry, but the state of the universe does not: this is called spontaneous symmetry breaking. A pencil balanced on its tip is a common picture. The situation is symmetric, but the pencil must fall in some direction.

The theory predicted a particle, a ripple in the Higgs field, and that particle was found at CERN in 2012, as the Particle physics file tells. Note the limit: the Higgs gives mass to the fundamental particles, but most of the mass of ordinary matter comes from the strong force, as Chapter 06 explained.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A theory that predicted its own evidence

The Standard Model matters because it underlies chemistry, stars and technology, and because it showed that abstract principles can predict new things in nature.

Chapter 09

The physics under everything else

In principle, the Standard Model plus gravity underlies all of chemistry, biology and everyday physics. Electromagnetism, through QED, explains atoms, light, chemical bonds and every material property. The strong force explains why nuclei hold together and how much energy fission and fusion release. The weak force is essential to the Sun. The first step of the fusion chain that powers the Sun turns a proton into a neutron, which only the weak force can do.

That step is so slow that a typical proton in the Sun's core waits billions of years before it fuses. If the weak force were much stronger, the Sun would burn through its fuel far too fast for life to evolve. In practice, nobody calculates biology from quarks. But knowing that the foundations are secure is part of why the rest of science can build on them.

That is the strongest kind of evidence a scientific theory can have, and it is why physicists trust the Standard Model within its range even while hunting for what lies beyond it.

Chapter 12

The hierarchy problem and the question of naturalness

One problem bothers theorists more than the others. Quantum effects should push the Higgs boson's mass up towards the highest energy scales in physics, such as the scale where gravity becomes strong, which is about a hundred million billion times higher. Yet the measured mass is small. Keeping it small seems to require an extraordinarily precise cancellation between unrelated quantities. This is the hierarchy problem.

For decades, many physicists expected the LHC to find new particles, most popularly those predicted by supersymmetry, which would cancel those quantum effects naturally. As of 2025 it has found none. Some see this as a sign the idea of "naturalness" was a poor guide. Others argue the new particles are simply heavier than hoped. This is a live disagreement among experts, and it shapes arguments about which experiments to build next.

Chapter 14

A map that is accurate, but not the whole territory

Most physicists now see the Standard Model as an effective theory: a description that is extremely accurate within a certain range of energies, the way Newton's laws are extremely accurate for bridges and planets but fail near the speed of light. Newton was not wrong; his theory turned out to be a very good approximation of a deeper one. The Standard Model is expected to be the same.

Where the deeper theory begins is unknown. It could be just beyond the LHC's reach, or at energies no machine will ever touch, revealed only through tiny effects in precise measurements, through cosmology, or through dark matter. The Standard Model is the best map humanity has ever drawn of the physical world, and its blank edges are clearly marked. Knowing exactly where our knowledge stops is itself one of its achievements.

The core idea

The Standard Model describes seventeen fundamental particles and the electromagnetic, strong and weak forces. Particles are ripples in fields, forces come from gauge symmetries, and the Higgs field gives the fundamental particles their mass. It has predicted many particles before their discovery, but it leaves out gravity, dark matter and dark energy.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 17 particles: 12 fermions, 4 kinds of force carrier, and the Higgs.
  • 3 generations of matter; ordinary matter uses only the first.
  • Quarks give only about 1% of a proton's mass; the rest is strong-force energy.
  • About 19 free parameters, more with neutrino masses.
The common mistake

Saying "the Higgs gives everything its mass". It gives mass to the fundamental particles; most of the mass of you comes from the strong force inside protons and neutrons.

Can you list three things the Standard Model does not explain, and say why physicists still trust it? If you can, you understand both its success and its limits.

The words, in plain words
Standard Model
The theory of the fundamental particles and the electromagnetic, strong and weak forces.
Fermion
A matter particle, such as a quark or electron. No two identical ones can share the same state.
Boson
A particle such as a photon or gluon that carries a force; many can share a state.
Generation
One of three sets of matter particles, each a heavier copy of the one before.
Quantum field theory
The framework in which particles are ripples in fields filling all of space.
Quantum electrodynamics (QED)
The theory of light and charged particles.
Quantum chromodynamics (QCD)
The theory of the strong force between quarks and gluons.
Colour charge
The strong-force version of electric charge, with three values.
Confinement
The rule that quarks are always bound in groups and never found alone.
Electroweak force
The single force that electromagnetism and the weak force come from.
Gauge symmetry
A kind of symmetry that, when required, produces the force-carrying fields.
Higgs field
A field filling space whose interaction with particles gives them mass.
Spontaneous symmetry breaking
When the laws are symmetric but the actual state of things is not.
Effective theory
A theory that is accurate within a range of conditions but not beyond it.
