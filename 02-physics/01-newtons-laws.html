Newton's Laws. Four short rules that still fly every spacecraft we launch.

In 1687 Isaac Newton published a book that explained, with a few rules, why an apple falls, why the tides rise, why a cannonball curves and why the planets go round the Sun. Nobody had ever explained the sky and the ground with the same rules before. Three of those rules describe motion, and one describes gravity. More than three centuries later we know they are not the final truth. They fail for things that are very fast, very small or very heavy. But for almost everything a human being builds, drives, throws or launches, they are still the rules engineers actually use. This file explains them properly: what they say, why they work, and where they stop working.

Question 1 · What is it?

A small set of rules for how things move

Newton's laws are not about particular objects. They are about the relationship between force and motion, and they apply to a football, a ship and a planet in the same way.

Chapter 01

The book that joined heaven and Earth

Newton's book is usually called the Principia, short for its Latin title, which means "The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy". It appeared in 1687. Before it, scholars generally believed that the heavens followed one set of rules and the Earth another. Planets moved in perfect circles because that was their nature. Things on Earth fell because that was theirs.

Newton built on work by others. Galileo Galilei had studied balls rolling down slopes and realised that motion continues unless something stops it. Johannes Kepler had found three precise patterns in the orbits of the planets, but could not say why they held. Newton showed that a single set of laws of motion, plus one law of gravity, produced Kepler's patterns and Galileo's falling balls at the same time. That was the revolution. Physics became one subject, everywhere.

Newton himself was generous about this in one famous line. In a 1676 letter to the scientist Robert Hooke he wrote that if he had seen further, it was "by standing on the shoulders of giants". (The two men were rivals, and historians still argue about how sincere he was being.)

Two words need care. Force is a push or a pull. Acceleration in physics means any change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. A car taking a corner at a steady 50 km/h is accelerating, because its direction is changing. That single definition removes a lot of confusion later.

Chapter 03

Mass, weight and the newton

Everyday language mixes up mass and weight. Physics separates them. Mass is how much matter something has, and also how hard it is to get it moving. It is measured in kilograms and it is the same on Earth, on the Moon and in deep space. Weight is the force of gravity pulling on that mass. It changes from place to place. An astronaut with a mass of 80 kg still has a mass of 80 kg on the Moon, but weighs only about a sixth as much there.

Force is measured in a unit named after Newton, the newton (N). One newton is the force that makes one kilogram accelerate by one metre per second, every second. That sounds abstract, so here is a handle: the weight of a small apple, about 100 grams, is roughly one newton. A person of 70 kg weighs about 690 newtons on Earth. Bathroom scales show kilograms because they assume you are on Earth and quietly divide by the strength of gravity.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Each law, taken apart

The laws look simple. Each one hides an idea that took people a long time to accept, and each one is easy to misuse.

Chapter 04

The first law: motion is the normal state

Galileo's argument for the first law is a beautiful piece of reasoning. Roll a ball down one ramp and up another. It rises to almost the same height it started from. Make the second ramp gentler and the ball travels further to reach that height. Now imagine the second ramp is perfectly flat. The ball can never reach its starting height, so it should roll forever. Friction spoils the real experiment, but the pattern points to a world where motion needs no engine to keep going.

Space shows this directly. The Voyager 1 probe, launched in 1977, has been coasting for decades with its engines essentially idle. It keeps moving at about 17 kilometres per second because nothing out there slows it down. The same law explains why a loose object in a braking car flies forward, and why a spacecraft must fire an engine to slow down, not just to speed up.

The first law also defines the setting in which the other laws hold. Physicists call it an inertial frame: a point of view that is not itself accelerating. Inside a train moving smoothly at constant speed, a dropped coin falls straight down, exactly as on the platform. Inside a train that is braking hard, the coin seems to drift forward with no force behind it. That "drift" is not a real force. It is what Newton's laws look like from a point of view that is itself changing speed.

Chapter 05

The second law: force, mass and acceleration

The second law is the working engine of mechanics. It says force equals mass times acceleration, F = m × a. Read it in three directions and it answers three kinds of question.

How hard must I push?

A car of 1,000 kg that you want to accelerate at 2 metres per second, per second, needs a net force of 2,000 newtons from its wheels.

How fast will it speed up?

The same 2,000 N on a 2,000 kg van gives only half the acceleration. Twice the mass, half the response.

How heavy is it?

Measure the acceleration a known force produces, and you have measured mass. Astronauts on the space station weigh themselves this way, on a spring-mounted chair.

The key word is net. What matters is the total of all forces added together, with their directions. A car cruising at a steady 100 km/h on a motorway still has its engine pushing hard. But air resistance and road friction push back by exactly the same amount. The net force is zero, so the acceleration is zero, and the speed stays the same. That is the first law again, seen from inside the second.

Newton actually wrote the law in terms of momentum, which is mass times velocity. Force is the rate at which momentum changes. This version is more general. It works for a rocket that is losing mass as it burns fuel, where "mass times acceleration" alone would give the wrong answer.

Chapter 06

The third law: forces come in pairs

The third law says that forces always come in pairs. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you just as hard. The secret to understanding it is this: the two forces act on different objects. One acts on the wall, the other on you. They never cancel each other, because they are not acting on the same thing.

That clears up a classic puzzle. If a horse pulls a cart, and the cart pulls back on the horse equally, how does anything move? Answer: the horse moves because the ground pushes it forward. Its hooves push backwards on the ground, and by the third law the ground pushes forward on the horse. If that forward push is bigger than the cart's backward pull, the horse accelerates. On ice, with no grip, the horse goes nowhere.

You walk the same way. Each step pushes the Earth backwards a tiny amount, and the Earth pushes you forwards. A rocket works the same way without any ground. It throws hot gas backwards at enormous speed, and the gas pushes the rocket forwards. This is why rockets work in the vacuum of space, a point that some critics doubted in the early twentieth century. In 1920 a New York Times editorial mocked the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard for believing a rocket could work in a vacuum. The paper published a correction in July 1969, as Apollo 11 flew to the Moon.

Chapter 07

Gravity: the inverse-square law

Newton's law of gravity says every mass pulls on every other mass. Double one of the masses and the pull doubles. Double the distance between them and the pull falls to a quarter. Triple the distance and it falls to a ninth. This is called an inverse-square law, and it appears again and again in physics, for light and for electric charge as well.

Newton tested it with the Moon. The Moon is about 60 times further from the centre of the Earth than you are. If gravity weakens with the square of distance, the Moon should feel a pull 60 × 60 = 3,600 times weaker than an apple does. Newton calculated how fast the Moon must be falling towards Earth to stay in its curved path. It matched that prediction closely. The same force that drops an apple, weakened by distance, steers the Moon.

Newton drew a picture like this himself. Fire a cannonball from a very tall mountain. It curves down and lands. Fire it faster and it lands further away. Fire it at about 8 kilometres per second near the Earth's surface, ignoring air, and the Earth's curve drops away beneath it exactly as fast as it falls. It is in orbit. The International Space Station moves at about 7.7 km/s and circles the Earth roughly every 90 minutes. The astronauts float not because gravity is absent (at that height it is still about 90 percent as strong) but because they and the station are falling together.

The strength of gravity is set by a number called the gravitational constant, G. It is tiny, which is why two people standing next to each other feel no pull at all. It was first measured in a laboratory in 1798 by Henry Cavendish, using lead balls on a sensitive twisting balance. That experiment is often described as "weighing the Earth", because once G is known, the Earth's mass follows.

Newton knew his law described gravity without explaining it. How could the Sun pull on the Earth across empty space with no contact? In the second edition of the Principia (1713) he wrote that he would "frame no hypotheses" about the cause. The answer would wait for Einstein, covered in Volume 25, Gravity is not a force.

Chapter 08

Friction: why the world looked Aristotelian

If the first law is true, why does everything on Earth stop? Because of friction, the force that resists one surface sliding over another, and its cousin, air resistance. Friction comes from tiny bumps and sticky contacts between surfaces. It always pushes against the direction of sliding.

There are two kinds that matter in daily life. Static friction holds things still: it is why a box on a slope does not slide, and why your shoes grip. Kinetic friction acts once something is sliding, and it is usually smaller. That is why a heavy wardrobe is hardest to move at the start, and suddenly easier once it begins to slide. It is also why anti-lock brakes matter. A tyre that is still rolling grips with static friction. A locked, skidding tyre has only the weaker kinetic friction, so it stops the car less well and cannot steer.

Friction is not a nuisance to be removed everywhere. Without it you could not walk, a car could not move, a nail would not hold and a knot would not stay tied. Engineers spend as much effort adding friction (brakes, tyres, climbing shoes) as removing it (bearings, lubricants).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Safety, spaceflight and everything engineers build

Newton's laws are the most used physics in the world. They decide how cars are designed, how bridges stand, and how probes reach other planets.

Chapter 09

Crashes, crumple zones and the time it takes to stop

The second law, written with momentum, gives one of the most useful ideas in safety engineering. To stop a moving body you must remove its momentum. The force needed depends on how quickly you do it. Remove the same momentum over a longer time and the force is smaller. The product of force and time is called impulse.

A rough, illustrative calculation shows how big the difference is. A 70 kg person in a car at 50 km/h (about 14 metres per second) carries roughly 980 units of momentum. Stop them in a tenth of a second and the average force is about 9,800 newtons, around fourteen times their own weight. Stop them in a hundredth of a second, as a rigid dashboard would, and the force is ten times larger again. That gap is often the difference between bruises and fatal injury.

Almost every car safety feature is a way to stretch that stopping time or spread the force. Crumple zones are parts of the car designed to fold in a controlled way, so the cabin decelerates over a longer distance. Seat belts stretch slightly and apply the force to the strong bones of the hips and chest. Airbags give the head a soft surface that slows it over centimetres instead of millimetres. Bicycle helmets use foam that crushes. Gymnasts land on mats and bend their knees for the same reason.

Chapter 10

A planet found with a pencil

The most dramatic test of Newton's gravity came in 1846. Astronomers had noticed that the planet Uranus was not quite following the path Newton's laws predicted. Either the laws were wrong, or something unseen was pulling on it. The French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier assumed the laws were right and calculated where an unknown planet would have to be. He sent his prediction to the astronomer Johann Galle in Berlin, who found Neptune within about a degree of the predicted spot on the first night of searching. (The English mathematician John Couch Adams had made a similar calculation, and credit was disputed at the time.)

Spaceflight is Newton's laws in action every day. The paths of the Apollo missions to the Moon were calculated almost entirely with Newtonian mechanics. Probes like Voyager use a gravity assist: they swing close past a planet and steal a little of its orbital momentum, which bends their path and boosts their speed without fuel. By the third law the planet slows down too, by an amount far too small to measure.

Chapter 11

Why buildings stand up

A great deal of engineering is the study of objects that do not move at all. If something is still and staying still, the second law says the net force on it is zero. This branch is called statics. Every beam in a bridge, every floor of a tower and every shelf bracket is designed by adding up the forces on each part and making sure they balance, with a safety margin for wind, crowds and earthquakes.

The third law matters here too. A shelf pushes down on its brackets, so the brackets push up on the shelf. The brackets push on the wall, so the wall pushes back. Trace the forces all the way to the ground and you have the logic of a structural calculation. The same thinking runs structural engineering and mechanical engineering.

A habit of mind

Newton's laws also teach a way of thinking. When something changes, look for the cause. When nothing changes, look for the balance. If a moving thing slows down, something is pushing on it. If a heavy thing stays up, something is holding it with an equal force. That habit works in physics and is a useful reflex well beyond it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant approximation with clear edges

Newton's laws are not perfectly true. They are extremely accurate inside a wide range and fail outside it. Knowing where the edges are is part of understanding them.

The Mercury case is a lovely story because it rhymes with Neptune. Le Verrier, the man who found Neptune, also noticed that Mercury's orbit slowly shifted in a way Newton could not quite explain. He suggested another hidden planet, which was even given a name, Vulcan. It does not exist. This time the laws, not the solar system, were incomplete. Einstein's general relativity predicted the missing shift exactly, and that success was one of the first signs his theory was right.

Chapter 13

Why a "wrong" theory is still taught first

If Einstein replaced Newton, why does every engineer still use Newton? Because the newer theories include Newton as a special case. When speeds are small compared with light and gravity is weak, the equations of relativity become Newton's equations, almost exactly. Newton is not overturned; he is fenced in. Inside the fence he is as good as anything we have, and far simpler to calculate with.

There are places where the fence matters in daily life. Satellite navigation, for example, needs relativistic corrections to the satellites' clocks or positions would drift by kilometres a day. That story is told in Volume 12, GPS and why it is really a clock. But the orbits of those same satellites are still calculated, at heart, with Newton.

What the laws do well

Simple to state and to calculate. Universal across scales from a marble to a moon. Predictive enough to find a planet. A sound base for all of engineering. A model of how science should work: few assumptions, many testable consequences.

What they get wrong or leave out

No speed limit. No explanation of what gravity is. Instant action across any distance. Nothing to say about atoms, light or heat on their own. And they invite a clockwork picture of the universe that quantum physics later undermined.

Chapter 15

The stubborn intuition in every head

The biggest open problem with Newton's laws may be in classrooms, not laboratories. Research on physics teaching has shown again and again that students who can solve F = m × a problems on paper still carry Aristotle's intuition underneath. Asked what force keeps a thrown ball moving upwards, many say "the force of the throw", which no longer exists once the ball leaves the hand. A widely used test of these ideas, the Force Concept Inventory, published in 1992, found that traditional lectures changed these intuitions surprisingly little.

Teaching methods that make students predict, argue and then test do much better. That matters beyond physics. The first law is a direct challenge to what your eyes tell you, and learning to trust a well-tested rule over a strong first impression is a skill worth having. Simulations, cheap motion sensors and virtual labs are making that kind of teaching easier, and it is likely to spread.

Newton's laws will also keep running in places most people never notice: the software that keeps a self-driving car on the road, the controllers that balance a drone, the engines that make objects in a film fall convincingly. The future of Newton is mostly invisible, and enormous.

The core idea

Forces change motion; they are not needed to keep it. The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Forces always come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects. And every mass attracts every other with a pull that weakens with the square of the distance.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The Principia appeared in 1687.
  • A small apple weighs about 1 newton; gravity at Earth's surface gives about 9.8 m/s² of acceleration.
  • Low Earth orbit needs about 8 km/s; the space station circles in about 90 minutes.
The common mistake

Thinking action and reaction cancel. They act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. What decides motion is the total force on one object.

Test yourself

Can you explain why astronauts float on the space station even though gravity there is still about 90 percent as strong as on the ground? If you can, you understand gravity, orbits and the first law together.

The words, in plain words
Force
A push or a pull, measured in newtons.
Net force
All the forces on one object added together, taking their directions into account.
Mass
How much matter an object has, and how hard it is to change its motion. Measured in kilograms.
Weight
The force of gravity on a mass. It changes from planet to planet; mass does not.
Velocity
Speed together with direction.
Acceleration
Any change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down or turning.
Inertia
The tendency of an object to keep doing what it is already doing.
Inertial frame
A point of view that is not itself accelerating, where Newton's laws hold without extra terms.
Momentum
Mass times velocity. Force is the rate at which it changes.
Impulse
Force multiplied by the time it acts. Stretching the time lowers the force.
Friction
The force that resists one surface sliding over another.
Inverse-square law
A rule where an effect falls with the square of distance: twice as far, a quarter as strong.
Orbit
A path where an object falls towards a body but moves sideways fast enough to keep missing it.
Say this · five sentences you can use
  1. "Things don't need a force to keep moving. They need a force to change how they move."
  2. "An orbit is just falling while moving sideways so fast that you keep missing the ground."
  3. "Crumple zones and seat belts work by making the stop take longer, because a slower stop means a smaller force."
  4. "Neptune was found on paper before anyone saw it, because Uranus wasn't moving the way Newton said it should."
  5. "Einstein didn't throw Newton out. He showed where Newton's laws stop working, and inside that range they're still what engineers use."
