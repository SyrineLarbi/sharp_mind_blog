How the extracts work

Where a book is in the public domain, the passages are the real thing, at length. Where it is still in copyright, you get a short quoted line and a description of what the passage does — which is the honest limit of what I can reproduce, and is marked as such.

The verdict chips: read it whole the book earns its length · read this part one great section inside a long book · know of it worth understanding, not worth the hours.

Part I

The Russians

Book 01

Anna Karenina

Leo Tolstoy · 1878 · ~850 pages · public domain

read it whole

Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.

Tolstoy · opening line

The most famous opening in fiction, and almost everyone misremembers what it is for. It is not a sentimental observation. It is a thesis about causation, and the eight hundred pages that follow are the demonstration: happiness requires many things to go right at once, and there are countless ways for one of them to fail. The claim is close to what the disasters volume said about complex systems.

But the passage worth carrying is elsewhere. Levin, having spent the book searching for meaning in philosophy and finding nothing, is mowing a field with his peasants and discovers that he has stopped thinking entirely:

The longer Levin mowed, the oftener he experienced those moments of oblivion when his arms no longer seemed to swing the scythe, but the scythe itself his whole body, so conscious and full of life; and as if by magic, regularly and definitely without a thought being given to it, the work accomplished itself of its own accord. These were the most blessed moments.

Anna Karenina · Part III, chapter 5

That is the answer the novel gives to the question the novel is asking, and it is given in an agricultural scene rather than in an argument — which is what novels can do that essays cannot. Levin finds meaning by ceasing to look for it. Anna, pursuing fulfilment directly and intelligently, is destroyed. The book puts the two beside each other and declines to explain.

Why read it: it is the most psychologically accurate novel ever written, and unusually readable for its length — a social novel with real gossip, real money trouble and real marriages. If you read one very long novel in your life, this is the one that repays it.

Book 02

Crime and Punishment

Fyodor Dostoevsky · 1866 · ~550 pages · public domain

read it whole

A poor student convinces himself, by argument, that extraordinary people are entitled to step over ordinary morality for a greater end — and murders a pawnbroker to prove he is one of them. What follows is not a detective story. It is the argument failing, from the inside, over four hundred pages.

“I didn't kill a human being, but a principle! I killed a principle, but as for surmounting the barriers, I did not surmount them, I remained on this side… All I managed to do was kill. And I didn't even manage that, as it turns out.”

Crime and Punishment · Raskolnikov, Part III

This is Volume 37 in fictional form and it arrived seventy years before the psychology did. Raskolnikov does exactly what the dark side volume described: he renames the deed, he compares himself favourably, he makes the victim into a principle rather than a person. And Dostoevsky's point is that the machinery does not hold — the self-justification cracks not because he is caught but because it was always a construction.

Why read it: it is a thriller that happens to be one of the great works of moral philosophy, it moves quickly, and it is the best account anyone has written of what it feels like to reason yourself into something terrible.

Book 03

The Brothers Karamazov

Fyodor Dostoevsky · 1880 · ~800 pages · public domain

read this part

An enormous, sprawling, often exhausting novel containing perhaps the single greatest chapter in European fiction — and you can read that chapter on its own. In The Grand Inquisitor, Ivan tells his brother a story: Christ returns to sixteenth-century Seville and is arrested by the Church, which explains to him why he must not interfere.

“Nothing has ever been more insupportable for a man and a human society than freedom… In the end they will lay their freedom at our feet, and say to us, ‘Make us your slaves, but feed us.’”

The Brothers Karamazov · The Grand Inquisitor, Book V chapter 5

The argument is that people do not actually want freedom, because freedom means responsibility and uncertainty, and that any institution offering to take it away in exchange for security and certainty will be embraced. It is the most serious case ever made against the position its own author held, which is why it has outlived every polemic written since.

Ivan's preceding chapter is the other half: he refuses to accept any explanation of the world, however true, that requires the suffering of one child as its price. That is the strongest statement of the problem of evil in any literature, and Dostoevsky put it in the mouth of the character he disagreed with.

The honest verdict: read Books V and VI — about eighty pages. The rest is magnificent and demanding, and life is finite.

Part II

The twentieth century's warnings

Book 04

Nineteen Eighty-Four

George Orwell · 1949 · ~330 pages · in copyright in many territories

read it whole

Everyone thinks they know this book, and what most people retain — surveillance, cameras — is the least interesting part of it. The real subject is language, and the manipulation of the past.

The central move is that the Party does not merely lie; it insists that objective reality is whatever the Party currently says, and that your own memory is the thing in error. O'Brien's demand of Winston is not obedience but sincere belief that two plus two makes five — because a person who will concede that has no ground left to stand on. The famous line is “Who controls the past controls the future: who controls the present controls the past.”

What makes it land is that this is not fiction about a distant tyranny. It is the manipulation technique from Volume 37 — contesting someone's account of reality until they doubt their own memory — applied at the scale of a state. Orwell understood that the most effective control is not over what people do but over what they are able to think, and that the instrument for that is vocabulary. Take away the word and you take away the thought.

Why read it: it is short, fast, and the appendix on Newspeak is the most useful twelve pages in political fiction. Read it alongside Volume 47 and notice how much of it is about evidence becoming deniable.

Book 05

Brave New World

Aldous Huxley · 1932 · ~270 pages · in copyright in many territories

read it whole

The other dystopia, and the one that has aged more accurately. Orwell feared a boot; Huxley feared that we would be given everything we wanted and stop caring. No one is oppressed here. Everyone is comfortable, entertained, medicated and content — and something has nonetheless been lost.

The decisive exchange comes near the end, when the Controller explains that the society has traded art, religion and science for stability, and the Savage refuses the bargain. He says he does not want comfort; he wants God, poetry, real danger, freedom, goodness, sin — he is claiming, as the Controller puts it, the right to be unhappy.

That is the whole argument in one phrase, and it is more uncomfortable than Orwell's because nobody in the book is a villain. The case for the system is genuinely strong. Suffering has been abolished; so has meaning, and the novel will not tell you the trade was obviously wrong.

Why read it: because the mechanism it describes — distraction and pleasure as a form of control — is closer to what actually happened than surveillance was, and because the argument it stages is the same one Volume 40 says most moral disagreements turn out to be.

Book 06

The Trial

Franz Kafka · 1925 · ~250 pages · public domain

read this part

A man is arrested one morning and never told what he is accused of. He is not imprisoned; he goes on working. The case proceeds. He cannot find out what it concerns, who is deciding, or how to answer — and everyone he meets treats his confusion as naive.

The famous chapter is the parable Before the Law, which works entirely on its own. A man comes seeking admittance to the Law and is told by the doorkeeper that he cannot enter now. He waits. He waits for years, he bribes, he grows old, and at the end he asks why no one else has ever come:

“No one else could ever be admitted here, since this gate was made only for you. I am now going to shut it.”

The Trial · Before the Law

What makes it unbearable is that the gate was open the whole time, and that permission was never actually required — only requested. It is the most precise description ever written of what a bureaucracy does to a person, and of the particular helplessness of a system with no one in it who can be argued with.

The honest verdict: the parable is two pages and worth reading tonight. The novel is unfinished, deliberately airless, and rewards patience you may prefer to spend elsewhere.

Book 07

The Stranger

Albert Camus · 1942 · ~130 pages · in copyright in many territories

read it whole

It opens with a man noting that his mother has died, possibly yesterday, he is not sure — and that flat affect is the whole book. Meursault does not perform grief. Later he kills a man, largely without motive, and at his trial it becomes clear that he is being condemned not for the killing but for not having cried at his mother's funeral.

That is the argument: society punishes the failure to perform expected feeling more reliably than it punishes the act. Meursault's crime is refusing to pretend, and the court cannot tolerate it.

The closing pages, where he finally becomes angry at a chaplain and then finds a kind of peace in the indifference of the universe, are among the most quoted in modern literature — the famous final image has him opening himself to what he calls the gentle indifference of the world.

Why read it: it is a hundred and thirty pages, the prose is the plainest in serious fiction, and it will take you an evening. Read it before anything else in this volume if you want to start.

Part III

Society, seen clearly

Book 08

Pride and Prejudice

Jane Austen · 1813 · ~300 pages · public domain

read it whole

Dismissed as a romance by people who have not read it. It is a novel about money, and what happens to women who have none, written by someone who found the arrangement absurd and could not say so directly.

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Austen · opening line

Read it again. It is not a statement about men; it is a joke at the expense of everyone who believes it, and the whole novel's method is in it — a sentence that says the opposite of what it appears to say, delivered without comment. Austen never tells you she is being ironic. That is the pleasure.

The passage that carries the book is Elizabeth, after re-reading Darcy's letter, realising that her confident judgement of both men was entirely wrong:

“How despicably have I acted!” she cried. “I, who have prided myself on my discernment! I, who have valued myself on my abilities!… Had I been in love, I could not have been more wretchedly blind. But vanity, not love, has been my folly.”

Pride and Prejudice · volume II, chapter 13

That is confirmation bias, described exactly, in 1813 — she liked Wickham, so she believed him, and found Darcy's evidence unwelcome. Volume 35 named the mechanism; Austen showed what it costs.

Why read it: it is genuinely funny, which nobody warns you about, and it is the best novel ever written about how badly people read each other.

Book 09

Middlemarch

George Eliot · 1872 · ~900 pages · public domain

read this part

Frequently called the greatest novel in English, and it is a serious claim. It follows a whole town, and its subject is the gap between what people intend to do with their lives and what they end up doing — ambitious young doctor, idealistic young woman, both worn down not by catastrophe but by ordinary compromise.

Its most extraordinary passage is a sudden widening of attention, in which Eliot stops the narrative to say something about the limits of sympathy:

If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence. As it is, the quickest of us walk about well wadded with stupidity.

Middlemarch · chapter 20

And its final paragraph is the best defence ever written of an ordinary life — that the growing good of the world depends partly on unhistoric acts, and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been because of people who lived faithfully a hidden life and rest in unvisited tombs.

The honest verdict: it is nine hundred pages and slow. Read the last chapter and chapter 20 now; read the whole thing in a year when you have time, and it will mean more than it would have at twenty.

Book 10

Things Fall Apart

Chinua Achebe · 1958 · ~200 pages · in copyright

read it whole

Written as a direct answer to a century of European novels in which Africa appeared as scenery. It shows an Igbo community in Nigeria as a complete society — with its own law, religion, humour, cruelty and internal arguments — and then shows what colonialism did to it.

The decisive line is near the end, and it is devastating in its restraint. The British commissioner, having just overseen events that destroyed a man and a world, reflects that the whole business might make a reasonable paragraph in the book he is planning to write — and he has already decided on its title, which refers to the pacification of the primitive tribes of the Lower Niger.

Two hundred pages of a fully realised civilisation, reduced to a paragraph in someone else's book, by a man who considers himself reasonable. That is the entire argument of Volume 22, made in a single sentence.

Why read it: it is short, plain and devastating, and it is the necessary companion to the history track — which mostly tells you what happened, while this tells you what it was like to be on the receiving end.

Book 11

One Hundred Years of Solitude

Gabriel García Márquez · 1967 · ~420 pages · in copyright

read this part

Seven generations of one family in a town called Macondo, told in a register where the extraordinary is reported in exactly the same tone as the ordinary — a woman ascends into the sky while folding sheets, and the family's main concern is the loss of the sheets. That flatness is the technique, and it is why the book changed what novels could do.

Its opening sentence is among the most admired in any language: a man facing a firing squad remembering a distant afternoon when his father took him to discover ice. Three times sit in one sentence — the present, the remembered past, and the future the memory is being recalled from — and the whole novel's relationship with time is announced before anything has happened.

The passage most worth knowing is the insomnia plague, when the town loses its memory and begins labelling every object with its name and use, until someone realises that a day will come when the labels can still be read but not understood.

The honest verdict: magnificent and genuinely hard to follow — the family reuses the same five names across seven generations, deliberately. Read the first fifty pages. If the spell works, continue; if it does not, it will not start working on page three hundred.

Part IV

Five more, briefly

Books 12–16

The rest of the shelf

Moby-Dick · Herman Melville · 1851 · public domain · read this part — A book about a man hunting a whale that is substantially a book about everything else, with long chapters on rope, on the colour white, on whaling law. Ahab's refusal to accept an indifferent universe is the American answer to Camus, arrived at ninety years earlier and with much more shouting. Read chapters 1, 36 and 42.

Beloved · Toni Morrison · 1987 · in copyright · read it whole — A formerly enslaved woman is haunted, literally, by the daughter she killed to prevent her being taken back into slavery. It is the most serious novel written about what slavery did to the interior lives of the people who survived it, and it refuses every consolation. Difficult, and the difficulty is the point.

Don Quixote · Miguel de Cervantes · 1605 · public domain · know of it — The first modern novel, and the source of the idea that a person can be destroyed by the stories they believe. Enormously long and genuinely funny in places. Know what it does; read a good abridgement without guilt.

The Great Gatsby · F. Scott Fitzgerald · 1925 · public domain in many territories · read it whole — A hundred and eighty pages about a man who invents himself to win back a woman who represents a past that never existed. The famous last line — boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past — is the best closing sentence in English, and the book earns it.

Invisible Man · Ralph Ellison · 1952 · in copyright · read it whole — A Black man in mid-century America discovers that people refuse to see him, in every institution and on every side, including the ones claiming to help. The prologue alone is among the finest openings in the language, and the book is far funnier and stranger than its reputation.

If you are going to read one

Start with The Stranger or Things Fall Apart. Both are short, plain, and finished in an evening — and both will change what you notice. Save Anna Karenina for a holiday, and read The Grand Inquisitor on its own tonight, because it is thirty pages and it is the best thirty pages here.

Reference

What to carry forward

The four that earn their length

Anna Karenina — meaning found by ceasing to look for it · Crime and Punishment — self-justification failing from the inside · Pride and Prejudice — confirmation bias, described in 1813 · Things Fall Apart — a civilisation reduced to a paragraph in someone else's book.

The two warnings

Orwell feared control of language and of the past; Huxley feared we would be given everything we wanted and stop caring. Huxley has aged more accurately, and his Savage claims what the Controller calls the right to be unhappy.

Read the part, not the book

The Grand Inquisitor (thirty pages) · Before the Law (two pages) · Middlemarch chapter 20 and its final page · Moby-Dick chapters 1, 36 and 42. Each is the best thing in a book you may never finish.

The evening ones

The Stranger and Things Fall Apart — each under two hundred pages, each plain enough to read in a sitting, each likely to change something. Start there rather than with the Russians.
