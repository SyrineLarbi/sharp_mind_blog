Don't do that. The everyday dangers that are counterintuitive — and the ones people walk into calmly.

Everything in this volume shares one property: the dangerous thing does not look dangerous, or the instinctive response is the wrong one. That is why these particular items kill ordinary, careful people. Each entry is short — the rule, why it is not obvious, and where the full mechanism lives — because the value here is in having read it before, not in looking it up after.

Part I

Water, heat and the house

Chapter 01

Drowning does not look like drowning

This is the most under-known fact in the volume and it changes what you watch for at every beach and pool for the rest of your life.

The splashing, shouting, arm-waving drowning of film is almost never what happens. A drowning person usually cannot call out, because the mouth is barely at the surface and breathing takes absolute priority over speech. They cannot wave, because the arms are instinctively pressing down on the water to lift the mouth clear. They are upright in the water, making no progress, with their head tilted back and mouth open, and they are quiet.

It lasts perhaps twenty to sixty seconds before submersion. And it happens routinely within a few metres of other people who simply do not recognise it, often with a parent nearby watching, because everyone is looking for thrashing.

What to watch for: a head low in the water with the mouth at surface level, head tilted back, eyes glassy or closed, vertical in the water, hair over the face, trying to swim and going nowhere, and silence. If a child playing in water suddenly goes quiet, that is the signal to act. Ask them if they are alright — someone who can answer you is probably fine; a blank look means you have seconds.

Two additions. Rip currents pull outward, not under, and the instinct to swim straight back to shore exhausts even strong swimmers against a flow faster than they can swim. The answer is to swim parallel to the beach until out of the pull, or to float and signal. And cold water causes an involuntary gasp on entry that inhales water and can incapacitate strong swimmers within a minute — jumping into cold water is far more dangerous than it looks on a hot day.

Chapter 02

The house rules people learn too late

Each of these has its mechanism in Volume 46; here is the short form, because the short form is what you need in the moment.

Never put water on a burning pan. It flashes to steam beneath the oil and throws burning fat across the room. Turn off the heat, smother it, and leave it where it is. Do not carry it outside.

Never bring anything that burns fuel indoors — barbecue, camping stove, patio heater, generator — including into a garage, tent or caravan, and including a barbecue brought in to cool down. Carbon monoxide has no smell and its symptoms are indistinguishable from flu, with confusion among them, so it removes the judgement you would need to leave.

If you smell gas, do not touch a switch. Not the light, not the extractor, not the doorbell, not your phone indoors. A spark is the ignition source. Open doors, leave, call from outside.

Do not charge things on a bed or sofa, or in the hallway. Lithium batteries can enter a runaway reaction that generates its own oxygen — it cannot be smothered and it reignites. A charging e-bike in the hall blocks the exit you would need.

Button batteries swallowed are an emergency within hours, not days. They burn through tissue chemically, often with no early symptoms. Go immediately.

Close internal doors at night. Smoke kills far more people than flames, mostly in sleep, and a closed door buys a remarkable amount of time.

Never decant anything into a drinks bottle. This is how children and adults drink chemicals. Original containers, original labels.

Part II

The body

Chapter 03

Medicines that do not behave as expected

Paracetamol has a narrow margin and delayed symptoms. It hides in cold and flu remedies, night-time medicines and hot drink sachets — so taking a cold remedy plus a painkiller, both as directed, can exceed the safe amount. Someone who has taken too much may feel fine for a day while irreversible liver damage proceeds. Suspected excess is an immediate medical matter regardless of how well they feel, and there is an antidote that works best early.

Alcohol with sedatives or strong painkillers is the combination that most often kills accidentally. Both suppress breathing, and the effect together is greater than either alone.

Do not take someone else's medication or leftover antibiotics, and tell your doctor about herbal products and supplements — St John's wort in particular accelerates the clearance of a long list of essential medicines, including contraceptives, and grapefruit blocks a pathway that raises the level of many others for hours.

Never give aspirin to a child unless a doctor specifically directs it.

Do not stop a prescribed medicine because you feel better. Blood pressure medication in particular is working because the numbers look normal, and stopping abruptly is dangerous for several classes of drug.

Chapter 04

Signs people talk themselves out of

The common thread here is that the correct response feels like an overreaction, and the delay is what causes the harm.

Chest discomfort. Often pressure or tightness rather than dramatic pain, and frequently dismissed as indigestion. In women, older people and people with diabetes it may present without chest pain at all — as breathlessness, unusual fatigue, nausea, or jaw and back discomfort. Call an ambulance rather than driving, and do not wait to see whether it passes.

Stroke signs. Face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty — and note the time symptoms started, because it determines which treatments remain possible. Symptoms that resolve within an hour are not a relief: they signal a substantially raised risk of a full stroke in the following days and are an emergency in their own right.

A sudden severe headache unlike any before — described as the worst of someone's life, arriving in seconds — needs immediate assessment.

A head injury followed by lucidity. Someone can be knocked out, recover, seem completely fine, and deteriorate hours later from bleeding inside the skull. Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, drowsiness, unequal pupils or a seizure after any head injury means emergency care, even if they seemed fine.

A swollen, painful calf after a long journey or period of immobility, and particularly if followed by sudden breathlessness or chest pain, needs urgent attention.

Severe allergic reaction — swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, collapse. Adrenaline first, then ambulance regardless of improvement, because a second wave can follow hours later.

And a general rule that covers cases not listed: sudden and severe, in anything, is the pattern that warrants urgency. Emergency departments are not annoyed by false alarms of this kind, and being wrong costs you an evening.

Chapter 05

Heat, cold, and choking

Never leave a person or an animal in a parked car. The interior heats far faster and far higher than people expect, shade and a cracked window do not prevent it, and children overheat considerably faster than adults. "Only a few minutes" is exactly how this happens.

Heatstroke is distinct from heat exhaustion and is a medical emergency. The distinguishing signs are confusion, loss of coordination, and skin that has stopped sweating — at that point the body's cooling has failed. Cool them aggressively and call an ambulance. Heat exhaustion — heavy sweating, weakness, nausea — responds to shade, rest and fluids, and must not be allowed to progress.

Choking. Someone who can cough forcefully should be encouraged to cough — coughing is more effective than anything you can do. Someone who cannot speak, cough or breathe needs immediate back blows and abdominal thrusts. Silence is the danger sign, as in the water. The foods that most often do this to children are round and firm — grapes, nuts, cherry tomatoes, hard sweets — and cutting round food lengthways rather than into discs genuinely matters.

Burns. Cool running water for a long time — considerably longer than feels necessary, twenty minutes — and nothing else. No ice, no butter, no cream, no oil. Remove jewellery and loose clothing near the area before swelling, and do not burst blisters.

Part III

Out in the world

Chapter 06

Roads and vehicles

Road danger is badly distributed relative to how people feel about it, which is the risk perception problem from Volume 39 in its most consequential form.

Tiredness at the wheel behaves like alcohol and is not something you can decide to override. Microsleeps of a few seconds happen without the person knowing, and the classic defences — opening the window, loud music, coffee — do essentially nothing. The only real remedies are stopping and sleeping, and not starting a long drive already tired.

Reversing vehicles are a specific and under-appreciated hazard, particularly to small children in driveways, and particularly with large vehicles that have substantial blind zones.

The door of a parked car kills cyclists. Opening with the far hand forces your body to turn and look — a small habit with a genuinely large effect.

Aquaplaning and black ice both remove grip suddenly, and the instinctive responses — braking hard, steering sharply — make both worse. Ease off the accelerator, keep the steering steady, and do not brake abruptly. Black ice is most likely where roads are shaded, on bridges, and in the early morning after a clear cold night.

And two boring ones that outperform almost everything else: seatbelts in the back matter as much as in the front — an unrestrained rear passenger is lethal to the person in front — and child seats are very commonly fitted incorrectly, which removes much of the protection.

Chapter 07

The social and financial traps

Different kind of danger, same structure: the correct response feels rude, and the pressure not to be rude is the mechanism.

Anything urgent, secret or guaranteed is a fraud. The urgency exists specifically to prevent the twenty-four hours that would defeat it. Volume 47 has the full catalogue; the compressed rule is to hang up and call back on a number you already had, and to note that objecting to that is itself the tell.

Never read out a code that was sent to you, to anyone, for any reason. And there is no such thing as a safe account to move your money to.

Do not sign what you have not read, and be specific about three things people routinely miss: what happens when an introductory rate ends, whether it renews automatically, and what the cancellation terms are. A free trial requiring card details is a subscription with a delay.

Guaranteeing someone else's loan means agreeing to repay it, not agreeing to help if things go badly. People agree to this as a gesture of support without understanding that the obligation is unconditional.

Do not leave a drink unattended, and if someone becomes disproportionately impaired for what they drank, treat it as a medical situation and stay with them.

And the social one that underlies several of the above, which Volume 36 explains: the discomfort of seeming rude is a smaller cost than almost anything it prevents. Leaving a situation that feels wrong requires no justification, and people who make leaving difficult are telling you something.

Chapter 08

The three rules underneath all of it

If you retain nothing else from this volume, retain these — because they generate the right answer in cases this chapter never listed.

Silence and stillness are the danger signals, not noise. The drowning person is quiet. The choking person cannot cough. The carbon monoxide victim is drowsy. The quiet child in the water. Human attention is drawn to commotion, and the serious situations are frequently the still ones.

The delayed presentation is what kills. Paracetamol, head injuries, allergic reactions, transient stroke symptoms, a burning plaque of an artery — the common structure is a period of feeling fine that persuades everyone the danger passed. Feeling better is not evidence in these specific situations, and knowing which ones they are is most of this chapter's value.

The instinctive response is often the wrong one. Water on burning oil. Swimming straight back to shore against a rip. Braking hard on ice. Waving off a false alarm to avoid making a fuss. In each case the instinct is strong and wrong, which is why these must be learned in advance — you will not reason your way there in the moment.

Scope, and the one thing to actually do

This is a reference to have read, not a substitute for training. A few hours on a first aid course is worth more than every word here, because compressions, the recovery position and choking response are physical skills that reading cannot install. Volume 34 covers the emergency procedures; this volume covers the avoidance. And nothing here should delay calling emergency services.

What to carry forward

Water

Drowning is silent. Upright, head back, mouth at the surface, no waving, no shouting, twenty to sixty seconds. If a child in water goes quiet, act. In a rip current, swim parallel to the beach, not toward it.

House

No water on a pan fire · nothing that burns fuel indoors, ever · no switches if you smell gas · no charging on beds or in hallways · a swallowed button battery is an emergency within hours · close doors at night · never decant into a drinks bottle.
