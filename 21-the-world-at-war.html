The world at war. How industry, pride and a railway timetable killed seventy million people.

Two wars, a generation apart, that between them defined the century. The first is the harder to explain, because nobody especially wanted it and everybody got it anyway. The second is easier to explain and far worse. Both are stories about what happens when the productive capacity of the industrial revolution is turned to the opposite purpose.

Part I

The war nobody chose

Chapter 01

A continent wired to explode

Europe in 1914 was richer, more educated and more interconnected than it had ever been. Its royal families were cousins. Its economies traded with each other constantly. A widely read book had recently argued that a major war between such interdependent nations had become economically impossible.

Underneath, four things were quietly loaded.

An alliance system. Two blocs, each bound by treaty to defend the other's members. Designed to deter war by making it too large to contemplate, it guaranteed that any small conflict would become a general one.

An arms race. Particularly at sea, where Britain and Germany spent extraordinary sums building battleships neither ultimately dared risk.

Nationalism. Not the mild sort — an intoxicating conviction, taught in schools and printed in mass-circulation newspapers, that one's nation was superior and that war would be invigorating.

And mobilisation timetables. This is the detail people miss and it may be the most important. Every general staff had a plan for moving millions of men by rail, and the plans were built on precise schedules where a day's delay meant defeat. Once you began mobilising you could not pause without unravelling the whole thing. The machinery removed the option of slowing down.

Chapter 02

Five weeks

An Austrian archduke was shot in Sarajevo by a Bosnian Serb nationalist. It was the second attempt that morning; the first had failed, and the car took a wrong turning and reversed directly in front of the assassin. History is full of these.

Austria-Hungary, with German backing, issued Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected. Serbia accepted almost all of it. Austria declared war anyway. Russia, protector of Serbia, mobilised. Germany, facing a two-front threat, demanded Russia stop and then declared war. The German plan for a two-front war required knocking out France first, quickly, which required going through neutral Belgium — and Britain had a treaty obligation to Belgium.

Five weeks from a murder to a continental war. At each step the decision-makers believed they were responding to someone else's move and had little choice. That belief is the most frightening thing in the whole sequence.

Chapter 03

The trench

Everyone expected a short war of movement. What they got was the consequence of an imbalance nobody had thought through: defensive technology had advanced far beyond offensive technology.

Machine guns, barbed wire, and artillery that could saturate a square mile meant that men advancing across open ground were simply killed. So both sides dug. The line ran from the Channel to Switzerland and barely moved for three and a half years.

The tactical problem was genuinely hard, and it is worth stating fairly, because the generals are often dismissed as fools. To break a line you must cross ground swept by machine guns, get through wire, and then keep going faster than the enemy can bring up reinforcements by rail. No army in 1915 had the means to do the third part. The offensives failed not because commanders were stupid but because the problem had no solution with the equipment available — and the equipment that eventually solved it, the tank and the aircraft and radio, arrived only at the very end.

What the men lived in, meanwhile, was mud, rats, lice, corpses in the parapet, and shelling that could last for days. A new phrase entered the language for what prolonged bombardment did to the mind. It was called shell shock and treated, at first, as cowardice; men were executed for it.

Verdun and the Somme, in one line each

Verdun was a deliberate attempt to draw the French army into a place it could not abandon for reasons of pride, and bleed it to death. It lasted ten months and cost around seven hundred thousand casualties. The Somme cost Britain some twenty thousand dead on the first morning — the worst day in the history of the British army — and, after four and a half months, moved the line about six miles.

Chapter 04

The peace that set the next one

The war ended not with an invasion of Germany but with its collapse — blockade, hunger, mutiny, revolution, and an army that could no longer replace its losses. The armistice took effect at eleven in the morning on the eleventh of November 1918. Men were killed that morning in attacks ordered after the time was known.

At Versailles, Germany was required to accept responsibility for the war, pay reparations it could not afford, surrender territory and reduce its army to a token. It was not consulted; it was presented with the terms and told to sign.

The settlement was harsh enough to enrage and not harsh enough to disable — the worst combination. It left a large, humiliated, still fundamentally powerful country with a grievance, a story that its army had been betrayed rather than beaten, and an economy about to be wrecked first by hyperinflation and then by global depression.

One of the negotiators is said to have remarked that it was not peace but a twenty-year truce. He was out by about two months.

Part II

The war that was chosen

Chapter 05

How a democracy votes for its own end

The Weimar Republic was a genuine democracy with an admirable constitution, and it was born carrying defeat, blamed for a settlement it had not made, and then hit by two economic catastrophes.

The first was hyperinflation, which destroyed savings so completely that money became firewood — the photographs of children with bundles of banknotes are real. The second was the Depression, which put millions out of work and convinced a great many people that the existing arrangement had failed utterly.

The Nazi party did not seize power in a coup. It was the largest party in the Reichstag, and Hitler was appointed chancellor legally by a president who despised him, on the advice of conservatives who believed they could use and contain him. Within months an emergency decree had suspended civil liberties, and an enabling act — passed by a parliament with communist deputies already arrested and stormtroopers in the corridors — handed the cabinet the power to make law without it.

The machinery of the democracy was used, step by legal step, to dismantle the democracy. Every step was defended at the time as temporary and necessary.

The thing worth remembering from this chapter

It did not arrive in uniform announcing itself. It arrived as a legal appointment, an emergency measure, and a series of things that reasonable people told each other would probably be fine. The people who miscalculated most badly were not the fanatics — they were the respectable conservatives who thought they were the ones doing the using.

Chapter 06

Six years

The war opened with something genuinely new. Where the first war had been static, the second began with speed — concentrated armour, aircraft, radio — punching a hole and driving deep to cut off whole armies before they could react. Poland fell in weeks. France, whose army was not smaller or worse equipped, fell in six.

Britain, alone, survived an air campaign intended to force it out. The turning points came in 1941 and 1942 and they were mostly about arithmetic. Germany invaded the Soviet Union and found itself in the same trap as Napoleon, against a country that could absorb catastrophic losses and keep producing tanks beyond the Urals. Japan attacked the United States and brought into the war an economy larger than everyone else's combined.

From then on the outcome was, in the long run, industrial. The Soviet Union absorbed the overwhelming majority of German military casualties — a fact often underweighted in western accounts — and American production supplied everybody. It took three more years and appalling cost.

It is worth keeping the scale in mind: somewhere between seventy and eighty-five million dead, the majority of them civilians. The Soviet Union alone lost perhaps twenty-seven million. These are not numbers anyone can really feel, which is part of why the next chapter is written the way it is.

Chapter 07

The Holocaust

Six million Jews were murdered, along with Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners, political opponents and homosexuals. It was not a by-product of the war. It was a deliberate programme, planned in offices, with budgets, schedules and a civil service.

That last point is the one that matters most and is hardest to sit with. It required railway timetables and men to compile them. It required companies to tender for contracts. It required administrators to maintain the lists of who was to be collected, and clerks to file the paperwork, and guards, and drivers, and the neighbours who took the flats afterwards. It was industrial, and it was bureaucratic, and it was carried out overwhelmingly by ordinary people doing their part of a process.

The question people ask is how anyone could. The more useful question, and the more disturbing one, is how a system arranges matters so that no individual ever has to decide to commit a murder — only to approve a requisition, meet a quota, or process a transfer. The psychology of that is in Volume 31's reading list, and it does not let anybody off.

It ended because the war ended. It was not stopped from inside.

Chapter 08

Two mornings in August

The war in Europe ended in May 1945. In the Pacific it did not.

On 6 August a single aircraft dropped a single bomb on Hiroshima, and around eighty thousand people died that morning; on 9 August the same happened to Nagasaki. Tens of thousands more died afterwards of burns, injury and radiation. Japan surrendered within days.

The argument about whether it was necessary has run ever since and will not be settled here. The case made at the time was that an invasion would have cost more lives on both sides; the counter-case is that Japan was already seeking terms, that the Soviet declaration of war mattered as much, and that a demonstration might have sufficed.

What is not arguable is what it established. From that morning, humanity possessed the means to destroy its own cities in an afternoon, and within four years more than one country had it. Every political calculation for the rest of the century was made underneath that fact, which is where the next volume begins.

Reference

What to carry forward

How the first one happened

Alliances that turned a local quarrel into a continental one, an arms race, mass nationalism, and mobilisation timetables that removed the option of slowing down. Five weeks from a murder to a general war, with every decision-maker convinced he was merely responding.

Why the trenches

Defensive technology had outrun offensive technology. The generals were not simply fools — with 1915 equipment the problem of crossing swept ground and then exploiting the gap had no solution. The tools that solved it arrived at the end.

How a democracy ends

Not with a coup. A legal appointment, an emergency decree, an enabling act — each step defended as temporary and necessary, and the worst miscalculation made by respectable people who believed they were the ones in control.

The hardest fact

The Holocaust was bureaucratic and industrial — timetables, contracts, quotas, filing. Arranged so that almost nobody had to decide to kill anyone, only to complete their part of a process. It ended because the war ended, not from within.
