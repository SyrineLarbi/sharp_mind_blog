What's actually coming. Sorted by whether there's a demo, an engineering problem, a physics problem, or a press release.

Every prediction about the future is made by someone, and most of them are made by someone selling something. So this volume does not rank technologies by how exciting they are. It sorts them by what kind of problem is left — which is the only question that reliably predicts whether something arrives in five years, in fifty, or never. It is Volume 39's discipline, pointed forward.

Part I

The method

Chapter 01

Four kinds of "coming soon"

When someone tells you a technology is nearly here, the useful question is not how impressive the demonstration was. It is what remains to be solved, and there are only four answers.

It works and is scaling. The thing exists, it is deployed, and what remains is cost, manufacturing and regulation. These are real obstacles and they are the kind that money and time reliably solve. Predictions here are usually about speed, not possibility.

It works in the lab and has an engineering problem. The principle is demonstrated; making it reliable, affordable and scalable is unsolved. These arrive — often a decade or two later than promised, and sometimes in a different form than anticipated. The tell is that nobody disputes it can work.

It has a physics or biology problem. Something in the way the universe works is obstructing it, and no amount of investment removes it. These may arrive in fifty years, or in a form nobody currently imagines, or not at all. The tell is that the same fundamental obstacle has been described in the same terms for decades.

It is a press release. A demonstration under controlled conditions, a funding round, an animal result, a simulation. Not dishonest, necessarily — but the gap between this and a product is where the overwhelming majority of announced futures quietly die.

Two further heuristics are worth having before the survey.

Things that touch physical infrastructure move slowly; things that are only software move fast. This explains most of the variation in adoption speed across everything in this volume. Replacing every vehicle, every boiler, every power station is a multi-decade undertaking regardless of how good the replacement is.

And the demonstration is the easy ten per cent. A self-driving car that works on a sunny road is a fundamentally different problem from one that works in snow, in roadworks, at night, in a city that behaves unpredictably. The last stretch of reliability routinely takes longer than everything before it.

Part II

Biology

Chapter 02

Gene editing — already here, and the part that isn't

The tools for making precise changes to DNA arrived faster and cheaper than nearly anyone predicted, and the category is genuinely split.

Already working and scaling: treatments where cells are removed from a patient, edited outside the body, checked, and returned. This has produced approved therapies for inherited blood disorders — conditions that were lifelong and are now, in some patients, effectively resolved. That is not a press release; it is treatment that has happened.

Engineering problem: editing cells inside the body. Getting the machinery to the right organ, into enough cells, without immune reaction and without editing the wrong place. This is the active frontier, it is progressing, and it is what would extend the approach from a handful of diseases to many.

Physics-and-biology problem, plus the ethics: most conditions are not caused by a single gene. Height, intelligence, most disease risk — these involve thousands of variants with small effects, interacting with environment. Editing them is not currently limited by the editing; it is limited by not knowing what to edit, and that is a knowledge problem with no timeline.

And the question everyone asks: editing embryos, so that changes are inherited. It has been done, once, notoriously, resulting in imprisonment and near-universal condemnation. The scientific obstacle is real — off-target effects, and consequences that only appear in the next generation — and the ethical one is that the person affected cannot consent. The line between correcting a fatal disease and selecting traits is genuinely hard to draw, and this is one of the places where Volume 40's three frameworks give different answers.

Chapter 03

Ageing, longevity and the honest state of it

This field contains a striking mixture of serious science and outright selling, and separating them is worth doing carefully.

The serious part. Ageing is no longer regarded as a single inevitable process but as several identifiable ones — accumulated damage to DNA, shortening of chromosome ends, cells that stop dividing but refuse to die, declining ability to clear cellular waste, mitochondrial decline. Each is being studied as something potentially modifiable. And in laboratory animals, lifespan has been extended substantially by several interventions. That is real.

The gap. Extending life in a short-lived, genetically uniform animal in a controlled environment has repeatedly failed to translate to humans, and the experiment that would settle it takes decades by definition. Anyone claiming a proven human lifespan intervention is ahead of the evidence.

The reframing that matters. The serious goal in this field is not lifespan but healthspan — the years lived without disability — and on that, the interventions with actual evidence are the deeply unglamorous ones from Volume 42: physical activity and especially strength, sleep, blood pressure control, not smoking, hearing and vision correction, and social connection. That list is not exciting and it is what the evidence keeps producing.

The press release. Supplements marketed on the basis of cell-culture or mouse results, clinics selling unproven infusions and stem-cell treatments, and the confident claim that someone alive today will live to a great age because of a technology that does not yet exist. Volume 44's rules apply exactly: in people or in mice? replicated? who is selling?

Part III

Energy, computing and machines

Chapter 04

Fusion — the honest version of the joke

Fusion power has been thirty years away for about seventy years, and the joke is fair but it obscures what has actually changed.

The physics is not in doubt — it is what powers the sun, and it has been achieved on Earth. What is hard is doing it in a way that releases more usable energy than the whole plant consumes, continuously, for years, in a machine that survives the conditions inside it.

Recent experiments crossing the threshold of getting more energy out of the reaction than was delivered into the fuel are genuine milestones, and they are routinely reported in a misleading way: the comparison is with energy reaching the target, not with the energy consumed by the entire facility, which remains far larger. That is not a scandal — it is a real step — but the headline overstates it.

Where it sits: a serious engineering problem, not a physics problem. Materials that survive prolonged bombardment, continuous rather than pulsed operation, fuel supply, and cost per unit of electricity against alternatives that keep getting cheaper. Meaningful contribution to the grid is a multi-decade proposition at best, and the honest framing is that fusion is not a solution to the climate problem on the timescale that problem has — which is the strongest argument for not treating it as one.

Meanwhile, the boring energy story is the one actually transforming things: solar and battery costs have fallen by amounts that would have been dismissed as fantasy two decades ago, and that — not fusion — is what is changing electricity now. The unsolved parts are storage across seasons rather than hours, grid infrastructure, and the industrial processes that need extreme heat rather than electricity.

Chapter 05

Quantum computing — real, and not what people think

The popular description — it tries all answers at once — is wrong in a way that produces completely unrealistic expectations.

What is actually true is that quantum systems can hold combinations of states, and that certain algorithms are able to arrange those states so that wrong answers cancel out and the right one is more likely to be measured. That trick works for a narrow set of problem types, not for computation generally.

Which means two things people rarely hear. It will not make your computer faster, or your spreadsheets, or your video. And it is not a general replacement for anything — the realistic picture is specialised machines used alongside ordinary computers for specific tasks.

The problems where it genuinely could matter are worth naming: simulating molecules and materials, which is a natural fit because those are themselves quantum systems, and which could plausibly transform chemistry and drug design; certain optimisation problems; and breaking some of the encryption in use today — which is why the migration to encryption designed to resist it is already underway, and why data stolen now may be stored in anticipation.

The obstacle is severe and physical rather than merely engineering: quantum states are extraordinarily fragile, disturbed by heat, vibration and stray fields, and errors accumulate far faster than in ordinary computing. Error correction works in principle and requires an enormous number of physical components per usable one. Progress is real and the remaining distance is large. This sits between an engineering problem and a physics problem, and it is the most frequently over-announced field in this volume.

Chapter 06

Robots, vehicles and why the easy things are hard

There is a principle here that explains the whole field and is worth knowing by name: what is hard for people is easy for machines, and what is effortless for people is extraordinarily hard for machines. Chess fell decades ago. Reliably picking up an unfamiliar object, or walking across a cluttered room, remains genuinely difficult — because those abilities were refined over hundreds of millions of years and are invisible to us precisely because they run without effort.

So: working and scaling — robots in structured environments, doing repetitive precise tasks in known surroundings. Warehouses, factories, inspection. Unglamorous and already transforming specific industries.

Engineering problem — robots in unstructured environments, the ones that look impressive in videos. The videos are real and also heavily selected, and the gap between a demonstration and a machine that works unsupervised in an ordinary house for years is very large. Manipulation of unfamiliar objects remains the central unsolved piece.

Self-driving deserves a paragraph because it is the clearest illustration of the last-ten-per-cent principle in the whole volume. It has genuinely arrived — driverless vehicles carry passengers commercially in several cities. And it took far longer than confidently predicted, and it works in carefully mapped areas with favourable weather. The remaining difficulty is not average driving; it is the endless tail of unusual situations, each individually rare and collectively constant. Expect gradual geographic expansion rather than a sudden general arrival, and expect the hardest questions to be about liability and regulation rather than capability.

Chapter 07

Space, brains and the further edges

Space has had a genuine change, and it is economic rather than technological: reusable rockets cut the cost of reaching orbit dramatically, which is why satellite constellations, commercial launch and renewed lunar programmes all became feasible at once. Working and scaling.

Beyond that the honesty gets harder. Returning people to the Moon is an engineering and funding problem. Mars is a different category: the radiation exposure over the journey, the physiological effects of prolonged low gravity, the absolute reliability required with no possibility of rescue, and the fact that nobody has demonstrated a closed life-support system running for years. Those are not solved, and the confident dates are not evidence. Large-scale settlement belongs in the press release column, and it is worth noticing that it is usually announced by people raising money.

Brain-computer interfaces are genuinely working for a narrow purpose: restoring function to people with severe paralysis — controlling a cursor, a limb, producing speech. That is real and remarkable. Everything beyond it is much further away. The obstacles are that implanted electrodes provoke scarring and degrade, that non-invasive methods lack the resolution to do much, and — the deep one — that nobody knows how information is encoded in the brain well enough to write to it meaningfully. Reading simple intentions is very different from inserting a thought, and the second is not an engineering problem.

Synthetic biology — designing organisms to produce useful substances — is quietly working already: medicines, materials, food ingredients, enzymes. It attracts less attention than it deserves and is likely to matter more than several things that attract more.

Climate technology splits sharply. Renewables, batteries, heat pumps and electric vehicles are working and scaling, and the obstacles are infrastructure and politics rather than invention. Capturing carbon directly from the air works and is currently far too expensive and too small to matter at the necessary scale. Deliberate intervention in the climate — reflecting sunlight — is technically plausible, cheap enough that a single country could attempt it, and carries consequences nobody can model reliably, which makes it primarily a governance problem rather than a technical one.

Chapter 08

How to read the next announcement

You will meet a hundred of these claims and this volume will be out of date. What should survive is the method.

Which category? Deployed, engineering problem, physics problem, or press release. Ask what specifically remains unsolved — a vague answer is itself informative.

In people, in animals, or in simulation? The Volume 44 question, and it applies far beyond medicine.

Who is announcing, and what are they raising? Not disqualifying, and it tells you which direction the error runs.

What is the comparison being hidden? Fusion's energy balance measured against the target rather than the plant. A battery's performance in the laboratory rather than over years of charging. A model's score on a test rather than in use.

Does it need new infrastructure? If yes, add a decade at least, whatever the technology.

And has this been announced before? Many of the items in this volume have been imminent repeatedly. A field with a long history of confident announcements has told you something about the reliability of its announcements.

The closing thought for the whole library

The technologies that actually changed ordinary life — antibiotics, refrigeration, sanitation, the shipping container, the transistor, cheap solar — were mostly not the ones that were loudly predicted. Volume 24 makes the same point looking backwards: the moments we learned something rarely announced themselves. Which is a reason for humility about this entire volume, and a reason to distrust anyone, including me, who sounds certain about what is coming.

Reference

What to carry forward

The four categories

Deployed and scaling · an engineering problem (arrives, late) · a physics or biology problem (maybe never, in this form) · a press release. Ask what specifically remains unsolved — and remember that the demonstration is the easy ten per cent.

Where things actually sit

Real now: gene editing outside the body, reusable launch, solar and batteries, robots in structured spaces, synthetic biology. Engineering problems: editing inside the body, fusion, humanoid robots, driverless expansion. Further: Mars settlement, writing to the brain, editing complex traits.

Two corrections

Quantum computing will not speed up your computer — it suits a narrow class of problems, chiefly simulating molecules and breaking some current encryption. And fusion is not a solution on the timescale the climate problem has; solar and batteries already are.

Reading any announcement

Which category? In people or in simulation? Who is raising money? What comparison is hidden? Does it need new infrastructure — if so, add a decade. And has it been announced before? The technologies that changed ordinary life were mostly not the loudly predicted ones.
