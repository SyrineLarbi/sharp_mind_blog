A cultured mind · Volume 18 of 54 · history

Empires of faith and trade. The thousand years Europe spent being the quiet corner.

School histories tend to go straight from Rome to the Renaissance, as though the intervening millennium were a waiting room. It was not. While western Europe was poor and fragmented, Baghdad was the largest city on Earth, Mali's king was handing out so much gold he wrecked an economy, Chinese merchants were using paper money, and a cavalry army out of Mongolia assembled the largest land empire there has ever been. This is that story.

Part I

After Rome

Chapter 01

What actually went dark

When the western empire stopped functioning, something real was lost, and it is worth being precise about what.

The roads stopped being repaired. Long-distance trade shrank, so a village that had once bought pottery from three hundred miles away now made its own, worse. Cities emptied — Rome itself fell from perhaps a million people to maybe thirty thousand. Literacy collapsed outside the church. Coinage got scarce. Buildings got smaller and were made of wood.

Those are genuine losses, and for the people living through them life did get harder and poorer and more local. But calling the whole period a "dark age" smuggles in two errors. The first is that it describes one region — western Europe — as though it were the world. The second is that "dark" often just means we have fewer written records, which says more about our sources than about their lives.

And what replaced Rome was not chaos but a different arrangement. With no state to provide safety, people obtained it privately: a local strongman offered protection, and in exchange you worked his land and could not leave it. That bargain, repeated everywhere and formalised over centuries, is what we call feudalism. It is not a system anybody designed. It is what happens when a state stops answering and everybody has to arrange their own security.

The institution that kept the thread

The one organisation that survived the collapse intact was the Church — the only body with literate staff, a network across the whole former empire, and a reason to copy old books. Monks copying manuscripts in damp scriptoria are the reason we still have any Latin literature at all. They were not preserving it for us; they were preserving it because a text was valuable and copying was what you did with valuable texts. We are the accidental beneficiaries.

Part II

The house of wisdom

Chapter 02

Baghdad, and the translation of everything

In the eighth century, a new capital was laid out on the Tigris — a perfect circle, built from nothing, called the City of Peace and known to everyone as Baghdad. Within a century it was probably the largest and certainly the richest city in the world.

Its rulers did something with consequences far beyond their reach. They decided to collect and translate every book they could find — Greek, Persian, Indian, Syriac — and they paid extremely well for it. Manuscripts were hunted across the known world. Translators became wealthy men. The institution that grew around this effort is remembered as the House of Wisdom.

What came out of it was not merely preservation, though that mattered enormously — a great deal of Aristotle reached Europe centuries later only because it had gone into Arabic first and come back. What came out of it was new work.

Scholars working in Arabic gave us the word algebra, from a book about "restoring and balancing" equations, and algorithm, from the name of the man who wrote it. They took a place-value number system with a symbol for nothing from India and made it usable, which is why you are reading these numerals and not Roman ones — try multiplying MCMXLVII by XXIII and you will see immediately why the change mattered. Ibn al-Haytham worked out that we see because light enters the eye, rather than because the eye emits something, and insisted that claims be tested by experiment. A physician called Ibn Sina wrote a medical encyclopaedia that was still a standard European textbook five hundred years later.

Why it happened there, then

The same conditions keep recurring in this book. Wealth from trade, a state willing to pay for knowledge, a common language across an enormous area, and a genuine mixing of traditions — Greek logic, Indian mathematics, Persian administration, all in one library, in one language, with money behind it. Ideas get invented where different ideas are forced to meet.

Chapter 03

The road that was not a road

The Silk Road was never a road, and almost nobody travelled its full length. It was a chain of overlapping journeys — a merchant carrying goods a few hundred miles to a market, selling to another merchant who carried them a few hundred more — running from China through Central Asia to the Mediterranean.

Silk went west, which is where the name comes from, because for centuries only China knew how to make it and guarded the method ferociously. Paper went west too, and that mattered more. Spices, glass, horses and silver went in various directions.

But the cargo that changed the most was the cargo nobody was carrying deliberately. Religions travelled these routes — Buddhism walked from India into China along them. Technologies travelled. Ideas travelled. And so did disease, which is the subject of a later chapter and the reason this network eventually became lethal.

Part III

The empire of the horse

Chapter 04

Temüjin

He was born to a minor clan on the Mongolian steppe. His father was poisoned when he was a boy; his family was abandoned by their clan and survived on roots and small game. He was captured and kept in a wooden collar. By any reasonable expectation he should have died anonymous.

Instead he spent twenty years welding the steppe tribes into one people, and in doing so broke the thing that had always kept them weak: he promoted by ability rather than birth, and he deliberately mixed the old clans into new units so that a man's loyalty ran to his commander and to Temüjin rather than to his relatives. The tribes acclaimed him Genghis Khan — universal ruler.

What he then commanded was, in its moment, the most effective military instrument in the world. Every man could ride before he could properly walk, and shoot a bow accurately from a moving horse. Each warrior travelled with several remounts and could cover distances nobody believed possible. The army was organised in tens, hundreds and thousands with a discipline that European armies would not match for centuries. And it used terror deliberately and explicitly as policy — a city that surrendered was taxed, a city that resisted was destroyed utterly, and word was allowed to travel ahead. It meant most cities surrendered.

At its greatest extent the Mongol empire ran from Korea to Hungary. It is the largest contiguous land empire there has ever been, and it was assembled in a single lifetime and the one following it.

Chapter 05

The peace that followed the slaughter

The destruction was real and enormous. Baghdad was sacked in 1258; the libraries were destroyed, and it is said the Tigris ran black with ink. Whole regions lost most of their population and some never recovered.

And then, having conquered everything between China and the Black Sea, the Mongols made it safe to travel. One authority, one set of rules, protection for merchants, no borders. It became possible for goods and people and letters to move from one end of Asia to the other in a way that had never been possible before and would not be again for a long time. Historians call it the Mongol peace.

This is why a Venetian named Marco Polo could travel to the court of Kublai Khan and come back with stories nobody believed. It is why paper money, gunpowder and printing — all Chinese — began their long journey west. It is also why, in the next century, a disease carried by fleas on rats in the trading caravans could travel from central Asia to the ports of Italy, and from there kill something like a third of everyone in Europe.

The pattern worth noticing

Connection is not a neutral good. The same network that carries goods, ideas and money carries disease, and it carries it at exactly the speed of the trade. Every increase in human connection in this book — the Silk Road, the Atlantic crossing, the steamship, the aeroplane — brings both, and the second one is always a surprise to the people who built the first.

Part IV

The worlds Europe had not met

Chapter 06

Mali, and the man who broke the gold price

In 1324 the ruler of Mali went on pilgrimage to Mecca. Mansa Musa travelled with an entourage that contemporaries numbered in the thousands, with camels carrying gold, and he gave it away as he went — to the poor, to officials, to anyone who impressed him.

He passed through Cairo and spent so freely that he depressed the value of gold in Egypt for roughly a decade. One man's generosity, on one journey, distorted a regional economy. It is the only time in history anyone has managed it by accident.

Mali's wealth came from sitting astride the trade in West African gold and Saharan salt — two commodities that were, weight for weight, of comparable value in their respective markets, which tells you how much people will pay for something they cannot live without. Timbuktu, at the desert's edge, became a city of scholars with libraries of manuscripts, some of which survive.

It is worth saying plainly that a medieval European hearing about this would not have been surprised. Mali appears on European maps of the period, with its king drawn holding a lump of gold. The idea that Africa had no history before Europeans arrived is not an old prejudice inherited from the Middle Ages. It is a much more recent invention, manufactured largely to justify what came later.

Chapter 07

Cities on a lake

Across the Atlantic, entirely unaware of any of this, other people were building other things.

The Maya had cities in the rainforest with stepped pyramids, a fully developed writing system, and astronomy accurate enough to predict eclipses and track Venus across decades. They worked out a concept of zero independently. Their cities were largely abandoned centuries before any European arrived, for reasons still argued over — drought, exhausted soil, war, or all three.

The Inca ran an empire down the spine of the Andes, held together by thousands of miles of engineered road, with relay runners who could carry a message hundreds of miles in a day, and terraced farming that turned mountainsides into fields. They did it without the wheel, which is useless in that terrain, and without writing as we would recognise it — they kept records on knotted cords, and we can still only partly read them.

And in the Valley of Mexico, the Aztecs built Tenochtitlan on an island in a lake, with causeways, aqueducts, floating gardens and a market that astonished the Spanish soldiers who first saw it. One of them wrote that some of the men who had been to Constantinople and Rome said they had never seen a market so large and so orderly. It held perhaps two hundred thousand people. Almost no European city was close.

Every one of these societies was doing sophisticated work on problems that mattered to them. Volume 19 is partly about what happened when the two halves of the world finally met, and it is not a happy chapter.

Part V

Three hinges

Chapter 08

The document that was meant to fail

In 1215 a group of English barons, thoroughly fed up with a king who had lost their French lands and taxed them to pay for losing them, cornered John in a meadow and made him seal a list of their grievances.

Magna Carta was not a charter of liberties for the people. It was a peace treaty between a king and about forty aristocrats, most of its clauses concerned fish weirs and debts to moneylenders and the rights of widows, and John repudiated it within weeks. The pope annulled it. The war resumed. As an immediate political settlement it was a complete failure.

But buried in it were two sentences saying that no free man could be imprisoned or stripped of his property except by the lawful judgement of his peers or the law of the land, and that justice would not be sold or delayed.

Those sentences were reissued, argued over, forgotten, rediscovered by lawyers four hundred years later looking for a weapon against a different king, and eventually written into the constitutions of countries that did not exist when the ink dried. The principle that matters is not any of the specific clauses. It is the precedent that the ruler is inside the law rather than above it — and that a written document can be produced later to prove it.

Chapter 09

The year a third of Europe died

It arrived at the Sicilian port of Messina in 1347, on ships from the Black Sea whose crews were mostly dead. Within three years it had reached almost every part of Europe.

The Black Death killed somewhere between a third and a half of the population. There is no modern comparison; nothing in any recent war approaches it. Whole villages were emptied and never resettled — you can still see their outlines in English fields from the air. Nobody understood what caused it, and the explanations people reached for were the ones available to them: divine punishment, bad air, planetary conjunction, and, hideously, poisoning by Jews, which produced massacres across the Rhineland that had nothing to do with the disease and everything to do with what frightened people do.

And then something unexpected happened, and it is the reason this chapter is here rather than in the disasters volume. The survivors found themselves better off.

With a third of the workforce dead, labour was suddenly scarce and therefore valuable. Peasants who had been legally tied to land could now simply walk to the next estate, where somebody would pay them. Wages rose. Rents fell. Landowners passed laws to freeze wages and the laws did not hold, because a landlord with empty fields will quietly break the rules rather than lose his harvest. The bargain that had held western Europe together for six hundred years came apart, not because anybody argued against it, but because the arithmetic changed.

A hard idea, stated carefully

Nothing about this makes the plague good. Tens of millions of individual deaths are not redeemed by a change in the labour market. But it is a genuine and uncomfortable historical pattern that catastrophes reshuffle arrangements that had seemed permanent, and that the people who benefit are never the people who paid. You will see the same shape again after both world wars.

Chapter 10

The walls that had held for a thousand years

Constantinople had been the capital of the surviving Roman empire since the western half stopped functioning. Its walls had turned back every attacker for a thousand years. By 1453 the empire behind them had shrunk to little more than the city itself — a capital with almost no country left.

The Ottoman sultan Mehmed II was twenty-one. He brought an enormous cannon, cast by a Hungarian engineer who had offered it first to the defenders and been told they could not afford him. It fired a stone ball the size of a man and it took hours to reload, and the walls that had held for a millennium came down in weeks.

The last emperor is said to have stripped off his imperial insignia and died fighting in the breach as an ordinary soldier. Whether or not that is exactly true, it is how it was remembered, and it is the end of the Roman world — not in 476, but here, in 1453, with gunpowder.

Two consequences ran outward from it. Greek scholars fled west carrying manuscripts, arriving in Italian cities just as those cities were getting rich enough to be interested — one of the sparks of the Renaissance. And the overland trade routes to Asia now ran through a power that Europe was hostile to, which made the sea routes suddenly worth enormous risk. Within forty years men were sailing west across an empty ocean hoping to find Asia on the other side.

Which is where the next volume begins.

Reference

What to carry forward

The correction

There was no global dark age. While western Europe was poor and local, Baghdad, Chang'an, Timbuktu and Tenochtitlan were among the largest and richest cities on Earth. "Dark" mostly means we have fewer records.

Where new ideas come from

The same conditions recur: wealth from trade, a patron willing to pay, a shared language over a wide area, and different traditions forced to meet. Baghdad had all four. So, later, will Renaissance Italy.

The double edge of connection

The Mongol peace let goods, letters and ideas cross Asia safely — and let a flea-borne disease do the same. Every leap in connection in this book carries both, and the second is always unforeseen.

The two hinges

Magna Carta failed immediately and left behind the idea that a ruler sits inside the law. The Black Death killed a third of Europe and, by making labour scarce, dissolved serfdom — a reminder that catastrophes reshuffle arrangements argument never could.
