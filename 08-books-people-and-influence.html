Part I · Negotiation

Three books on getting to an agreement

The architecture, the moves inside it, and the principles being used on you.

Book 01

Never Split the Difference

Chris Voss · 2016 · ~270 pages

The one idea

Negotiation is not argument and it is not splitting the difference. You get what you want by making the other side feel deeply understood first, and then letting them talk themselves toward your position.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ LABELLING — the core move, and the hedge is the technique
  Name the emotion you observe. ALWAYS hedged, never
  asserted, then SILENCE.
    "It seems like the deadline is the real worry."
    "It sounds like you've been burned by a vendor before."
    "It looks like there's something about this you don't
     like."

  ⚠ NEVER "I'm hearing that you..." — that puts YOU in
    the sentence and makes it about your perception.
  ⭐ WHY THE HEDGE MATTERS: an assertion about someone's
    inner state invites a CORRECTION ("no, I'm not
    annoyed"). A hedge invites ELABORATION ("well, it's
    more that...") — and the elaboration is the whole
    prize.
  ⭐ THEN SHUT UP. The silence after a label is where the
    information arrives. Filling it wastes the move.

⭐ MIRRORING — absurdly simple, absurdly effective
  Repeat the last one to three words they said, with an
  upward inflection. Then stop.
    "...we just can't move the date."
    "Can't move the date?"
    "Well — not the launch date. The internal one we
     could probably..."
  ⇒ costs nothing, requires no cleverness, and produces
    elaboration roughly every time. The hardest part is
    tolerating how strange it feels.

⭐ CALIBRATED QUESTIONS — open, and starting with HOW or WHAT
  ⚠ NEVER "WHY". In essentially every language "why" is
    accusatory. Convert it: "what made that the right
    call?" not "why did you do that?"

  ⭐ THE BEST LINE IN THE BOOK:
    "HOW AM I SUPPOSED TO DO THAT?"
  It says no without saying no, it is impossible to take
  offence at, and it hands YOUR problem to THEM to solve.
  Delivered calmly, it is the most useful sentence in this
  entire shelf.

  Others worth having:
    "What's the biggest challenge you face here?"
    "What about this is important to you?"
    "How would you like me to proceed?"
    "What happens if we do nothing?"

⭐ "THAT'S RIGHT" vs "YOU'RE RIGHT"
  "YOU'RE RIGHT" means: please stop talking. It is what
  people say to end a conversation, and hearing it should
  worry you.
  "THAT'S RIGHT" means: you have genuinely captured my
  position. You get it by summarising their view so
  accurately they confirm it.
  ⇒ aim for "that's right" and treat "you're right" as
    a warning that you have lost them.

NO IS THE START, NOT THE END
  People feel SAFE saying no — it protects autonomy. So
  design for it.
    ✗ "Do you have a minute?"      → pressure
    ✓ "Is now a bad time?"          → a safe no
    ✗ "Are you still interested?"   → ignored
    ✓ "Have you given up on this?"  → answered, because
                                      "no" is easy
  ⇒ Voss's famous unanswered-email fix is the last one.

THE ACCUSATION AUDIT
  List the worst things they could say about you — out
  loud, first, before they do.
    "You're going to think I'm being unreasonable. You
     may feel I've wasted three weeks of your time."
  ⇒ saying it first drains it. Unsaid, it sits there
    powering their position.

THE THREE VOICES
  ① LATE-NIGHT FM DJ  slow, downward-inflecting, calm.
                      For anchoring a point. Use rarely
                      and deliberately
  ② POSITIVE/PLAYFUL  the DEFAULT. A genuine smile
                      changes the sound of your voice
                      and the other side can hear it
  ③ DIRECT/ASSERTIVE  almost never. It invites pushback

LOSS-AVERSION FRAMING
  People take more risk to avoid a loss than to secure
  a gain (Kahneman, file 07). So frame in terms of what
  they stand to LOSE by not agreeing, not what they gain.

BLACK SWANS
  The two or three pieces of information you do not know
  that would change the whole negotiation. You find them
  by listening, not by preparing — which is the argument
  for labelling and mirroring rather than for scripts.

Use it tomorrow

Label one emotion this week with "it seems like" and then stay quiet. Replace one "why" with "what" or "how". And the next time you want to refuse something, try "how am I supposed to do that?" instead.

Worth memorising

"How am I supposed to do that?"

"That's right" is the win. "You're right" means they want you to stop talking.

● Green · worth reading fully

Short, every chapter carries one technique, and written by someone who did this for a living rather than someone who studied it. The stories are gripping and they are also the teaching method, so skimming loses more than usual.

One real caution. It is an FBI hostage frame applied to business, and a few techniques — the Ackerman bargaining ladder, aggressive anchoring, the accusation audit used tactically — read as manipulative in a relationship where you will see the person again on Monday. The listening half (labelling, mirroring, calibrated questions, "that's right") is safe and valuable everywhere. The bargaining half belongs in genuine one-shot negotiations. Voss does not draw this line; you should.

Already yours: tactical empathy, labelling, mirroring, calibrated questions, "that's right", embracing no and the DJ voice are all in master-yourself/phase-9, file 02 §04.

Book 02

Getting to Yes

Roger Fisher & William Ury · 1981 · ~200 pages

The one idea

Stop bargaining over positions. Separate the people from the problem, and negotiate over the interests underneath the positions — because positions are one solution each, and interests usually have several.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ POSITIONS vs INTERESTS — the orange
  Two people want the same orange. They split it. One
  wanted the juice; the other wanted the zest for baking.
  Both could have had everything they wanted.
  ⇒ A POSITION is one solution. An INTEREST is what the
    solution was for. Positions collide; interests very
    often do not.
  ⇒ the question that gets you there: "what is this
    FOR?" or "what would this solve for you?"

⭐ THE FOUR PRINCIPLES
  ① SEPARATE THE PEOPLE FROM THE PROBLEM
     be soft on the people, hard on the problem. Sit on
     the same side of the table, literally if you can
  ② FOCUS ON INTERESTS, NOT POSITIONS
     and note: behind opposed positions there are
     usually SHARED and merely DIFFERENT interests as
     well as conflicting ones
  ③ INVENT OPTIONS FOR MUTUAL GAIN
     ⚠ SEPARATE INVENTING FROM DECIDING. Judging while
       generating kills generation. Brainstorm first,
       explicitly with no commitment, then evaluate
  ④ INSIST ON OBJECTIVE CRITERIA
     market rate, precedent, professional standards,
     what a court would award. So the outcome turns on
     a standard rather than on whose will is stronger
     ⇒ "on what basis?" is the question that does this

⭐ BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement
  The most valuable single concept in the negotiation
  literature.
  YOUR POWER IS YOUR BATNA, not your rhetoric or your
  confidence. What happens if you walk away?

  THREE THINGS TO DO WITH IT
    ① KNOW yours, concretely — not "I'd find something
      else" but the actual alternative
    ② IMPROVE it BEFORE negotiating. This is the only
      reliable way to gain power in a negotiation, and
      it happens before the room
    ③ ESTIMATE THEIRS. Their behaviour makes sense once
      you know what their walk-away looks like

  ⚠ AND DO NOT REVEAL YOURS IF IT IS WEAK. A weak BATNA
    disclosed is the whole negotiation lost.

NEGOTIATION JIU-JITSU — when they will not play
  Do not push back; sidestep.
    they attack your position → ask questions about
                                theirs, do not defend
    they attack you           → recast it as an attack
                                on the problem
    they state a demand       → ask what problem it
                                solves
  ⇒ "silence is one of your best weapons" — after an
    unreasonable proposal, say nothing. People fill
    silence with concessions.

Use it tomorrow

Before any negotiation — salary, deadline, scope — write down your actual BATNA in one concrete sentence, and spend an hour improving it. That hour is worth more than any tactic. And when someone states a demand, ask what it is for.

Worth memorising

BATNA. The word alone changes how you prepare.

"On what basis?" — turns a contest of will into a question about standards.

● Green · worth reading fully

Two hundred pages, and BATNA alone earns them. It reads slightly dated — 1981, and the examples are of their time — but the structure has not been improved on in forty years, which is its own recommendation.

Read it alongside Voss, and notice that they disagree. Fisher and Ury give you the architecture and are, Voss argues, too rational about emotion; Voss gives you the moves inside the architecture and is, they would argue, too tactical for relationships that continue. The disagreement is more instructive than either book alone: interests tell you what to aim at, labelling tells you how to find out what the interests actually are.

Already yours: principled negotiation, BATNA and ZOPA are in master-yourself/phase-9, file 02 §02–03.

Book 03

Influence

Robert Cialdini · 1984, expanded 2021 · ~320 pages

The one idea

Seven principles trigger near-automatic compliance in humans, because they are shortcuts that are usually right. Cialdini wrote the book as a defence manual, and reading it as a persuasion manual is the common misuse.

The seven, with the mechanism of each
① RECIPROCITY
   We feel obliged to return favours — including
   uninvited ones, including ones we did not want, and
   in amounts disproportionate to what we received.
   ⭐ THE TECHNIQUE BUILT ON IT: rejection-then-retreat
     (door-in-the-face). Make a large request, get
     refused, then make the smaller one you wanted. The
     retreat reads as a concession, and concessions
     demand reciprocation.
   ⇒ DEFENCE: a gift given to create obligation is not
     a gift, it is a sales tactic. Relabel it that way
     in your own head and the obligation evaporates —
     Cialdini's own recommended counter.

② COMMITMENT AND CONSISTENCY
   Once we commit, we behave consistently with it. And
   commitment binds hardest when it is ACTIVE, PUBLIC,
   EFFORTFUL and FELT TO BE FREELY CHOSEN.
   ⭐ built on it: foot-in-the-door (tiny yes, then the
     real one), and low-balling (agree a price, then
     change it — people stay, because they have already
     become the kind of person who is buying this)
   ⇒ DEFENCE: "knowing what I know now, would I make
     this commitment again?" And practically: do not
     state a position publicly before you have thought,
     because the statement itself will bind you.

③ SOCIAL PROOF
   We look at what similar others do, and it is
   strongest under UNCERTAINTY and SIMILARITY — which
   means it bites hardest precisely when you have least
   information and most need to think for yourself.

④ LIKING
   We say yes to people we like. The drivers are
   depressingly mechanical: physical attractiveness,
   similarity, compliments (they work even when
   transparently insincere), familiarity through
   repeated contact, and association with good things.
   ⇒ DEFENCE: notice when you have started liking
     someone unusually quickly. That speed is the signal.

⑤ AUTHORITY
   Titles, clothes and trappings — SYMBOLS rather than
   substance. An actor in a white coat outperforms a
   real doctor in a jumper.
   ⇒ DEFENCE: "is this authority genuinely expert IN
     THIS, and what are they gaining here?"

⑥ SCARCITY
   Less available means more valued. Stronger for things
   NEWLY scarce than always scarce, and strongest under
   direct competition. Deadlines, limited runs, "one
   other person is looking at this flat".
   ⇒ DEFENCE: scarcity changes how much you WANT
     something, not how USEFUL it is. Ask what you
     wanted it for.

⑦ UNITY — added in the 2021 edition
   Shared identity: not "similar to me" but "one of us".
   Family, place, ethnicity, team, and — the actionable
   one — CO-CREATION: people who build something with
   you become "us".
   Cialdini argues this is the strongest of the seven.

⭐ THE FRAME THAT MAKES THE BOOK WORTH IT
  These are heuristics that are usually correct. That is
  why they work and why you cannot simply switch them
  off. The realistic goal is not immunity — it is
  noticing the trigger and asking one question:
    "WOULD I STILL WANT THIS IF THE TRIGGER WEREN'T
     HERE?"

Use it tomorrow

Notice one scarcity trigger aimed at you this week and name it. Before agreeing to anything shortly after receiving a favour, check whether you are exercising judgement or discharging an obligation.

Worth memorising

"Would I still want this if the trigger weren't here?"

And: a gift designed to create obligation is a sales tactic, not a gift.

● Amber · extract only, or one chapter

The seven principles are above and they are the book; the remaining three hundred pages are anecdotes demonstrating them. If you open it, read the chapter on commitment and consistency — it is the strongest, the least intuitive, and the one whose defences are hardest to apply.

Note on editions: the 2021 New and Expanded adds Unity and refreshed research. The original is otherwise fine, and cheaper.

Already yours: all six original principles plus unity, and the defence section, are in master-yourself/phase-9, file 02 §06 and §09.

Part II · Hard conversations

Four books on saying the difficult thing

The first of these is the best book in this library. The other three are variations on it with different packaging.

Book 04

Difficult Conversations

Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton & Sheila Heen · Harvard Negotiation Project · 1999 · ~250 pages

The one idea

Every difficult conversation is three conversations happening at once. Almost every failure comes from arguing the first one while the real trouble is in the third.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE THREE CONVERSATIONS

  ① THE "WHAT HAPPENED" CONVERSATION
     Who is right · who meant what · whose fault it is.
     This is the one people think they are having, and
     it is the least important of the three.

  ② THE FEELINGS CONVERSATION
     The unstated emotions on both sides. Unnamed, they
     do not go away — they LEAK into conversation ① as
     tone, and then everyone argues about tone.

  ③ ⭐ THE IDENTITY CONVERSATION
     What this says about ME. Underneath almost every
     hard conversation sit three questions:
       Am I competent?
       Am I a good person?
       Am I worthy of love?
     ⇒ THIS IS WHERE THE REAL ANXIETY LIVES, on both
       sides, and it is never said out loud. When
       someone reacts far more strongly than the content
       warrants, you have hit ③.

⭐ THE THREE SHIFTS THAT DO THE WORK

  FROM TRUTH → CONTRIBUTION
    Stop arguing about who is right. Map what each of
    you CONTRIBUTED to the situation.
    ⇒ BLAME looks backward, assigns cost, and produces
      defence. CONTRIBUTION looks at the system and
      produces change. They feel similar and behave
      completely differently.
    ⇒ and go first: name your own contribution before
      naming theirs. It is the single most effective
      move in the book.

  FROM INTENTIONS → IMPACT
    ⭐ WE JUDGE OURSELVES BY OUR INTENTIONS AND OTHERS
      BY THEIR IMPACT.
    You do not know their intentions. You have never
    known anyone's intentions. Asserting them — "you
    wanted to undermine me" — guarantees a fight,
    because they know their intentions and yours is
    wrong.
    ✗ "You were trying to make me look bad."
    ✓ "When that happened in front of the team, the
       impact on me was that I felt undermined."
    ⇒ impact is yours to report and cannot be argued
      with. Intention is theirs and cannot be known.

  FROM BLAME → JOINT PROBLEM
    "We have a problem" rather than "you have a
    problem" — but only when it is true.

⭐ THE THIRD STORY — the best opener in the literature
  Open from the position of a neutral observer. Not your
  story, not theirs.
    ✗ "I want to talk about how you handled Tuesday."
    ✓ "It seems like we see Tuesday quite differently.
       I'd like to understand how it looked from your
       side, and share how it looked from mine."
  ⇒ it makes the conversation describable to both
    parties before either has to defend anything.

THE IDENTITY QUAKE, AND HOW TO STEADY IT
  When a conversation threatens your self-image you
  either go RIGID (defend at all costs) or COLLAPSE
  (accept everything, agree to anything).
  ⇒ THE FIX IS "COMPLEXIFYING" YOUR IDENTITY: move from
    all-or-nothing to and.
      "I am competent"  →  "I am competent AND I make
                            mistakes"
  ⇒ the three grounding admissions that make you
    unshakeable in a hard conversation:
      · I will make mistakes
      · my intentions are complex
      · I have contributed to this problem
    Accept those three in advance and there is very
    little anyone can say that destabilises you.

Use it tomorrow

Open your next hard conversation with the third story. Replace any sentence containing "you intended" with "the impact on me was". And before the conversation, write down your own contribution — then say it first.

Worth memorising

We judge ourselves by our intentions and others by their impact.

I will make mistakes · my intentions are complex · I have contributed.

● Green · worth reading fully — the best book on this shelf

If you read one book from all three of these files, read this. It comes out of the Harvard Negotiation Project, it is carefully argued rather than anecdote-driven, and the three-conversations structure is genuinely load-bearing — it explains why conversations fail in a way the other books on this shelf only describe.

It is also the least gimmicky book here. There is no acronym, no proprietary vocabulary, and nothing to buy afterwards.

Already yours: mentioned but not built out in master-yourself/phase-9, file 01 §08. The organisational version of the same material is in sharp-mind file 05 §06 and §08.

Book 05

Crucial Conversations

Patterson, Grenny, McMillan & Switzler · 2002 · ~290 pages

The one idea

When stakes are high, opinions differ and emotions run strong, people default to silence or violence. The skill is noticing safety break and repairing it before continuing.

What the book actually gives you
THE DEFINITION — three conditions together
  high stakes + differing opinions + strong emotions
  ⇒ useful because it tells you WHEN to switch modes.
    Most conversations are not this and do not need any
    of the machinery below.

⭐ SILENCE vs VIOLENCE — the two failure modes
  SILENCE   masking (sarcasm, understating), avoiding
            (changing subject), withdrawing (leaving)
  VIOLENCE  controlling (cutting off, overstating),
            labelling (putting people in boxes),
            attacking
  ⇒ most people have ONE default. Knowing yours is
    worth the price of the book. Watch what you did
    the last three times something got hard.

⭐ MAKE IT SAFE — the central mechanism
  People go to silence or violence when they feel
  UNSAFE, not when they disagree. Safety has exactly two
  conditions:
    MUTUAL PURPOSE  do they believe you care about
                    their goals, not just yours?
    MUTUAL RESPECT  do they believe you respect them?
  ⇒ WHEN SAFETY BREAKS, STEP OUT OF THE CONTENT.
    Arguing the point harder when someone has gone
    quiet is the classic error — the content was never
    the problem.

  ⭐ CONTRASTING — the repair tool, and it is excellent
    A don't/do statement that fixes a misunderstanding
    WITHOUT retreating from your position.
      "I DON'T want you to think I'm questioning your
       commitment — I know you've been carrying this.
       I DO want to talk about the deadline, because
       I think we'll miss it."
    ⇒ the "don't" half restores safety; the "do" half
      keeps the point intact. Most people either soften
      the point or lose the person. This does neither.

⭐ STATE MY PATH — the order is the technique
  S  SHARE YOUR FACTS      start here. Facts are the
                           least controversial and the
                           most persuasive, and almost
                           nobody starts with them
  T  TELL YOUR STORY       your interpretation, clearly
                           labelled as an interpretation
  A  ASK FOR THEIR PATH    and mean it
  T  TALK TENTATIVELY      soften the CLAIM, not the
                           content ("I'm starting to
                           wonder whether...")
  E  ENCOURAGE TESTING     genuinely invite disagreement
  ⇒ the value is in "facts first, story labelled as
    story". Most conflict is two people exchanging
    conclusions with the facts left out.

MASTER MY STORIES — the path from fact to action
  see/hear → TELL A STORY → feel → act
  The story step is invisible and it is where the
  choice actually is.
  ⭐ THE THREE "CLEVER STORIES" WE TELL:
    VICTIM     "it's not my fault"
    VILLAIN    "it's all your fault"
    HELPLESS   "there's nothing else I could do"
  ⇒ counter each with: WHAT AM I PRETENDING NOT TO
    KNOW? and "why would a reasonable, decent person
    do this?"

MOVE TO ACTION
  Decide HOW to decide — command, consult, vote,
  consensus — and say which one you are doing. Then
  record who does what by when.
  ⇒ the most common failure of a good conversation is
    that it ends warmly and nothing happens.

Use it tomorrow

Work out whether your default is silence or violence. Use contrasting the next time someone misreads your intent. And start your next hard point with facts rather than with your conclusion.

Worth memorising

"I don't want you to think X. I do want Y."

What am I pretending not to know?

● Amber · extract only

Contrasting, the silence/violence split and "facts first" are genuinely good and they are above. The rest is corporate-training padding: heavy acronyms, repeated summaries, and a workbook tone. The book has sold enormously and is often mandated, so knowing the vocabulary has some social value.

If you are choosing between this and Difficult Conversations, read Difficult Conversations. It is better argued, better written, and covers the same ground more deeply. This one's unique contribution is contrasting and the safety mechanism.

Book 06

Nonviolent Communication

Marshall Rosenberg · 1999 · ~220 pages

The one idea

A four-step grammar for saying a hard thing without triggering defence — and the step everyone skips is the one that does the work.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE FOUR STEPS

  ① OBSERVATION — what a camera would have recorded
     ✗ "You're unreliable"          ← evaluation
     ✓ "You've been late three times this week"
     ⇒ the test: could two people who disagree about
       everything still both agree this happened?

  ② FEELING — what I feel
     ⚠ "I FEEL THAT YOU..." IS NOT A FEELING. It is a
       judgement wearing a feeling's clothes, and it is
       the single most common error.
       ✗ "I feel that you don't respect my time"
       ✓ "I feel frustrated"

  ③ ⭐ NEED — the universal human need underneath
     This is the step everyone skips and it is the one
     that resolves things.
     reliability · respect · autonomy · safety ·
     understanding · rest · contribution · fairness
     ⇒ "I feel frustrated BECAUSE I need to be able to
       plan my week."

  ④ REQUEST — specific, doable, present-tense, refusable
     ✗ "Be more considerate"        ← not doable
     ✓ "Would you be willing to message me by 9 if
        you're going to be late?"
     ⚠ A REQUEST YOU WILL PUNISH SOMEONE FOR REFUSING
       IS A DEMAND. If you cannot hear "no" without it
       costing them, do not pretend it is a request.

⭐ NEEDS vs STRATEGIES — the most useful idea in the book
  NEEDS are universal and rarely conflict.
  STRATEGIES are specific and conflict constantly.
  Two people who both need "reliability" can fight for
  a year about calendars. The fight is at the strategy
  level; the agreement is available at the need level.
  ⇒ so when stuck, go UP a level: "what do we each
    actually need here?" — and then look for a third
    strategy neither of you proposed.

RECEIVING EMPATHICALLY — the same four steps, pointed at them
  "Are you feeling X because you need Y?"
  ⇒ even when you guess wrong, the guess demonstrates
    you are trying, and they correct you — which gets
    you the real answer. Wrong guesses work almost as
    well as right ones.
● Amber · take the four steps, skip the book

The framework is excellent and it is above in full. The book is repetitive, the tone is earnest to a degree many readers find hard to take, and the example dialogues are unrealistically smooth — everyone responds beautifully, which is not what happens.

One practical note: the NVC vocabulary sounds strange at work and can read as a technique being performed on someone. Use the structure — observation, feeling, need, request — in your own ordinary words. Nobody needs to hear the phrase "I have a need for".

Already yours: the four steps, in both directions, are in master-yourself/phase-9, file 01 §06.

Book 07

Radical Candor

Kim Scott · 2017 · ~250 pages

The one idea

Care personally and challenge directly. The two are independent axes, and almost all management failure is doing one without the other.

The 2×2, and the quadrant you are probably in
                  CHALLENGE DIRECTLY
                          │
   OBNOXIOUS              │      ⭐ RADICAL
   AGGRESSION             │         CANDOR
   front-stabbing.        │      care personally AND
   Honest, and it lands   │      challenge directly.
   as contempt            │      Rare, and it is a
                          │      BEHAVIOUR, not a
                          │      personality
  ────────────────────────┼────────────────────────
                          │         CARE PERSONALLY
   MANIPULATIVE           │      ⚠ RUINOUS
   INSINCERITY            │         EMPATHY
   neither. Passive       │      you care, so you say
   aggression, political  │      nothing. THE MOST
   backstabbing, praise   │      COMMON FAILURE BY
   you don't mean         │      FAR, and the most
                          │      damaging, BECAUSE IT
                          │      FEELS KIND

⭐ RUINOUS EMPATHY IS THE DEFAULT FAILURE OF DECENT PEOPLE
  You withhold the criticism to spare their feelings.
  Six months later the fixable problem is a performance
  process. The kindness was to yourself: you avoided an
  uncomfortable ten minutes and charged them the cost.
  ⇒ THE TEST: "am I not saying this to protect them, or
    to protect myself from an awkward conversation?"
    The honest answer is usually the second.

GUIDANCE THAT WORKS — the checklist
  HUMBLE     "here's what I observed", not "here's what
             you are"
  HELPFUL    intended to help, and say so
  IMMEDIATE  within days, not at the review
  IN PERSON  and two-way
  PRIVATE if criticism · PUBLIC if praise
  ⭐ NOT ABOUT THE PERSON — about the work, the
     behaviour, the impact

⭐ SOLICIT CRITICISM BEFORE GIVING IT — and how to actually do it
  "Any feedback for me?" gets you nothing. The moves:
    ① ASK A SPECIFIC QUESTION
      "What could I do, or stop doing, that would make
       it easier to work with me?"
    ② THEN SHUT UP AND COUNT TO SIX. The silence is the
      technique. People fill it eventually, and what
      comes after the silence is the real answer.
    ③ REWARD THE CANDOUR VISIBLY — act on something
      and say you did. Otherwise you get one honest
      answer and never another.

ROCK STARS AND SUPERSTARS
  ROCK STAR   wants stability, is excellent where they
              are, is not trying to be promoted
  SUPERSTAR   steep growth trajectory, needs new
              challenges
  ⇒ both are valuable and they need opposite management.
    Treating a rock star as an underachieving superstar
    is a common and destructive mistake.
● Amber · the 2×2 is the book

Ruinous empathy is a genuinely useful name for a genuinely common failure, and the solicit-first sequence is good. Both are above. The rest is Silicon Valley memoir — Google, Apple, and a lot of anecdotes about famous people — which you may enjoy but will not need.

Worth knowing the vocabulary because many managers use it, and worth noticing that "radical candor" is frequently invoked to justify plain rudeness, which is the obnoxious-aggression quadrant with a better name.

Part III · Relationships

Four books on the long game

The 1936 classic, the reciprocity research, the term that launched a thousand trainings, and the marriage research that transfers straight to work.

Book 08

How to Win Friends and Influence People

Dale Carnegie · 1936 · ~290 pages

The one idea

People act from self-interest and want to feel important. Almost all social friction comes from behaving as though that were not true — including your own.

The contents page IS the book · all thirty principles
HANDLING PEOPLE
  ① Don't criticise, condemn or complain
  ② Give honest and sincere appreciation
  ③ Arouse in the other person an eager want

MAKING PEOPLE LIKE YOU
  ④ Become genuinely interested in other people
  ⑤ Smile
  ⑥ Remember and use a person's name
  ⑦ Be a good listener; encourage others to talk about
     themselves
  ⑧ Talk in terms of the other person's interests
  ⑨ Make the other person feel important — sincerely

WINNING PEOPLE TO YOUR THINKING
  ⑩ ⭐ The only way to get the best of an argument is
     to avoid it
  ⑪ Show respect for the other's opinions. Never say
     "you're wrong"
  ⑫ If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically
  ⑬ Begin in a friendly way
  ⑭ Get the other person saying "yes, yes" immediately
  ⑮ Let the other person do a great deal of the talking
  ⑯ ⭐ Let the other person feel the idea is theirs
  ⑰ Try honestly to see things from their point of view
  ⑱ Be sympathetic with their ideas and desires
  ⑲ Appeal to the nobler motives
  ⑳ Dramatise your ideas
  ㉑ Throw down a challenge

CHANGING PEOPLE WITHOUT GIVING OFFENCE
  ㉒ Begin with praise and honest appreciation
  ㉓ Call attention to mistakes indirectly
  ㉔ Talk about your own mistakes first
  ㉕ Ask questions instead of giving orders
  ㉖ ⭐ LET THE OTHER PERSON SAVE FACE
     the most important one in the entire list, and
     the one most often ignored by people who are
     right
  ㉗ Praise the slightest improvement, and every
     improvement
  ㉘ Give a person a fine reputation to live up to
  ㉙ Use encouragement; make the fault seem easy to
     correct
  ㉚ Make the other person happy about doing what you
     suggest

⚠ THE WORD DOING ALL THE WORK IS "SINCERELY", AND IT
  APPEARS THROUGHOUT. Carnegie is describing genuine
  interest in other people, not a performance of it.
  Read as a manipulation manual — which is easy, and
  common — every principle becomes transparent and
  fails. Read as instructions for actually caring, they
  work.
● Amber · read the contents page, not the book

Every principle survives ninety years of scrutiny. None of them needs its chapter — the chapters are 1930s anecdotes about people you have never heard of, and the prose is of its time.

Print the list above, keep it, and reread it once a year. That is the correct relationship with this book. Principles ⑩, ⑯ and ㉖ are the three that most change outcomes.

Already yours: cited in master-yourself/phase-11, file 02.

Book 09

Give and Take

Adam Grant · 2013 · ~300 pages

The one idea

Three reciprocity styles — givers, takers, matchers. Givers occupy both the bottom and the top of most performance distributions, and what separates the two ends is whether the giving is strategic.

What the book actually gives you
⭐ THE THREE STYLES
  TAKER    gets more than they give. Manages UP well,
           which is why they are hard to spot
  MATCHER  strict reciprocity. The default, and the
           majority
  GIVER    gives more than they get

⭐ AND THE FINDING THAT MAKES THE BOOK
  GIVERS ARE AT BOTH ENDS OF THE DISTRIBUTION.
    bottom · burnt out, exploited, saying yes to
             everything, no time for their own work
    top    · because generosity compounds into
             networks, reputation and goodwill, and
             because working with a giver makes the
             whole team better

  ⇒ SO THE QUESTION IS NOT WHETHER TO GIVE. IT IS HOW.

⭐ "OTHERISH" GIVING — the sustainable form
  Two independent dials: concern for others, and
  concern for self.
    SELFLESS  high others, LOW self   → the bottom
    OTHERISH  high others, HIGH self  → the top
  ⇒ otherish givers help a great deal AND protect their
    own interests, time and goals. This is not a
    compromise of generosity; it is the version that
    lasts more than two years.

THE FIVE-MINUTE FAVOUR
  Do things that cost you very little and help others a
  lot: an introduction, a document you already have,
  thirty seconds of feedback, an answer you know cold.
  ⇒ high return, low cost, and it does not deplete —
    which is precisely why it is the sustainable form.

SPOTTING A TAKER — harder than it sounds
  Takers are usually pleasant to anyone with power over
  them. Two reliable tells:
    ① how they treat people who CANNOT help them
    ② "I" versus "we" when describing shared work
  ⇒ and note Grant's finding that agreeable/disagreeable
    is a separate axis from giver/taker. The most
    valuable person in an organisation is often a
    DISAGREEABLE GIVER — gruff, blunt, and genuinely
    working in your interest. The most dangerous is the
    agreeable taker.

POWERLESS COMMUNICATION
  Asking questions, speaking tentatively, admitting
  weakness — outperforms assertive communication for
  givers in influence and selling, because it signals
  you are not trying to win.
  ⚠ this cuts against file 04's hedging advice, and the
    resolution is the same one: bounding your claim is
    fine, apologising for having one is not.

CHUNKING YOUR GIVING
  Concentrate helping into blocks rather than scattering
  it through the day. Same total help, far lower cost,
  because the cost was mostly interruption.
● Amber · extract only

The taxonomy, otherish giving, the five-minute favour and the disagreeable-giver observation are the value, and they are above. Grant writes very well and the book is a pleasure; it is also built in the standard one-study-per-anecdote style, so the reading time buys you enjoyment rather than additional information.

Already yours: the five-minute favour appears in sharp-mind file 05 §07. Nothing in master-yourself covers the giver/taker taxonomy.

Book 10

Emotional Intelligence

Daniel Goleman · 1995 · ~350 pages

The one idea

A set of emotional competences — recognising and managing emotion in yourself and others — matters for life outcomes, and unlike IQ they can be deliberately improved.

What is worth taking
⭐ THE FOUR DOMAINS — two axes, and this is the whole model
                  RECOGNITION        REGULATION
               ┌─────────────────┬─────────────────┐
     SELF      │ SELF-AWARENESS  │ SELF-MANAGEMENT │
               │ knowing what    │ doing something │
               │ you feel, as    │ other than the  │
               │ you feel it     │ first impulse   │
               ├─────────────────┼─────────────────┤
     OTHERS    │ SOCIAL          │ RELATIONSHIP    │
               │ AWARENESS       │ MANAGEMENT      │
               │ reading the     │ influencing,    │
               │ room            │ repairing,      │
               │                 │ leading         │
               └─────────────────┴─────────────────┘
  ⇒ the value of the grid is DIAGNOSTIC: most people are
    strong in one column and assume the other follows.
    Reading a room well and doing nothing differently is
    the most common pattern.

THE AMYGDALA HIJACK
  The emotional response can begin before the slower
  cortical processing finishes — so you can act before
  you have thought, and reconstruct the reason
  afterwards.
  ⇒ the practical value is vocabulary: "that was a
    hijack" is a more useful thing to say to yourself
    than "I lost it", because it implies a mechanism
    with a countermeasure (delay, breathe, leave).
  ⚠ the neuroscience as popularised here is simplified
    well past what the research supports. Treat it as
    a useful metaphor, not an anatomy lesson.

AFFECT LABELLING
  Naming an emotion reduces its intensity. One of the
  better-supported findings in the book, and it connects
  directly to Voss's labelling at the top of this file —
  naming works on yourself for the same reason it works
  on other people.
● Amber, close to red · take the grid, distrust the headline

The four domains are worth having and the term "emotional intelligence" earned its place in the language. But the book's headline claim — that EQ matters more than IQ for success, popularly transmitted as something like "80 percent versus 20 percent" — is not supported by the evidence, and Goleman was criticised for it by Salovey and Mayer, the researchers who coined the term and whose work he popularised.

What the research actually supports is more modest: measured emotional intelligence predicts some work outcomes, with small-to-moderate effect sizes, and overlaps substantially with the personality traits conscientiousness and agreeableness — so part of what it measures is not new. That is a real finding. It is not the finding the book sells.

Take the four domains. Discard the magnitudes.

Already yours: the four domains with the same 2×2, plus affect labelling and emotion regulation, are in master-yourself/phase-1, file 02 §7–8.

Book 11

The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work

John Gottman · 1999 · ~280 pages

The one idea

Relationship failure is predictable from specific observable behaviours — and the predictors live in small daily interactions rather than in big conflicts.

What the book gives you — and it transfers to work almost unchanged
⭐ THE FOUR HORSEMEN — the behaviours that predict failure
  ① CRITICISM     attacking CHARACTER rather than
                  behaviour
                  complaint: "you didn't send the file"
                  criticism: "you never follow through"
  ② ⭐ CONTEMPT   the single strongest predictor.
                  Eye-rolling, sarcasm, mockery,
                  name-calling, talking down. Contempt
                  is criticism plus superiority, and it
                  is corrosive in a way nothing else is
  ③ DEFENSIVENESS counter-attacking, or playing the
                  innocent victim. Both are ways of
                  saying "the problem is not me"
  ④ STONEWALLING  withdrawal, going blank, shutting
                  down. Usually a flooding response
                  rather than indifference

  THE ANTIDOTES
    criticism     → complain about the specific
                    behaviour, and state a need
    contempt      → build a culture of appreciation.
                    ⇒ THIS IS PREVENTIVE, NOT A REPAIR.
                      Contempt grows in the absence of
                      expressed appreciation over months
    defensiveness → accept a part of it. Any part
    stonewalling  → stop, self-soothe for twenty
                    minutes, and RETURN. Leaving without
                    returning is the damaging version

⭐ THE 5:1 RATIO
  Stable relationships run roughly five positive
  interactions for every negative one DURING CONFLICT.
  ⇒ the practical reading: criticism is not forbidden,
    it is EXPENSIVE. You have to have banked the credit.

⭐ BIDS FOR CONNECTION AND TURNING TOWARD
  A "bid" is any small attempt at connection — "look at
  that bird", a question, a joke. You can turn TOWARD
  (engage), AWAY (miss it), or AGAINST (snap).
  ⇒ turning toward small bids is the most predictive
    daily habit Gottman identifies, and it costs
    seconds. It is also exactly what "do you see me?"
    means in sharp-mind file 05 §01.

69% OF CONFLICTS ARE PERPETUAL
  Most recurring disagreements are not solvable — they
  come from stable differences in personality or values.
  ⇒ THE GOAL IS DIALOGUE, NOT RESOLUTION. Couples who
    do well are not the ones who solved these; they are
    the ones who can discuss them with humour and
    affection instead of gridlock.
  ⇒ this transfers directly to long-running work
    disagreements. Some of them are not going to be
    resolved, and treating them as solvable is what
    makes them toxic.

REPAIR ATTEMPTS
  Any effort to de-escalate mid-conflict — a joke, a
  softening, a hand on the arm, "wait, let me start
  again". Whether repair attempts are ACCEPTED matters
  more than how severe the conflict was.
● Amber · extract only, and note the caveat

The Four Horsemen, the ratio, bids and perpetual problems are the book and they are above. The remainder is exercises and questionnaires for couples, which is either exactly what you want or not relevant at all.

The honest caveat: Gottman's famous predictive-accuracy figures — the "I can predict divorce with 90-plus percent accuracy from a short conversation" claim — have been criticised on methodological grounds, chiefly that several of the models were fitted and evaluated on overlapping data rather than genuinely predicting new couples. The behavioural observations — the horsemen, contempt's toxicity, turning toward — are widely replicated and robust. The dramatic accuracy numbers are the weak part.

Already yours: the Four Horsemen, the 5:1 ratio, repair attempts and turning toward are in master-yourself/phase-9, file 03 §08.

Reference

Don't bother — and what to read instead

● Red · the people-and-influence shelf
  • The body-language genre — The Definitive Book of Body Language, What Every Body Is Saying, and the airport shelf generally. The central promise — that specific gestures reliably decode specific internal states — does not survive testing. Crossed arms mean cold, or comfortable, or crossed arms. Deception-detection research consistently finds untrained people barely above chance and self-described experts frequently no better. Instead: file 04, which is about what you signal — which is real and controllable — rather than about decoding other people, which mostly is not.
  • The "7-38-55 rule" — that words are 7% of communication, tone 38%, body language 55%. The most misquoted statistic in the field. Mehrabian's studies concerned a narrow case: judging a speaker's attitude when the verbal and vocal channels were deliberately inconsistent. Mehrabian himself has said repeatedly that it should not be generalised. Any book that leads with it has told you something useful about the book.
  • Surrounded by Idiots, and the four-colour personality genre. The underlying DISC-style typology has poor evidence for validity or predictive power, and sorting colleagues into four boxes is worse than useless — it licenses you to stop actually observing them. Instead: file 05 §03, which treats register as something you learn per person rather than a type you assign.
  • MBTI-based books — same problem. Poor test-retest reliability, and the dichotomies are not how the traits are actually distributed. Interesting as a conversation starter; not a basis for decisions about people.
  • Men Are from Mars, Women Are from Venus — the communication differences it describes as categorical are, in the research, small and heavily overlapping. It produces confident expectations about individuals that are usually wrong.
  • The pickup / "game" genre — built on a model of interaction as a system to be defeated, which is both unpleasant and, on its own terms, less effective than genuine interest. Instead: file 06 §06.
