Sharp Mind · History · Topic 11 of 20

The Enlightenment. The century that decided ordinary people could think for themselves, and should.

In 1762 a Protestant shopkeeper in Toulouse named Jean Calas was tortured and executed for a murder that almost certainly never happened. A sixty-seven-year-old writer living near Geneva read about the case, decided it was religious prejudice dressed up as justice, and spent three years shaming France until the verdict was overturned. The writer was Voltaire. The episode captures the Enlightenment in miniature: a belief that reason could expose cruelty, that public opinion could check power, and that a pen could beat a court. Most of the political ideas you take for granted, from human rights to the separation of powers, were argued into shape in that century.

Question 1 · What is it?

A movement of ideas, roughly 1680 to 1800

The Enlightenment was a broad intellectual movement in Europe and its colonies that tried to apply reason and evidence to religion, politics, law and society, as the Scientific Revolution had applied them to nature.

Chapter 01

Kant's answer: dare to know

In 1784 a Berlin magazine asked its readers a question: what is enlightenment? The philosopher Immanuel Kant, then sixty and a professor in Königsberg, sent the most famous reply.

Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's own understanding without the guidance of another. … Sapere aude! Have courage to use your own understanding!

Immanuel Kant · "An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?" · 1784

"Immaturity" here means letting a priest, a ruler or a book do your thinking for you. Kant called it "self-incurred" because, he said, the cause is usually not lack of intelligence but lack of courage. Sapere aude is Latin for "dare to know". Notice what Kant did not say. He did not say his own age was already enlightened. He said it was an age of enlightenment: a process under way, not a finished state. The Enlightenment was less a set of answers than a permission to ask questions in public.

Enlightenment thinkers disagreed about a great deal, but most shared five commitments.

The geography was wide. Paris was the most famous centre, but Edinburgh produced a remarkable group, the Scottish Enlightenment, including the philosopher David Hume and the economist Adam Smith. There were active circles in Amsterdam, Berlin, Milan, Naples, Philadelphia and elsewhere.

Chapter 03

One Enlightenment, or many?

Historians increasingly speak of Enlightenments in the plural. The historian Jonathan Israel has argued for a sharp split between a radical Enlightenment, influenced by the Dutch philosopher Baruch Spinoza, which rejected religious authority altogether and pushed for democracy and equality, and a moderate Enlightenment, such as Locke's or Voltaire's, which wanted reform within a world that kept God, monarchy and property. His interpretation is influential and also disputed.

Others stress national differences. The French writers were often anticlerical, hostile to the Catholic Church. The British and Americans were mostly Protestant believers who wanted toleration rather than unbelief. There was even a Catholic Enlightenment of reforming clergy. What united them was method more than conclusions: the habit of putting every institution on trial before the public.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The ideas, and the machinery that spread them

The thinkers matter, but so does the infrastructure: cheap print, new places to talk, and rulers willing to listen, or at least to borrow.

Chapter 04

Locke and Montesquieu: the architecture of government

The English philosopher John Locke published his Two Treatises of Government in 1689, just after England's Glorious Revolution had replaced one king with another by act of Parliament. Locke argued that people are born with natural rights to life, liberty and property. They create government by agreement, a social contract, to protect those rights. A government that violates them loses its legitimacy, and the people may resist. In the same year he published A Letter Concerning Toleration, arguing that the state has no business forcing anyone's conscience.

The French judge and writer Montesquieu took the next step in The Spirit of the Laws (1748). Studying many countries, and admiring what he saw in England, he argued that liberty is safest when power is divided: one body makes laws, another carries them out, a third judges disputes. This is the separation of powers. Its genius is that it does not depend on good rulers. It assumes rulers will try to grab power and arranges for them to block each other. The framers of the United States Constitution of 1787 cited Montesquieu constantly. The modern version is covered in Separation of powers.

Chapter 05

Voltaire, Rousseau and Hume: three different temperaments

Voltaire (1694–1778)

Poet, playwright, historian and the century's greatest campaigner. He attacked religious fanaticism and the power of the Church with wit, most famously in the short novel Candide (1759). His fight for Calas, described at the top of this page, ended with the verdict reversed in 1765. He was a reformer, not a democrat, and was happy to flatter kings.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)

A Genevan who distrusted much of the "progress" his friends celebrated, arguing that civilisation had corrupted people. The Social Contract (1762) opens: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." He argued that legitimate laws express the "general will" of the people, an idea that inspired democrats and that critics later blamed for justifying tyranny in the people's name.

David Hume (1711–1776)

The Scottish philosopher pushed scepticism furthest. He argued that we never directly observe cause and effect, only one thing regularly following another, and that reason alone cannot tell us what we ought to do. He was cautious in public and kept some of his sharpest writing on religion unpublished until after his death.

The point of seeing them together is that the Enlightenment was an argument, not a choir. Rousseau and Voltaire came to loathe each other. Hume's scepticism undermined the confident rationalism of others. That internal criticism is one reason the movement produced ideas strong enough to survive.

Chapter 06

Beccaria and the reform of punishment

One of the most practical Enlightenment books was written by a 26-year-old Milanese nobleman, Cesare Beccaria. On Crimes and Punishments (1764) argued that torture is both cruel and useless, since it tests a suspect's endurance rather than guilt; that punishments should be proportionate, certain and public; and that the death penalty is neither necessary nor effective. It was translated quickly and read across Europe and America.

Its effect was real. In 1786 the Grand Duchy of Tuscany, under Grand Duke Leopold, became one of the first states in the world to abolish the death penalty. Many European states abolished judicial torture during the late eighteenth century. The modern idea that punishment must be justified by its purpose, not by revenge, comes largely from this one short book.

Chapter 07

The machinery: print, salons, coffeehouses

Ideas need infrastructure. The great project of the French Enlightenment was the Encyclopédie, edited by Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert and published from 1751 to 1772 in seventeen volumes of text and eleven of illustrations. It aimed to sum up all knowledge, including detailed plates of crafts and machines, and slipped critical ideas into ordinary entries. The French authorities banned it in 1759; publication continued anyway, partly through official tolerance behind the scenes.

Places to talk

In Paris, salons were regular gatherings in private homes, often hosted by educated women such as Madame Geoffrin, where writers, aristocrats and visitors discussed new books. In London, Edinburgh and Amsterdam, coffeehouses played a similar role for a wider public, with newspapers on the tables. Learned academies, reading clubs and Masonic lodges added more meeting places.

Ways around the censor

Books banned in France were printed in the Dutch Republic, Switzerland or elsewhere and smuggled back. Publishing anonymously, or as a supposed translation, gave cover. The historian Robert Darnton has shown how much of what Parisians actually read was cheap, illegal and often scandalous rather than philosophical.

The German philosopher Jürgen Habermas later called this new space the public sphere: a place between private life and the state where private people came together to discuss public matters. Public opinion became a power that kings had to reckon with, which is exactly what Voltaire exploited in the Calas case.

Chapter 08

Enlightened despots and paper revolutions

Some monarchs adopted Enlightenment language. Frederick the Great of Prussia corresponded with Voltaire, restricted torture soon after taking the throne in 1740 and called himself the first servant of the state. Catherine the Great of Russia bought Diderot's library and invited him to St Petersburg. Joseph II of Austria issued an Edict of Toleration in 1781 and began abolishing serfdom. Historians call them enlightened absolutists, or enlightened despots: rulers who used reason to strengthen and modernise their states, while keeping power firmly in their own hands.

Elsewhere the ideas went further. The American Declaration of Independence (1776) states that all men are created equal with unalienable rights, in language close to Locke's. The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) declared that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Within one generation, ideas argued in salons had become the founding documents of new states. Volume 20 tells what happened next.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The operating system of modern politics, with known bugs

Much of how modern democracies are built comes from this period. So do some of the sharpest criticisms of them.

Chapter 09

What we inherited

Look at almost any modern democratic constitution and you find Enlightenment ideas: rights held by individuals, government by consent, separation of powers, freedom of religion and of the press, equality before the law, and punishment limited by law. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations in 1948, opens by recognising the "inherent dignity" and "equal and inalienable rights" of all members of the human family, language with clear Enlightenment roots, though its drafters came from many traditions.

Less obviously, the Enlightenment gave us the habit of judging institutions by their results. Adam Smith asked what actually makes nations prosperous. Jeremy Bentham, writing at the end of the century, proposed judging laws by whether they increase overall happiness, the basis of outcome-based ethics. The question "does this policy work, and for whom?" is an Enlightenment question.

Chapter 10

The great contradiction: universal rights, limited people

The movement preached universal rights while many of its leading figures accepted or profited from slavery and the exclusion of women. Locke held shares in the Royal African Company, which traded in enslaved Africans. Thomas Jefferson, who wrote "all men are created equal", enslaved more than six hundred people over his lifetime. Hume and Kant both wrote passages ranking human races that are plainly racist.

Yet the same ideas were the main weapons used against these injustices. In 1788 a French society, the Friends of the Blacks, campaigned against slavery; its members included the philosopher Condorcet, who also argued for women's rights. In 1791 the writer Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman, mirroring the 1789 text; she was executed in 1793. Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication followed in 1792. The Haitian revolutionaries quoted the Rights of Man back to France. The ideals were narrower in practice than in principle, and that gap is what later reformers used as a lever.

Chapter 11

The case against: reason as domination

In the twentieth century some thinkers argued the problem went deeper than hypocrisy. In Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944, revised 1947), written in exile during the Second World War, the German philosophers Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno argued that Enlightenment reason, by treating everything as something to measure and control, could turn into a tool of domination, ending in modern bureaucracy, propaganda and even totalitarianism. Postcolonial critics later argued that ideas of "civilisation" and "progress" helped justify colonial rule.

Defenders reply that the cure for the misuse of reason is more reason, not less. The psychologist Steven Pinker, in Enlightenment Now (2018), argued with data on life expectancy, poverty, literacy and violence that the values of reason, science and humanism have made life dramatically better since the eighteenth century. His critics accused him of simplifying both the history and the statistics. This argument, whether the Enlightenment is the source of modern freedom or of modern domination, is still very much alive.

What it got right, and what it missed

Chapter 13

The critics who were partly right

The Irish-born politician Edmund Burke, in Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), warned that tearing down institutions in the name of abstract rights would end in violence and dictatorship, years before the Terror and Napoleon confirmed much of his prediction. He argued that traditions contain practical wisdom that no single generation can fully reason out. Modern conservatism often traces itself to Burke.

The German writer Johann Gottfried Herder argued that each people has its own language and culture that cannot be ranked on one scale of reason, an idea that fed both respect for cultural difference and, later, nationalism. The Romantic movement that followed stressed feeling, nature and imagination over cold calculation. Many of these critiques were absorbed rather than defeated: modern liberal democracies combine Enlightenment rights with respect for tradition, culture and gradual reform.

Chapter 15

A more global Enlightenment

Historians are also widening the story. Some study reformers outside Europe who argued for reason, education and toleration in their own traditions, such as the Bengali reformer Ram Mohan Roy in the early nineteenth century, or the debates of Ottoman, Chinese and Japanese scholars of the same era. Others trace how European thinkers drew on reports of China, Persia and the Americas, often idealised, to criticise their own societies. Montesquieu's Persian Letters (1721) did exactly that, viewing France through the eyes of imaginary Persian visitors.

Kant's own point suggests the right attitude. Enlightenment is a process, not a possession. No society has finished it, and none owns it. The live question is not whether to be "for" or "against" the Enlightenment, but how to keep its best habits, free inquiry, toleration and equal rights, while repairing the blind spots its own principles exposed.

The core idea

The Enlightenment, roughly 1680–1800, applied reason and evidence to religion, politics and law. It produced natural rights, the social contract, the separation of powers and religious toleration, spread them through print, salons and coffeehouses, and wrote them into new constitutions. Its universal ideals were applied narrowly, and the argument over its legacy continues.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1689: Locke's Two Treatises; 1748: Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws.
  • 1751–1772: the Encyclopédie; 1764: Beccaria against torture.
  • 1776 and 1789: the American and French declarations.
  • 1784: Kant's "Dare to know".
The common mistake

Treating the Enlightenment as one unified doctrine of cold reason. It was an argument among people who disagreed, and many of its sharpest critics, like Rousseau and Hume, were inside it.

Test yourself

Can you explain how the same ideas could justify Jefferson's Declaration and be used against slavery? If you can, you have understood the gap between principle and practice.

The words, in plain words
Enlightenment
The movement of roughly 1680–1800 that applied reason and evidence to society, religion and government.
Sapere aude
Latin for "dare to know", Kant's motto for the Enlightenment.
Natural rights
Rights people have simply by being human, not by permission of a ruler.
Social contract
The idea that government rests on an agreement among the governed.
Separation of powers
Dividing law-making, law-enforcing and judging between different bodies.
General will
Rousseau's term for what the people as a whole rightly want for the common good.
Toleration
Allowing beliefs and religions the state does not share.
Anticlerical
Opposed to the power of the clergy and the Church in public life.
Encyclopédie
The French reference work, 1751–1772, that summed up knowledge and spread critical ideas.
Salon
A regular private gathering for conversation about books and ideas, often hosted by women.
Public sphere
The space where private people discuss public matters and form public opinion.
Enlightened absolutism
Rule by monarchs who used reforming ideas while keeping total power.
Counter-Enlightenment
Thinkers who criticised the Enlightenment's faith in abstract reason and progress.
