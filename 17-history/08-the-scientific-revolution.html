Sharp Mind · History · Topic 08 of 20

The Scientific Revolution. The moment people agreed to settle arguments by looking, not by quoting.

In 1500 an educated European believed the Earth sat still at the centre of the universe, that the heavens were made of a perfect substance unlike anything on Earth, and that the best way to learn about the body was to read a Greek doctor who had died thirteen centuries earlier. By 1700 all three beliefs were gone among the leading thinkers. What replaced them was not just a new set of facts. It was a new way of deciding what counts as a fact. That method is still running, and you use its results every time you take a medicine or check the weather.

Question 1 · What is it?

About 150 years that changed the rules of knowledge

The Scientific Revolution is the name historians give to a cluster of changes in European thought, usually dated from 1543 to 1687, that produced recognisably modern science.

Chapter 01

Two books in one year

Historians like to start in 1543 because two books appeared that year. The first was On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres by Nicolaus Copernicus, a Polish church administrator and astronomer. It put the Sun, not the Earth, at the centre of the planets' movements. Copernicus died the same year, reportedly receiving a printed copy on his deathbed.

The second was On the Fabric of the Human Body by Andreas Vesalius, a 28-year-old anatomy teacher at the University of Padua. For over a thousand years, medical teaching had followed the Greek physician Galen, who worked in the second century CE and had dissected mainly animals, not humans. Vesalius dissected human bodies himself and showed, with superb illustrations, that Galen was wrong on a number of points. For example, Galen described a network of blood vessels at the base of the brain that exists in some animals but not in people.

The two books share a quiet message. The old authorities could be checked, and when they were checked they were sometimes wrong. Neither author set out to overturn everything. Copernicus still used perfect circles; Vesalius still believed much of Galen's theory. But they opened a door.

Chapter 02

What actually changed

It helps to name the shifts precisely, because each one is still part of how science works.

Before (roughly 1500)After (roughly 1700)Why it mattered
Earth at the centre; heavens perfect and unchangingSun at the centre of the planets; the same laws on Earth and in the skyNature became one system that could be studied as a whole
Knowledge mainly from ancient texts, above all Aristotle and GalenKnowledge from observation and experiment that others could repeatEvidence began to outrank reputation
Explanation by purpose ("a stone falls because it seeks its natural place")Explanation by mechanism and mathematics ("it falls by this law, at this rate")Predictions became precise and testable
Learning held by universities and the ChurchNew societies, journals and networks of lettersResults were shared, checked and argued about in public

The deepest of these is the third. Aristotle explained things by their purpose, or "final cause": what they were for. The new thinkers increasingly treated nature as a mechanism, like a clock, explained by the movement of its parts and described with numbers. Galileo put it memorably in 1623, writing that the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics.

Chapter 03

Was it really a revolution?

The term itself became popular among historians in the mid-twentieth century, through writers such as Alexandre Koyré and Herbert Butterfield. Since then it has been questioned. The historian Steven Shapin opened his 1996 book with a sentence that every student of the subject knows:

"There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it."

Steven Shapin · The Scientific Revolution · 1996

His point was that the word "revolution" suggests something sudden, unified and complete. The real changes took well over a century, happened unevenly, and grew out of medieval and Islamic work that came before. Many of the heroes also believed things we would now call unscientific. Most historians keep the term but use it carefully, as a label for a real and very large change that was neither sudden nor the work of a few lone geniuses.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Data, instruments, method and institutions

The revolution was not a single idea. It was four things reinforcing each other: better data, new instruments, explicit methods, and new ways of sharing results.

Chapter 04

The relay from Copernicus to Newton

The rebuilding of the cosmos is the best example of how the pieces fitted together, because it took four very different people, each depending on the one before.

How the new cosmos was built, step by step
 1543  COPERNICUS   idea: the Sun at the centre       (still circles, still inaccurate)
          |
 1570s-1590s  TYCHO BRAHE   data: the best naked-eye measurements ever made
          |
 1609-1619  KEPLER   pattern: orbits are ellipses, with exact mathematical laws
          |
 1610  GALILEO      evidence: telescope shows moons of Jupiter, phases of Venus
          |
 1687  NEWTON       explanation: one law of gravity produces Kepler's ellipses

Tycho Brahe, a Danish nobleman, built an observatory on an island and spent about twenty years measuring planetary positions with large instruments, before telescopes existed, more accurately than anyone before him. He also showed that a new star in 1572 and a comet in 1577 lay far beyond the Moon, so the heavens were not unchanging after all.

Johannes Kepler, his German assistant, inherited the data when Tycho died in 1601. He spent years trying to fit the orbit of Mars to circles and failed by a tiny amount, about eight minutes of arc, roughly a quarter of the width of the full Moon. He refused to ignore the gap. That refusal led him to ellipses, slightly squashed circles, and to three exact laws of planetary motion. A small error, taken seriously, overturned two thousand years of perfect circles.

Galileo's telescope supplied visual evidence, as Volume 24 tells. Finally Isaac Newton, in his Principia of 1687, showed that one law of universal gravitation, plus his laws of motion, mathematically produced Kepler's ellipses. The puzzle was complete. Newton himself put the debt politely, in a 1676 letter to the rival scientist Robert Hooke: "If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants."

Chapter 05

New instruments, new worlds

Instruments did more than measure. They revealed things that no amount of reading could have predicted.

Seeing the very small

Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) showed magnified fleas and lice in huge engravings and used the word "cell" for the tiny boxes he saw in cork. In the 1670s the Dutch draper Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, grinding his own tiny lenses, described living "animalcules" in pond water and in scrapings from teeth: the first observations of microorganisms.

Measuring the invisible

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli made the first mercury barometer, showing that air has weight and pressure. Robert Boyle used an air pump to create a partial vacuum and studied how gas pressure and volume relate, now called Boyle's law (1662). Thermometers turned "hot" and "cold" into numbers.

The pattern matters. A new instrument extends the senses, and an extended sense finds things the old theories never imagined. The same pattern recurs in every later age of science, from X-rays to gene sequencers.

Chapter 06

The body as a machine: Harvey and the blood

Galen had taught that blood was made continuously in the liver from food and consumed by the body. In 1628 the English physician William Harvey published a short book arguing that the heart is a pump and that the same blood circulates round and round.

His most persuasive argument was a simple calculation. He estimated how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat and multiplied by the number of beats. Even with cautious figures, the total in an hour far exceeded the weight of the whole body. The liver could not possibly make that much. The blood had to be going round. A piece of arithmetic anyone could check defeated fourteen centuries of authority. Harvey could not see how blood passed from arteries to veins; the tiny capillaries were seen with a microscope by Marcello Malpighi a few decades later.

Chapter 07

Two recipes for knowing: Bacon and Descartes

As discoveries piled up, writers tried to describe the method behind them. Two became especially influential, and they disagreed.

Francis Bacon: collect, then generalise

The English lawyer and statesman argued, in Novum Organum (1620), that knowledge should be built up from many careful observations and experiments, then cautiously generalised. This is induction: from particular cases to a general rule. He also warned against the mind's habitual errors, which he called "idols". He saw science as a cooperative project for human benefit.

René Descartes: doubt, then deduce

The French philosopher, in his Discourse on Method (1637), proposed doubting everything that could be doubted and rebuilding knowledge by step-by-step reasoning from certain first principles, like geometry. This is deduction: from general principles to particular conclusions. He pictured the physical world, including animal bodies, as pure mechanism.

Modern science uses both. You propose a general idea, deduce what it predicts, and test the prediction against observation. Neither pure collecting nor pure reasoning works alone, and the long argument between the two traditions helped people see why.

Chapter 08

Science becomes social: societies and journals

Perhaps the most underrated change was organisational. In 1660 a group in London founded what became the Royal Society. Its motto, Nullius in verba, means roughly "take nobody's word for it". In 1665 it began publishing Philosophical Transactions, one of the two earliest scientific journals, alongside the French Journal des sçavans of the same year. In 1666 the French crown founded the Académie des sciences.

These bodies did something new. Experiments were performed before witnesses, written up in detail so others could repeat them, and published for anyone to criticise. Scholars across Europe exchanged letters constantly, forming what they called the Republic of Letters, an informal community that crossed borders and, sometimes, religious divides. Science became a public, checkable conversation rather than private wisdom, which is arguably the change that made all the others last.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Why there, why then, and what it set off

Explaining why this happened in seventeenth-century Europe is one of history's great open questions. What followed from it is less disputed.

Chapter 09

Why Europe? Several partial answers

The British biochemist and historian Joseph Needham spent decades documenting China's huge record of invention, including paper, printing, the compass and gunpowder. He then asked why modern science nevertheless took shape in Europe. This is known as the Needham question, and it has no agreed answer. Historians usually point to a combination of factors:

  • Printing. From the 1450s the press made identical copies of diagrams, tables and data cheap, so results could be compared exactly. See What cheap copying did to authority.
  • Inherited learning. Greek works, many preserved, translated and extended in the Islamic world, reached Europe through Arabic and Latin translations. Ibn al-Haytham's work on optics, written around 1011–1021, influenced European writers for centuries. Some historians argue that geometrical devices used by Copernicus resemble those of astronomers at Maragha in Persia; how he learned of them is still debated.
  • Competition between states. Europe was split into rival kingdoms and cities. A thinker in trouble in one place could publish in another, as several did in the Dutch Republic.
  • Practical demand. Navigation, gunnery, mining and trade needed better maps, clocks and mathematics, and paid people to provide them.

None of these alone is enough, and comparisons with China, India and the Islamic world are genuinely contested. What is clear is that the revolution drew on knowledge from far beyond Europe, including along the routes described in The Silk Road.

Chapter 10

Science and religion: not the war you heard about

Galileo's trial in 1633, when the Roman Inquisition forced him to renounce the view that the Earth moves, is often told as the start of an eternal war between science and religion. Most historians of science reject that framing, sometimes called the conflict thesis.

The facts are more mixed. Copernicus was a church official. Kepler was a devout Lutheran who saw his laws as reading God's design. Boyle funded lectures defending Christianity. Newton wrote more on theology and on alchemy, the attempt to transform substances such as metals, than on physics. The economist John Maynard Keynes, who bought many of Newton's private papers, famously concluded that Newton "was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians."

The Galileo affair was real and it had a chilling effect, especially in Catholic Italy. But it was also about Church politics after the Reformation, Galileo's sharp tongue, and the fact that in 1633 the evidence for a moving Earth was strong but not yet conclusive. In 1992 Pope John Paul II formally acknowledged that the Church had erred. The real conflict was over who had the authority to settle questions about nature, not over faith as such.

Chapter 11

What it set in motion

The success of Newton's physics impressed educated Europe enormously. If one law could explain the planets, perhaps reason could also explain and improve society, government and morality. That hope became the Enlightenment, the next great movement, which is why the two topics are always taught together.

The link to the Industrial Revolution is more debated. Early steam engines were mostly built by practical engineers rather than university scientists. But the culture the Scientific Revolution created, of measuring, testing and publishing, and of believing nature could be understood and used, spread into workshops and mines. Some historians, such as Joel Mokyr, stress this culture of "useful knowledge" as a key cause of sustained economic growth. Volume 20 covers the industrial side in Coal, water and the end of muscle.

The biggest legacy is the method itself. Anyone, anywhere, can in principle check a result, and a result that cannot be reproduced does not count. That rule is why modern medicine, engineering and computing exist.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A great achievement with real blind spots

Admiring the revolution does not require pretending it was neutral, complete or open to everyone.

Chapter 13

The people left out of the picture

The standard cast is male and European, but the period was more varied. Margaret Cavendish, an English duchess, published works of natural philosophy and in 1667 became the first woman to attend a meeting of the Royal Society, as a visitor; she was not admitted as a member. Maria Sibylla Merian, a German-born artist and naturalist, travelled to Suriname in 1699 and published detailed studies of insect life cycles, at a time when many still believed insects arose spontaneously from mud.

Much knowledge also came from people whose names were not recorded: instrument makers, midwives, miners, and Indigenous guides and healers in the Americas and Asia, whose knowledge of plants such as cinchona bark, the source of the malaria drug quinine, entered European medicine. Recovering these contributions does not shrink the revolution. It shows that knowledge was always more collective than the hero stories suggest.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Is the method still working?

The revolution's rules are under pressure and under renewal at the same time. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 15

Why the history still matters

Public trust in science has become politically divided in several countries. Knowing the history helps in a practical way. The revolution did not produce certainty; it produced a method for being less wrong over time. Kepler's eight minutes of arc, Harvey's arithmetic and the Royal Society's witnesses all point to the same habit: take small discrepancies seriously, show your working, and let others check.

The historians' debate about the term "revolution" will continue, and so will research on the non-European roots of modern science. Expect the story to become more global and less centred on a few famous men. The core rule, evidence that anyone can check outranks any authority, is likely to outlast every particular theory it has produced.

The core idea

Between roughly 1543 and 1687, European thinkers replaced reliance on ancient authority with observation, experiment and mathematics, rebuilt the cosmos (Copernicus to Newton) and the body (Vesalius, Harvey), and created institutions that made knowledge public and checkable. It drew on older Greek and Islamic work, and it was slower and more collective than the hero stories suggest.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1543: Copernicus and Vesalius publish.
  • 1628: Harvey on the circulation of the blood.
  • 1660: the Royal Society; 1665: the first scientific journals.
  • 1687: Newton's Principia.
The common mistake

Telling it as science versus religion, or as a few geniuses against the dark. Most of the pioneers were religious, and the real change was a new rule for who settles questions about nature.
