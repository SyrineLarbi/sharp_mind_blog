Sharp Mind · History · Topic 18 of 20

The history of the Middle East. Where farming, writing and three world faiths began, and where the past is never quite over.

The Middle East appears in the news mostly as a place of wars, oil and religious conflict. That is a very thin slice of a very long story. This is the region where people first planted wheat, built cities, wrote things down and wrote laws. It is the birthplace of Judaism, Christianity and Islam. For centuries its cities were the richest and most learned on Earth. Its modern troubles make much more sense once you see the layers underneath them, and once you notice how recent some of them are.

Question 1 · What is it?

A region named by outsiders, crowded with peoples and faiths

The term itself is a clue. It describes the region by its distance from Europe, not by anything the people living there would have chosen.

Chapter 01

"Middle" of what?

"Middle East" became common in English in the early twentieth century, when British and American strategists needed a word for the lands between the "Near East" (the Ottoman Balkans and Turkey) and the "Far East" (China and Japan). The American naval writer Alfred Thayer Mahan is often credited with popularising it in 1902. The name describes the region from London's point of view. Arabic speakers often use it today too, but older local names divided the world differently, by faith and by empire.

There is no single official definition. The core usually includes Egypt, Israel and the Palestinian territories, Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Iraq, Iran, Turkey and the states of the Arabian Peninsula: Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait. Wider definitions add North Africa, and the term "MENA" (Middle East and North Africa) is often used for that larger area. Egypt sits in Africa geographically but belongs to the Middle East politically and culturally, which is why its ancient history also appears in The history of Africa.

Chapter 02

Religion adds another layer. Most people are Muslim, and most Muslims in the region are Sunni. Shia Muslims are the majority in Iran, Iraq and Bahrain, and a large share in Lebanon. There are also ancient Christian communities, such as the Copts of Egypt and the Maronites of Lebanon, along with Jews, Druze, Yazidis and others. Knowing who lives where explains much of the region's politics, because many states contain several of these groups inside borders none of them chose.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Five layers, from the first farms to the modern borders

The region's history stacks up in layers: the ancient empires, the three faiths, the caliphates, the Ottoman and Persian empires, and the settlement after 1918. Each layer is still visible in the one above it.

Chapter 03

The cradle: the first farms, cities, scripts and laws

An arc of fertile land runs from the Nile up through the Levant and down the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Archaeologists call it the Fertile Crescent. Here, from around 10,000 years ago, people first domesticated wheat, barley, sheep and goats. In southern Iraq, the Sumerians built some of the first cities, and by about 3200 BCE they had invented writing, pressing wedge-shaped marks into clay. That script is called cuneiform, from the Latin for "wedge". It began, as Volume 17 explains, as a way of keeping accounts.

Around 1750 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had a list of laws carved onto a stone pillar, now in the Louvre in Paris. It set punishments for theft, fixed fees for doctors and builders, and included the principle often summed up as "an eye for an eye". Later, around 550 BCE, Cyrus the Great founded the Persian (Achaemenid) empire, the largest the world had yet seen. It governed many peoples through local rulers and generally let them keep their own religions. Many basic tools of civilisation, such as farming, cities, writing and written law, first appear in this region.

Chapter 04

Three faiths from one family tree

Judaism, Christianity and Islam all trace themselves back to Abraham, and all three see Jerusalem as holy. That is why they are called the Abrahamic religions. Judaism took shape among the ancient Israelites. Christianity began in the first century CE among the followers of Jesus of Nazareth, a Jew from Galilee, and became the official religion of the Roman empire in the fourth century.

Islam began in Arabia. Muhammad, born in Mecca around 570 CE, began preaching around 610. In 622 he and his followers moved to the town of Medina. That journey, the hijra, marks year one of the Islamic calendar. By his death in 632, much of Arabia had accepted Islam. Within about a century, Arab armies had conquered an area stretching from Spain to Central Asia, one of the fastest expansions in history.

Muhammad's death raised a question that still divides Muslims: who should lead the community? Most accepted leaders chosen from among his companions. A minority believed leadership belonged to his cousin and son-in-law Ali and Ali's descendants. That minority became the Shia ("the party of Ali"); the majority became the Sunni. In 680 Ali's son Husayn was killed at the Battle of Karbala in Iraq, an event Shia Muslims still mourn every year. The split began as a dispute about succession, not about core beliefs, though different practices and traditions grew from it over centuries.

Chapter 05

The caliphates and their golden age

The leader of the Muslim community was called the caliph, meaning "successor". The Umayyad dynasty ruled from Damascus from 661. In 750 it was overthrown by the Abbasids, who built Baghdad and made it a centre of learning, translation and trade, as Volume 18 describes. Scholars writing in Arabic, many of them Persian, Jewish or Christian, advanced mathematics, astronomy, medicine and philosophy.

Political unity did not last. Rival caliphates arose in Spain and Egypt, and real power often passed to military rulers. From 1096, European Crusaders attacked the Levant and took Jerusalem in 1099. The Kurdish-born sultan Saladin retook it in 1187, and the last Crusader stronghold fell in 1291. In 1258 the Mongols sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph there. Historians once called that the end of Islam's golden age. Many now argue that decline was slower and patchier than that single date suggests, since Cairo, Persian cities and later Istanbul remained great centres.

Chapter 06

Ottomans and Safavids: the last great empires

The Ottoman empire grew from a small Turkish state in north-west Anatolia around 1300. In 1453 it took Constantinople and made it its capital, Istanbul. At its height in the sixteenth century, under Suleiman the Magnificent, it ruled south-eastern Europe, Anatolia, the Arab lands from Iraq to Algeria, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. It lasted about six hundred years.

The Ottomans ran a multi-faith empire. Under the millet system, religious communities such as Orthodox Christians, Armenians and Jews ran many of their own affairs, including marriage and inheritance, under their own leaders. This was not equality, since non-Muslims paid extra taxes and faced limits. But it allowed many peoples to live side by side for centuries.

Next door, the Safavid dynasty took power in Iran in 1501 and made Shia Islam the state religion, converting a mostly Sunni population over the following centuries. This is the main reason Iran is the centre of the Shia world today. Ottoman Sunni and Safavid Shia empires fought for over a century, and the border between them roughly survives as the border between Turkey and Iran.

Chapter 07

1914–1923: the settlement that drew the modern map

The Ottoman empire joined the First World War on Germany's side and lost. During the war, Britain made three promises that did not fit together. In letters known as the Hussein–McMahon correspondence (1915–1916), it encouraged the Sharif of Mecca to lead an Arab revolt in return for Arab independence. In the secret Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916), it agreed with France to divide the Arab lands into zones of control. And in the Balfour Declaration (1917), it announced support for "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine.

After the war, the League of Nations gave Britain and France mandates, a form of supervised rule meant in theory to prepare territories for independence. France took Syria and Lebanon. Britain took Iraq, Palestine and Transjordan. Turkey, under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, fought off occupation and became a secular republic in 1923. Saudi Arabia was declared a kingdom in 1932. Many borders of today date from these years.

A common oversimplification

It is often said that "Sykes–Picot drew the borders". The final borders were actually set by later agreements and differ from the 1916 map. The deeper grievance is less about exact lines than about outsiders deciding them, after promises that contradicted each other.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Oil, one small land, and a series of turning points

The region's modern history affects energy prices, migration, security and religion far beyond its borders. Four threads explain most of it.

Chapter 08

Oil: the blessing with a catch

Oil was found in Iran in 1908, in Iraq in 1927 and in Saudi Arabia in 1938. The Gulf turned out to hold a large share of the world's proven oil reserves. At first Western companies controlled most of the profits. Over the following decades producer states took control, and in 1960 several founded OPEC, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries. In 1973, during a war with Israel, Arab producers cut supplies to countries that backed Israel. Oil prices roughly quadrupled, and the West learned how much it depended on the region.

Oil made some small Gulf states among the richest places on Earth per person. But it also had a hidden cost. Political scientists describe a rentier state: a government that lives on income from selling a resource rather than on taxes from its citizens. Such a government does not need to bargain with the people, so it has fewer reasons to share power. The broader idea that resource wealth can harm a country is called the resource curse. It is a tendency, not a law; Norway is the usual counter-example. Several Gulf states have also used oil money to build huge welfare systems and modern cities.

Chapter 09

Israel and the Palestinians

No subject in the region is more disputed, and the two sides often describe the same events in completely different words. Here is the basic sequence. Jewish immigration to Palestine grew under the British mandate, especially as persecution rose in Europe and after the Holocaust (Volume 21). In 1947 the United Nations proposed dividing Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state. Jewish leaders accepted; Arab leaders rejected it. In 1948 Israel declared independence, and neighbouring Arab states attacked.

Israelis remember 1948 as their War of Independence. Palestinians remember it as the Nakba, "the catastrophe", when around 700,000 Palestinians fled or were driven from their homes. In the following years, many Jews left or were pushed out of Arab countries, and most settled in Israel. In the Six-Day War of 1967, Israel took the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, the Golan Heights and the Sinai. Egypt made peace with Israel in 1979 and got the Sinai back. The Oslo Accords of 1993 created limited Palestinian self-rule but never produced a final agreement.

On 7 October 2023, Hamas, the armed group that had governed Gaza since 2007, attacked southern Israel, killing about 1,200 people and taking around 250 hostages. Israel's war in Gaza that followed killed tens of thousands of Palestinians, according to Gaza's health authorities, and destroyed much of the territory. The core dispute, two peoples claiming the same land, is unresolved, and the main proposals, two states or some other arrangement, each face deep opposition.

Chapter 10

1979, the year that reshaped the region

Three events in one year changed the politics of the Middle East for decades. In February 1979, the Iranian Revolution overthrew the Shah, a pro-Western monarch, and created an Islamic Republic led by Ayatollah Khomeini. In November, armed militants seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca, shaking the Saudi monarchy, which responded partly by giving more power to conservative clerics. In December, the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan, and volunteers from across the Muslim world went to fight it, some of whom later formed jihadist networks including al-Qaeda.

What followed was a long chain of wars. Iraq under Saddam Hussein invaded Iran in 1980, starting an eight-year war. Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990 and was expelled by a US-led coalition in 1991. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, the United States invaded Afghanistan and, in 2003, Iraq. The Iraq invasion removed Saddam but led to years of insurgency and sectarian violence. Many of today's alignments, such as the rivalry between Iran and Saudi Arabia, trace back to 1979.

Chapter 11

The Arab Spring and after

In December 2010 a Tunisian street vendor, Mohamed Bouazizi, set himself on fire after officials confiscated his goods. Protests spread across the Arab world. Within weeks, the presidents of Tunisia and Egypt had fallen. Libya's Gaddafi was overthrown with NATO air support. Syria and Yemen slid into civil wars.

The results were mostly bitter. Egypt returned to military rule in 2013. In the chaos of Syria and Iraq, the group calling itself Islamic State declared a "caliphate" in 2014, which was defeated militarily by 2019. Syria's war killed hundreds of thousands and displaced millions, and the Assad government finally fell in December 2024. The Arab Spring showed how strong the demand for dignity and accountable government was, and how hard it is to build new institutions after a dictator falls.

Chapter 13

The myth of "ancient hatreds"

A common explanation of the region's wars is that its peoples have hated each other for thousands of years. Most historians reject this. Sunni and Shia Muslims, and Muslims, Christians and Jews, lived together in many places for long periods. Most of today's conflicts are modern: they come from the collapse of empires, disputed borders, oil, Cold War rivalry, authoritarian governments and foreign intervention.

That does not mean religion and history are irrelevant. Leaders use old memories, such as Karbala, the Crusades or 1948, to mobilise people. The better picture is this: old identities provide the language, but modern politics provides the fuel. When you hear a conflict described as "centuries old", ask what changed in the last hundred years.

Chapter 15

The young region

One fact gets less attention than it deserves. Outside Israel and the Gulf, much of the region has a young population, with a large share of people under thirty. Young people need jobs, housing and a voice. The protests of 2011 were driven largely by them, and by unemployment and frustration with corrupt and closed governments.

The biggest question for the region's future is whether its economies and governments can offer a young generation a reason to stay and hope. History suggests that when they cannot, people either leave, protest or, at worst, turn to violence. When they can, as parts of the Gulf have shown, change can be surprisingly fast.

The core idea

The Middle East is where farming, cities, writing and three world faiths began. Its history stacks up in layers: ancient empires, the caliphates, the Ottoman and Safavid empires, and the post-1918 settlement that drew most modern borders. Oil, the Israeli–Palestinian conflict and the turning point of 1979 explain much of its present.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Farming began in the Fertile Crescent about 10,000 years ago.
  • Writing (cuneiform) appears around 3200 BCE.
  • The hijra of 622 is year one of the Islamic calendar.
  • The Ottoman empire lasted about 600 years, until 1922.
The common mistake

Explaining every conflict as "ancient hatred". Most of the region's wars are modern, driven by collapsed empires, borders, oil and power.
