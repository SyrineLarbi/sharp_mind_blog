Sharp Mind · History · Topic 17 of 20

The history of Asia. Not one story but several, and for most of history the centre of the world.

Many people learn world history as a European story with Asia appearing at the edges: a source of silk, a destination for explorers, a set of colonies. That picture is upside down. For most of the last two thousand years, the largest cities, the biggest economies and the oldest continuous states were in Asia. Europe's lead lasted roughly two centuries, and it is already shrinking. To understand the present, you need the longer view: how Asia's great traditions formed, why they fell behind in the nineteenth century, and how they came back.

Question 1 · What is it?

A word invented by outsiders for most of humanity

"Asia" is not a natural unit. It is a label that covers several separate civilisations, which mostly thought of themselves as the centre, not as part of a continent.

Chapter 01

A continent defined from the outside

The word "Asia" comes from the ancient Greeks, who used it for the lands east of the Aegean Sea. Europeans later stretched it from Turkey to Japan. Nobody in Ming China or Mughal India called themselves "Asian"; a Chinese scholar thought of the Middle Kingdom, the civilised centre of the world. The idea of one shared "Asian" identity is mostly modern, and it grew partly as a reaction against European colonial rule.

The size is the first fact to hold on to. About six in every ten people alive today live in Asia, roughly 4.8 billion out of about 8.1 billion in 2024. The two most populous countries, India and China, each hold more than 1.4 billion people. For most of recorded history, Asia has held well over half of humanity. A world history that treats it as a side story leaves out most of the people.

One recurring pattern is the tension between settled farming states and mobile steppe peoples. China built the Great Wall, in several versions, against northern horsemen, and northern India was invaded again and again through its north-western passes.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rivers, ideas, states and seas

The long story can be told in five movements: the first cities, the great philosophies, the bureaucratic state, the empires of India, and the networks of sea and steppe that tied them together.

Chapter 03

The first cities: a script nobody can read and bones that talk

Around 2600 BCE, cities with straight streets, standard-sized bricks and covered drains appeared in the Indus valley, in today's Pakistan and north-west India. The best known are Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. The Indus people traded with Mesopotamia and used a set of signs on small stone seals. Nobody has deciphered the Indus script, so we do not know their language, their kings or their gods. The cities declined after about 1900 BCE, probably for several reasons together, including shifts in rivers and climate. The cause is still debated.

China's earliest written records come from the Shang dynasty, around 1200 BCE. Shang kings asked questions of their ancestors by carving them on turtle shells and the shoulder bones of cattle, then heating them until they cracked. A diviner read the cracks as the answer. These oracle bones carry an early form of the same writing system that Chinese people still use. That continuity is extraordinary. A literate Chinese reader today can recognise characters written over three thousand years ago, something no reader of English can do with texts from even a thousand years back.

Chapter 04

The age of the great teachers

Between about 600 and 300 BCE, several thinkers appeared in Asia whose ideas still shape the lives of billions. In northern India, a prince named Siddhartha Gautama left his comfortable life, looked for the cause of suffering, and became known as the Buddha, "the awakened one". His exact dates are uncertain; most scholars place his death in the fifth or early fourth century BCE. In China, Confucius (traditionally 551–479 BCE) taught that a good society rests on proper relationships: ruler and subject, parent and child, elder and younger, each with duties to the other. The Daoist tradition linked to Laozi taught harmony with the natural way of things.

Confucianism was less a religion than a philosophy of government and family. For two thousand years it trained China's ruling class, and it spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Buddhism travelled out of India along the trade routes and is now far more common in East and Southeast Asia than in India, where it began.

Chapter 05

China and the machine of government

In 221 BCE the ruler of the state of Qin conquered his rivals and called himself Qin Shi Huangdi, the First Emperor. He standardised the written script, weights, measures, coinage and even the width of cart axles, so that wagons fitted the same road ruts everywhere. His dynasty collapsed within a few years of his death, but the idea of a single China survived it. The Han dynasty that followed lasted about four centuries, and Chinese people still call themselves "Han" after it.

Two ideas held the Chinese state together. The first was the Mandate of Heaven: an emperor ruled because heaven approved of him, and floods, famines and rebellions were signs that the approval had been withdrawn. This gave Chinese politics a recurring rhythm, often called the dynastic cycle: a strong founding, slow decay, disaster, revolt, and a new dynasty. The second idea was rule by examined officials. From around the seventh century CE, and fully under the Song, men won government posts by passing written examinations on the Confucian classics. The system lasted until 1905. China ran a professional civil service chosen by exam about a thousand years before most European states did.

Under the Tang and Song dynasties (618–1279), China was arguably the most advanced society on Earth. It produced woodblock printing, paper money, gunpowder and the magnetic compass, several of which later travelled west, as Volume 18 describes.

Chapter 06

India: many kingdoms, many faiths, one civilisation

India was united under one ruler far less often than China. Its history is more like Europe's: a shared culture spread across many competing states. The first great empire, the Maurya, covered most of the subcontinent in the third century BCE. Its most famous ruler, Ashoka, fought a brutal war against the kingdom of Kalinga, around 261 BCE. Afterwards, according to his own inscriptions carved on rocks and pillars, he was horrified by the slaughter, turned to Buddhism, and promised to rule by righteousness. Those inscriptions are among the oldest readable texts in India.

In the later Gupta period (roughly the fourth to sixth centuries CE), Indian mathematicians developed the place-value decimal system with a symbol for zero, which reached Europe through the Arab world and gave the world the digits on this page. From the eleventh century, Turkic and Afghan Muslim armies moved into the north, and the Delhi Sultanate was founded in 1206. In 1526 Babur, a descendant of both Timur and Genghis Khan, founded the Mughal empire. His grandson Akbar ruled a mostly Hindu population with a policy of religious tolerance unusual for its time. Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal as a tomb for his wife.

The key idea in India is layering: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Muslim, Sikh and later Christian communities lived side by side, sometimes peacefully and sometimes not. Modern quarrels over how to remember the Mughals, as foreign conquerors or as part of India's own story, are live political arguments today.

Chapter 07

The sea and the steppe: Asia's two highways

The land routes across Central Asia get most of the attention, but the sea carried more. The monsoon, a seasonal wind that blows towards the land in summer and away from it in winter, let ships cross the Indian Ocean on a timetable. A merchant could sail from Arabia to India on one season's wind and return on the next. Indian, Arab, Persian, Malay and Chinese traders built a web of ports from East Africa to China. Along it travelled pepper, cotton cloth, porcelain, and also Hinduism, Buddhism and later Islam. That is why the country with the most Muslims in the world today is Indonesia, which was reached by traders and preachers rather than conquered by armies.

Southeast Asian kingdoms grew rich on this traffic. The Khmer empire built Angkor Wat in the twelfth century, the largest religious monument in the world, first as a Hindu temple and later used for Buddhist worship. Between 1405 and 1433, the Ming admiral Zheng He led seven great voyages with fleets far bigger than anything Europe had, reaching India, Arabia and the coast of East Africa. Then the court stopped them, for reasons historians still debate.

On land, the steppe nomads were the great disruptors. The Mongols in the thirteenth century built the largest land empire in history and briefly joined China, Central Asia, Persia and Russia under one rule. Their story, including the destruction they caused, is told in Volume 18 and in The Silk Road.

Chapter 08

Japan and Korea: borrowing, then going their own way

Japan and Korea took a great deal from China: the writing system, Buddhism, Confucian ideas and models of government. Then each changed what it borrowed. In 1443 King Sejong of Korea had scholars create a new alphabet, hangul, so that ordinary people could read and write. It was announced in 1446. With only a few dozen letters built on logical principles, it is often praised by linguists as one of the most efficient writing systems ever designed.

Japan developed a warrior class, the samurai, and from the twelfth century real power passed to military rulers called shoguns, while emperors stayed as symbolic figures. After a long period of civil war, the Tokugawa family took control in 1603 and ruled for over 250 years. They expelled most Europeans, banned Christianity, and limited foreign trade to a few tightly controlled channels, including a small Dutch trading post at Nagasaki. Historians now stress that Japan was controlled rather than sealed: it kept trading with China and Korea, and it watched European science through Dutch books.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The great divergence, and the great return

The most important question in modern world history is why Western Europe briefly overtook Asia, and the most important story of our time is Asia catching up again.

Chapter 09

When Asia was the world economy

Long-run economic estimates, most famously those of the economist Angus Maddison, suggest that around 1700 China and India together produced close to half of the world's goods. These figures are reconstructions from incomplete evidence, and the details are debated. But the broad picture is not in doubt. Until about 1800, the richest regions of China, such as the Yangtze delta, were comparable to the richest regions of Europe in living standards, markets and farming productivity.

Then the paths split. Britain, followed by the rest of Western Europe and North America, industrialised in the nineteenth century, while Asia did not. The historian Kenneth Pomeranz called this the Great Divergence in a book of that name in 2000. Some explanations stress Europe's institutions, science and competing small states. Pomeranz stressed luck: Britain's accessible coal and colonies supplying cotton and sugar. Most historians combine several causes. The industrial side is in Coal, water and the end of muscle.

Chapter 10

The nineteenth century: humiliation and response

Industrial Europe came to Asia with steamships and guns. Britain bought vast amounts of Chinese tea and needed something China would buy in return. It found opium, grown in British India. When the Chinese government tried to stop the trade, Britain went to war. In the Opium Wars (1839–1842 and 1856–1860), China was defeated, forced to open ports, and gave up Hong Kong, which returned to China only in 1997. At the same time, the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) caused the deaths of tens of millions. Chinese nationalists still call the period from 1839 to 1949 the "century of humiliation", and the phrase is central to Chinese politics today.

In India, the British East India Company expanded from trading posts into a territorial power. After a major uprising in 1857, which British writers called the Mutiny and many Indians call the First War of Independence, the British government took direct control. The British Raj lasted until 1947. The wider story is in Colonialism.

Japan chose a different path. After American warships under Commodore Perry forced it to open in 1853–54, a group of reformers overthrew the shogun in 1868 in the Meiji Restoration. Within a generation, Japan built railways, factories, a modern army and a constitution. In 1905 it defeated Russia, the first time in the modern era that an Asian power beat a European great power in a major war. Japan proved that modernisation was not the same as Westernisation, but it also became a colonial empire itself, taking Taiwan, Korea and later much of China. Its wartime atrocities, such as the massacre at Nanjing in 1937, are still bitterly remembered across the region.

Chapter 11

The twentieth century: war, revolution and the economic miracle

Japan's empire collapsed in 1945 after the atomic bombs (Two mornings in August). India and Pakistan became independent in 1947. In 1949 Mao Zedong's Communists won China's civil war. The Korean War (1950–1953) ended in an armistice, not a peace treaty.

Revolution brought disasters of its own. Mao's Great Leap Forward, a forced drive to industrialise and collectivise farming from 1958, caused a famine in which tens of millions died; estimates vary widely, and the exact number is disputed. The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) turned students against teachers and officials. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge regime (1975–1979) killed or starved to death roughly a fifth to a quarter of the population.

Then came the most dramatic economic change in human history. Japan rebuilt and became the world's second-largest economy. South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, the "four tigers", followed, growing rich by manufacturing for export. From 1978 Deng Xiaoping opened China to markets and foreign investment. According to the World Bank, the number of Chinese people in extreme poverty fell by roughly 800 million over the following four decades. India opened its economy from 1991. No other region has lifted so many people out of poverty so fast.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What worked, what failed, and the myths to drop

For a region this large, "strengths and weaknesses" means looking at the patterns that recur across its history, and the ones that held it back.

Chapter 13

Two myths, and one honest debate

Myth: the "unchanging East"

European writers long described Asia as timeless and stuck, waiting for the West to wake it up. In Orientalism (1978), Edward Said argued that such images said more about Europe than about Asia. The evidence agrees: Asian societies changed and invented constantly.

Myth: Asia was simply "behind"

Asia was not behind for most of history. It led in printing, ship-building and state organisation for long periods. The gap opened late and closed fast.

The honest debate is about "Asian values". In the 1990s, leaders such as Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew argued that East Asia's success came from Confucian traditions of family, education and respect for authority, and that Western-style liberal democracy did not suit it. Critics answered that Japan, South Korea and Taiwan became prosperous democracies, and that "culture" often excused one-party rule. The question of whether economic success needs or erodes political freedom is still open.

Chapter 15

History as a live argument

In much of Asia, history is not a closed subject. China's government uses the "century of humiliation" to explain its goals. Japan and its neighbours still argue over school textbooks and over visits by politicians to the Yasukuni Shrine, which honours Japan's war dead, including convicted war criminals. Claims in the South China Sea are argued partly with old maps.

When an Asian government or newspaper talks about the past, it is usually also talking about the present. Knowing the long story, from the oracle bones to the Opium Wars, is what lets you hear what is really being said.

The core idea

Asia is several great traditions, chiefly China, South Asia, Japan and Korea, Southeast Asia and the steppe, linked by sea and land trade. For most of history it held most of humanity and much of the world's wealth. Europe's lead after about 1800 was a short exception, and Asia's return since 1950 is the biggest economic story of our time.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 6 in 10 people live in Asia (about 4.8 billion of 8.1 billion, 2024).
  • China unified in 221 BCE; its exam-based civil service lasted until 1905.
  • Around 1700, China and India together made close to half of world output, by long-run estimates.
  • Extreme poverty in China fell by roughly 800 million people after 1978 (World Bank).
The common mistake

Treating the nineteenth century as normal. Asia was not always behind; it was ahead for long periods, fell behind late, and caught up fast.

The words, in plain words

Shells and bones used by Shang kings for divination, carrying the earliest Chinese writing.
Confucianism
A philosophy of duty, family and good government that shaped East Asia for two thousand years.

Seasonal winds that reverse direction and made Indian Ocean sailing predictable.
Shogun

The split after about 1800 when Western Europe industrialised and Asia did not.
