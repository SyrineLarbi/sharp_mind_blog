Sharp Mind · History · Topic 19 of 20

The history of technology. Not a parade of lone geniuses, but a slow pile of combinations that suddenly changes everything.

The usual story of technology is a list of heroes and dates: Gutenberg in 1450, Watt in 1769, Edison in 1879. Each name is real, but the list hides how technology actually moves. Almost every great invention combined things that already existed. Most took decades to matter. And the ones that changed ordinary lives most, such as fertiliser, sewers and the shipping container, are the ones people rarely name. Understanding that pattern is the best guide we have to judging the technologies arriving now.

Question 1 · What is it?

Older than our species, and wider than machines

Technology is not just electronics. It is any tool, technique or system people use to make the world do what they want, and it began before humans did.

Chapter 01

Tools older than humans

In 2015, researchers announced the discovery of stone tools at a site called Lomekwi, near Lake Turkana in Kenya. They were about 3.3 million years old, older than the earliest known members of our own genus, Homo. Someone, perhaps an earlier human relative, was already striking one stone against another to make a sharp edge. Our species, Homo sapiens, appeared only about 300,000 years ago. Technology did not come after humanity; it helped shape it. Tools and cooked food changed what our ancestors ate, and some researchers argue that this changed their bodies and brains too.

That is why historians use a broad definition. A technology is any tool, technique or system that people use to reach a goal. A plough is technology. So is the alphabet, crop rotation, double-entry bookkeeping, a vaccine schedule and the container ship. Some of the most powerful technologies in history are methods rather than machines.

Chapter 02

The pattern is acceleration. Stone tools barely changed for hundreds of thousands of years. Since about 1800, ordinary people have seen several revolutions in one lifetime. Someone born in 1900 could have seen the first powered flights and then the Moon landings.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Combination, delay, and the technologies that change everything

Technologies are not usually born fully formed. They are assembled from older parts, they take a long time to spread, and a few of them reorganise the whole economy.

Chapter 03

New things are made of old things

Gutenberg did not invent printing, paper, ink or the press. As Volume 19 explains, he combined a wine-style screw press, oil-based ink and precisely cast metal type. The Wright brothers combined engines, propellers and their own wind-tunnel data on wing shapes. The smartphone combined a touchscreen, a mobile phone, a camera, GPS and a small computer, each of which existed already.

The economist W. Brian Arthur, in his book The Nature of Technology (2009), argues that this is how technology evolves in general: new technologies are mostly new combinations of existing ones. That explains two things. First, why invention speeds up: the more parts exist, the more possible combinations there are. Second, why the same invention often appears in several places at once. The telephone, for example, was claimed by Alexander Graham Bell and Elisha Gray on the same day in 1876. When the parts are ready, someone will put them together.

Chapter 04

Invention is the easy part

Historians separate three stages. Invention is making something work for the first time. Innovation is turning it into a product or practice people use. Diffusion is its spread across a society. The gaps between them are often decades long.

The classic example is electricity in factories. Electric motors were available in the 1880s, yet in 1990 the economic historian Paul David pointed out that American factories took about forty years to gain big productivity benefits from them. The reason was not the motor. Old factories were built around one huge steam engine driving a long rotating shaft, with every machine connected to it by belts. Simply replacing the steam engine with an electric one changed little. The gains came only when managers redesigned whole factories, with a small motor in each machine and a layout that followed the flow of work. The big payoff of a new technology usually needs new ways of organising work, and that takes a generation. David's paper compared this explicitly with computers, whose productivity gains were slow to appear in the statistics.

Chapter 05

General-purpose technologies

Most inventions do one job. A few become platforms that transform almost everything. Economists call these general-purpose technologies, a term made popular in the 1990s by Timothy Bresnahan and Manuel Trajtenberg. They share three features: they spread across many industries, they keep improving, and they make other inventions possible.

Steam power

First pumped water from mines. Then drove factories, railways and ships, and freed production from rivers and muscles.

Electricity

Delivered power anywhere a wire could reach. Made possible electric light, motors, refrigeration, radio and, eventually, computers.

Computing

Turned information into something machines could store, copy and process, and then connected it all through the internet.

Other candidates include writing, the wheel, printing and the internal combustion engine. Many people now ask whether artificial intelligence is the next one. History suggests that if it is, its full effect will take decades and will depend on how organisations change, not just on how clever the software is.

Chapter 06

Power: from muscle to fossil fuel

For almost all of history, the energy available to a person came from their own muscles, animals, wind and falling water. The great break was learning to turn heat into motion. Thomas Newcomen built a practical steam engine in 1712 to pump water from mines. James Watt's separate condenser, patented in 1769, made engines far more efficient. Coal gave Britain, and then the world, access to energy stored by plants millions of years ago.

Electricity came next. Thomas Edison's Pearl Street station in New York began supplying power in 1882. A long rivalry followed between Edison's direct current and the alternating current championed by George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla. Alternating current won because it could be sent over long distances more efficiently. Oil and the internal combustion engine then powered cars, tractors and aircraft. Cheap energy is the hidden foundation of almost every other technology of the last two centuries. Volume 20 tells the start of this story in Coal, water and the end of muscle.

Chapter 07

Information: from clay to the pocket

A second line of technology concerns information: storing it, copying it and sending it. Writing appeared around 3200 BCE. Printing made copying cheap. Then came a chain of breakthroughs in sending information faster than a person could travel.

The transistor, built at Bell Labs in 1947, was a tiny electronic switch that replaced fragile vacuum tubes. In 1958–59, Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce separately developed the integrated circuit, many transistors on one piece of material. In 1965 Gordon Moore observed that the number of components on a chip was doubling at a regular rate, later settling at about every two years. This became known as Moore's law, though it is an industry trend, not a law of nature. The internet grew from ARPANET in 1969, and Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web, proposed in 1989, made it usable by everyone. In about sixty years, the cost of computing fell by a factor so large it has no everyday comparison.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The escape from poverty, and the reshaping of daily life

Technology is the main reason most people today live longer and richer lives than kings did three centuries ago. It also quietly rearranged how people work, think and organise time.

Chapter 08

The hockey stick

Draw a graph of average income per person over the last two thousand years. For most of it, the line is nearly flat. Then, around 1800, it turns sharply upward, like the blade of a hockey stick. Before that point, any gain in productivity tended to be eaten up by population growth, a trap named after the economist Thomas Malthus. After it, sustained technological change let output grow faster than population for the first time.

The results are measurable. Global life expectancy was around 30 years in 1800, held down mainly by the deaths of young children. By the early 2020s it was above 70. The economist William Nordhaus estimated how much labour it took to buy an hour of light, from candles and oil lamps to electric bulbs. By his calculations, the true cost of light fell by something like a thousandfold over two centuries. The poorest people in a rich country today have access to light, medicine and information that no king in 1700 could buy.

Chapter 09

The unglamorous technologies that did the most

Ask people to name important inventions and they choose the visible ones: the car, the plane, the computer. Ask which ones saved and fed the most people, and the answer changes.

Clean water and sewers

Filtered water and sewage systems in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries cut deaths from cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea in cities dramatically. In many cities they did more for life expectancy than any medicine of the time.

Synthetic fertiliser

The Haber–Bosch process, developed in Germany around 1910, turns nitrogen from the air into ammonia for fertiliser. Without it, the world could feed far fewer people. See Volume 12.

Vaccines and antibiotics

Smallpox, which killed hundreds of millions of people over history, was declared eradicated in 1980. Penicillin and later antibiotics made common infections survivable.

The shipping container

Malcolm McLean's standard steel boxes, first used commercially in 1956, cut the cost of moving goods across oceans and made modern global trade possible.

The technologies that change the most lives are often systems, not gadgets: pipes, standards, chemistry and logistics that nobody sees once they work.

Chapter 10

Technology changes people, not just things

The mechanical clock appeared in European towns around the fourteenth century. Over the centuries, and especially with factories, work came to be measured by the hour rather than by the task. The historian E. P. Thompson described this shift in a famous 1967 essay on time and work-discipline. In 1883, American and Canadian railways adopted standard time zones because trains could not run on dozens of local times. A technology built for one purpose ended up setting the rhythm of daily life.

Printing had a similar effect on authority. Cheap pamphlets helped Martin Luther's ideas spread across Germany after 1517 far faster than earlier reformers' ideas had. The telegraph created modern news. The smartphone changed how people date, shop, navigate and pay attention. In each case the technology did not decide the outcome alone. People chose how to use it, and those choices differed from place to place. Historians call the view that technology decides history technological determinism, and most of them reject the strong version of it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every gain sends a bill, often to someone else

The long-run benefits of technology are clear. The costs are real too, and they often fall on different people from those who enjoy the gains.

Chapter 12

The Luddites were not fools, and the pause was real

Between 1811 and 1816, English textile workers known as Luddites broke machines in protest. Today "Luddite" means someone who hates technology. That is unfair. They were mostly skilled workers protesting against machines used to cut their wages and replace them with cheaper, less skilled labour, at a time of hunger and war. Their target was what the machines were doing to their livelihoods.

Their fears were not foolish either. The economic historian Robert Allen has described an "Engels' pause", named after Friedrich Engels, who wrote about the conditions of the English working class in 1845. In the early decades of industrialisation, output per worker in Britain rose while ordinary wages barely moved for a long time. In the long run, wages rose far higher than before. Both things are true: technology made later generations much richer, and the generation living through the change often paid for it. This is the honest background to today's arguments about automation and AI.

Chapter 14

Three lessons for judging the next big thing

The first lesson is often called Amara's law, after the American researcher Roy Amara: we tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run. The early internet bubble burst in 2000, but the internet still changed everything. Expect hype, disappointment, and then deep change.

The second lesson is to be careful with famous predictions. A line often attributed to IBM's Thomas Watson, that the world would need "maybe five computers", has never been reliably sourced and is probably a myth. Stories of foolish experts are often invented, and the real record of forecasting is messier: some experts were right, many were wrong in both directions.

The third lesson is that the technology rarely decides alone. Rules, prices, habits and power decide who benefits. Electricity, cars and the internet looked very different in different countries because of choices people made. That is the most useful thing history offers about technologies now arriving: the outcome is still partly ours to choose.

The core idea

Technology is any tool, technique or system, and it is older than our species. New technologies are mostly combinations of old ones, they take decades to pay off because work has to be reorganised, and a few general-purpose technologies, such as steam, electricity and computing, transform everything. Together they ended the long age of flat living standards.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The oldest known stone tools are about 3.3 million years old.
  • Global life expectancy rose from about 30 in 1800 to over 70 today.
  • Electric motors took about 40 years to transform factory productivity (Paul David).
The common mistake

Believing in the lone genius and the overnight revolution. Most inventions are combinations, and most revolutions are slow.

Test yourself

Can you explain why putting an electric motor into an old factory changed so little at first? If you can, you understand why new technologies take a generation to pay off.
