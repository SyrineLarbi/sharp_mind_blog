Colonialism. How a handful of small countries came to rule most of the planet, and why it is still argued about.

In 1914, a few European states and their offshoots controlled most of the Earth's land surface. Britain alone ruled roughly a quarter of the world's people. Within about sixty years nearly all of that was gone. Yet the effects did not leave with the governors. They are visible in the borders of Africa, the languages spoken in Latin America, the cricket played in South Asia, the wealth of certain cities and the poverty of certain regions. Colonialism is one of the few historical subjects on which educated people still disagree sharply, so this file sets out the facts first and the arguments second.

Question 1 · What is it?

Ruling other people's land from far away

Colonialism means one society taking political control of another territory and its people, usually to benefit the ruling power. Its modern European form is the one that shaped today's world.

Chapter 01

Colonialism, imperialism, and two kinds of colony

Two words are often used as if they meant the same thing. Imperialism is the broader idea: a state extending its power over others, by conquest, by control of trade or by political pressure. Colonialism is one form of it: direct rule over a territory, often with settlers or administrators sent from the home country, which historians call the metropole.

Historians also distinguish two main kinds of colony, because they produced very different societies.

Settler colonies

Large numbers of Europeans moved in permanently, took the land and became the majority or the ruling minority. Examples: the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Argentina, and, as a ruling minority, South Africa, Algeria and Kenya. Indigenous peoples were displaced, killed by war and disease, or confined to reserves.

Colonies of exploitation

A small number of officials, soldiers and businessmen ruled a large local population, mainly to extract taxes, crops and minerals. Examples: British India, the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia), most of tropical Africa. Few Europeans ever lived there; at times only a few thousand British officials governed hundreds of millions in India.

Empire is not a European invention. The Romans, the Mongols, the Ottomans, the Chinese dynasties, the Aztecs and the Incas all conquered neighbours. In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries Japan built an empire in Korea, Taiwan and much of East Asia, and Russia expanded across Siberia and Central Asia over land. What made modern European colonialism different was its reach: it covered almost the whole globe, crossed oceans, and was tied to a new global economy.

Chapter 02

Two great waves

European colonialism came in two main waves, with very different targets.

WaveWhenMain powersMain targets
First waveAbout 1490s–1820sSpain, Portugal, then Britain, France, the NetherlandsThe Americas, the Caribbean, coastal trading posts in Africa and Asia
IntervalAbout 1770s–1820s—Most of the Americas wins independence: the US, Haiti, Spanish and Portuguese America

The speed of the second wave is striking. Around 1870, Europeans controlled only a small fringe of Africa, mostly coastal. By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. The "Scramble for Africa" happened within a single lifetime.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Tools, companies, intermediaries and ideas

Small countries cannot rule huge ones by force alone. Colonial rule depended on a technological edge, private companies, local collaborators, and a story that made it seem right to the rulers.

Chapter 03

The tools of empire

The historian Daniel Headrick, in a 1981 book called The Tools of Empire, argued that nineteenth-century technology made the second wave possible. Three tools stand out.

  • Steamships could travel up rivers against the current and did not depend on the wind, opening the interiors of Africa and Asia.
  • Quinine, a drug from the bark of the South American cinchona tree, protected Europeans against malaria. Before it, West Africa was known to Europeans as "the white man's grave", because so many died there within months.
  • Rapid-firing guns, above all the Maxim machine gun of the 1880s, gave small forces overwhelming firepower. The English writer Hilaire Belloc put it bluntly in a satirical poem of 1898: "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not."

At the Battle of Omdurman in Sudan in 1898, British-led forces with machine guns and artillery killed around ten thousand Sudanese fighters while losing only a few dozen men of their own. The gap in firepower, not some general superiority, explains how so few could conquer so many so quickly. Where that gap was smaller, colonisers often failed, as Chapter 12 shows.

Chapter 04

Empire as a business: the chartered companies

Much early colonialism was run not by governments but by companies. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600, the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials VOC, in 1602. Their charters gave them monopolies over trade, and also the right to make war, sign treaties, mint money and govern territory. The VOC is often described as one of the first companies to sell shares to the public.

The English company began as a trader buying cotton cloth, pepper and tea. After it defeated the ruler of Bengal at the Battle of Plassey in 1757, it gained the right to collect Bengal's taxes in 1765. A trading company had become a government, collecting revenue from tens of millions of people. It recruited a large private army, mostly of Indian soldiers called sepoys. After a major uprising in 1857, which British writers long called the Mutiny and many Indians call the First War of Independence, the British state abolished the company's rule and governed India directly from 1858.

Chapter 05

Ruling through others

No colonial power had enough officials to run everything itself. Most relied on local intermediaries: kings, chiefs, princes, landlords, clerks, soldiers and police. In British India, hundreds of "princely states" kept their own rulers under British supervision. In Northern Nigeria the administrator Frederick Lugard made indirect rule a formal doctrine: govern through existing chiefs, who collected taxes and kept order in return for British backing.

Indirect rule was cheap, but it changed what it touched. Where no clear chief existed, officials sometimes appointed one. Flexible customs were written down as fixed "customary law". Many historians argue that colonial censuses and classifications hardened identities that had been more fluid, such as caste categories in India or ethnic labels in parts of Africa. The scale of that effect is debated. But the general pattern, sometimes summed up as "divide and rule", left divisions that outlived the empires. Volume 22's chapter on Rwanda shows one of its most terrible consequences.

Chapter 06

The economics of extraction

Colonial economies were usually organised to send raw materials to the metropole and to buy its manufactured goods. Colonies grew one or two cash crops for export, such as cotton, cocoa, rubber, coffee or sugar, or dug out minerals such as copper, gold and tin. Railways mostly ran from the interior to the ports, not between neighbouring regions.

To push people into this economy, governments often imposed taxes that had to be paid in cash, such as "hut taxes" per household. Farmers had to grow export crops or work for wages to pay. Forced labour was common, legally or in practice, well into the twentieth century.

The extreme case

From 1885 to 1908 the Congo Free State was the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium. To meet rubber quotas, its agents took hostages, burned villages and cut off hands. Reports by the British consul Roger Casement and a campaign by the journalist E. D. Morel exposed it, and Belgium took the territory over in 1908. The population fell sharply; estimates of the deaths run into the millions but are uncertain and debated. It remains a byword for what unchecked extraction does.

Chapter 07

The justifying story

Colonisers rarely described themselves as thieves. They spoke of a civilising mission: bringing Christianity, commerce, education, medicine and order to people they considered backward. The French called it the mission civilisatrice; Rudyard Kipling's 1899 poem "The White Man's Burden" urged Americans to take up the same task in the Philippines.

Some of this was sincere. Missionaries founded schools and hospitals, and campaigners pushed Britain to suppress the slave trade at sea after 1807. But the story rested on a hierarchy of races, supported in the late nineteenth century by pseudo-scientific theories that ranked humans by skull shape and skin colour, now completely discredited. The belief that colonised people were not ready to govern themselves was the argument used, again and again, to delay independence. The Enlightenment sharpened the contradiction: Europe preached universal rights while denying them abroad, a gap that anti-colonial leaders would later turn against it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The present is full of its traces

Colonial rule ended mostly within living memory. Its borders, languages, economies and grievances still shape daily life and international politics.

Chapter 08

Borders, languages and states

Most of the world's national borders outside Europe were drawn or fixed during the colonial era. In Africa they were largely agreed among Europeans after the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, as Volume 22 explains. In 1964 the Organisation of African Unity agreed to keep those borders anyway, because redrawing them risked endless war. That decision has held remarkably well, with a few exceptions such as the independence of Eritrea in 1993 and South Sudan in 2011.

Language is the most visible legacy. Spanish, Portuguese, English and French are now spoken by hundreds of millions of people far from Europe, and English and French serve as official languages in dozens of countries. That brings practical advantages in trade and science, and a long debate about whether schools should teach mainly in local languages instead. Colonialism created many of the states it later handed over, with their capitals, armies, civil services and legal systems.

Chapter 09

Did colonialism make some rich and others poor?

This is the central economic question, and it is genuinely contested. Some facts are widely agreed. According to long-run estimates by the economic historian Angus Maddison, India and China together produced around half of world output in 1700, and far less by 1950. Europe and its settler offshoots became much richer. The question is how much of that was caused by colonialism and how much by other forces, especially industrialisation.

The "drain" view

Writers from the Indian nationalist Dadabhai Naoroji in the nineteenth century to the Guyanese historian Walter Rodney, in How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972), argued that colonies were systematically stripped of wealth through taxes, unfair trade, forced labour and the destruction of local industries such as Indian textiles.

The "institutions" view

Economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson argued in a 2001 paper that colonisers built very different institutions in different places: protective ones where Europeans settled, extractive ones where they did not. Those institutions persisted after independence. The three won the 2024 Nobel prize in economics partly for this work.

Both views agree on one thing: colonial rule designed economies and institutions for the benefit of the rulers, and many of those designs outlasted it. They disagree about how much today's gaps between countries can be traced to that, versus to geography, post-independence politics and the global economy.

Chapter 10

Famine, violence and memory

Some of the bitterest arguments concern famines under colonial rule, such as those in India in the late nineteenth century and in Bengal in 1943, when roughly two to three million people died. Historians disagree on how much blame falls on wartime conditions, crop failure and colonial policy, but most agree that government responses were inadequate and that food continued to be exported or diverted in some cases.

Colonial wars and repression also left deep scars: Germany's campaign against the Herero and Nama peoples in today's Namibia between 1904 and 1908, which Germany officially acknowledged as genocide in 2021; the British response to the Mau Mau uprising in Kenya in the 1950s; the French war in Algeria from 1954 to 1962. For many families in former colonies this is not distant history but grandparents' memory, which is why it surfaces so quickly in politics and diplomacy.

Migration ties the story together. After 1945, people from former colonies moved to Britain, France, the Netherlands and Portugal, often invited to fill labour shortages. Their descendants now form a large part of those countries' populations and have pushed the colonial past into national debates about identity, racism and what schools should teach.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The balance-sheet argument, and its limits

People still argue about whether colonialism did any good. Here are the main claims and counter-claims, set out fairly.

Chapter 11

Claimed benefits against documented costs

Some writers, such as the historian Niall Ferguson in Empire (2003), have argued that the British Empire, despite its crimes, spread institutions such as the rule of law, parliamentary government and open trade. Critics, such as the Indian politician and author Shashi Tharoor in Inglorious Empire (2017), argue that such benefits were incidental, built for the rulers' needs, and far outweighed by the damage.

AreaClaimed benefitDocumented cost or counterpoint
InfrastructureRailways, ports, telegraphs, irrigation canalsBuilt mainly to move troops and exports; paid for largely by colonial taxes
Law and administrationWritten legal codes, courts, civil services, some of which surviveLaws often discriminated by race; emergency powers and detention without trial were common
Health and educationVaccination campaigns, mission schools, universities in some coloniesAccess was very limited; at independence many colonies had few graduates or doctors
TradeIntegration into world marketsOn terms set by the rulers; local industries were sometimes deliberately undercut
Ending some abusesSuppression of slave raiding and some practices the colonisers opposedThe same powers had run the Atlantic slave trade and used forced labour themselves

The deepest problem with any balance sheet is the counterfactual: what would have happened without colonial rule. Japan, Thailand and Ethiopia were never colonised (Ethiopia only briefly occupied by Italy from 1936 to 1941) and modernised in their own ways, with very different results. Without a reliable alternative history, many historians consider the question of "net benefit" unanswerable, and they focus instead on who decided, who paid and who gained.

Chapter 12

Resistance and agency

Telling colonialism only as something done to people hides how much they shaped it. At the Battle of Adwa in 1896, the Ethiopian army of Emperor Menelik II, well equipped with modern rifles, decisively defeated an Italian invasion, which is why Ethiopia stayed independent. The Maji Maji rising in German East Africa (1905–07), the Indian rebellion of 1857 and countless smaller revolts made rule expensive and uncertain.

Others negotiated, traded, served in colonial armies, converted, studied in European universities or used colonial courts for their own ends. Many future independence leaders, from Gandhi to Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, were educated within the colonial system and turned its own ideals of rights and self-government against it. Colonised people were never simply passive, and much of the colonial map was shaped by their choices as well as by Europe's.

Chapter 14

Is colonialism really over?

Formally, almost. The United Nations still lists under twenty "non-self-governing territories", most of them small islands. In 2025 Britain signed a treaty to hand the Chagos Islands to Mauritius while keeping the military base on Diego Garcia under a long lease, ending one of the last disputes over a colonial territory in Africa.

The harder question is about neo-colonialism, a term coined by independence leaders such as Nkrumah for continued control through economics and politics after formal independence. People use it today about many things: French military bases and the CFA franc currency in West Africa, debt to Western lenders, and also China's growing lending and investment. Between 2022 and 2025 French troops left several countries in West and Central Africa at their governments' request. Whether these relationships are partnership or domination depends on the case, and on who is asked.

The most likely future is that the argument continues, but with more voices from the former colonies setting its terms, through their own historians, museums, courts and governments. For an African view of the longer story, see The history of Africa.

The core idea

Colonialism was direct rule over other peoples' land, in two great European waves (the Americas from the 1490s, Africa and Asia from the 1870s). It worked through a technological edge, chartered companies, local intermediaries and an ideology of racial hierarchy, and it built economies for extraction. Its borders, languages and institutions still shape the world, and its overall effects remain contested.

The numbers worth knowing
  • By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia in Africa were independent.
  • The Berlin Conference: 1884–85.
  • Britain ruled roughly a quarter of the world's people at its peak.
  • Most of Asia and Africa became independent between 1945 and 1975.
The common mistake

Arguing about a single "net balance" of good and bad. The counterfactual is unknowable, and the more useful questions are who decided, who paid and who benefited.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a few thousand officials could rule hundreds of millions of people? If you can, you have understood firepower, indirect rule and the role of local intermediaries together.

The words, in plain words
Colonialism
Direct political control of a territory and its people by a foreign power.
Imperialism
The broader extension of a state's power over others, by rule, trade or pressure.
Metropole
The home country that rules a colony.
Settler colony
A colony where many Europeans settled permanently and took the land.
Colony of exploitation
A colony ruled by a small foreign elite mainly to extract wealth.
Chartered company
A company given state rights to trade, fight and govern, such as the East India Company.
Indirect rule
Governing through local chiefs and rulers backed by the colonial power.
Cash crop
A crop grown for sale and export rather than for local food.
Civilising mission
The colonial claim to be bringing progress to supposedly backward peoples.
Scramble for Africa
The rapid European partition of Africa, roughly 1880–1914.
Decolonisation
The process by which colonies became independent, mostly 1945–1975.
Neo-colonialism
Continued outside control through economics or politics after formal independence.
Restitution
Returning objects or property taken unjustly.
Counterfactual
An imagined alternative history used to judge what a cause really changed.
