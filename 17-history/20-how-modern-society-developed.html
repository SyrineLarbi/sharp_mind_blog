How modern society developed. Several revolutions that fed each other, until the village became the city and the subject became the citizen.

Imagine an ordinary person in 1700. They probably farm, cannot read, will never travel more than a day's walk from home, and expect several of their children to die young. Their ruler is a king or emperor who claims authority from God or tradition. Now think of an ordinary person today: living in a city, schooled for over a decade, voting, with a passport, a bank account and a life expectancy over seventy. The change between those two lives is what historians and sociologists call modernisation. It did not come from one cause. It came from several changes that kept pushing each other forward.

Question 1 · What is it?

A bundle of changes, not a single event

"Modern society" names a particular way of organising life: around industry, cities, strong states, markets, schools and individual rights. It is the bundle, not any one item, that makes it modern.

Chapter 01

What sociologists meant by "modern"

Sociology was born in the nineteenth century largely to explain this change. Its three founding thinkers each picked out a different engine. Karl Marx saw capitalism: an economy in which owners of capital hire workers for wages and compete by constantly changing how things are made. Émile Durkheim saw the division of labour: as people specialised, they stopped being alike and became dependent on each other, which needed new ways of holding society together. Max Weber saw rationalisation: the spread of rules, calculation, written records and bureaucracy into more and more areas of life.

Each was describing part of the same elephant. Today most scholars treat modernity as several linked transformations: economic (industry and markets), political (the nation-state and citizenship), social (cities, smaller families, mass schooling) and intellectual (science and, in many places, a declining role for religious authority in public life). Where they came together, life changed faster than at any time since the invention of farming.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Six changes that kept feeding each other

No single cause produced modern society. States, markets, machines, cities, schools and rights grew together, and each made the others easier.

Chapter 03

The state: war, taxes and paperwork

The modern state is a government that claims final authority over a fixed territory and runs it through a professional bureaucracy. In Europe, its growth was driven above all by war. Armies with gunpowder weapons and standing troops were expensive. To pay for them, rulers had to count their people, tax them, borrow money and hire officials. The sociologist Charles Tilly summed this up in a famous line: "War made the state, and the state made war."

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which ended the Thirty Years' War, is often described as the birth of the system of sovereign states, each with authority over its own territory. Historians now argue that this is an oversimplification, and that the system took much longer to form. But the direction is clear. By the nineteenth century, states were mapping their land, registering births, building roads and running police forces. Weber described the modern state as the body that successfully claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of force within its territory. That idea is still how political scientists define it.

Chapter 04

Markets, money and the factory

Trade and markets are ancient. What changed was their reach. From the seventeenth century, joint-stock companies, banks, stock exchanges and government bonds allowed money to be pooled and invested on a new scale. In 1776 Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations argued that specialisation and free exchange, guided by self-interest within rules, would make nations richer. His famous example was a pin factory, where dividing the work into many small steps let a few workers make thousands of pins a day.

Then came the Industrial Revolution, starting in Britain in the late eighteenth century, as Volume 20 and The history of technology describe. Factories moved work out of the home and into buildings governed by clocks and supervisors. For the first time, most people's income came from selling their labour for a wage, rather than from land or a family workshop. That single shift changed family life, the meaning of childhood and the politics of class.

Chapter 05

Ideas: from subjects to citizens

The Enlightenment of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries argued that reason and evidence, not tradition alone, should guide belief and government. Thinkers such as John Locke claimed that people have natural rights and that government rests on the consent of the governed. The American Revolution of 1776 and the French Revolution of 1789 put these ideas into documents: the Declaration of Independence and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.

At first, "rights" applied in practice to a narrow group: mostly property-owning men of the majority religion and race. Much of the next two centuries was a struggle over who counted. Slavery was abolished, country by country, through the nineteenth century. Voting was extended to working men and then to women; New Zealand gave women the vote in national elections in 1893, and many countries followed after 1918. The idea of equal citizenship came first; making it real took about two hundred years, and the work is unfinished.

Chapter 06

Cities, and the demographic transition

In 1800, only a small minority of the world's people, well under one in five, lived in towns and cities. According to United Nations estimates, more than half have lived in urban areas since around 2007. Factories needed workers in one place, and farming needed fewer hands as it became more productive. Early industrial cities were crowded and deadly, until sewers, clean water and housing rules made them healthier. The full story is in Urbanization.

Alongside this came the demographic transition, the shift from high birth and death rates to low ones. First, death rates fall, thanks to better food, sanitation and medicine. Populations then grow fast, because birth rates stay high for a while. Later, birth rates fall too, as children survive, schooling becomes expensive, women gain education and work outside the home, and contraception spreads.

Every country that has modernised has gone through this, though at very different speeds. Britain took well over a century. South Korea did it in a few decades. The demographic transition explains why the world's population grew so fast in the twentieth century, and why it is now slowing.

Chapter 07

Schools and nations

For most of history, few people could read. Estimates assembled by researchers at Our World in Data suggest that around 1820 only about one in eight adults worldwide was literate; today the large majority are. The great expansion came from compulsory state schooling. Prussia moved towards it in the eighteenth century, and Britain and France introduced national systems of elementary education in the 1870s and 1880s.

Schools did more than teach reading. They taught a standard national language, a national history and loyalty to the nation. The political scientist Benedict Anderson, in Imagined Communities (1983), argued that nations are communities of people who will never meet each other but imagine themselves as one people, an idea spread by newspapers, books and schools. In 1789 a large share of people living in France did not speak standard French as their everyday language. The nation-state, so natural to us now, had to be built, and schools, armies and newspapers built it.

Chapter 08

The welfare state: protecting people from the market

Industrial wage work brought a new kind of risk. A farmer who fell ill still had land; a factory worker who fell ill had nothing. In the 1880s the German chancellor Otto von Bismarck introduced state health insurance, accident insurance and old-age pensions, partly to win workers away from socialist parties. In Britain, the Beveridge Report of 1942 proposed protection "from the cradle to the grave", which led to the National Health Service in 1948.

By the late twentieth century, most rich countries spent a large share of their national income on pensions, health, education and unemployment support. The level and design differ widely: the Nordic countries spend more and tax more, the United States relies more on private provision. The welfare state is modern society's answer to the insecurity that markets create, and how big it should be remains one of the main dividing lines of politics.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

It is the water we swim in

Almost every institution you deal with, from your school to your passport to your job contract, is a product of this transformation. Seeing it as historical, not natural, changes how you judge it.

Chapter 09

The transformation of ordinary lives

The most dramatic change is in survival. Before modernisation, in most societies, something like a third or more of children died before the age of five. Today, in the richest countries, fewer than one in a hundred do. Global average life expectancy has more than doubled since 1800. Families shrank as a result: the world's average number of children per woman fell from around five in the 1950s to about 2.3 by the early 2020s.

Childhood itself changed. Children moved from work in fields and factories into schools, after long campaigns for child-labour laws. Adolescence and retirement became normal stages of life. People gained more choice over whom to marry, where to live and what to believe. The modern individual, choosing a life rather than inheriting one, is a recent and unusual creation.

Chapter 10

One modernity, or many?

For much of the twentieth century, a theory known as modernisation theory held that all societies would follow the Western path: industry would bring cities, secular attitudes and democracy, in roughly that order. The evidence has not been kind to the strong version.

Different routes

Japan modernised fast after 1868 while keeping its monarchy and much of its culture. China became an industrial giant under one-party rule. The Gulf states built modern cities and economies under hereditary monarchies. The sociologist Shmuel Eisenstadt called this "multiple modernities".

Religion did not disappear

The secularisation thesis predicted that religion would fade as societies modernised. It largely did in much of Western Europe. But the United States stayed comparatively religious, and religion remains strong across much of the world. Scholars still debate how far the thesis holds.

The balanced view is that some features of modernity, such as industry, cities, smaller families and mass schooling, do appear almost everywhere development happens. Others, such as democracy and secularism, depend much more on history, culture and political choice.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A bargain with real winnings and real losses

Modern society brought prosperity, health and freedom on a scale never seen before. Its founding critics also saw clearly what it threatened.

Chapter 12

The dark side: modern tools, modern crimes

It is tempting to tell the story of modernity as pure progress. The twentieth century makes that impossible. The sociologist Zygmunt Bauman argued in Modernity and the Holocaust (1989) that the genocide was not a return to barbarism but something only a modern society could do: it used railways, bureaucracy, record-keeping and a division of labour in which each official did one small task. The Holocaust is told in Volume 21.

Modern states also ran colonial empires, using industrial weapons and administrative systems to rule much of the world (Colonialism). Soviet and Chinese planners caused famines in the name of rational, scientific progress. The same capacities that let a modern state vaccinate a nation let it persecute one. That is why modern societies also invented constraints: constitutions, courts, a free press and human-rights law.

Chapter 14

Is modernity finished?

From the 1970s, some thinkers argued that the West had entered a "postmodern" age, with less faith in grand stories of progress and more emphasis on identity and diversity. Others, such as the sociologist Anthony Giddens, preferred terms like "late modernity": the same basic system, but more global, more uncertain and more self-questioning. The labels are debated; the trends they describe, such as globalisation, digital life and shifting identities, are real.

The most useful conclusion is simple. Modern society was built by people, over a few centuries, through choices and conflicts. It can be reformed the same way. The next library sections, on law and government and on society, look at the institutions that now carry that work.

The core idea

Modern society is a bundle of linked changes: strong states, markets and industry, cities, mass schooling, nations and expanding rights. They reinforced each other, mostly after about 1750, and transformed survival, family and identity. The path has not been the same everywhere, and its tools have been used for great good and great harm.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than half of humanity has lived in cities since about 2007 (UN).
  • Adult literacy rose from about 1 in 8 people around 1820 to a large majority today.
  • Average children per woman fell from about 5 in the 1950s to about 2.3 in the early 2020s.
The common mistake

Treating modern institutions as natural and permanent. Nations, schools, wages and welfare states are recent human inventions, and they keep changing.

Test yourself

Can you explain how factory work, falling child deaths and compulsory schooling together led to smaller families? If you can, you understand how the parts of modernity feed each other.

The words, in plain words
Modernisation
The broad shift from agrarian, traditional societies to industrial, urban and bureaucratic ones.
Capitalism
An economy in which privately owned capital hires workers for wages and competes in markets.
Division of labour
Splitting work into specialised tasks, making people more productive and more dependent on each other.
Rationalisation
Weber's term for the spread of rules, calculation and bureaucracy into daily life.
Bureaucracy
Administration by trained officials following written rules.
Nation-state
A state whose people are meant to share one national identity.
Demographic transition
The move from high birth and death rates to low ones.
Welfare state
Government programmes that protect people against illness, old age and unemployment.
Anomie
Durkheim's word for the confusion that follows when shared social rules weaken.
Secularisation thesis
The prediction that religion declines as societies modernise; still debated.
Multiple modernities
The idea that societies can become modern in different ways, not only the Western one.
Replacement fertility
About 2.1 children per woman, the level that keeps a population stable over time without migration.
