The history of Africa. The longest human story on Earth, and the one most often told badly.

Every person alive descends from people who lived in Africa. Humans have been in Africa for around three hundred thousand years, in Europe for under fifty thousand. Yet for much of the twentieth century, respected European scholars taught that Africa had no history worth studying before Europeans arrived. They were wrong in a way that is now well documented: Africa had empires, trading cities, universities, scripts, coinage and churches older than most of Europe's. This file gives the long story in outline, explains how historians know it, and shows why it matters for the continent that will hold a quarter of humanity by 2050.

Question 1 · What is it?

A continent of 54 countries and 300,000 years

"African history" is not one story. It is the history of a landmass bigger than China, India, the United States and most of Europe combined, with roughly a third of the world's languages.

Chapter 01

Where everyone's story begins

The oldest known fossils of our own species, Homo sapiens, come from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco and are dated to around 300,000 years ago. Other early remains come from Ethiopia, Kenya and South Africa, which suggests that modern humans emerged across Africa as a whole rather than in one single "Garden of Eden". In Blombos Cave in South Africa, people engraved patterns on pieces of ochre, a red pigment, around 75,000 years ago or more, one of the earliest signs of symbolic thinking.

Genetic studies show that most people outside Africa descend from a group who left the continent roughly 50,000 to 70,000 years ago. Because the people who stayed had far longer to diversify, there is more genetic variety among Africans than among everyone else on Earth combined. Two people from different parts of Africa can be more different genetically than a person from Europe and a person from East Asia. Any idea of a single "African" type is biologically wrong.

Chapter 02

The myth of a continent without history

In lectures given in the 1820s and published after his death, the German philosopher Hegel declared that Africa was "no historical part of the World". As late as 1963 the Oxford historian Hugh Trevor-Roper said in a lecture that there was only "the history of Europeans in Africa", and that the rest was darkness. These views were influential, and they were convenient for colonial rule.

The main reason given was that much of Africa had no written records. That was only partly true, and it confused writing with history. Historians now use several kinds of evidence together:

From 1964 UNESCO sponsored a General History of Africa, written mostly by African scholars, which helped make the field mainstream. Africa was never without history; outsiders simply lacked the tools, and often the will, to read it.

Chapter 03

Geography: deserts, rivers and the tsetse fly

Africa's environment shaped its history strongly. The Sahara was much wetter until roughly five to six thousand years ago, with lakes, grasslands and rock art showing cattle herders. As it dried, people moved toward the Nile, the Sahel to the south and the coasts. The Sahel, an Arabic word for "shore", is the dry belt along the Sahara's southern edge, and it became a great zone of trade.

Further south, the tsetse fly spreads a disease fatal to horses and cattle across much of the tropical belt. That limited cavalry, ploughing and transport by animal. Many great rivers have waterfalls and rapids close to the sea, making them hard to use for trade inland. And for most of history, much of Africa had plenty of land but relatively few people. The political scientist Jeffrey Herbst argued that this made control over people, not territory, the basis of power for many African states, which helps explain why borders were often loose and why later colonial lines on maps fit so badly. His argument is influential but debated.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The long story in six movements

From farming and iron to the slave trades, the history follows a few recurring engines: the spread of peoples, trade across deserts and oceans, and states built to control both.

Chapter 04

Farming, iron and the Bantu expansion

Farming developed in several parts of Africa independently, with local crops such as sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, yams and coffee. Ironworking was established in parts of West and Central Africa by the first millennium BCE. The Nok culture of central Nigeria, known for its striking terracotta sculptures, combined farming and ironworking from roughly 500 BCE.

Over a period of thousands of years, starting perhaps four to five thousand years ago, peoples speaking Bantu languages spread from the region of today's Nigeria–Cameroon border south and east across most of the southern half of the continent, carrying farming and, later, iron. Today several hundred million people speak Bantu languages, including Swahili, Zulu, Kikongo and Shona. It was one of the largest movements of peoples and languages in human history, and it explains why languages spoken thousands of kilometres apart share related words.

Chapter 05

Kush and Aksum: the Nile and the Horn

Egypt, covered in Volume 17, was only one of the Nile's civilisations. To its south, in what is now Sudan, lay the kingdom of Kush, also called Nubia. In the eighth century BCE Kushite kings conquered Egypt and ruled it for several decades as its 25th dynasty. Later, from their capital at Meroë, they built hundreds of steep pyramids. Sudan has more pyramids than Egypt.

In the highlands of today's Ethiopia and Eritrea, the kingdom of Aksum rose in the first centuries CE. It controlled trade between the Red Sea, the Indian Ocean and the interior, minted its own gold, silver and bronze coins, raised giant carved stone stelae, and had its own script, Ge'ez. In the fourth century its king, Ezana, adopted Christianity, making Aksum one of the earliest Christian states anywhere. Ethiopia's Orthodox Church continues that tradition, and its rock-cut churches at Lalibela, carved downward into solid rock around the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, are still in use. Christianity in Ethiopia is older than in most of northern Europe.

Chapter 06

Gold, salt and the empires of the Sahel

Camels, which spread across North Africa in the early first millennium CE, made regular crossings of the Sahara possible. Caravans carried salt, cloth, horses and books south, and gold, ivory and enslaved people north. Three great empires grew rich in the Sahel by taxing this trade.

Islam spread along the trade routes, mostly peacefully, through merchants and scholars. Rulers often converted while many subjects kept older beliefs. Timbuktu became famous for its mosques and scholars, and families there preserved tens of thousands of manuscripts on law, astronomy, medicine and theology. When armed jihadist groups occupied the city in 2012–13, local librarians smuggled most of the manuscripts to safety, although some were burned. The ancient-Ghana name was later borrowed by the Gold Coast when it won independence in 1957, a deliberate claim on a proud past, even though modern Ghana lies far to the south.

Chapter 07

The Swahili coast, Great Zimbabwe and the forest kingdoms

Along the east coast, from Somalia to Mozambique, a chain of trading towns, including Kilwa, Mombasa, Lamu and Zanzibar, flourished from around the tenth century. They were part of the Indian Ocean network described in The Silk Road, trading gold, ivory and enslaved people for cloth, beads and Chinese porcelain. Their language, Swahili, is a Bantu language with many Arabic words, and their culture blended African, Arab and Persian influences. Kilwa minted its own coins, and Ibn Battuta called it one of the most beautiful towns he had seen.

Inland, on the plateau of today's Zimbabwe, people built Great Zimbabwe between about the eleventh and fifteenth centuries: massive curving stone walls fitted without mortar, at the centre of a state that controlled gold exports to the coast. Its population at its height was perhaps in the low tens of thousands, though estimates vary. In the colonial era, Rhodesia's white-minority government pressured archaeologists to suggest outsiders had built it, because African builders did not fit its politics. The evidence that it was built by ancestors of the local Shona-speaking people is overwhelming, and the modern country took its name from the site.

In the forests of West and Central Africa stood other powerful states: the kingdom of Benin, famous for its brass and bronze sculpture; the Asante, Oyo and Dahomey states; and the kingdom of Kongo. In 1526 Kongo's Christian king, Afonso I, wrote to the king of Portugal complaining that Portuguese slave traders were seizing his subjects, even nobles, and asking him to stop it. He was ignored.

Chapter 08

The slave trades and what they did

Slavery existed in many African societies, as it did across the ancient and medieval world. What changed after 1500 was scale and destination. The Atlantic trade, told in Volume 19, carried around twelve and a half million people across the ocean between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. Trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades carried millions more, over a longer period; their numbers are far less certain.

The trade depended on African sellers as well as European buyers: some coastal states and merchants grew rich by capturing and selling people from other groups. Saying so is not a way of sharing blame equally. European demand, ships and plantations drove the system, and its racial justification was European. But it shows how the trade turned neighbours into raiders and rewarded violence. The economist Nathan Nunn found in 2008 that the regions that lost most people to the slave trades tend to be poorer today, and later work with Leonard Wantchekon linked them to lower levels of trust. Those findings are widely cited; how strong the link is remains debated.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Conquest, independence and a very young continent

The last 150 years, colonial rule and its aftermath, explain much of Africa's present. The longer history explains what Africans brought to it.

Chapter 09

Conquest and independence, in brief

Between about 1880 and 1914, European powers conquered almost the entire continent, as Colonialism explains. Africans resisted, and in 1896 Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa and kept its independence. Colonial economies were built for export, education was limited, and borders were drawn in Europe.

Independence came quickly after 1945. Libya, Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia went first, in the 1950s; Ghana in 1957 is usually counted as the first colony south of the Sahara; 1960 became known as the "Year of Africa", when seventeen countries became independent. Portugal's colonies followed in the mid-1970s, Zimbabwe in 1980, Namibia in 1990, and apartheid in South Africa ended with the election of Nelson Mandela in 1994. Volume 22 covers the end of empire in Africa, and the borders.

Chapter 10

After independence: hopes, coups and contrasts

The first leaders, such as Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana and Julius Nyerere of Tanzania, promised rapid development and African unity. Many governments soon became one-party states, and military coups became common; Africa has had more coup attempts since 1950 than any other region. The Cold War made things worse, as the United States and the Soviet Union backed rival sides in Angola, the Congo, Ethiopia and elsewhere. In the 1980s falling commodity prices and heavy debts forced many countries to accept structural adjustment, loans from the International Monetary Fund and World Bank on condition of spending cuts and market reforms, which remain controversial.

The averages hide huge contrasts. Botswana, one of the poorest countries at independence in 1966, used diamond revenues carefully, kept regular elections and became one of the continent's richer states. Mauritius built a diverse economy and a stable democracy. Others suffered terrible wars: Nigeria's civil war over Biafra (1967–70), the genocide in Rwanda (1994), and wars in Congo, Sudan and Somalia. Africa is not one story of failure or success, but fifty-four national stories.

Chapter 11

Africa today: young, urbanising and connected

Africa had roughly 1.5 billion people in 2024, with a median age under twenty, by far the youngest of any continent. United Nations projections suggest around 2.5 billion by 2050. Cities such as Lagos, Kinshasa and Cairo are among the largest in the world. Africa has often adopted technology in its own way: Kenya's M-Pesa, launched in 2007, made paying by mobile phone normal for millions of people who had no bank account, years before it spread elsewhere.

Institutions are changing too. The African Union replaced the Organisation of African Unity in 2002, and the African Continental Free Trade Area began trading in 2021, aiming to create one of the world's largest markets by number of countries. At the same time, a wave of coups since 2020, especially in the Sahel (Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger) and also in Guinea and Gabon, and a devastating civil war in Sudan since 2023, show how fragile some states remain. Mobile money and new connectivity are part of the story of fintech worldwide.

Chapter 13

The argument about blame, and the stories outsiders tell

One long debate is about responsibility for Africa's poverty. One side stresses the slave trades, colonial extraction, unfair trade terms and Cold War interference. The other stresses post-independence choices: corruption, coups, poor economic policy. Most serious scholars now see both as real and interacting: colonial states left weak institutions, and some post-independence leaders exploited them rather than repairing them.

A related problem is the outside narrative. In 2000 The Economist ran a cover calling Africa "The hopeless continent". In 2011 it ran another titled "Africa rising". Both were sweeping. The Nigerian novelist Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, in a widely viewed 2009 talk, called this "the danger of a single story". The most useful habit is to ask "which country, which period, which people?" before accepting any claim about "Africa" as a whole.

Chapter 15

Reclaiming the past

The way Africa's history is written is also changing. African universities, museums and writers increasingly set the agenda. Looted objects such as the Benin Bronzes are beginning to return, as described in Colonialism. Ancient DNA is revealing migrations no written source recorded, and digital archives are making manuscripts from Timbuktu and Ethiopia readable worldwide.

The likely direction is a history that is less about what happened to Africa and more about what Africans did: the states they built, the trade they ran, the religions they shaped, and the ideas they sent abroad, from early human culture to modern music and literature. For a continent whose young population will soon be one of the world's largest, knowing that longer story is not a matter of pride alone. It is a basis for confidence about what comes next.

The core idea

Africa is where humanity began, and it built a long series of complex societies: Kush, Aksum, the Sahel empires, the Swahili coast, Great Zimbabwe, Benin and Kongo, linked by trade across deserts and oceans. The slave trades and colonial rule did deep damage, and the independent states of the last seventy years have had very different paths. The old claim that Africa had no history was a myth with a political purpose.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Homo sapiens: about 300,000 years in Africa (Jebel Irhoud).
  • About 12.5 million people carried in the Atlantic slave trade.
  • 1960: seventeen countries became independent.
  • About 1.5 billion people in 2024; around 2.5 billion projected for 2050.
The common mistake

Talking about "Africa" as if it were one country with one story. Ask which country, which period and which people, and most sweeping claims fall apart.

Test yourself

Can you explain how historians know about African societies that left few written records? If you can name three kinds of evidence, you have understood why the "no history" myth failed.
