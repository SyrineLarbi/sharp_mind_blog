The Silk Road. Not a road, not mostly silk, and more important than either.

The name suggests a single highway with camels plodding from China to Rome. Nothing like that existed. What existed was a web of short trips, oasis towns, mountain passes and sea lanes, used for about fifteen hundred years by people who mostly never saw either end. Goods moved along it slowly, passing from hand to hand. Ideas, religions, technologies and diseases moved along it too, and those are what changed the world. If you want to understand why paper, Buddhism, the stirrup and the Black Death all spread the way they did, this is where to look.

Question 1 · What is it?

A web of routes, given a name long after it ended

The Silk Road is a modern label for a very old reality: overland and sea connections across Eurasia, roughly from the second century BCE to the fifteenth century CE.

Chapter 01

A German geographer names a thing nobody had called it

No merchant in Samarkand or Chang'an ever said "I am travelling on the Silk Road". The phrase was invented in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, who was studying routes for a possible railway across China. He wrote Seidenstraße, "silk road", in the singular, to describe the overland links between Han China and the Roman world. Later writers made it plural, the Silk Roads, which is more accurate.

The label stuck because it is vivid, and it has shaped how people imagine the past. It suggests a fixed line on a map, one main product, and two great empires at either end. The reality was a shifting network with many centres, many products and many middlemen. Routes moved when an oasis dried up, a war closed a pass or a new ruler offered protection. Historians keep the name because everyone knows it, but they use it as a shorthand for "the connections across Asia", not as a description of a road.

Chapter 02

The map: deserts, passes and oases

Geography decided almost everything. Between western China and Persia lie some of the hardest landscapes on Earth: the Taklamakan Desert, the Tian Shan and Pamir mountains, and the high grasslands of the steppe, the vast belt of grass that runs from Mongolia to Hungary. Travellers could not cross the middle of the Taklamakan, so routes split around it, north and south, from one oasis to the next. An oasis is a fertile spot in a desert, fed by underground water or rivers from melting mountain snow.

Chapter 03

The sea road, which carried more

The overland routes get the romance, but a great deal of the trade, and over time probably most of the bulk trade, went by sea. Ships sailed from southern China through the Strait of Malacca, around India, to the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea and East Africa. Historians call this the maritime silk road, or the Indian Ocean trade.

The engine was the monsoon, the seasonal wind that blows from the south-west in summer and from the north-east in winter across the Indian Ocean. Sailors who knew the timetable could cross in one direction, trade, wait for the wind to turn, and sail home. Ships carry far more than camels, so heavy goods such as ceramics, pepper, timber and metals went by water. In 1998 divers off the Indonesian island of Belitung found a ninth-century Arab-style ship loaded with tens of thousands of pieces of Chinese pottery. It is one of the clearest physical proofs of how large this trade was.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Relay trade: many short journeys, not one long one

Understanding the Silk Road means understanding a relay. Goods crossed continents; people mostly did not.

Chapter 04

How it started: a diplomat looking for allies

The usual starting point is a Chinese envoy called Zhang Qian. Around 138 BCE Emperor Wu of the Han dynasty sent him west to find allies against the Xiongnu, a powerful nomadic confederation on China's northern frontier. The Xiongnu captured him and held him for about ten years. He escaped, continued west, failed to secure the alliance, and was captured again on the way home. He finally returned to the Han court roughly thirteen years after leaving.

His mission failed as diplomacy and succeeded as information. Zhang Qian reported kingdoms, markets and goods that the Han court had barely heard of, above all the large horses of the Ferghana valley, which the Chinese called "heavenly horses". The Han wanted them for cavalry, fought a campaign to get them, and pushed garrisons and watchtowers west into the Gansu Corridor. The routes opened because of security needs, not because of a love of trade. That pattern, states protecting roads for military reasons and merchants using them, repeats through the whole history.

Trade across Eurasia did not begin with Zhang Qian. Goods had moved between neighbouring peoples for centuries before him. What changed in the Han period was scale and regularity: a large state deliberately keeping corridors open.

Chapter 05

The relay: why nobody went all the way

A trader from Chang'an typically sold to someone in Dunhuang or Turfan, who sold to someone further west, and so on. Each stage added a profit margin and a tax. By the time a bolt of Chinese silk reached Rome it had passed through many hands and cost many times its original price.

Rome and Han China knew of each other only dimly. Around 97 CE a Chinese general sent an envoy, Gan Ying, to reach the Roman Empire. He got as far as the Persian Gulf region and turned back, reportedly after being told the sea crossing was long and dangerous. Some historians suspect the Parthians, the Persian empire in the middle, had every reason to discourage direct contact, because they profited from being the middleman. That is a plausible reading, not a proven one.

The relay system is the key to the Silk Road's character. Because goods changed hands so often, knowledge and customs changed hands too. Every market town needed translators, money changers, guarantors and guesthouses. That is why the routes spread languages, scripts and religions so effectively. The people in the middle were constantly meeting strangers.

Chapter 06

The Sogdians, the traders in the middle

For several centuries the most important merchants on the overland routes were the Sogdians, an Iranian-speaking people centred on Samarkand and Bukhara, in today's Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. They built communities in towns all along the way into China, rather as later trading diasporas did elsewhere. A diaspora is a population living outside its homeland but keeping its ties.

We know them partly through a lucky find. In 1907 the explorer Aurel Stein found a bag of letters in a ruined watchtower near Dunhuang, now called the Sogdian Ancient Letters, generally dated to the early fourth century CE. One is from a woman named Miwnay, abandoned in Dunhuang by a husband who had gone off on business. She writes that she would rather have married a dog or a pig. It is one of the most human documents of the ancient world, and it shows the routes were made of families, debts and broken promises as much as of goods.

Chapter 07

Caravans, camels and caravanserais

Overland, goods moved in caravans, groups of traders travelling together for safety. The workhorse was the Bactrian camel, the two-humped camel of Central Asia, which tolerates both desert heat and freezing mountain winters and can carry a heavy load for weeks. Mules, horses and donkeys worked the mountain passes.

Along many routes, especially in Persia and later the Islamic world, rulers and wealthy patrons built caravanserais. These were walled inns around a courtyard, often roughly a day's travel apart, where animals could be watered and goods locked away overnight. Building them was a way for a ruler to earn taxes and loyalty. Some still stand in Iran, Turkey and Uzbekistan.

What a caravan feared

Bandits, thirst, sandstorms and avalanches, yes. But traders also feared something more ordinary: instability. A dead ruler, a war between two kingdoms or a new tax could close a route for years. The Silk Road flourished in periods when large states kept order over long distances (the Han, the Tang, the early Islamic caliphates, the Mongols) and shrank when they fell apart.

Chapter 08

What was actually traded

Silk was the star export of China for centuries, partly because only China knew how to make it. Silk comes from the cocoons of the silkworm, which feeds on mulberry leaves, and the process of raising the worms and unwinding the thread, called sericulture, was a closely held skill. According to the Byzantine historian Procopius, monks smuggled silkworm eggs to Constantinople around the 550s, in the reign of Justinian, and Byzantium began its own silk industry.

Silk was also money. Tang China paid its frontier garrisons partly in bolts of silk, and in oasis towns such as Turfan those bolts circulated as currency. The historian Valerie Hansen, studying documents from the region, argued that this government spending on soldiers drove much of the local economy, more than private long-distance trade did.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The cargo nobody paid for

The goods were often luxuries for a small elite. What travelled alongside them, religions, technologies, art and germs, changed the lives of everyone.

Chapter 09

Religions on the move

Buddhism began in northern India and reached China along the routes, probably in the first century CE. Monks and merchants carried it together, since merchants often funded monasteries as acts of merit. The cave temples at Dunhuang, Kizil and elsewhere were painted and maintained for centuries with that kind of patronage. In the seventh century the Chinese monk Xuanzang walked to India and back, a journey of about sixteen years, and returned with hundreds of Buddhist texts that he spent the rest of his life translating.

Buddhism was not alone. A stone monument in Xi'an, set up in 781 CE, records a Christian community of the Church of the East (sometimes called Nestorian) in Tang China. Manichaeism, a Persian religion now extinct, became the state religion of the Uyghur empire in the eighth century. From the seventh century onward Islam spread across Central Asia, and later into western China, often through merchants and scholars rather than armies alone. The routes made Central Asia one of the most religiously mixed regions on Earth for a thousand years.

Chapter 10

Technologies: paper and printing

Paper was invented in China, with the court official Cai Lun traditionally credited with improving it around 105 CE. It spread west slowly. A famous story says Chinese prisoners taken at the Battle of Talas in 751, where Abbasid and Tang forces clashed in Central Asia, taught papermaking to craftsmen in Samarkand. Many historians now treat that story as a simplification, because paper was already known in Central Asia before then. Either way, by the ninth century Baghdad had paper mills, and cheap writing material fed the great age of Islamic scholarship and, centuries later, Europe's printing revolution.

The single most famous object from the routes is a printed book. In 1900 a Daoist monk named Wang Yuanlu found a sealed chamber in the Mogao Caves at Dunhuang, holding tens of thousands of manuscripts hidden around the year 1000. Among them was a copy of the Diamond Sutra, a Buddhist text, printed from woodblocks and dated 868 CE. It is the oldest surviving printed book with a firm date. Stein bought it in 1907, and it is now in the British Library. Who should own such objects remains a sore point, which Question 5 returns to.

Many other techniques and crops travelled the same way, in both directions: grape-growing into China, cotton and sugar cultivation across Asia, the stirrup and better harnesses, and later gunpowder and the magnetic compass westward. For some of these, how exactly they crossed is still debated.

Chapter 11

Germs: the deadliest export

Every network that carries goods also carries disease. The Plague of Justinian in the 540s CE, and much more dramatically the Black Death of the 1340s, both followed trade routes. The Black Death was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas on rodents and spread among people in several ways. In 2022 a team of researchers analysed ancient DNA from graves near Lake Issyk-Kul in today's Kyrgyzstan, dated to 1338–39, and argued that the pandemic strain emerged in that region of Central Asia, right on the routes.

From there it reached the Black Sea ports and, in 1347, Mediterranean ships carried it to Sicily and beyond. Within a few years it killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's population, and huge numbers in the Middle East and Asia as well. The routes that had made Eurasia richer for a millennium also made it one disease zone, which is the darker half of what connection means. Volume 18 tells the European side in The year a third of Europe died.

Chapter 13

Was it big or small? The historians' argument

One live debate is about scale. Popular books such as Peter Frankopan's The Silk Roads (2015) present the routes as the centre of world history, the place where empires rose and fell. Other historians, including Valerie Hansen in The Silk Road: A New History (2012), stress how small and local most overland trade looks in the surviving documents: modest quantities, short trips, and much of the economy driven by state spending rather than private merchants.

The two views are less opposed than they sound. The volume of goods may have been small while the impact of ideas and germs was enormous. A single monk carrying a few books, or a few fleas in a sack of grain, can change more than a thousand bolts of silk.

A second myth worth correcting is about the end. People often say the Ottoman Turks "closed" the Silk Road after taking Constantinople in 1453, forcing Europeans to sail around Africa. Most historians see a more gradual decline with several causes: the breakup of the Mongol empires in the fourteenth century, the spread of plague, the Ming dynasty's turn inward, and then the Portuguese sea route to India after Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1497–98, which made direct sea trade cheaper. The Ottomans themselves traded heavily with Europe. The overland routes faded because the sea became cheaper, not because a door slammed shut.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

An old name in new politics

The Silk Road ended centuries ago, but its name is now a tool of foreign policy, and its objects are part of a debate about who owns the past.

Chapter 14

New silk roads, sorted by confidence

In 2013 China's leader Xi Jinping announced what became the Belt and Road Initiative: a "Silk Road Economic Belt" over land and a "Maritime Silk Road" by sea. It funds ports, railways, power plants and roads across Asia, Africa, Europe and Latin America, with well over a hundred countries signing cooperation agreements. Supporters see much-needed infrastructure. Critics worry about debt, opaque contracts and political leverage. Both sides can point to real cases, and the balance differs a lot from country to country.

Chapter 15

Who owns the Silk Road's past?

In 2014 UNESCO listed a large section of routes running from the old Chinese capital through the Tian Shan mountains as a World Heritage site, nominated jointly by China, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. It was a sign that the routes now count as shared heritage across borders. But the treasures removed in the early twentieth century, from Dunhuang and other sites, are spread across museums and libraries in London, Paris, St Petersburg, Berlin, Tokyo and elsewhere. A long-running international project has put many of the Dunhuang manuscripts online, which lets scholars reunite collections without moving them.

The debate over whether such objects should be returned is part of a wider argument about colonial collecting, covered in Colonialism. The most likely future is digital sharing first and physical returns case by case, rather than a single settlement. The deeper lesson of the routes, that connection brings both enrichment and contagion, is one every new global network has to relearn.

The core idea

The Silk Road was a web of land and sea routes across Eurasia, active from about the second century BCE to the fifteenth century CE. Goods moved by relay, through many middlemen such as the Sogdians, and flourished when large states kept order. Its biggest effects came from what travelled unpriced: religions, technologies and disease.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The name dates from 1877 (Ferdinand von Richthofen).
  • Zhang Qian's mission began around 138 BCE.
  • The Diamond Sutra from Dunhuang, printed in 868 CE, is the oldest dated printed book.
  • The Black Death reached Europe in 1347 and killed perhaps a third or more of its people.
The common mistake

Picturing one road with merchants going from China to Rome. Almost nobody travelled the whole way, much of the trade went by sea, and silk was only one product among many.
