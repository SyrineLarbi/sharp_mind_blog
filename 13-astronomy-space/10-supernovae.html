Supernovae. The explosions that end stars and supply the raw material for everything else.

A few times in recorded history, a new star has suddenly appeared in the sky, bright enough to see in daylight, and faded over months. These were supernovae: stars exploding. For a few weeks a single one can outshine the billions of stars in its galaxy. Supernovae make and scatter many of the elements in your body, trigger the birth of new stars, and, because some kinds explode with a predictable brightness, they measure the universe itself. One of them gave the first clue that the expansion of the universe is speeding up.

Question 1 · What is it?

The explosive death of a star

A supernova is a star blowing itself apart, either because its core collapses or because a dead star is pushed past its limit. Long before anyone knew what they were, they changed how people thought about the sky.

Chapter 01

The new star that broke the heavens

On an evening in November 1572, a young Danish nobleman named Tycho Brahe looked up and saw a star in the constellation Cassiopeia that had not been there before. It was brighter than Venus. Following Aristotle, most European scholars taught that the heavens beyond the Moon were perfect and unchanging, so anything new had to be a nearby event in the atmosphere, like a comet was then thought to be.

Tycho tested that. If the new star were close, it would shift slightly against the background stars as Earth turned, just as a nearby finger shifts against a distant wall when you close one eye and then the other. He measured carefully and found no shift. The new star was far beyond the Moon. The heavens could change. His 1573 book on it, De nova stella ("On the new star"), gave us the word "nova". It was one of the blows that, together with Copernicus and later Galileo, broke the ancient picture of the cosmos.

Tycho had not seen a new star. He had seen an old one dying. The term supernova was coined in the 1930s by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky to separate these enormous explosions from ordinary novae, which are much smaller outbursts on the surface of a white dwarf.

Astronomers also use a letter system based on the light (Type I has no hydrogen in its spectrum, Type II does), with subtypes. The letters are historical and a little confusing. What matters is the physics: one kind is a collapse, the other is a detonation.

Chapter 03

Explosions in the historical record

The brightest one recorded appeared in 1006, in the southern constellation Lupus. Observers in China, Japan, the Arab world and Europe wrote about it. The Egyptian astronomer Ali ibn Ridwan described it as several times larger than Venus. In 1054 Chinese astronomers recorded a "guest star" in Taurus that was visible in daylight for weeks. Its debris is the Crab Nebula, with a pulsar spinning at its centre (Neutron stars).

The last supernova clearly seen with the naked eye in our galaxy was in 1604, studied by Johannes Kepler. Since then, none. That is odd, because astronomers estimate the Milky Way produces one or two per century. The explanation is dust. Most supernovae in our galaxy happen behind thick clouds that hide them in visible light. Radio and X-ray telescopes have found the remains of at least two explosions in the last few centuries that nobody saw at the time.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A collapse that rebounds, and a bomb that detonates

The two kinds of supernova are driven by different physics. Both release in seconds as much energy as a star like the Sun produces in its whole life, or far more.

Chapter 04

Core collapse, step by step

A massive star spends millions of years fusing hydrogen into helium, then helium into carbon and oxygen, and so on to heavier elements. Each stage is shorter than the last. The final stage, building iron, may last only about a day. By then the star has layers like an onion, with lighter elements outside and an iron core at the centre.

The last moments of a massive star
  1. ONION      H | He | C,O | Ne,Mg | Si | Fe core    iron fusion gives no energy
  2. COLLAPSE   core (~1.4 Suns) falls in             under a second
  3. BOUNCE     core becomes neutron matter, stiffens infalling layers hit it and rebound
  4. NEUTRINOS  a flood of neutrinos heats the gas    the stalled shock is revived
  5. EXPLOSION  outer layers blasted out              brightens over days to weeks
  6. REMNANT    neutron star or black hole            plus an expanding debris cloud

Fusing iron absorbs energy instead of releasing it, so the core has nothing left to resist gravity. It collapses in a fraction of a second. Electrons are crushed into protons, making neutrons and an enormous burst of neutrinos. When the core reaches the density of an atomic nucleus it suddenly stiffens, and the material still falling in rebounds off it, sending a shock wave outward.

About 99 per cent of the energy of a core-collapse supernova leaves as neutrinos. The visible explosion, bright enough to outshine a galaxy, is only the remaining one per cent or so.

Chapter 05

Why it explodes at all

For decades, computer simulations of collapsing stars had an embarrassing problem: the stars would not explode. The shock wave from the bounce lost energy as it ploughed through the infalling material and stalled a few hundred kilometres out. The star simply fell back in.

The leading answer is that the neutrinos revive it. Although neutrinos barely interact with matter, there are so many of them that a small fraction absorbed behind the shock adds enough heat to push it outward again. Simulations confirmed something else: the process only works well in full three dimensions, where hot gas can churn and bubble like a boiling pot. With modern supercomputers, many 3D simulations now produce explosions. The basic mechanism is now widely accepted, but predicting which stars explode and which collapse quietly into black holes remains an active research area.

Chapter 06

The white dwarf bomb

A star like the Sun ends as a white dwarf: a hot core about the size of Earth, made mostly of carbon and oxygen, with no fusion. It is held up by electron degeneracy pressure, a quantum effect similar to the one in neutron stars. In 1930 the young Indian physicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar calculated that this support fails above about 1.4 times the Sun's mass. That is now called the Chandrasekhar limit, and it later contributed to his 1983 Nobel Prize.

If a white dwarf in a close pair gains mass and approaches that limit, the pressure and heat in its centre ignite carbon fusion. In ordinary stars, extra heat makes the gas expand and the fusion slows, a built-in thermostat. In a white dwarf there is no thermostat. The fusion runs away and, within seconds, burns a large part of the star and blows it completely apart.

Exactly how the white dwarf reaches that point is genuinely debated. In one picture it slowly pulls gas from a normal companion star. In another, two white dwarfs spiral together and merge. Evidence supports both, and both may occur. Searches for surviving companion stars near old Type Ia remnants have mostly come up empty, which some researchers take as evidence for mergers.

Chapter 07

The long afterglow

The flash of the explosion itself is brief. What keeps a supernova shining for weeks and months is radioactivity. The explosion makes large amounts of an unstable form of nickel. It decays into cobalt within days, and the cobalt decays into stable iron over months. Each decay releases energy that heats the expanding debris and makes it glow. Astronomers can see this in the way the light fades at a rate matching those decays.

Over thousands of years, the debris ploughs into surrounding gas and forms a supernova remnant: a glowing, expanding shell of hot gas, such as the Crab Nebula or Cassiopeia A. The shock waves at their edges are thought to accelerate many of the cosmic rays, the high-energy particles that constantly strike Earth's atmosphere. Remnants stay visible for tens of thousands of years before they fade into the gas of the galaxy.

Chapter 08

SN 1987A: the one we watched

On 24 February 1987 astronomers discovered a supernova in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy orbiting the Milky Way about 168,000 light-years away. It was the nearest seen since Kepler's in 1604, bright enough to see with the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere.

The best part came from underground. About three hours before the first light was seen, detectors in Japan, the United States and Russia, built to look for other things, recorded a total of about two dozen neutrinos within a few seconds of each other. Out of the vast numbers that passed through Earth, those few were caught. They were direct evidence of core collapse, exactly as theory predicted, and the start of neutrino astronomy beyond the Sun.

For decades the expected neutron star could not be seen. In 2024 a team using the James Webb Space Telescope reported emission from the centre that is best explained by a neutron star heating the surrounding gas. If that holds up, astronomers have watched the full life of one supernova, from the first neutrinos to the remnant.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Elements, cosmic distances and the birth of new stars

Supernovae make and scatter much of the matter we are made of, serve as the best measuring sticks for the distant universe, and stir galaxies into making new stars.

Chapter 09

The elements of life, delivered

The universe began with almost only hydrogen and helium. Stars make heavier elements by fusion, but those elements stay locked inside unless something releases them. Supernovae both make elements and throw them into space, where they enrich the gas that forms the next generation of stars and planets.

The division of labour is roughly this. Core-collapse supernovae supply much of the universe's oxygen, the most common element in Earth's crust and in your body by mass, along with neon, magnesium, silicon and calcium. Type Ia supernovae produce a large share of the iron, including much of the iron in your blood. The very heaviest elements, such as gold and uranium, need extra neutrons. Neutron-star mergers are one confirmed source of those (Neutron stars), and whether some rare supernovae contribute significantly is still debated.

Chapter 10

Measuring the universe, and the surprise of 1998

To measure a distance in space, astronomers need a standard candle, an object whose true brightness is known. Compare that with how bright it looks, and you get its distance. Type Ia supernovae are nearly ideal. They are bright enough to see across billions of light-years, and their peak brightness is fairly uniform. Even better, the brighter ones fade more slowly, so astronomers can correct for the small differences.

In the 1990s two rival teams used Type Ia supernovae to measure how the expansion of the universe had changed over time. Both expected it to be slowing under gravity. In 1998 both reported the opposite: distant supernovae were fainter than expected, meaning the expansion is accelerating. The cause was named dark energy. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The whole story is in Physics · Dark energy.

Supernovae are also at the centre of a live dispute called the Hubble tension. Measurements of today's expansion rate that use supernovae and pulsing stars give a value about 8 per cent higher than the value inferred from the Big Bang's leftover glow. Whether this points to new physics or to hidden measurement errors is one of the most debated questions in cosmology as of 2026.

Chapter 11

Sculptors of galaxies, and traces on Earth

The shock waves of supernovae compress nearby gas clouds, which can trigger the collapse of new stars. They also heat and stir the gas of galaxies, and in small galaxies they can blow gas out altogether, slowing star formation. Galaxy simulations need this supernova feedback to produce realistic galaxies.

Our own neighbourhood bears the marks. The Sun sits inside the Local Bubble, a region of thin, hot gas several hundred light-years across, thought to have been carved out by a series of supernovae over the last several million years. Even Earth holds a trace: deep-sea sediments and crusts contain small amounts of iron-60, a radioactive form of iron that does not occur naturally on Earth today and is made in stellar explosions. Its dating points to one or more supernovae a few hundred light-years away, roughly two to three million years ago. Supernova debris has literally fallen on our planet. Whether those events had any effect on life is an open, and so far speculative, question.

Creators and destroyers

The deepest point is that creation and destruction are the same event. The explosion that could strip a nearby planet's atmosphere is also what supplied the oxygen and iron for planets to exist at all. Without earlier supernovae, Earth would not be here to worry about the next one.

Chapter 14

Betelgeuse, and whether one could hurt us

The red giant Betelgeuse, the bright reddish star at Orion's shoulder, is a massive star near the end of its life. In late 2019 and early 2020 it dimmed dramatically, and there was public excitement that it was about to explode. Later studies showed the "Great Dimming" was mostly caused by a cloud of dust the star had puffed out, combined with a cooler patch on its surface. Betelgeuse is expected to explode at some point within roughly the next hundred thousand years. That could be next year or long after recorded history ends.

When it does, it will be a spectacular sight, perhaps as bright as a half Moon for weeks and visible in daylight. But it is far too distant to harm Earth, at roughly 500 to 700 light-years. Estimates of the danger zone, where a supernova's radiation could seriously damage the ozone layer, are usually in the range of a few tens of light-years. No star that close is expected to explode in the next few million years.

So the honest summary for anyone worried is: supernovae are among the most violent events in nature, but the risk to Earth in any human timescale is very low. For astronomers, the problem is the opposite. They want one close enough to study with every modern instrument at once, and nature has not obliged since 1604.

The core idea

A supernova is a star exploding, in one of two main ways. In a core-collapse supernova, a massive star's iron core collapses and the rebound, helped by neutrinos, blasts out its outer layers, leaving a neutron star or black hole. In a Type Ia supernova, a white dwarf near 1.4 solar masses undergoes runaway fusion and is destroyed. Both make and scatter elements.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Core collapse needs a star of more than about 8 solar masses.
  • About 99 per cent of a core-collapse supernova's energy leaves as neutrinos.
  • The Milky Way has roughly one or two per century; the last one seen was in 1604.
The common mistake

Thinking that all supernovae are the same kind of event, or that the Sun will become one. The Sun is far too light; it will end quietly as a white dwarf.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Type Ia supernovae work as measuring sticks while core-collapse supernovae do not? If so, you understand the link between their physics and their use.

The words, in plain words
Supernova
The explosion of a star, briefly as bright as a whole galaxy.
Nova
A much smaller outburst on the surface of a white dwarf, which survives it.
Core-collapse supernova
The explosion of a massive star whose iron core has collapsed.
Type Ia supernova
The thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf in a pair of stars.
White dwarf
The dense, Earth-sized remnant of a Sun-like star.
Chandrasekhar limit
The maximum mass a white dwarf can have, about 1.4 times the Sun's.
Neutrino
A nearly massless particle that passes through almost anything; supernovae release vast numbers.
Supernova remnant
The expanding cloud of gas and debris left after the explosion.
Standard candle
An object of known true brightness, used to measure distance.
Cosmic rays
High-energy particles from space, many probably accelerated by supernova remnants.
Hubble tension
The disagreement between two ways of measuring how fast the universe expands today.
Local Bubble
The cavity of hot, thin gas around the Sun, carved by past supernovae.
