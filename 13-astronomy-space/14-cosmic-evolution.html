Cosmic evolution. How a smooth, hot glow turned into galaxies, stars and you.

380,000 years after the Big Bang the universe was almost perfectly smooth: a thin, even gas of hydrogen and helium with no stars, no planets and no carbon. Today it is a web of galaxies, full of heavy elements, with at least one planet that has readers on it. Cosmic evolution is the story of how one became the other. The driving force is surprisingly simple. Gravity takes tiny differences and makes them larger, for billions of years.

Question 1 · What is it?

The universe has a history, not just a layout

Cosmic evolution means the way the universe's structure and contents have changed over 13.8 billion years. It has nothing to do with biological evolution, apart from the shared idea that the present was built step by step.

Chapter 01

A universe with a biography

For most of history people imagined the heavens as fixed. Stars were eternal points on a sphere. Even after Copernicus and Galileo, the universe was a place, not a process. The Big Bang changed that. If the universe began hot and smooth, then everything in it now, from galaxies to the iron in your blood, had to be made at some point. Astronomy turned into a kind of archaeology.

Its great advantage over ordinary archaeology is that the past is still visible. Light from a galaxy ten billion light years away left it ten billion years ago, so we see that galaxy as it was then. Telescopes looking deeper and deeper are looking at earlier and earlier chapters. The universe does not just have a history. It shows it to us, in order, as a function of distance.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Gravity amplifies, stars cook, and galaxies merge

Three processes do almost all the work. Gravity pulls slightly denser regions into denser ones. Stars turn hydrogen into heavier elements. Galaxies grow by swallowing gas and each other.

Chapter 03

The rich get richer

The early universe was smooth, but not perfectly. The cosmic microwave background shows differences of about one part in 100,000. That is the whole starting capital. A region slightly denser than average has slightly more gravity, so it pulls in matter from around it, which makes it denser still. Gravity is an amplifier: small differences grow, and they never shrink back on their own.

Ordinary matter could not do this alone, at least not fast enough. In the early universe it was tied to light, which kept pushing it apart. Dark matter, which ignores light, began clumping earlier. It formed invisible clouds called halos, and once ordinary gas was free it fell into them like water into holes in the sand. Without dark matter, computer models cannot build today's galaxies in the time available. That is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that it exists, as Dark matter explains.

Chapter 04

The dark ages and the first stars

After the cosmic microwave background was released, the universe went dark. There was no source of visible light at all, just cooling gas slowly gathering in dark matter halos. This period, the cosmic dark ages, lasted perhaps a hundred million years or more.

It ended when gas at the centre of a halo became dense and hot enough for nuclear fusion. The first stars switched on, an event called cosmic dawn. These first stars were made of pure hydrogen and helium. Theory says that without heavier elements to help gas cool and fragment, they were probably very massive, perhaps tens to hundreds of times the Sun, and short-lived. Astronomers call them Population III stars. None has been seen directly. As of 2026 there are only tentative hints from the James Webb Space Telescope of galaxies that may contain some.

The other way to study the dark ages is through hydrogen itself. Neutral hydrogen gives off or absorbs a faint radio signal at a wavelength of 21 centimetres. Mapping that signal from the early universe would show the dark ages directly. In 2018 a small Australian experiment called EDGES reported a detection that was much stronger than expected. A 2022 experiment in India, SARAS 3, did not see it. The question is open, and larger radio telescopes are being built to settle it.

Chapter 05

Clearing the fog a second time

The first stars and galaxies poured out ultraviolet light. Ultraviolet light is energetic enough to knock electrons off hydrogen atoms, which is called ionising them. Bubbles of ionised gas grew around each early galaxy, joined up, and eventually filled space. This is reionisation. It was mostly finished by about one billion years after the Big Bang.

The evidence comes partly from distant quasars, the brilliant cores of galaxies powered by giant black holes. Light from the most distant ones shows a clear sign of neutral hydrogen along the way: a band of their light is absorbed almost completely. In slightly closer quasars that band is no longer blocked. Somewhere in between, the fog cleared.

The record for the most distant galaxy confirmed keeps moving. As of 2025, Webb had confirmed galaxies seen as they were less than 300 million years after the Big Bang. Many are brighter than models expected for such an early time, which is now one of the field's liveliest puzzles.

Chapter 06

Galaxies grow by eating

Galaxies were not born at full size. Small dark matter halos formed first. They merged into bigger ones, and the galaxies inside merged with them. Astronomers call this hierarchical, or bottom-up, formation. Our own Milky Way is still doing it: it is slowly pulling apart and absorbing several small companion galaxies, leaving streams of their stars across the sky.

On the largest scales the result is the cosmic web. Galaxy surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, which measured the distances of millions of galaxies, show that galaxies are not spread evenly. They lie along long filaments and sheets, with huge empty regions called voids between them, like the walls of foam with air inside. Galaxy clusters sit where filaments cross. Computer simulations that start from the CMB's ripples and add gravity and dark matter produce the same pattern. That match between a simulation and a sky map is one of the great quiet triumphs of modern science.

Two kinds of galaxy

Galaxies come in two broad families. Blue spirals still have gas and still form stars. Red ellipticals, often the products of big mergers, have mostly run out of usable gas and contain old stars. Over cosmic time, more and more galaxies have moved from the first family to the second. More in Galaxies.

Chapter 07

Cosmic noon and the long afternoon

By adding up star formation in galaxies at different distances, astronomers can chart the universe's birth rate of stars over time. It rose quickly after cosmic dawn, peaked about 10 billion years ago, a period nicknamed cosmic noon, and has fallen roughly tenfold since.

Why the decline? Galaxies need cold gas to make stars, and several things cut off the supply. Gas gets used up. The expansion of the universe slows the flow of new gas into galaxies. And energy from exploding stars and from feeding black holes heats or blows out gas that could have made stars, a process called feedback. The black hole at a galaxy's centre and the galaxy itself appear to grow together, and the biggest galaxies seem to have been switched off by their own black holes.

One puzzle sits here. Some quasars are powered by black holes of more than a billion Suns within the first billion years. It is not clear how black holes grew that big that quickly. Ideas include very massive "seeds" formed directly from collapsing gas clouds. See Black holes.

Chapter 08

Each generation of stars enriches the next

The Big Bang made almost only hydrogen and helium. Everything heavier was made later. Stars fuse light nuclei into heavier ones in their cores. When big stars explode as supernovae, they release those elements and make more in the blast. Collisions of neutron stars make many of the heaviest, including much of the gold and platinum. In 2017 such a collision was detected both in gravitational waves and in light, and the light showed fresh heavy elements.

Each new generation of stars forms from gas polluted by earlier ones, so the share of heavy elements rises over time. Astronomers, with an odd habit, call every element heavier than helium a "metal". The oldest stars in our galaxy have very low metal content. The Sun, born 4.6 billion years ago, is a later-generation star with heavy elements making up around one to two per cent of its mass. That small percentage is the raw material for rocky planets and for life.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

You could not have existed early

Cosmic evolution explains why the universe had to be billions of years old before planets like Earth could form, and it is one of the best tests of physics on the largest scale.

Chapter 09

Why it took nine billion years to make a solar system

A rocky planet needs silicon, oxygen, iron and magnesium. Life as we know it needs carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and more. In the first few hundred million years those elements barely existed. They had to be built and scattered by generations of stars first. Earth needed a universe that had already been working for billions of years.

This also explains some practical facts. The calcium in your bones was made in stars. The iron in your blood came largely from exploding stars. Uranium in nuclear power stations was made in violent events before the Sun existed, and it is still slowly decaying, which is why its age can be used as a clock. The phrase "we are made of star stuff", popularised by Carl Sagan, is literally true for every element in you except hydrogen and some helium (Sagan's wider work is in Volume 53).

Chapter 10

A test bench for physics

The universe is the only laboratory big enough to test gravity, dark matter and dark energy at full scale. The approach is this. Take the initial conditions measured in the CMB. Add known physics plus dark matter and dark energy. Run a computer simulation for 13.8 billion years. Compare the result with the real sky.

Over large scales the agreement is striking: the cosmic web, the number of galaxy clusters, the way galaxies cluster at different distances. A wrong theory of gravity or of dark matter would produce a visibly different universe. That is how cosmologists can rule out whole classes of idea, such as dark matter made of fast-moving particles, which would wash out small structures that we see.

It also shapes what we expect to find elsewhere. If heavy elements are rare in the early universe and common now, then rocky planets are a fairly recent development in cosmic terms, which matters for the question in The possibility of extraterrestrial life.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Where the model succeeds, and where it strains

For a scientific picture, the strengths are what it explains and predicts. The weaknesses are what it cannot yet explain, or explains only by adjustment.

The pattern is honest science. The broad history is firm. The details of the first billion years are where the surprises are. Much of this tension appeared only after Webb began work in 2022, so expect the picture to keep shifting for some years. Note also the difference between two kinds of problem. A surprise in how galaxies formed is a problem for messy astrophysics, such as gas, stars and black holes. It is not, by itself, a problem for the Big Bang or for expansion.

The next discoveries, and the very long future

Two kinds of future: what we will learn in the coming decades, and what will happen to the universe over billions and trillions of years. Both are sorted by confidence.

Chapter 13

The long future of the universe

Here the sorting by confidence matters more than anywhere.

Fairly sure

The Sun will brighten slowly and make Earth too hot for oceans in roughly a billion years. In about five billion years it will swell into a red giant. The Andromeda galaxy is approaching ours. A merger was long thought certain in about five billion years. A 2025 study put the odds within the next ten billion years closer to even.

Plausible

If dark energy stays roughly as it is, distant galaxies will slip out of view over the next hundred billion years or more, leaving each group of galaxies alone in the dark. Star formation will dwindle. Small red dwarf stars, which can shine for trillions of years, will be the last.

Speculative

After the stars, black holes slowly evaporate over unimaginable times, and the universe approaches a cold, even state with nothing left to happen, often called heat death. If dark energy changes, other endings, such as a "big rip" or a recollapse, are possible. Recent hints that it may be weakening keep this open.

The deep lesson is about time. We live early. The universe is 13.8 billion years old, but stars will keep forming for vastly longer than that. Present-day astronomers live in a lucky window when other galaxies are still visible and the cosmic microwave background can still be measured. Far-future observers in a lonely galaxy might find no evidence that a Big Bang ever happened.

The core idea

Starting from tiny ripples, gravity, helped by dark matter, built halos, then the first stars, then galaxies that merged into a cosmic web. Each generation of stars enriched the gas with heavier elements, until rocky planets and life became possible.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First stars: roughly 100 to 200 million years after the Big Bang (uncertain).
  • Reionisation mostly complete by about 1 billion years.
  • Star formation peaked about 10 billion years ago and has fallen about tenfold.
  • The Sun formed 4.6 billion years ago, when the universe was about 9 billion years old.
The common mistake

Treating Webb's surprising early galaxies as a disproof of the Big Bang. They challenge models of galaxy formation, not the expansion of the universe.

Can you explain why Earth could not have formed in the first billion years? If you can, you have joined chemical enrichment to the timeline.

Cosmic dawn
The switching-on of the first stars.
Population III stars

Building big structures by merging smaller ones.

The era about 10 billion years ago when star formation peaked.

Energy from stars and black holes that heats or expels gas and slows star birth.
Metals (in astronomy)
Every element heavier than helium.
