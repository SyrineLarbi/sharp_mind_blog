The Milky Way. Mapping a city of stars while standing in one of its suburbs.

On a dark night far from towns, a pale band of light crosses the sky. That band is our own galaxy, seen edge-on from inside. Every star you can see with the naked eye belongs to it. Working out its shape has been hard for a simple reason: we live inside it, behind clouds of dust, and cannot step outside to take a picture. Yet in the last decade a single spacecraft has measured the positions and motions of nearly two billion of its stars, and the galaxy's history has turned out to be more violent than anyone expected.

Question 1 · What is it?

A barred spiral galaxy, seen from inside

The Milky Way is a disc of a few hundred billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across, with a bar of stars across its centre and a giant black hole at its heart. The Sun is one of its stars, about halfway out.

Chapter 01

A river of milk

Many cultures named the band of light in the night sky. To the ancient Greeks it was spilled milk, and the word "galaxy" comes from the Greek gala, milk. Elsewhere it was a river, a road for souls, or a seam in the sky. What it actually was stayed unknown until 1610, when Galileo turned his small telescope on it and saw that the glow was made of countless faint stars, too dim and too close together for the eye to separate.

Why does the galaxy look like a band rather than filling the sky evenly? Because it is flat. Picture yourself inside a very wide, thin pancake of stars. Look up or down, through the thin direction, and you see relatively few stars. Look along the pancake, and you see stars piled behind stars into the distance. That direction is the band. The Milky Way in the sky is our galaxy's disc seen edge-on from within.

Chapter 02

Mapping a forest from inside the trees

In the 1780s the astronomer William Herschel and his sister Caroline counted stars in hundreds of directions to work out the galaxy's shape. They concluded it was a flattened disc with the Sun near the middle. They were right about the disc and wrong about the middle. The reason they were wrong took over a century to find.

Space between the stars is not empty. It contains thin clouds of gas and fine dust, which dim and redden the light behind them, like fog around streetlights. Looking along the disc, you can see only a few thousand light-years before the dust hides everything. Herschel saw roughly the same number of stars in every direction along the band, not because he was at the centre but because the fog hid everything beyond a certain distance equally.

In 1918 the American astronomer Harlow Shapley took a different approach. He studied globular clusters, tight balls of hundreds of thousands of old stars that orbit above and below the disc, where there is little dust. They were not spread evenly around the sky. They were concentrated towards the constellation Sagittarius. Shapley argued that they must surround the galaxy's true centre, which therefore lay far away in that direction. He moved the Sun from the centre of the galaxy to its outskirts, a demotion almost as large as the one Copernicus made for Earth.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Anatomy, motion and a violent past

The galaxy has distinct parts, each with its own population of stars and its own history. Everything in it is moving, including us, and its shape still carries the scars of the smaller galaxies it swallowed.

The disc is where most stars, gas and dust are, and where new stars are born. It holds the spiral arms. The bar and bulge are a dense central region of mostly old stars. In the 2000s, infrared surveys that see through the dust confirmed that the central part is elongated into a bar, making the Milky Way a barred spiral.

The halo is a thin, roughly spherical cloud of old stars and about 150 globular clusters around the whole galaxy. Beyond and through it all spreads the dark matter halo, which accounts for most of the galaxy's mass. Its presence shows in the way stars far out still orbit fast, exactly as described in Galaxies.

How many major spiral arms the Milky Way has is still debated. Some studies favour two main arms, others four, with several smaller branches. That uncertainty is a measure of how hard it is to map the disc from inside it.

Chapter 05

The Sun's long orbit

The Sun is not standing still. It orbits the centre of the galaxy at roughly 230 kilometres per second, about 800,000 kilometres an hour. Even so, one lap takes about 230 million years, sometimes called a "galactic year". The last time the Sun was at this point in its orbit, the first dinosaurs were just appearing. Since it formed, the Sun has gone round perhaps twenty times.

The Sun lies in a minor arm segment called the Orion Arm or Orion Spur, between two major arms. Its neighbourhood is fairly quiet: no very close massive stars, and no dense star cluster. The Earth's air and the Sun's magnetic bubble shield us from most of the radiation that passes through this part of space.

Chapter 06

The giant at the centre

At the centre of the galaxy lies a compact radio source named Sagittarius A* (pronounced "A-star"). It is invisible in ordinary light because dust blocks the view, but infrared light gets through. From the 1990s two teams, one led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany and one by Andrea Ghez in the United States, tracked individual stars orbiting it over many years.

One star, called S2, completes an orbit in about sixteen years and at its closest passes at a few per cent of the speed of light. From such orbits the teams calculated that the central object has about four million times the mass of the Sun packed into a region smaller than our solar system. Nothing except a black hole fits. Genzel and Ghez shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work, and in 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope released an image of the black hole's shadow. The physics is covered in Physics · Black holes.

Today Sagittarius A* is very quiet, feeding only on scraps. It was not always so. In 2010 the Fermi gamma-ray telescope found two enormous bubbles of energetic gas extending tens of thousands of light-years above and below the centre. Their cause is not settled, but a past outburst from the black hole, or a burst of star formation near the centre, are the leading ideas.

Chapter 07

A cannibal galaxy

The European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, spent over a decade measuring the positions, distances and motions of nearly two billion stars before it was retired in 2025. It turned the galaxy's history into something that can be read, like rings in a tree.

In 2018 astronomers using Gaia data found a large group of halo stars moving on unusual, stretched orbits, many of them travelling in the opposite direction to most stars. They are the remains of a fairly large galaxy that collided with the Milky Way roughly 8 to 11 billion years ago. It has been given two names, Gaia-Enceladus and the Gaia Sausage, the second from the sausage-like shape its stars make in a plot of their speeds. That merger probably puffed up part of the early disc and supplied much of the inner halo.

The swallowing continues. The Sagittarius dwarf galaxy, discovered in 1994, is being torn apart right now on the far side of the galaxy, and its stars are strung out in a stream that wraps around the Milky Way. Dozens of other stellar streams trace smaller victims. Even the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, the two bright satellite galaxies visible from the Southern Hemisphere, will probably be absorbed in the distant future. The Milky Way was built partly by eating its neighbours.

Chapter 08

Still making stars

The Milky Way is not a dead galaxy. It forms new stars at a modest rate, roughly the equivalent of one to a few Suns' worth of gas each year. Most are small. The nurseries are giant clouds of cold gas, such as the Orion Nebula, about 1,300 light-years away and visible as a fuzzy patch in Orion's sword.

The galaxy also loses stars in explosions. Astronomers estimate a supernova in the Milky Way every few decades on average, yet the last one clearly seen from Earth was in 1604. Most are hidden behind dust, which is why infrared and neutrino detectors are now watching for the next one (Supernovae). The material they throw out is mixed into new clouds, and the cycle continues.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Our address, our laboratory, and a sky we are losing

The Milky Way is where we live, the one galaxy we can study star by star, and a shared human heritage that most people alive today can no longer see.

Chapter 09

Knowing where we are

A full address for Earth would read: third planet of the Sun, in the Orion Spur of the Milky Way, a barred spiral in the Local Group, on the outskirts of the Virgo Supercluster. Each step outward was a discovery, and each one moved humanity further from the centre of things.

Knowing the address has practical uses too. Our place in the galaxy affects which parts of the sky we can see clearly, which nearby stars might host planets that telescopes can study, and how much cosmic radiation reaches the solar system. Some researchers have proposed a galactic habitable zone, a ring that is neither too crowded with exploding stars near the centre nor too poor in heavy elements at the edge. The idea is plausible in outline but contested in detail, and it should not be taken as a firm map of where life can exist.

Chapter 10

The only galaxy we can see star by star

For other galaxies, astronomers usually see blended light from billions of stars. In the Milky Way they can measure individual stars: their ages, their chemistry, their orbits. This is called galactic archaeology. Stars keep the chemical makeup of the gas they formed from, so grouping stars by chemistry and motion reveals which ones were born together and which were captured from other galaxies.

The Milky Way is therefore the testing ground for theories of all galaxies. If a model of how galaxies grow cannot reproduce the Milky Way, star by star, it is in trouble. Gaia's data, freely released to scientists worldwide, has already led to many thousands of research papers, and its final data releases are expected over the rest of this decade.

Chapter 11

The disappearing night sky

For nearly all of human history, the Milky Way was part of everyone's night. It shaped myths, calendars and navigation. Today artificial light washes it out over most of the populated world. A widely cited 2016 study of night-sky brightness concluded that about a third of humanity, and around 80 per cent of people in North America, can no longer see the Milky Way from where they live.

This matters beyond nostalgia. Light pollution disturbs migrating birds, nesting turtles and insects, and wastes energy. It also hinders astronomy. A growing number of bright satellites in low orbit adds streaks to telescope images, a problem professional astronomers have raised with regulators. Unlike most environmental problems, this one can be reduced quickly and cheaply, with shielded lights that point downward and lower brightness. Several places now protect their skies as designated dark-sky reserves.

Astronomers work around the blind spots by looking in other kinds of light. Infrared and radio waves pass through dust, which is why the galactic centre is studied in those bands. They also compare the Milky Way with similar galaxies seen from outside, such as Andromeda, to check what our inside view might be missing.

Chapter 14

The meeting with Andromeda

Most galaxies are moving away from us as the universe expands. Andromeda is an exception. It is falling towards the Milky Way at roughly 110 kilometres per second, pulled by gravity within the Local Group. A widely publicised 2012 study using the Hubble Space Telescope predicted a head-on collision in about four to five billion years.

Newer work has made the picture less certain. A 2025 study that combined Hubble and Gaia data and included the pull of other Local Group galaxies, especially the Large Magellanic Cloud and the Triangulum galaxy, concluded that the chance of a merger within the next ten billion years is roughly even, not near-certain. The result depends on masses and motions that are still imperfectly measured.

If the collision happens, it will not be a catastrophe for the Sun. Stars are so far apart that almost none will collide. The two galaxies would swing through each other, form tidal tails, and eventually settle into one large, probably elliptical, galaxy, sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda". By then the Sun will be near the end of its life anyway (The Sun). For a future observer, the night sky would change completely, but the solar system would very likely survive the encounter.

The core idea

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy of a few hundred billion stars, seen from inside as a band across the sky. The Sun orbits about 26,000 light-years from a central black hole of four million solar masses. The galaxy grew partly by swallowing smaller galaxies, and it is still doing so.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 100,000 light-years across, about 1,000 thick.
  • One solar orbit takes about 230 million years.
  • Gaia measured nearly two billion stars.
The common mistake

Thinking the Milky Way is just the band of light, or that photos of "the Milky Way galaxy" seen from above are real. No one has ever photographed our galaxy from outside; those images are artists' impressions based on measurements.

The words, in plain words
Barred spiral
A spiral galaxy whose arms begin at the ends of a central bar of stars.
Galactic disc
The thin, flat part of the galaxy holding most stars, gas and the spiral arms.
Bulge
The dense central region of old stars.
Halo
The sparse, roughly spherical region of old stars and globular clusters around the disc.
Globular cluster
A tight ball of hundreds of thousands of very old stars orbiting in the halo.
Interstellar dust
Fine grains between the stars that dim and redden light, like fog.
Galactic year
The time the Sun takes to orbit the galaxy's centre, about 230 million years.
Orion Spur
The minor arm segment where the Sun lives.
Sagittarius A*
The supermassive black hole at the galaxy's centre.
Stellar stream
A long trail of stars pulled from a small galaxy or cluster being torn apart.
Galactic archaeology
Reconstructing a galaxy's history from the ages, chemistry and orbits of its stars.
Local Group
The small group of galaxies that includes the Milky Way, Andromeda and dozens of dwarfs.
