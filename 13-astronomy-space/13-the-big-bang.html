The Big Bang. Not an explosion in space, but space itself starting to stretch.

A century ago most scientists, Einstein included, assumed the universe had always existed and would always look roughly the same. Today the standard view is that it had a hot, dense beginning about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding and cooling ever since. That change of mind came from three pieces of evidence that nobody could explain any other way.

Question 1 · What is it?

A theory about how the universe changed, not about how it was made

The Big Bang theory describes the universe's history from a fraction of a second after the start. It says a great deal about what happened next and almost nothing about why there was a start at all.

Chapter 01

The universe that was supposed to stand still

In 1917 Albert Einstein applied his new theory of gravity, general relativity, to the universe as a whole. The equations gave him a problem. They said a universe full of matter could not stay still. Gravity would pull it together, or it would have to be flying apart. Einstein, like almost everyone at the time, believed the universe was static. So he added an extra term to his equations, the cosmological constant, to hold everything in balance.

Others took the equations more seriously than he did. In 1922 the Russian mathematician Alexander Friedmann showed that relativity naturally allows universes that expand or contract. In 1927 the Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître went further. He combined the equations with early measurements of galaxies and argued that our universe is in fact expanding. A few years later he proposed that it began as a single "primeval atom". The Big Bang began as a prediction of general relativity, before the observations forced the issue.

Chapter 02

What the theory says, and what it does not

The name came from an opponent. In a BBC radio talk in 1949 the British astronomer Fred Hoyle, who preferred a universe without a beginning, called the rival idea "this big bang". The label stuck, and it misleads in two ways.

First, it was not an explosion in space, with debris flying out from a centre into empty surroundings. It was an expansion of space, happening everywhere at once. Every distant galaxy sees every other galaxy moving away, and none of them is the middle. Second, it was not loud. There was no air to carry sound.

The theory makes three claims. The universe was once much hotter and denser than today. It has been expanding and cooling ever since. And that history can be traced, with known physics, from about a second after the start until now. It does not claim to know what happened at "time zero", and it does not say the universe was ever squeezed into a point sitting somewhere. The whole universe, possibly infinite, was simply denser.

Chapter 03

Three numbers worth carrying

Modern measurements, mainly from the European Space Agency's Planck satellite (2009 to 2013), give these figures. They are known to about one per cent or better.

How can we see 46 billion light years away in a universe 13.8 billion years old? Because the light did not cross a fixed distance. The space it travelled through expanded behind it. The objects that sent the oldest light are now much further away than they were when it set off.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Run the expansion backwards and the past gets hot

The logic is simple. If things are getting further apart, they used to be closer. If matter was squeezed closer, it was hotter. Physics we can test in laboratories then tells us what a hot, dense universe must have done.

Chapter 04

Measuring the runaway galaxies

The evidence needed two kinds of measurement: how far away galaxies are, and how fast they move.

The speed came first. From about 1912 the American astronomer Vesto Slipher measured the light of spiral "nebulae", which we now know are galaxies. Most of them showed a redshift: the dark lines in their spectrum were moved towards the red end. That shift means the source is moving away, in the same way a passing siren drops in pitch.

The distance came from a woman who was paid to catalogue photographs. At Harvard, Henrietta Swan Leavitt studied stars called Cepheid variables, which brighten and fade in a regular rhythm. In 1912 she published the key finding: the slower the rhythm, the brighter the star really is. Time the pulse, compare true brightness with apparent brightness, and you have the distance.

In 1924 Edwin Hubble used Cepheids to show that Andromeda lies far outside our galaxy. By 1929 he had combined distances with Slipher's speeds. The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. Lemaître had found the same relation two years earlier in a little-read Belgian journal, which is why the International Astronomical Union now recommends calling it the Hubble–Lemaître law.

A useful early error

Hubble's first expansion rate was about seven times too high, which made the universe younger than the Earth. The fault was in the distance scale, corrected in the 1950s when astronomers found there were two kinds of Cepheid. A correct idea can arrive with the wrong numbers.

Chapter 05

The first few minutes

Run the film backwards and the universe gets denser and hotter. Below a certain age, it was hot enough to behave like the inside of a particle accelerator, and physicists can calculate what happened with confidence. Here is the outline.

The few minutes of fusion are called Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The window was short because the universe was cooling and thinning fast. The calculation predicts that ordinary matter should come out as roughly three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by mass, with tiny traces of other light elements. That is almost exactly what astronomers find in the oldest stars and the most untouched gas clouds.

Chapter 06

The moment the fog cleared

For its first 380,000 years the universe was opaque. It was so hot that electrons could not stay attached to nuclei. Free electrons scatter light, so light bounced from particle to particle like car headlights in thick fog.

When the temperature fell to about 3,000 degrees, electrons settled into atoms. This is called recombination, although it was really the first combination. Suddenly the fog lifted, and the light that had been trapped flew off in all directions. It has been travelling ever since. Expansion has stretched its wavelength about a thousand times, from visible and infrared light to microwaves. That is the cosmic microwave background, or CMB.

In 1948 Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, working with George Gamow, predicted that such a glow should exist, at a few degrees above absolute zero. The prediction was largely forgotten. In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs in New Jersey found a hiss in their radio antenna that would not go away, whatever direction they pointed it. Nearby at Princeton, Robert Dicke's group was building an instrument to search for exactly this signal. When the two teams spoke, Dicke is said to have told his colleagues they had been scooped. Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Chapter 07

The baby picture, read in detail

Finding the glow was strong evidence. Measuring it carefully turned cosmology into a precise science. Three satellites did most of the work.

COBE (launched 1989)

NASA's satellite showed the CMB has the exact spectrum of an object at one temperature, and found tiny differences across the sky. John Mather and George Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize.

WMAP (launched 2001)

NASA's follow-up mapped those differences in far more detail.

Planck (launched 2009)

ESA's mission made the sharpest full-sky map, the source of today's standard figures.

The differences are small: about one part in 100,000. Imagine a calm lake where the highest ripple is the thickness of a sheet of paper over a kilometre. Those ripples are the seeds of everything. Slightly denser regions pulled in more matter, and over hundreds of millions of years they became the first galaxies. The story of how is told in Cosmic evolution.

The ripples also carry a fingerprint. Before the fog cleared, matter and light formed a hot fluid in which sound waves travelled. When light broke free, those waves froze in place. Their pattern of sizes depends on how much ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy the universe contains. Fitting it gives the percentages in Chapter 03. A six-number model fits the whole map remarkably well, which is one reason cosmologists trust it.

Chapter 08

Inflation: the first fraction of a second

The basic theory had two puzzles. The first is the horizon problem. The CMB has almost the same temperature in opposite directions of the sky, yet in the plain Big Bang picture those regions never had time to exchange heat. Why do they match?

The second is the flatness problem. Space on large scales turns out to be very nearly "flat", meaning parallel lines stay parallel. In the equations, flatness is unstable, like a pencil balanced on its tip. Any small early tilt should have grown enormous by now.

In 1980 the American physicist Alan Guth proposed an answer, refined soon after by Andrei Linde and others. In a tiny fraction of the first second, the universe went through a burst of extremely fast, accelerating expansion called inflation. A region small enough to have evened out its temperature was stretched to a size larger than everything we can see. Any curvature was stretched flat, as the Earth looks flat to someone standing on it. Microscopic quantum jitters were stretched too, and became the seeds of the ripples in the CMB.

Inflation predicted the statistical pattern of those ripples, and later measurements matched it. But inflation is not confirmed in the way the CMB is confirmed. Nobody knows what drove it, and there are many versions. Most cosmologists accept some form of it as the best explanation. A respected minority, including Paul Steinhardt, one of its early developers, argue that it is too flexible to be properly tested.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

It settled a real argument, and it tells you where you came from

The Big Bang is not just a fact for quizzes. It won a long scientific contest on evidence, it explains the raw material of your body, and it touches questions people have asked for as long as there have been people.

Chapter 09

How it beat the steady state

In 1948 Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold proposed the steady state theory. It accepted that the universe expands, but said new matter appears slowly to fill the gaps, so the universe looks the same at every time. It made a clear prediction: the distant, early universe should look like the nearby one.

That prediction failed. In the 1950s and 1960s radio astronomers found that powerful radio sources, and later quasars, the brilliant cores of young galaxies, were far more common at great distances. The universe used to be different. Then came the CMB, which the steady state could not explain in any natural way. By the late 1960s most astronomers had switched. Hoyle never fully did.

This is the scientific method working as advertised. Two theories made different predictions, and observations chose between them. Hoyle's opposition was useful too. His work on how stars make heavier elements became a pillar of modern astronomy.

Chapter 10

The oldest thing you own

Every hydrogen atom in your body was made in the Big Bang. The hydrogen in the water you drank this morning is about 13.8 billion years old. The heavier elements, the carbon, oxygen, calcium and iron, were made much later inside stars and in stellar explosions. You are built from two batches of material: one from the first minutes of the universe, and one cooked in stars.

The theory also explains why the universe has the history it has. Stars, galaxies and planets did not always exist. They had to form, and that took time. That idea, a universe with a history rather than an eternal stage, underlies almost all modern astronomy, from galaxies to planet formation.

Chapter 11

A beginning, and what people make of it

A universe with a beginning sounds, to many people, like creation. That reaction is old. In 1951 Pope Pius XII said modern cosmology supported the idea of a creator. Lemaître, himself a Catholic priest, was reportedly uneasy. He preferred to keep science and theology separate, and seems to have asked that the Pope not repeat the claim.

Stated fairly, the main positions are these. Some religious thinkers see the beginning as consistent with, or pointing to, a creator. Many scientists, religious or not, follow Lemaître: the theory describes how the universe developed and is silent on why it exists. Others argue that physics may one day explain the beginning without anything outside it. The evidence settles none of these. It settles the history from the first second onward, which scientists of every belief accept.

Careful with "before"

"What happened before the Big Bang?" may or may not be a sensible question. If time itself began then, there was no "before", in the same way there is nothing north of the North Pole. If time did not begin then, there was a before and we do not know what it was. Physics has not decided.

Strengths and weaknesses of the best story we have

For a scientific theory, "advantages and disadvantages" means what it explains well against what it leaves open or gets wrong.

Chapter 13

The live tensions

The Hubble tension. There are two main ways to measure how fast the universe is expanding today. One reads it from the CMB, using the standard model to project forward, and gives about 67 kilometres per second for every megaparsec of distance (a megaparsec is about 3.3 million light years). The other measures nearby galaxies directly with Cepheids and supernovae, and teams led by Adam Riess get about 73. Some other direct methods land in between. The cause may be a hidden measurement error or a gap in the standard model. As of 2026 this is genuinely contested.

The early galaxies. Since 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope has found bright, mature-looking galaxies earlier than many models expected. Headlines claimed this "broke" the Big Bang. It did not. Webb challenges our models of how fast galaxies formed afterwards, not the expansion or the first minutes.

Dark energy may be changing. Results from the DESI galaxy survey in 2024 and 2025 hinted that dark energy may have weakened over time instead of staying constant. The hints are not yet strong enough to count as a discovery. If they hold up, the simplest form of the standard model would need revising. The details are in Dark energy.

The big picture is stable. The hot, expanding history is about as secure as anything in science, and the open questions sit at its edges: the first instant, the dark ingredients, and one stubborn number. That is where the next generation of discoveries is likely to come from.

The core idea

The universe was once hot and dense and has been expanding and cooling for about 13.8 billion years. Three pieces of evidence support it: galaxies receding in proportion to distance, the cosmic microwave background, and the measured hydrogen-to-helium ratio. It describes the history after the start, not the start itself.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Age: about 13.8 billion years.
  • The CMB was released about 380,000 years after the start and is now about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.
  • Ordinary matter came out roughly 75% hydrogen, 25% helium by mass.
  • Expansion rate: about 67 or 73 km/s per megaparsec, depending on the method.
The common mistake

Picturing an explosion from a central point into empty space. There is no centre and no outside. Space itself is stretching, everywhere at once.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the oldest light we see comes from objects now 46 billion light years away, in a universe only 13.8 billion years old? If you can, you have understood what "expansion of space" means.
