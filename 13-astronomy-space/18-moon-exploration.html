Moon exploration. Twelve people walked there, then nobody went back for over fifty years.

Between 1969 and 1972, twelve American astronauts walked on the Moon. Then the programme stopped, and for more than half a century no human went further than a few hundred kilometres above the Earth. Now the Moon is busy again: Chinese robots on the far side, an Indian lander near the south pole, private companies attempting landings, and two rival plans to put people back. This file explains how we got there the first time, what we learned, why we stopped, and why the Moon matters again.

Question 1 · What is it?

Visiting the only other world humans have stood on

Moon exploration covers robotic probes, the crewed Apollo landings, and today's new wave of missions. It is the study of a body close enough to reach in three days.

The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, because its spin has been slowed until one turn takes exactly as long as one orbit. This is called tidal locking. The far side is not dark. It gets as much sunlight as the near side, just never faces us. That has a practical consequence: a lander on the far side cannot talk to Earth directly and needs a relay satellite.

Chapter 02

Where the Moon came from

Before Apollo there were three main ideas. The Moon split off a fast-spinning Earth. It was captured as it passed by. Or it formed alongside Earth from the same cloud. The rocks brought back fitted none of them well.

The leading explanation today is the giant impact hypothesis. About 4.5 billion years ago, a body roughly the size of Mars, often called Theia, struck the young Earth a glancing blow. Debris from both was thrown into orbit and gathered into the Moon. That explains why the Moon has a small iron core and why its rocks were once melted. It leaves a puzzle: Moon rocks are chemically almost identical to Earth's, more than a simple collision would suggest. Variations of the idea try to explain this, and the details are still debated, but some form of giant impact is the consensus.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Robots first, then the largest rocket ever flown with people

The first lunar race was a decade of stepping stones: hitting the Moon, photographing it, landing softly, then sending people. The method that won was a clever way of saving weight.

Chapter 03

The robotic race came first

The Soviet Union led almost every early step. Luna 2 hit the Moon in 1959, the first human-made object to reach another world. Luna 3, the same year, photographed the far side for the first time. Luna 9 made the first soft landing in February 1966 and sent pictures from the surface, settling a real fear: some scientists had worried the ground might be deep dust that would swallow a lander.

The United States answered with Ranger probes that photographed the surface as they crashed, Surveyor landers that tested the soil, and Lunar Orbiters that mapped possible landing sites. Later, the Soviet Union ran the first robotic rover, Lunokhod 1, in 1970, and brought back small samples with robotic Luna missions. Every crewed landing rested on years of robotic scouting, a pattern repeated today.

Chapter 04

How Apollo got there

The Saturn V rocket, still among the most powerful ever flown, was about 110 metres tall. Almost all of it was fuel for one purpose: sending a small spacecraft fast enough towards the Moon. The key was the plan. Instead of landing one huge ship and flying it home, NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous, an idea championed inside NASA by the engineer John Houbolt against early resistance.

The lunar module only had to work in lunar gravity with no air, so it could be light and fragile; its walls in places were very thin. Because only small pieces had to land and come back, the whole mission needed far less fuel, and one Saturn V could do it. On 20 July 1969 Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed Apollo 11's lunar module, while Michael Collins waited in orbit.

Chapter 05

Six landings and one rescue

There were six successful landings, from Apollo 11 in 1969 to Apollo 17 in December 1972. Apollo 13 in 1970 never landed: an oxygen tank exploded on the way, and the crew survived by using their lunar module as a lifeboat. The later missions carried a small electric car, the Lunar Roving Vehicle, and stayed up to three days. On Apollo 17 Harrison Schmitt became the only professional geologist to walk on the Moon.

Together the missions brought back about 382 kilograms of rock and soil. The astronauts also left instruments, including seismometers that recorded moonquakes and mirrors called retroreflectors. Observatories on Earth still fire lasers at those mirrors and time the reflection. That is how we know the Moon is moving away from Earth by about 3.8 centimetres a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow.

Chapter 06

Why it stopped, and the ice that brought us back

Apollo ended because it had achieved its political goal. The United States had beaten the Soviet Union, public interest fell, and the war in Vietnam and other priorities took the money. Three planned missions were cancelled. At their peak the Apollo years cost a huge share of the federal budget, as explained in Space exploration.

Interest returned through a discovery. Craters near the Moon's poles are deep enough that sunlight never reaches their floors. These permanently shadowed regions are among the coldest known places in the solar system, cold enough to trap water ice for billions of years. Missions in the 1990s found hints. In 2009 NASA's LCROSS crashed a rocket stage into one such crater and detected water in the plume, and India's Chandrayaan-1 had already mapped signs of water on the surface. How much ice there is, and in what form, is still uncertain.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A view of ourselves, a record of the past, and a new race

The Moon changed how people saw the Earth, it preserves the early history of the solar system, and it has become a test of national power again.

Chapter 07

The photograph that looked back

On Christmas Eve 1968 Apollo 8 became the first crewed spacecraft to orbit the Moon. As it came around the far side, astronaut Bill Anders photographed the Earth rising over the lunar horizon. The image, Earthrise, showed a small blue and white planet against black space. It is often credited with helping to build the environmental movement of the following years.

Many astronauts have described a similar change in outlook after seeing Earth from far away, sometimes called the "overview effect". The irony is widely noted. The trip meant to show off a superpower's reach is best remembered for making the whole planet look fragile.

Chapter 08

A museum of the early solar system

Earth recycles its surface. Plate tectonics, weather and life erase almost all traces of its first few hundred million years. The Moon has no plates, no weather and no life, so its surface has kept an almost untouched record of what was hitting the inner solar system billions of years ago.

Apollo samples made this record readable. Dating the rocks from known landing sites gave ages for areas with different numbers of craters. That turned crater counts into a clock, which scientists now use to estimate the ages of surfaces on Mars, Mercury and other bodies they have never sampled. The rocks also showed that the young Moon was covered by an ocean of molten rock, an idea that reshaped thinking about how rocky planets form (see Planet formation).

China has added to this record. Its Chang'e 4 made the first landing on the far side in 2019. Chang'e 5 returned fresh samples in 2020, the first since 1976, and Chang'e 6 brought back the first samples from the far side in 2024.

Chapter 09

The race for the south pole

The new destination is the region around the lunar south pole, because that is where the shadowed, icy craters are, next to high ridges that get sunlight for most of the time. Ice could provide drinking water, oxygen to breathe, and hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. Water on the Moon would be worth more than gold, because lifting it from Earth is so expensive.

Several nations want to be there. In August 2023 India's Chandrayaan-3 landed closer to the south pole than any earlier mission. The United States' Artemis programme aims to land astronauts in the region. China plans crewed landings before 2030 and, with Russia and other partners, an International Lunar Research Station in the 2030s. A US-led group of countries has signed the Artemis Accords on how to behave on the Moon, while China and Russia stand outside them. The legal background is in Space exploration.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A nearby destination with real hazards

The Moon's closeness is its great advantage. Its dust, its long nights and its cost are the main drawbacks.

Chapter 10

CostReusable rockets and commercial landers are cutting costs.NASA's SLS rocket costs several billion dollars per launch, according to its inspector general.

Landing is still hard, even for experienced teams. Several recent robotic landers crashed or tipped over, including private ones from Israel, Japan and the United States. In January 2024 Japan's SLIM landed within about 100 metres of its target, then toppled onto its nose but still worked. In February 2024 Intuitive Machines made the first landing by a private company, although its lander also tipped over, and in March 2025 Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost landed upright and completed its mission.

Chapter 11

How we know they really went

A persistent minority believes the Apollo landings were staged. The evidence that they happened is overwhelming and comes from many independent sources. The Soviet Union, which had every reason to expose a fake, tracked the missions and never disputed them. Hundreds of kilograms of samples have been studied by scientists around the world, and they differ from Earth rocks and meteorites in ways that could not be faked in 1969. The laser mirrors are still used. And since 2009 NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the landing sites, showing the descent stages, equipment and even the astronauts' tracks.

The useful lesson is about evidence. A claim that would require thousands of people and a rival superpower to keep a secret for decades is a weak claim. Volume 47 covers how to spot manipulative arguments more generally (The shape they all share).

Chapter 13

What a return would really be for

It is worth asking what the second wave is actually for, because the answer is not the same as in 1969. Four aims come up most often. Practice: the Moon is a place to test life support, landers, suits and in-situ resource use three days from home before trying Mars. Science: the polar ice may hold a record of water delivered to the inner solar system, and the far side is a quiet place for radio astronomy. Resources: making fuel from ice could turn the Moon into a refuelling stop. Presence: whoever is there first will help shape the rules.

Each aim can be challenged. Robots could do much of the science. The economic case for lunar fuel depends on a large traffic of spacecraft that does not yet exist. But the strategic aim is real, and it is the main reason the programmes keep their funding. The first Moon race was about getting there; the second is about staying. That is a harder problem, and a slower one.

The core idea

Robots scouted the Moon, then Apollo landed twelve people between 1969 and 1972 using lunar orbit rendezvous. Their samples revealed the Moon's violent origin and gave us a crater clock. The discovery of polar ice started a new race, this time to stay.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 384,000 km away, about three days by Apollo.
  • Six landings, twelve moonwalkers, about 382 kg of samples.
  • The Moon recedes about 3.8 cm a year.
  • Gravity about one-sixth of Earth's.
The common mistake

Calling the far side the "dark side". It gets as much sunlight as the near side. It is only hidden from Earth.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the south pole, not the equator where Apollo landed, is the target now? If you can, you have linked shadowed craters, ice, sunlit ridges and rocket fuel.

The words, in plain words
Tidal locking
When a moon's spin matches its orbit, so it always shows the same face.
Far side
The half of the Moon never seen from Earth. Not dark.
Giant impact hypothesis
The idea that the Moon formed from debris after a large body hit the young Earth.
Soft landing
Touching down gently enough for a spacecraft to keep working.
Saturn V
The giant rocket that launched the Apollo crews.
Lunar orbit rendezvous
Apollo's method: a small lander descends and later re-docks with a ship left in orbit.
Lunar module
The light two-person craft that landed on and left the Moon.
Retroreflector
A mirror that bounces laser light straight back to where it came from.
Permanently shadowed region
A polar crater floor that never sees sunlight and can trap ice.
Lunar regolith
The loose layer of dust and broken rock covering the Moon.
Artemis
NASA's programme to return people to the Moon.
Earthrise
The 1968 Apollo 8 photograph of Earth rising over the Moon.
