Space exploration. The hard part is not going up, it is going sideways fast enough.

Space is only about 100 kilometres away, roughly the distance from Paris to the edge of Normandy. A car could drive there in an hour if it could drive straight up. Yet reaching space and staying there is one of the hardest things humans do. The reason is physics, and the physics explains almost everything else: why rockets are mostly fuel, why launches used to cost so much, and why reusable rockets changed the industry in about ten years.

Question 1 · What is it?

Going there, by person or by machine

Space exploration means sending people or machines beyond the atmosphere to study, use or visit what is there. Most of it, by far, is done by robots.

Chapter 01

A beep heard around the world

On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, a polished metal ball about the size of a beach ball with four long antennas. It did almost nothing except transmit a simple radio beep. Radio amateurs around the world could hear it. In the United States the effect was something close to panic. A rival that could put a ball into orbit could also, in principle, put a nuclear warhead on the other side of the planet.

The next twelve years moved astonishingly fast. Yuri Gagarin became the first person in space in April 1961. In July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon. Space exploration began as a side effect of the missile race, and that origin still shapes it: governments, militaries and national prestige paid for nearly everything until the 2010s.

Today the field has three parts. Near-Earth activity: satellites, space stations and the launches that serve them. Robotic exploration: probes, landers and rovers sent across the solar system. And human exploration beyond low orbit, which has happened only during the Apollo programme from 1968 to 1972, and is now being attempted again.

Chapter 02

Where space begins, and why height is not the problem

There is no sharp edge. The air simply thins out. By convention, the Kármán line at 100 kilometres is the usual boundary, and the US military and NASA use 80 kilometres. The International Space Station flies at roughly 400 kilometres.

Reaching 100 kilometres is not that hard. Suborbital tourist flights do it, and fall straight back down after a few minutes of weightlessness. Staying up is a completely different problem. To stay in orbit you must travel sideways at about 7.8 kilometres per second, which is roughly 28,000 kilometres per hour, or a trip from London to New York in about twelve minutes. That speed, not the height, is what makes orbit expensive.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Falling, missing, and paying for every gram

Four ideas explain spaceflight: what an orbit really is, why rockets are mostly fuel, how spacecraft travel between planets, and how reusability changed the cost.

Chapter 03

An orbit is falling and missing

Isaac Newton described a thought experiment. Put a cannon on top of a very high mountain and fire it horizontally. The ball curves down to the ground. Fire it faster and it lands further away. Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it as quickly as it falls. The ball keeps falling forever and never lands. That is an orbit: continuous falling, combined with enough sideways speed to keep missing the Earth.

This explains why astronauts float. Gravity at the space station's height is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts are weightless because they and their station are falling together, like passengers in a lift whose cable has snapped, except that the fall never ends.

Higher orbits are slower. At about 35,800 kilometres up, one orbit takes exactly a day, so a satellite there seems to hang over one spot. That is the geostationary orbit used by many television and weather satellites. To leave Earth altogether you need about 11.2 kilometres per second, the escape velocity.

Chapter 04

The tyranny of the rocket equation

A rocket works by throwing mass backwards very fast. The exhaust goes one way and the rocket goes the other, as Newton's third law says (see Newton's laws). It does not push against air. It works better in a vacuum.

The catch is that a rocket must carry its own fuel, and that fuel must also be lifted. In 1903 the Russian schoolteacher Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out the consequence, now called the rocket equation. The extra speed you can gain grows only with the logarithm of how much of your rocket is fuel. In plain words, every increase in speed costs a multiple of fuel, not an addition. The result is that a rocket reaching orbit is typically around 85 to 95 per cent propellant at lift-off, and the useful payload is often only a few per cent.

Engineers cheat a little with staging. A rocket is built as several rockets stacked together. When the bottom stage has burned its fuel, it drops away, so the rest no longer carries its empty weight. Engineers measure every mission in delta-v, the total change of speed it needs. Delta-v is spaceflight's real currency, much more than distance.

Chapter 05

Travelling between planets

Spacecraft do not fly in straight lines. They coast along curved paths around the Sun, firing their engines only briefly. The cheapest path between two planets, worked out by Walter Hohmann in 1925, is an ellipse that touches both orbits. It only works when the planets are in the right positions. That is why missions to Mars leave in launch windows that open about every 26 months. Miss one and you wait two years.

The second trick is the gravity assist. A spacecraft that flies close behind a moving planet can steal a little of the planet's orbital energy and leave faster than it arrived. The planet slows by an amount too tiny to measure. In the late 1970s a rare alignment of the outer planets let NASA's two Voyager probes swing from Jupiter to Saturn, and Voyager 2 on to Uranus and Neptune. Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and Voyager 2 in 2018. Both were still sending data in 2025, with instruments gradually shut down to save power.

Chapter 06

Why people are the hardest payload

A probe needs electricity, temperature control and a radio. A person needs air, water, food, pressure, waste handling and protection, every minute, with no gap. Above the protection of Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field, crews also face radiation from the Sun and from deep space, which raises long-term cancer risk. Long periods without gravity weaken bones and muscles and can affect eyesight. The details are in Space stations.

Crewed flight has also cost lives. Three Apollo 1 astronauts died in a launch-pad fire in 1967. Three Soviet cosmonauts died when Soyuz 11 lost its air on the way home in 1971. The Space Shuttles Challenger (1986) and Columbia (2003) were lost with seven crew each. Each disaster traced back to known warning signs that organisations had learned to live with, which is why safety culture is now treated as part of the engineering.

Chapter 07

Landing the rocket changed the economics

For sixty years rockets were thrown away after one flight. It was like flying an airliner from Paris to New York and scrapping it on arrival. The Space Shuttle was meant to fix this, but its refurbishment between flights was so slow and costly that it ended up expensive per flight.

In December 2015 SpaceX landed the first stage of a Falcon 9 rocket upright after an orbital launch. Reflown boosters became routine within a few years, some flying more than twenty times. Combined with mass production and a fast pace of launches, this cut the price of reaching orbit dramatically. Exact figures depend on how you count, but the cost per kilogram to low orbit fell to a small fraction of the Shuttle era's. By the mid-2020s SpaceX was launching most of the world's payload mass to orbit.

Cheap launch made new things possible: constellations of thousands of satellites, private crewed flights, and much larger fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX's Starship, which was still in test flights in 2025. Other companies, including Blue Origin and Rocket Lab, and Chinese firms, are developing reusable boosters too.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Prestige, knowledge, and the satellites you never think about

Space exploration matters for three different reasons: geopolitics, science, and an economy on Earth that now quietly depends on orbit.

Chapter 08

The space race was about Earth

Apollo was not mainly a science programme. President Kennedy committed the United States to the Moon in 1961, weeks after Gagarin's flight and a failed US-backed invasion of Cuba. The aim was to win a visible contest in the Cold War. At its peak in the mid-1960s, NASA took more than 4 per cent of the US federal budget. Today it is less than half of one per cent.

Prestige still drives a lot. China flew its first astronaut, Yang Liwei, in 2003, built its own space station and landed robots on the far side of the Moon. In 2023 India's Chandrayaan-3 became the first spacecraft to land near the Moon's south pole. Japan's SLIM made a precision landing in 2024. Space is one of the few arenas where a country's technical ability is visible to the whole world in real time.

Chapter 09

The orbit economy you already use

Most people never think about space, yet they use it constantly. Satellite navigation runs on precise clocks in orbit, and banks and phone networks use the same signals to keep time. Weather forecasts depend on satellite data. Satellites relay television, internet to remote regions and ships, and pictures used to track crops, floods, wildfires and deforestation.

By 2025 there were more than ten thousand active satellites, most of them belonging to SpaceX's Starlink internet constellation. A modern economy that lost its satellites for a week would discover how much it depends on them: navigation, timing, forecasting and some payments would degrade at once. That is why militaries now treat space as a domain of conflict, like land, sea and air.

Chapter 12

The junk problem

Every launch leaves things in orbit: dead satellites, spent rocket stages, and fragments from explosions. At orbital speed even a fleck of paint hits like a bullet, and space station windows have been chipped by tiny particles. Space agencies track tens of thousands of objects larger than about 10 centimetres, and there are many more smaller pieces.

In 1978 NASA scientist Donald Kessler warned of a chain reaction: once orbits are crowded enough, collisions create fragments that cause more collisions. This is called the Kessler syndrome. Two events made it real. In 2007 China destroyed one of its own weather satellites with a missile, and in 2021 Russia did the same, each creating a large cloud of trackable fragments. In 2009 a dead Russian satellite and a working American one collided by accident.

The response so far is rules and habits rather than a fix. Operators are increasingly expected to bring satellites down within a few years of retirement, and low orbits clean themselves slowly because thin air drags objects down. Orbit is a shared resource with no owner, the classic setting for overuse, and international agreement on limits is still thin.

Chapter 14

Whose space is it?

The basic law is the Outer Space Treaty of 1967. It says space is free for all countries to explore, that no nation can claim the Moon or any other body, that nuclear weapons may not be placed in orbit, and that states are responsible for the activities of their companies. It was written when only two governments could reach space.

It leaves big questions open. Can a company own water it mines on the Moon? The United States passed a law in 2015 saying American companies may own resources they extract, and Luxembourg and others followed. Critics argue this is a claim of ownership through the back door. The US-led Artemis Accords, signed by more than fifty countries by 2025, set out principles for lunar activity. China and Russia have not joined and are planning their own lunar base. The direction is a slow drift from one shared rulebook towards rival blocs, and how that plays out may matter more than any single rocket.

The core idea

An orbit is falling while moving sideways fast enough to miss. That speed, about 7.8 km/s, forces rockets to be mostly fuel, which made space expensive. Reusable rockets broke that cost barrier in the 2010s, opening a commercial era alongside the old government programmes.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Space begins at about 100 km by convention.
  • Orbital speed: about 7.8 km/s. Escape speed: about 11.2 km/s.
  • A rocket is roughly 85–95% propellant at lift-off.
  • More than 10,000 active satellites by 2025.
The common mistake

Thinking astronauts float because there is no gravity in space. Gravity is nearly as strong up there. They float because they are falling around the Earth.
