Sharp Mind · Astronomy & Space · Topic 16 of 20

Space telescopes. The air that keeps you alive is a dirty window on the universe.

Stars twinkle. Poets like that. Astronomers do not, because twinkling is the atmosphere smearing their images. The air also blocks most kinds of light completely, including X-rays, most ultraviolet and much of the infrared. The fix is to lift the telescope above the air. It is expensive and risky, and once it is up there you usually cannot repair it. But a handful of space telescopes have changed what humanity knows about the universe more than almost any other instruments ever built.

Question 1 · What is it?

A telescope placed above the atmosphere

A space telescope is an observatory in orbit, or further away, that collects light the ground cannot see clearly, or cannot see at all.

Chapter 01

The problem with looking through air

Look at a coin at the bottom of a swimming pool while someone splashes the surface. The coin wobbles and blurs. The atmosphere does the same to starlight. Pockets of warm and cool air bend light by slightly different amounts, and they move constantly. Astronomers call the effect seeing. Even at the best mountain sites, it blurs detail that a large telescope could otherwise resolve.

In 1946 the American astrophysicist Lyman Spitzer wrote a paper arguing that a telescope in space would escape this blur and, just as important, see kinds of light the air absorbs. It took 44 years for his idea to fly as the Hubble Space Telescope. The point of a space telescope is not to get closer to the stars. A few hundred kilometres makes no difference to a distance of trillions. The point is to get out from under the air.

Chapter 02

Colours your eyes cannot see

Visible light is a narrow slice of the electromagnetic spectrum, the family of light-like waves that runs from long radio waves to very short gamma rays. Different objects shine in different parts of it, and most of the spectrum never reaches the ground.

The table explains a pattern in where telescopes are built. Radio astronomy lives on the ground; X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy can only exist in space. Visible and infrared are in between, which is why the big arguments about cost are about those.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A big mirror, a sensitive detector, and a very steady hand

Every telescope does two jobs: collect as much light as possible and focus it sharply. In space, both must be done by a machine that nobody can reach.

Chapter 03

What makes a telescope good

The most important number is the width of the main mirror, the aperture. A wider mirror is like a wider bucket in the rain: it collects more light, so it can see fainter objects. It also sees finer detail, because sharpness improves with size. Hubble's mirror is 2.4 metres across. Webb's is 6.5 metres, with about six times the collecting area.

Modern telescopes use mirrors rather than lenses, because a big lens sags under its own weight and splits colours. The focused light falls on electronic detectors, relatives of the sensor in a phone camera but far more sensitive. A space telescope can stare at the same patch of sky for hours or days, adding up faint light, because there is no night, no weather, and no atmosphere moving the image. That requires pointing stability. Hubble can hold its aim steadily enough to keep a beam of light on a coin hundreds of kilometres away.

Chapter 04

Hubble: the telescope that launched blurry

Hubble was carried into a low orbit, today a little over 500 kilometres up, by the Space Shuttle in April 1990. The first images were disappointing. Its main mirror had been ground to the wrong shape, by roughly one-fiftieth of the thickness of a human hair at the edge. A testing instrument at the factory had been assembled slightly wrong, and the error was not caught. The result was spherical aberration: light from the edge and the centre of the mirror came to focus in different places, giving fuzzy halos around stars.

Because the error was precisely known, it could be corrected, the way glasses correct an eye. In December 1993 a Shuttle crew installed corrective optics in a series of spacewalks. The first sharp images were a turning point for the whole space programme. Four more servicing missions followed, the last in 2009, replacing instruments, gyroscopes and batteries.

Hubble's long life came from one design decision: it was built to be visited and repaired. No crewed spacecraft can reach it since the Shuttle retired in 2011. Its gyroscopes, which sense how it is turning, have been failing one by one, and since 2024 it has worked on a single gyroscope, which limits how quickly it can switch targets.

Chapter 05

Webb: a giant mirror folded like origami

The James Webb Space Telescope, built by NASA with the European and Canadian space agencies, launched on an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana on 25 December 2021. Its 6.5-metre mirror was too big to fit in any rocket, so it is made of 18 hexagonal segments of beryllium, coated with a thin layer of gold, which reflects infrared light well. The mirror folded up for launch and opened in space.

Webb sees mainly in infrared, for two reasons. The light of the most distant galaxies has been stretched by the expansion of the universe from visible into infrared. And infrared light passes through dust that hides newborn stars. But an infrared telescope must be extremely cold, because anything warm glows in infrared and would drown the faint signal. Webb carries a sunshield about the size of a tennis court, made of five layers of thin film. The Sun side is hot. The telescope side sits below minus 230 degrees Celsius.

Unfolding it took about two weeks, with hundreds of mechanisms that each had to work the first time. Engineers had counted over three hundred single points of failure. Everything worked. The launch was so accurate that the telescope saved fuel, and NASA estimated it carries enough for well over the planned ten years of operation.

Chapter 06

Where to park a telescope

Orbit choice is a trade-off. Low Earth orbit, like Hubble's, is easy to reach and was reachable by astronauts, but Earth blocks half the sky at any moment, and the planet's warmth and reflected light get in the way. Webb instead sits near a point called L2, about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth on the side away from the Sun. There, the Sun, Earth and Moon all stay in roughly the same direction, so one sunshield can block all three, and the telescope stays cold.

The price of L2 is distance. It is about four times further than the Moon, and no repair mission can reach it. Webb had to work perfectly with no second chance. Several other missions, including ESA's Gaia and Euclid, also used L2 for the same thermal and viewing advantages.

Chapter 07

Not just pretty pictures

The famous images are a small part of the work. Much of the science comes from spectroscopy: spreading light into its colours, like a prism, and reading the dark and bright lines that reveal what an object is made of, how hot it is and how fast it is moving.

The colours in published images are also chosen. Webb sees infrared, which has no colour to the eye. Image processors assign visible colours to different infrared wavelengths, usually keeping the order: shorter waves bluer, longer waves redder. That is called representative or "false" colour. It is not fake. It is a translation, like a map using colours for altitude.

Some missions do not make images for the public at all. ESA's Gaia, which observed from 2013 to early 2025, measured the positions, distances and motions of nearly two billion stars, building the most detailed map of our galaxy ever made. NASA's Kepler (2009 to 2018) watched one patch of sky for tiny dips in starlight and found thousands of planets around other stars (see Exoplanets).

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

They answered old questions and showed us new ones

Space telescopes measured the age of the universe, found that its expansion is speeding up, showed that planets are common, and began reading the air of other worlds.

Chapter 08

The most important picture of nothing

In December 1995 the director of the institute that runs Hubble, Robert Williams, used his own allocation of observing time on a gamble. He pointed the telescope at a tiny, apparently empty patch of sky near the Big Dipper, about the size of a tennis ball seen from a hundred metres away, and kept it there for about ten days. Some colleagues worried it would waste precious time on nothing.

The result, the Hubble Deep Field, showed around three thousand galaxies, some seen as they were billions of years ago. If one blank speck of sky holds that many, the whole sky holds many billions. Later deep fields by Hubble and Webb went further. The image changed the public sense of scale more than any number could, and it showed astronomers how galaxies looked when the universe was young, which became the study of cosmic evolution.

Chapter 10

Images as shared culture

Hubble's 1995 image of the "Pillars of Creation", towers of gas and dust where stars are forming in the Eagle Nebula, became one of the best-known photographs of the century. Webb's first images in July 2022 were shown at the White House and appeared on front pages worldwide. Images from NASA missions are generally free for anyone to use, which helped them spread.

This matters beyond beauty. Telescopes are paid for by taxpayers, often over decades, and public support keeps them alive through budget fights. The pictures are part of how the science survives politically. They also give many people their only direct sense of what the universe looks like beyond the Moon and planets.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Space or ground: a trade-off, not a contest

Ground telescopes have improved enormously, so the choice is about which job needs space, and whether it is worth the price.

Chapter 11

Space against ground

FactorIn spaceOn the ground
SharpnessNo atmospheric blur at all.Adaptive optics, mirrors that flex hundreds of times a second to cancel the blur, now give very sharp images in some bands.

Repair and upgradeUsually impossible.New instruments can be fitted for decades.

The two work best together. A space telescope finds something interesting, and a giant ground telescope follows it up with more light-gathering power. Space buys clarity and range of colours; the ground buys size and flexibility.

Chapter 12

The cost problem

Flagship space telescopes are among the most expensive science projects ever. When Webb was first planned in the late 1990s, it was expected to cost around a billion dollars and launch around 2007. It launched in 2021 at about ten times that cost. In 2011 a US congressional committee proposed cancelling it altogether, before it was restructured and saved.

The overruns were not simple waste. Many of Webb's technologies, such as the folding mirror and the giant sunshield, had never been built before, and early estimates were optimistic. But the overruns had real victims. Every dollar over budget on a flagship comes out of smaller missions, and US space science budgets faced heavy proposed cuts in 2025, putting several operating missions at risk. The lesson space agencies draw is to mature the hard technologies before committing to a price.

Two further ideas are worth knowing. Cheaper launches, especially if very large rockets become routine, could allow bigger and less expensive space telescopes, or even ones assembled in orbit by robots. And the far side of the Moon is shielded from Earth's radio noise, which makes it attractive for radio telescopes that could listen to the cosmic dark ages. China placed a small radio experiment on the far side with its Chang'e 4 mission in 2019. The trend is clear even if the dates are not: wider views, colder instruments, and more of the spectrum.

The core idea

Space telescopes escape the blur of the atmosphere and see the kinds of light it blocks. Hubble showed the value of a serviceable telescope. Webb showed how far a cold infrared telescope at L2 can see. The trade-off is cost and no repairs.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hubble: 2.4 m mirror, launched 1990, just over 500 km up.
  • Webb: 6.5 m mirror, launched 2021, about 1.5 million km away.
  • Webb cost about 10 billion dollars.
  • The Hubble Deep Field showed about 3,000 galaxies in a speck of sky.
The common mistake

Thinking space telescopes see better because they are closer to the stars. They see better because they are above the air. The distance gained is irrelevant.
