The possibility of extraterrestrial life. A question with one example and no answer yet.

Every living thing we know of shares one family tree on one planet. That is the whole data set. From it, people have argued with equal confidence that the universe must be full of life and that we are probably alone. Neither side has evidence. What has changed in the last thirty years is that the question has become testable. We now know where to look, what to look for, and roughly what instruments would find it. This file explains the science, the failed claims and the honest state of the search.

Question 1 · What is it?

A scientific question, not a belief

The study of life beyond Earth is called astrobiology. It asks how life began here, where else it could exist, and how we would recognise it.

Chapter 01

The problem of a sample of one

Imagine trying to estimate how common cats are in the world after seeing exactly one cat. You could not do it. You would not even know if cats were common in your own street. That is the position of astrobiology. All known life, from bacteria to whales, descends from a single origin on Earth, and everything we think about life elsewhere is an extrapolation from that one case.

Yet one fact gives a hint. Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and its surface was too hostile for life for a long time at the start. Fossil evidence, including layered mounds built by microbes, shows life by about 3.5 billion years ago, and some disputed evidence is older. Life appeared fairly soon after conditions allowed it. That suggests, but does not prove, that simple life may not be hard to start. Complex life, by contrast, took about three billion years more.

Chapter 02

What life needs, as far as we know

There is no perfect definition of life. A working definition often used by NASA calls it a self-sustaining chemical system capable of evolving by natural selection. For the search, scientists focus on three requirements, all drawn from life on Earth.

A liquid

Liquid water is the best candidate. It dissolves many chemicals, lets them move and meet, and stays liquid over a wide range of temperatures. Some scientists ask whether other liquids, such as the liquid methane on Saturn's moon Titan, could work.

Energy

Sunlight, or chemical energy from reactions between rock and water. Some life on Earth never sees the Sun and lives off chemistry alone.

The right elements

Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur. Carbon can build long, varied chains, which is why life uses it (see Volume 26).

These are guesses shaped by the one example we have. They might be too narrow. But they give a practical rule: look first where there is, or was, liquid water. That rule has turned out to point to more places than anyone expected.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four ways to search

Scientists look for life in three places: on worlds in our solar system, in the air of planets around other stars, and in signals from technology. They also study life on Earth to learn what to expect.

Chapter 03

Life at the edges

In 1977 scientists in the submarine Alvin explored hot springs on the floor of the Pacific Ocean, more than two kilometres down. They expected empty rock. They found giant tube worms, clams and crabs, living in total darkness. The whole community depends on microbes that get energy from chemicals in the hot water, not from sunlight. The process is called chemosynthesis.

Since then, life has been found almost everywhere on Earth that has liquid water: in boiling springs, in acid, in salt crusts, in rock kilometres underground, and in Antarctic lakes under ice. Organisms that thrive in such places are called extremophiles. Their lesson is simple. Life does not need a pleasant surface. It needs water, energy and chemistry, and it can find them in places that look dead. That changed which worlds count as worth searching.

Chapter 04

How did life start here?

Nobody knows how life began, and this is one of the biggest open questions in science. In 1952 Stanley Miller and Harold Urey ran an experiment in Chicago. They sealed water and simple gases in glass, passed electric sparks through them to imitate lightning, and within days found amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. It showed that some ingredients of life can form easily.

Going from ingredients to a living cell is far harder. A popular idea, the RNA world, suggests early life used RNA, a molecule that can both store information and speed up chemical reactions, before DNA and proteins took over. Possible settings include warm ponds that dry and refill, and hot springs on the sea floor. If scientists ever make life from non-living chemistry in the lab, or find a second origin elsewhere, the odds for life in the universe would become much clearer. Neither has happened.

Chapter 05

Where to look

Around every star there is a range of distances where a planet with the right atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface: not so close that it boils, not so far that it freezes. This is the habitable zone, sometimes called the Goldilocks zone. Being in it does not make a planet habitable. Venus sits near its inner edge and is hot enough to melt lead.

Planets themselves are common. By 2025 more than six thousand had been confirmed around other stars, and statistical studies from the Kepler mission suggest that a significant fraction of stars have a rocky planet in their habitable zone. The estimates vary widely, but they imply billions of such planets in our galaxy alone. The nearest known, Proxima b, orbits the closest star to the Sun, about 4.2 light years away. The details are in Exoplanets.

The habitable zone is not the only option. Some icy moons far from the Sun have liquid water oceans under their crusts, heated by tides from their giant planets. Chapter 08 returns to them.

Chapter 06

Reading the air of a distant planet

We cannot visit planets around other stars. We can sometimes read their atmospheres. When a planet passes in front of its star, a little starlight shines through the planet's air on the way to us. Different gases absorb different colours, leaving a chemical fingerprint. The James Webb Space Telescope can do this for some planets (see Space telescopes).

The goal is a biosignature: a gas or mix of gases that life would produce and that is hard to make otherwise. On Earth, oxygen and methane together are a strong one. They react with each other and disappear, so finding both in large amounts means something keeps making them. The difficulty is false positives: almost every single gas has some non-living way to form, through volcanoes, sunlight splitting water, or chemistry nobody has thought of.

A live example shows how hard this is. In 2023 and 2025 a team led by Nikku Madhusudhan reported signs in Webb data of a sulphur compound, dimethyl sulphide, in the air of a planet called K2-18b, about 120 light years away. On Earth that gas is made mainly by marine life. Other researchers re-analysed the data and found the signal weak or absent, and argued the planet may not even have an ocean. As of 2026 the claim is strongly contested and not accepted as evidence of life.

Chapter 07

Listening for a signal

The search for extraterrestrial intelligence, or SETI, looks not for life but for technology. In 1960 the astronomer Frank Drake pointed a radio telescope at two nearby Sun-like stars in the first modern search. In 1961 he wrote down a way to organise the question, now called the Drake equation. It multiplies a chain of factors to estimate how many civilisations in our galaxy might be sending signals.

FactorWhat we know now
Rate of star formationWell measured.

The table shows the equation's real value. It does not give an answer. It shows exactly which numbers we are ignorant of, and the first two, which were once complete guesses, have now been measured. SETI searches today, including the privately funded Breakthrough Listen programme launched in 2015, scan millions of radio channels and look for laser flashes. Nothing convincing has been found.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Either answer would change how we see ourselves

Finding life would tell us whether biology is a cosmic habit or a fluke. The most promising places to start are closer than most people think.

Chapter 08

The ocean moons

The biggest surprise of solar system exploration may be how much liquid water there is far from the Sun. Three moons stand out.

Europa (Jupiter)

Data from NASA's Galileo probe in the 1990s showed signs of a salty ocean under the ice, probably holding more water than all of Earth's oceans. NASA's Europa Clipper launched in 2024 and should arrive in 2030. Europe's JUICE mission, launched in 2023, will also study Jupiter's icy moons.

Enceladus (Saturn)

A small moon that sprays jets of water from cracks near its south pole. The Cassini probe flew through them and found salts, organic molecules and hydrogen, a possible food source for microbes. Later analysis of its data found phosphorus too.

Titan (Saturn)

The only moon with a thick atmosphere, and lakes of liquid methane on its surface, with a water ocean deep below. NASA's Dragonfly, a nuclear-powered drone, is planned to launch in 2028 and fly between sites.

None of these is proof of life. But Enceladus is offering free samples of its ocean to any spacecraft that flies through its jets, which makes it one of the cheapest places in the universe to look. Mars is the other near target, for signs of past life rather than present, as Mars exploration explains.

Chapter 09

What either answer would mean

Suppose a probe found microbes in Europa's ocean, and they turned out to use a different genetic code, or no DNA at all. That would be a second genesis: life starting twice in one solar system. It would strongly suggest that life starts wherever it can, and that the galaxy is full of it, at least in simple forms. If, instead, the microbes shared our DNA, the likely explanation would be that rocks carried life between worlds, an idea called panspermia, and the question would remain open.

Finding nothing anywhere, after long careful searches, would mean something too. It would make Earth more unusual than the long tradition since Copernicus has assumed, that we are not special. Both answers would be among the most important discoveries in history, which is why astrobiologists insist on standards of evidence that sometimes seem slow and fussy. The questions also reach beyond science: religious traditions, philosophers and ethicists have all discussed how contact or discovery would affect human self-understanding, and they do not agree.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Strong methods, weak evidence so far

Each search method has strengths and blind spots, and the history of the field is full of exciting claims that did not survive checking.

Chapter 11

The claims that did not survive

Every few years a headline announces possible signs of alien life. So far each claim has faded under examination. The pattern is worth knowing.

  • The "Wow!" signal (1977). A strong, narrow radio burst picked up by a telescope in Ohio. The astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it on the printout and wrote "Wow!". It was never seen again despite many searches, and its origin is still unknown.
  • The Martian meteorite (1996). Possible fossil microbes in rock ALH84001. Most researchers now think the features formed without life.
  • Phosphine on Venus (2020). A team reported a gas in Venus's clouds that on Earth is linked to life. Later analyses found much less or none, and the claim remains disputed.
  • 'Oumuamua (2017). The first object seen passing through the solar system from another star. Its odd shape and motion led one Harvard astronomer to suggest alien technology. Most astronomers consider a natural object far more likely.
  • UFO reports. In 2024 a US Defense Department office reviewing reports of unidentified objects said it had found no evidence of extraterrestrial technology.

The lesson is not that scientists are closed-minded. Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence, a principle Carl Sagan made famous, and in this field the "first detection" headline is usually the beginning of the argument, not the end. A credible discovery will probably build slowly, from several instruments and independent teams, rather than arrive in one announcement.

Chapter 13

Where is everybody?

The story goes that in 1950 the physicist Enrico Fermi, at lunch with colleagues at Los Alamos, asked a version of the question "where is everybody?". The galaxy is over ten billion years old. A civilisation that spread from star to star, even slowly, could cross it in a few million years. If intelligent life were common, someone should have arrived or at least been heard by now. This tension is called the Fermi paradox.

Life, or intelligence, is rare

Perhaps one of the steps is extremely unlikely: life starting, complex cells forming, intelligence evolving. The economist Robin Hanson called such a step a Great Filter. The 2000 book Rare Earth, by Peter Ward and Donald Brownlee, argued that complex life needs an unusual combination of conditions.

They exist but we cannot tell

Perhaps civilisations are short-lived, or far apart in time. Perhaps they do not broadcast, or use methods we do not recognise. Our searches have covered only a tiny fraction of stars, frequencies and times. Some, more speculatively, suggest others deliberately keep quiet or avoid contact.

There is an uncomfortable twist. If the Great Filter is behind us, in the origin of life or of complex cells, we are lucky and rare. If it is ahead of us, something tends to end technological civilisations, and that would concern us too. The silence is data, but it is weak data, because we have barely started listening. The honest position, as Volume 29 says, is that confident answers in either direction are unearned. What is new is that, for simple life at least, the instruments that could settle it are being built now.

The core idea

We know of life in only one place, so its frequency is unknown. The search follows liquid water: Mars's past, the ocean moons, and planets whose atmospheres might carry biosignatures. SETI looks for technology. No claim has yet survived checking, and the silence remains unexplained.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Life on Earth by about 3.5 billion years ago, on a 4.5-billion-year-old planet.
  • More than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets by 2025.
  • Nearest known exoplanet in a habitable zone: Proxima b, about 4.2 light years.
  • SETI has been searching since 1960.
The common mistake

Treating "in the habitable zone" or "found organic molecules" as "found life". They describe conditions and ingredients, not living things.

Test yourself

Can you explain why finding life on Europa that does not use DNA would matter far more than finding life that does? If you can, you have understood second genesis and the problem of a sample of one.
