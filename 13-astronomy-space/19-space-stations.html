Space stations. A house that falls around the Earth sixteen times a day.

Since November 2000 there has not been a single day without people living in orbit. The International Space Station is the most expensive object ever built, the size of a football pitch, assembled piece by piece at 28,000 kilometres per hour. It is also a small, noisy, crowded home where the drinking water was recently urine and the crew must exercise two hours a day to stop their bones dissolving. It is due to be retired around 2030. What it taught us, and what replaces it, says a lot about where human spaceflight is going.

Question 1 · What is it?

A spacecraft people live in for months

A space station is a crewed spacecraft that stays in orbit for years, with crews arriving and leaving on other vehicles. It is a laboratory, a test bed for living in space, and a political project.

You can see it with the naked eye. Shortly after sunset or before sunrise, when the ground is dark but the station is still in sunlight, it crosses the sky in a few minutes as a bright, steady dot, brighter than any star. It does not blink, which is how you tell it from an aircraft.

The pattern is steady growth in size and time. The early stations proved people could survive in orbit for weeks; Mir and the ISS proved they could live and work there for months and years.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Assembly, air, water, and a daily routine

A station has to be built in orbit, kept in orbit, kept breathable, and kept supplied, all while its crew stays healthy and busy.

Chapter 03

Built in orbit, piece by piece

No rocket could launch the ISS whole. It was assembled from more than forty launches, starting with the Russian-built Zarya module in November 1998. Pressurised modules, metal cylinders where the crew lives and works, were joined to a long open frame called the truss, which carries the huge solar arrays and the radiators that dump waste heat into space. Much of the assembly was done by astronauts on spacewalks, using robotic arms built by Canada.

Keeping it up is a job too. At 400 kilometres there is still a trace of air, enough to slow the station slightly and make it sink. Every so often engines on the station or on a docked supply ship fire to reboost it. Without that it would eventually fall and burn up. The station also moves out of the way of tracked debris several times in some years, using the same engines.

Chapter 04

Air, water, and the coffee loop

The systems that keep a crew alive are called life support. On the ISS, oxygen is made mainly by splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen with electricity. Carbon dioxide breathed out by the crew is removed by chemical filters, because in weightlessness exhaled air does not rise away from your face. Without fans moving air, a sleeping astronaut could end up surrounded by a bubble of their own carbon dioxide.

Water is precious, since every litre launched from Earth costs a great deal. The US side of the station recycles water from sweat, breath moisture and urine. In 2023 NASA reported reaching about 98 per cent water recovery. Astronauts like to say that today's coffee is made from yesterday's coffee. The recycled water is cleaner than most tap water on Earth.

Not fully closed

Food still comes from Earth, on cargo ships such as SpaceX's Dragon, Northrop Grumman's Cygnus and Russia's Progress. Some lettuce and other plants have been grown on board as experiments, but no station feeds its crew. A fully closed system, where nothing needs resupplying for years, has never been demonstrated. That is the central obstacle for any trip to Mars.

Chapter 05

A day on board

The ISS runs on Greenwich Mean Time, a compromise between the control centres in Houston and Moscow. Crews follow a schedule planned on the ground, often minute by minute: experiments, maintenance, cleaning, and calls with mission control. Much of the work is repair and housekeeping. A station is a complicated machine that is always ageing.

Everything is harder without gravity. Crumbs and drops float into equipment, so food comes in pouches and drinks are sipped through straws. Astronauts sleep in small cabins, zipped into sleeping bags fixed to the wall. The toilet uses airflow instead of gravity. About two hours a day go on exercise: a treadmill with harnesses, a stationary bicycle and a machine that simulates weightlifting with vacuum cylinders. Without it, bones and muscles waste quickly, as Chapter 07 explains.

Chapter 06

Getting crews up and down

Crews travel in separate spacecraft that dock with the station and stay attached as lifeboats. For years Russia's Soyuz capsule did this. After the Space Shuttle retired in 2011, Soyuz was the only way any astronaut could reach the ISS for nine years, with the US paying Russia for seats.

That changed in May 2020, when SpaceX's Crew Dragon carried two NASA astronauts to the station, the first crewed orbital flight by a private company. NASA had also contracted Boeing to build a second capsule, Starliner. Its first crewed test in 2024 had thruster problems, and its two astronauts, planned to stay about a week, returned on a Dragon in March 2025, after about nine months on board. Having two independent ways to reach a station is a safety rule, which is why that delay mattered.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The body, the laboratory, and the partnership

The station's most valuable results are about people: what months in space do to a body. It is also a place to do experiments that need weightlessness, and a rare example of rivals working together.

Chapter 07

What weightlessness does to a body

Your body is built for gravity, and it adjusts quickly when gravity goes away. Fluids that normally pool in the legs shift towards the head, so astronauts get puffy faces and thin "bird legs" in the first days. Bones that no longer carry weight lose mineral, at roughly 1 to 1.5 per cent a month in the hips and spine without countermeasures. Muscles shrink. Many long-stay astronauts develop changes in the shape of the eye and the optic nerve, a condition now studied as spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome.

The best-known study used identical twins. In 2015 and 2016 NASA astronaut Scott Kelly spent about a year on the ISS while his twin brother Mark, also a former astronaut, stayed on Earth. Researchers compared them before, during and after. Most changes in Scott's body, including in gene activity and the immune system, returned to normal within months of landing. Some, such as small effects on thinking speed and certain genetic markers, lasted longer. A year in orbit is survivable and mostly reversible; nobody yet knows about three years, or living in low gravity.

General education

The health effects described here are general findings from spaceflight research, not medical advice.

Chapter 08

A laboratory with no up or down

Gravity affects almost every process on Earth. It makes hot air rise, heavy particles sink, and liquids settle. Removing it lets scientists study things that are normally hidden. Flames in weightlessness are round and burn differently, which helps fire safety research. Some protein crystals grow larger and more orderly, useful for working out their shape for drug design. Fluids behave in ways that matter for engineering.

The station also carries instruments that look outward, such as the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer, a particle detector studying cosmic rays, and Earth-observing cameras. And it is the test bed for the technology future missions will need: life support, recycling, spacesuits and robotics. The most important experiment on the ISS is the crew itself.

Chapter 09

A partnership between rivals

The ISS was born partly from diplomacy. After the Soviet Union collapsed, bringing Russia into the project in 1993 kept Russian engineers employed on peaceful work and joined two former rivals in one spacecraft. The partnership has survived serious tensions on the ground, including after 2014 and 2022, when relations between Russia and the West broke down. Russian and American crews have continued to fly together, each depending on the other: the Russian side controls much of the station's propulsion, and the US side most of its power.

China is not part of it. A 2011 US law, often called the Wolf Amendment, restricts NASA from working bilaterally with China. China built its own station, Tiangong, and has said it will host astronauts from other countries. Low Earth orbit now has two crewed stations, belonging to two different political camps, a fact that mirrors the split described in Moon exploration.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A remarkable achievement at a remarkable price

Few people doubt that the ISS is an engineering triumph. The argument is about whether its results justified its cost.

Chapter 11

The risks people live with

Stations are dangerous places. In 1997 Mir suffered a fire from an oxygen-generating canister that filled the station with smoke. A few months later an uncrewed supply ship collided with one of its modules during a docking test and punctured it, and the crew sealed off the module to save the rest. Both crews survived. Those events showed how small margins are in a sealed metal tube.

On the ISS, risks include slow air leaks, which have been traced in the Russian segment since 2019 and whose cause is still debated, ammonia leaks from cooling systems, and debris. When debris passes too close to move away from, the crew shelters in their docked capsules, ready to leave. Every crew lives with a small, permanent chance of having to abandon ship, and that is why a lifeboat must always be attached.

Chapter 13

What changes after the ISS

The biggest shift is in who owns the station. For fifty years governments built and ran stations. The plan now is for governments to become customers, renting space and buying crew time, while companies own the hardware and look for other paying users, such as researchers, drug companies, film-makers and private visitors. Whether enough of those customers exist is the central uncertainty. Several private astronaut missions have already flown to the ISS, but no company has yet shown a business that pays for a whole station.

Some things will not change. A station will still need a lifeboat, regular resupply, constant maintenance and careful debris avoidance. The health questions will remain, too, especially for the longer, deeper missions that stations are meant to prepare for. The ISS showed people can live in orbit; the next stations must show it can be done cheaply enough to keep doing it.

The core idea

A space station is a long-lived crewed spacecraft in low orbit, built from modules, kept up by reboosts, and kept alive by life support that recycles air and water but not food. Its main lesson is what months of weightlessness do to the human body.

The numbers worth knowing
  • ISS: about 400 km up, one orbit every 90 minutes.
  • Continuously crewed since November 2000.
  • About 98% of water recovered on the US side.
  • Bone loss of roughly 1–1.5% a month in key bones without exercise.
The common mistake

Thinking the ISS is "in space, beyond gravity". It is only about 400 km up, and gravity there is about 90% of what you feel on the ground. It is falling, continuously.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a station needs a capsule docked at all times, and why it would fall without reboosts? If you can, you have understood lifeboats, drag and orbits together.

The words, in plain words
Space station
A crewed spacecraft that stays in orbit for years while crews come and go.
Module
A pressurised cylinder that forms one room of a station.
Truss
The open frame that carries a station's solar arrays and radiators.
Reboost
An engine burn that raises a station's orbit after drag has lowered it.
Life support
The systems that provide air, water, pressure and temperature for a crew.
Microgravity
The near-weightless condition inside an orbiting spacecraft.
Fluid shift
The movement of body fluids towards the head in weightlessness.
Bone demineralisation
The loss of mineral from bones that no longer carry weight.
Spacewalk
Work done outside the spacecraft in a pressurised suit.
Deorbit
Deliberately lowering a spacecraft so it re-enters the atmosphere.
Tiangong
China's space station, completed in 2022.
Commercial station
A privately owned station that governments and others rent space on.
