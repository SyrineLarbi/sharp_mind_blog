Exoplanets. Thirty years ago we knew of none. Now there are more than six thousand.

For all of history until the 1990s, nobody knew whether any other star had planets. It seemed likely, but it was unproven. Then, within a few years, the first ones were found, and they were nothing like what anyone expected. Today we know that planets are common, that most stars have them, and that the commonest kinds are missing from our own solar system. Almost none of these planets has ever been seen directly. They are detected from tiny wobbles and shadows in the light of their stars.

Question 1 · What is it?

Planets that orbit other stars

An exoplanet, short for "extrasolar planet", is any planet outside our solar system. The first convincing ones were found only in the 1990s, and the very first was found around one of the least likely stars imaginable.

Chapter 01

The planet that should not have existed

In 1995 two Swiss astronomers, Michel Mayor and his student Didier Queloz, were measuring tiny changes in the light of nearby Sun-like stars. One star, 51 Pegasi, about 50 light-years away, was moving back and forth very slightly every 4.2 days. The simplest explanation was an unseen planet tugging on it. From the size of the tug, the planet had to be roughly half the mass of Jupiter.

That made no sense. In our solar system, giant planets are far from the Sun and take years to orbit. This one went round its star in four days, far closer to its star than Mercury is to the Sun, at a temperature of over a thousand degrees. Many astronomers were sceptical at first. Within weeks, an American team confirmed the signal. The planet, 51 Pegasi b, became the first of a new class called hot Jupiters. Mayor and Queloz shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019.

The lesson came straight away: nature makes planetary systems our theories had not imagined. Planets can move far from where they formed, as explained in Planet formation.

Chapter 02

The first ones circled a dead star

The 1995 discovery was the first planet around a Sun-like star. But three years earlier, in 1992, the astronomers Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail had found planets around a pulsar, the spinning, collapsed core left after a massive star exploded. A pulsar sends out radio pulses as regular as an atomic clock. Tiny delays in those pulses revealed small planets, a few times Earth's mass, orbiting it. Those planets probably formed from debris after the explosion, so they are a strange first example. Pulsars are explained in Neutron stars.

Since then the count has climbed steeply. NASA's catalogue passed 6,000 confirmed exoplanets in 2025, with thousands more candidates waiting for confirmation. Astronomers officially count something as a planet if it is too small to fuse hydrogen like a star, roughly below thirteen Jupiter masses, and orbits a star or stellar remnant.

Chapter 03

Why it took so long

Two problems made exoplanets nearly impossible to see. The first is glare. A star like the Sun is roughly ten billion times brighter than an Earth-like planet in visible light. Spotting one beside the other is like spotting a firefly next to a lighthouse from thousands of kilometres away.

The second is distance. Even the nearest stars are so far away that a planet and its star appear almost on top of each other. At the distance of the nearest star, the gap between the Sun and Earth would look about as wide as a small coin seen from five kilometres away.

So astronomers mostly gave up trying to see planets directly. Instead, they learned to see their effects on their stars. That shift in strategy is the key to the whole field.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Wobbles, shadows, and starlight filtered through air

Nearly every exoplanet has been found by measuring a star very precisely and noticing a pattern that only a planet explains. The same tricks can then reveal a planet's size, mass, and even the gases in its atmosphere.

Chapter 04

The wobble: radial velocity

A planet does not simply orbit a star. Both orbit their shared centre of mass. Because the star is far heavier, it moves only a little, in a small circle. That motion makes the star approach us, then recede, over and over. When it approaches, its light is squeezed slightly towards blue. When it recedes, it is stretched slightly towards red. This is the Doppler effect, the same reason a passing siren changes pitch.

Measuring that colour shift is the radial velocity method. "Radial" means along our line of sight. The best instruments can now detect a star moving back and forth at well under one metre per second, slower than a person walking. Jupiter makes the Sun wobble at about 12 metres per second. Earth makes it wobble at only about 9 centimetres per second, which is still beyond reach once the star's own surface noise is considered. The method gives a planet's minimum mass and its orbital period.

Chapter 05

The shadow: transits

If a planet's orbit happens to be lined up edge-on from our point of view, the planet passes in front of its star once every orbit. It blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light, and the star dims very slightly. This is a transit.

The depth of the dip tells you the planet's size compared with the star. Jupiter passing in front of the Sun would dim it by about 1 per cent. Earth would dim it by under one hundredth of 1 per cent. NASA's Kepler space telescope stared at about 150,000 stars from 2009 to 2018 and found thousands of planets this way, more than any other mission. Its successor, TESS (launched 2018), scans most of the sky, focusing on bright nearby stars.

Transits and wobbles work best together. The transit gives the size. The wobble gives the mass. Divide one by the other and you get the density, which tells you whether a planet is rock, water or gas. That is how astronomers can say, without ever seeing it, that a world is "probably rocky".

Direct imaging deserves a word. In 2008 astronomers released images of planets around the young star HR 8799, using a device that blocks the star's light, called a coronagraph, and adaptive optics that remove the blurring of Earth's atmosphere. The planets appear as faint dots, and over the years since, their slow orbital motion has been tracked image by image. Seeing a planet move around another star is one of the quiet wonders of modern astronomy.

Chapter 07

Reading an atmosphere through starlight

During a transit, a thin ring of starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere on its way to us. Each gas absorbs particular colours (Light carries a signature). By comparing the star's light during and outside the transit, astronomers can see which colours the atmosphere removed, and so which gases are present. This is called transmission spectroscopy.

The signal is minute, a change of a few parts in ten thousand or less. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021, made the method routine. In 2022 it found clear evidence of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of a hot giant planet called WASP-39b, along with water and other molecules, and even signs of chemistry driven by starlight. Webb has since measured dozens of atmospheres.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A galaxy full of worlds, and the oldest question

Exoplanets settled a question that philosophers argued about for over two thousand years, showed that our solar system is not the template, and turned "is there life elsewhere?" into a question telescopes can begin to test.

Chapter 08

Planets are the rule, not the exception

Because transit and wobble surveys miss many planets, astronomers correct for what they cannot see and estimate how common planets really are. The answer is striking. Studies of Kepler data and microlensing surveys suggest that, on average, every star in the Milky Way has at least one planet. With a few hundred billion stars, that means hundreds of billions of planets in our galaxy alone.

How many are roughly Earth-sized and at the right distance for liquid water? Estimates for Sun-like stars vary widely, from a few per cent to around half, depending on definitions and assumptions. Even the low end implies billions of such planets in the galaxy. The question is no longer whether Earth-sized planets in temperate orbits exist, but what they are actually like.

Chapter 09

The zoo: worlds unlike ours

Hot Jupiters

Giant planets in orbits of a few days. Famous because they were found first, but they orbit only around 1 per cent of Sun-like stars.

Super-Earths and sub-Neptunes

Between Earth and Neptune in size. The most common planets found so far, and absent from our solar system. Some are rocky, others wrapped in thick gas.

Lava worlds

Rocky planets so close to their stars that their day sides may be oceans of molten rock.

Circumbinary planets

Planets orbiting two stars at once, with double sunsets, like the fictional Tatooine. Kepler-16b, announced in 2011, was the first clear case found by transit.

One clue to how this zoo formed is a gap in planet sizes. Among small close-in planets there are fewer than expected at about 1.5 to 2 times Earth's width. This radius valley suggests two groups: rocky planets that lost their original gas envelopes, perhaps stripped by starlight, and slightly bigger planets that kept them. How the stripping works is still being studied.

Chapter 10

The habitable zone, and its limits

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with the right atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface: not so close that it boils, not so far that it freezes. It is a useful filter, but only a filter. Venus sits near the inner edge of the Sun's habitable zone and is a furnace. Being in the zone is about temperature, not about life.

Many of the most studied candidates orbit red dwarfs, small cool stars that are the most common in the galaxy. Their habitable zones are close in, so their planets transit often and are easier to study. The nearest exoplanet, Proxima Centauri b, found in 2016, orbits the nearest star to the Sun, about 4.2 light-years away, inside its habitable zone. In 2017 astronomers announced seven Earth-sized planets around a red dwarf called TRAPPIST-1, about 40 light-years away, several of them in its habitable zone.

Red dwarfs have drawbacks. Young ones flare violently, which may strip the air from nearby planets. And planets that close tend to become tidally locked, with one side always facing the star, like the Moon to Earth. Early Webb observations of the two innermost TRAPPIST-1 planets found no sign of thick atmospheres. Whether rocky planets around red dwarfs can keep atmospheres at all is one of the most important open questions in the field.

Chapter 11

The search for signs of life, and a cautionary tale

The ultimate goal is to find a biosignature: a gas, or a combination of gases, that is hard to explain without life. Earth's atmosphere would give itself away by having lots of oxygen and some methane at the same time. The two react with each other, so something must be constantly replacing them.

In 2023 and 2025 a team studying the sub-Neptune K2-18b, about 120 light-years away, reported methane and carbon dioxide and then a tentative signal of dimethyl sulfide, a gas produced on Earth mainly by marine life. The claim drew wide attention. Many other researchers reanalysed the data and found the evidence weak or explainable without life, and some questioned whether the planet has an ocean at all. As of 2026 the claim is widely regarded as unconfirmed. The episode is a useful lesson: an extraordinary claim based on a faint signal needs independent, repeated confirmation.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful methods with strong biases

Every detection method sees some planets easily and others not at all. The catalogue is therefore a distorted picture, and knowing the distortions is part of understanding the field.

One consequence is easy to miss. We still have not found a true analogue of Earth: a rocky planet of Earth's size, in a one-year orbit around a Sun-like star, with its mass and atmosphere measured. Such a planet produces a transit too shallow, too infrequent, and a wobble too small for current instruments. The planets we know about are mostly the ones easiest to find, not the ones most like home.

Chapter 14

What finding "another Earth" would and would not mean

Headlines regularly announce an "Earth twin". So far, every one has turned out to be something less: a planet of the right size around the wrong kind of star, or of the right temperature but the wrong size, or with no information at all about its atmosphere. The phrase should be read with care.

Even a genuine twin would answer only part of the question. A planet with Earth's size, temperature and an oxygen-rich atmosphere would be extraordinary evidence, but oxygen can sometimes be produced without life, for example when starlight splits water. Scientists expect life, if it is found this way, to be accepted gradually, as several independent lines of evidence point in the same direction, not in a single announcement.

What is already certain is a change in perspective. The ancient question of whether other worlds exist has been answered: they do, in vast numbers. The open question, whether any of them are alive, is taken up in The possibility of extraterrestrial life.

The core idea

Exoplanets are found mostly through their effects on their stars: the wobble they cause (radial velocity) and the shadow they cast (transit). Together these give mass, size and density. Starlight filtered through an atmosphere reveals its gases. Planets turn out to be more common than stars, and very varied.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets as of 2025.
  • First around a Sun-like star: 1995 (51 Pegasi b); first of all: 1992, around a pulsar.
  • Earth dims the Sun by under 0.01 per cent in transit; Jupiter by about 1 per cent.
The common mistake

Treating "in the habitable zone" or "Earth-sized" as meaning "Earth-like" or "has life". The habitable zone is about temperature only; Venus is close to the Sun's.
