The Solar System. One star, eight planets, and a great deal of empty space.

School drawings show the planets lined up like beads on a string, close together and roughly the same size. Almost nothing in that picture is right. The Sun holds nearly all the mass. The planets are tiny dots separated by enormous gaps. And past the last planet there is a huge cloud of icy bodies that no one has ever seen directly. Getting the real picture into your head is the first step in all of astronomy, because every other star we study is compared, sooner or later, with this one family.

Question 1 · What is it?

Everything the Sun's gravity holds on to

The solar system is the Sun plus every object that orbits it: planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and dust. It formed together, about 4.6 billion years ago, from one collapsing cloud of gas.

Chapter 01

A family photo taken from six billion kilometres

On 14 February 1990 the Voyager 1 spacecraft, by then far beyond Neptune, turned its camera back towards home. It took a series of pictures of the planets from about six billion kilometres away. In one of them, Earth is a single pale dot, smaller than a pixel, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight. The astronomer Carl Sagan, who had pushed for the picture to be taken, called it the "Pale Blue Dot".

The photo is famous because it shows the true proportions. From the edge of the planetary system, the planets are barely visible and the Sun is just a very bright star. Most of the solar system is empty space.

Our solar system is one of many: most stars probably have planets of their own, as the topic on Exoplanets explains. Ours is simply the only one we can visit.

Chapter 02

The inventory

Distances inside the solar system are measured in astronomical units (AU). One AU is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres. Light covers it in a little over eight minutes.

One number puts the rest in perspective. The Sun contains about 99.8 per cent of all the mass in the solar system. Jupiter has most of what is left. Earth, and everything on it, is a rounding error.

Chapter 03

Why Pluto stopped being a planet

For 76 years after its discovery in 1930, Pluto was the ninth planet. Then, in the early 2000s, astronomers began finding other icy worlds in the same region. One of them, Eris, found in 2005, turned out to be about as massive as Pluto, slightly more in fact. Either Eris was the tenth planet, and dozens more might follow, or the word "planet" needed a definition.

In August 2006 the International Astronomical Union, the body that names things in astronomy, voted on one. A planet must do three things. It must orbit the Sun. It must be massive enough that its own gravity has pulled it into a round shape. And it must have cleared its neighbourhood, which means it is by far the dominant object in its orbital zone. Pluto passes the first two tests and fails the third, because it shares its zone with thousands of Kuiper belt objects. It became a dwarf planet, alongside Eris and Ceres, the largest body in the asteroid belt.

Some planetary scientists still prefer a definition based only on what an object is like, which would make Pluto a planet again. The disagreement is about words, not facts: nobody disputes what Pluto is, only which box to put it in.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Gravity, heat and leftovers

Three things explain the layout. Gravity sets the orbits. The Sun's heat, early on, decided what could form where. And the material that never became a planet is still out there, telling us how the planets were built.

Chapter 04

Orbits: falling forever and always missing

Early in the 1600s the German astronomer Johannes Kepler, working from years of careful observations by Tycho Brahe, found three rules for planetary motion. Planets move on ellipses, slightly squashed circles, with the Sun at one focus. They speed up when they are closer to the Sun and slow down when farther away. And the farther out a planet is, the longer its year, by a precise mathematical rule. Jupiter's year is about twelve of ours; Neptune's is about 165.

Later in the same century Isaac Newton explained why. The same gravity that drops an apple pulls each planet towards the Sun. A planet is always falling towards the Sun. But it is also moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. An orbit is a fall that never lands. Astronauts float for the same reason: they are falling together with their station.

All the planets orbit in the same direction and in almost the same flat plane. That is the fingerprint of their birth from one spinning, flattened disc of gas and dust, the story told in Planet formation.

Chapter 05

Rocky inside, giant outside

The four inner planets are small and made of rock and metal. The four outer planets are huge and made mostly of gas or ices. The reason is temperature in the young solar system.

Close to the young Sun it was too hot for water to freeze. Only materials that stay solid at high temperature, such as rock and iron, could clump together, and there was not much of them. So the inner planets stayed small. Farther out, beyond a boundary astronomers call the frost line (or snow line), water, ammonia and methane froze into solid ice. Suddenly there was far more solid material to build with. Planet cores out there grew big enough, quickly enough, to pull in huge amounts of the hydrogen and helium gas around them before the gas blew away.

That produced two kinds of giant. Gas giants (Jupiter and Saturn) are mostly hydrogen and helium, like the Sun itself. Ice giants (Uranus and Neptune) are smaller and contain a larger share of water, ammonia and methane. "Ice" here is a chemist's word for those substances, not frozen lumps: deep inside, they are hot dense fluids.

A planet with no surface

You could not land on Jupiter. Its gas thickens with depth into liquid, and deeper still into hydrogen squeezed so hard it conducts electricity like a metal.

Chapter 06

Moons, rings and small worlds

The planets have hundreds of moons between them, and the count keeps rising as telescopes find small ones. As of 2025 Saturn holds the record, with well over 200 confirmed, mostly small captured rocks. Mercury and Venus have none.

A few moons are worlds in their own right. Ganymede, orbiting Jupiter, is the largest moon in the solar system and is wider than the planet Mercury. Io, another of Jupiter's moons, is the most volcanically active body known, heated by tides as Jupiter's gravity flexes it. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane. The European probe Huygens landed there in 2005.

Most surprising of all, several icy moons hide liquid water oceans under their crusts. Europa (Jupiter) and Enceladus (Saturn) are the best cases. The Cassini spacecraft flew through jets of water vapour and ice spraying from cracks near Enceladus's south pole. Those oceans are why many scientists now think the best places to look for life beyond Earth may be moons, not planets.

All four giants have rings, though only Saturn's are bright. Then there are the leftovers, the asteroids and comets. Asteroids are mainly rock and metal, mostly in the belt between Mars and Jupiter. Comets are mixtures of ice and dust from the cold outer regions. Near the Sun, their ice turns to gas and forms a glowing tail.

Chapter 07

Where does it end?

Before talking about the edge, it helps to shrink everything to a size you can picture.

The Sun constantly blows out a thin stream of charged particles called the solar wind. It inflates a vast bubble around the Sun, the heliosphere. Its outer boundary, where the solar wind finally gives way to the gas between the stars, is the heliopause. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018, both about 120 AU from the Sun. They are the only human-made objects to have sampled interstellar space.

But the Sun's gravity reaches much farther than its wind. In 1950 the Dutch astronomer Jan Oort proposed that long-period comets, which fall in from random directions every few thousand or million years, come from a giant spherical cloud far beyond the planets. Nobody has seen the Oort cloud. It is inferred from the orbits of the comets it sends us. If it exists as described, the true edge of the solar system is a fuzzy zone where the Sun's pull fades into that of its neighbours.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Our place, our safety, and our only laboratory

Studying the solar system moved humanity out of the centre of the universe, still decides whether we see the next dangerous asteroid in time, and is the only place where we can test our ideas about planets by going there.

Chapter 08

The great demotion, and a planet found with a pen

For most of history, astronomers in Europe and the Middle East put a motionless Earth at the centre. In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published the case for putting the Sun at the centre. In 1610 Galileo Galilei pointed an early telescope at Jupiter and saw four moons orbiting it, clear proof that not everything circles Earth. Kepler and Newton then turned the new picture into precise physics.

The physics became so precise that it could find planets nobody had seen. Uranus, discovered by William Herschel in 1781, kept drifting slightly off its predicted path. In 1846 the French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unseen planet would have to be to pull it that way. The German astronomer Johann Galle looked there and found Neptune within a degree of the prediction on the first night of searching. A planet was discovered by calculation before it was seen.

There is a sequel. Mercury also drifts slightly from Newton's predictions, and Le Verrier proposed another hidden planet, "Vulcan". It was never found. In 1915 Einstein's theory of gravity explained the drift exactly (Gravity is not a force pulling you down). The same method gave a new planet once and a new theory of gravity the second time.

Chapter 09

The rocks that hit us

The solar system is not finished being built. Small bodies still collide with planets. About 66 million years ago an object roughly ten kilometres wide struck what is now Mexico's Yucatán peninsula and left the Chicxulub crater. The impact is widely accepted as the main cause of the mass extinction that ended the age of the non-bird dinosaurs.

Smaller impacts are more frequent. In 1908 an object exploded over Siberia and flattened about 2,000 square kilometres of forest. In February 2013 a rock around 20 metres wide burst in the sky over Chelyabinsk in Russia. Its shock wave broke windows across the city and injured around 1,500 people, mostly from flying glass. It came from the direction of the Sun, where telescopes cannot look.

This is why governments now fund planetary defence. Survey telescopes catalogue near-Earth asteroids. Most of those over one kilometre wide have been found, and none threatens Earth in the coming century. In September 2022 NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into a small asteroid moon called Dimorphos and shortened its orbit by about half an hour. For the first time, humans changed the path of a natural object in space, showing that deflection can work if a threat is found years in advance.

Chapter 10

Three planets, three fates

Venus, Earth and Mars began with broadly similar ingredients. Today they could hardly be more different, and comparing them is one of the most useful things planetary science does.

Venus: too much

A thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect. The surface is around 465 °C, with a pressure about 90 times Earth's.

Earth: just enough

Oceans, plate tectonics and life have kept carbon dioxide in a range that holds the surface mild for billions of years.

Mars: too little

Small and quick to cool, Mars lost most of its magnetic field and much of its air. River valleys and old lake beds show it was once wetter. Now its surface water is frozen or gone.

The lesson is that a planet's climate is not fixed by its distance from the Sun alone. That matters for understanding Earth's own climate (Why the planet is habitable at all) and to judging which exoplanets might really be habitable.

The solar system is also a time capsule. The oldest solid grains in meteorites are about 4.57 billion years old, which is how the system itself is dated. Japan's Hayabusa2 and NASA's OSIRIS-REx have brought back pristine asteroid samples for laboratories to study.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A good place to live, an awkward place to generalise from

As a home, the solar system has been kind to us. As a scientific example, it has a weakness: it was, for centuries, the only planetary system we knew, and it turned out not to be typical.

Chapter 12

Is our solar system normal?

Before the 1990s, theories of planet formation were built to produce our solar system. Then the first planet found around a Sun-like star, in 1995, turned out to be a Jupiter-sized world orbiting in about four days, far closer than Mercury. Nothing in the old theories allowed that.

Thousands of discoveries later, a clearer picture has emerged. The most common kind of planet found so far is something our system lacks entirely: a world between Earth and Neptune in size, often called a super-Earth or sub-Neptune, frequently orbiting closer to its star than Mercury. Our system, with no such planet and nothing inside Mercury's orbit, looks somewhat unusual.

The caveat is that detection methods favour big planets close to their stars, so it is not yet clear how rare systems like ours are. What is clear is that "the solar system" is one way to build a planetary system, not the way.

Chapter 14

The very long view

The distant future is more predictable, because it is governed by the Sun, which is slowly brightening, by roughly ten per cent every billion years. In perhaps one to two billion years, that extra heat is expected to drive water from Earth's oceans into the atmosphere and make the surface uninhabitable for complex life.

In about five billion years, the Sun will run short of hydrogen in its core and swell into a red giant. Mercury and Venus will almost certainly be swallowed. Whether Earth is too depends on fine details of how much mass the Sun loses, and models disagree. Afterwards the Sun will shed its outer layers and shrink to a white dwarf, a hot, dense remnant about Earth's size. The outer planets will probably survive, circling a dim cinder.

The orbits themselves are mildly chaotic: tiny differences grow until exact long-range prediction is impossible. In a small fraction of computer simulations, around one per cent in well-known studies, Mercury's orbit becomes unstable before the Sun dies. Even the most regular-looking machine in nature is not a clock.

The core idea

The solar system is the Sun and everything its gravity holds. Small rocky planets formed close in where it was hot; giants formed beyond the frost line where ice added building material. The leftovers, asteroids and comets, preserve the conditions of its birth 4.6 billion years ago.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The Sun holds about 99.8 per cent of the system's mass.
  • One astronomical unit is about 150 million km, or 8 light-minutes.
  • Neptune orbits at about 30 AU; the Voyagers left the heliosphere at about 120 AU.
The common mistake

Picturing the planets as close together and similar in size. The solar system is almost entirely empty space, and the Sun outweighs everything else combined by a factor of hundreds.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Jupiter is a giant made of gas while Earth is a small ball of rock, using only the idea of temperature in the young solar system? If so, you understand the frost line.

The words, in plain words
Astronomical unit (AU)
The average Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km. The ruler of the solar system.
Orbit
The path of one object around another, held by gravity. A fall that keeps missing.
Ellipse
A slightly squashed circle. The shape of every planetary orbit.
Dwarf planet
A round body orbiting the Sun that has not cleared its orbital zone, such as Pluto or Ceres.
Frost line
The distance from a young star beyond which water freezes into solid ice.
Gas giant
A huge planet made mostly of hydrogen and helium, such as Jupiter.
Ice giant
A giant planet with a large share of water, ammonia and methane, such as Neptune.
Asteroid
A small rocky or metallic body orbiting the Sun, mostly between Mars and Jupiter.
Comet
A small body of ice and dust that grows a glowing tail when it comes near the Sun.
Kuiper belt
A ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune, including Pluto.
Oort cloud
A predicted, never-observed shell of comets surrounding the whole solar system.
Heliosphere
The bubble blown by the solar wind. Its edge is the heliopause.
