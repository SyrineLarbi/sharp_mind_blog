Neutron stars. More mass than the Sun, squeezed into the size of a city.

When a massive star explodes, its core can collapse into an object about twenty kilometres across that weighs more than the Sun. It is made mostly of neutrons packed as tightly as the inside of an atomic nucleus. Many spin hundreds of times a second and sweep beams of radio waves across space like a lighthouse. They are the densest things we can observe that still have a surface, and they have turned out to be precise clocks, natural laboratories for physics that cannot be tested on Earth, and the source of much of the gold on our planet.

Question 1 · What is it?

A star's core, crushed into a giant atomic nucleus

A neutron star is the collapsed core of a massive star after a supernova. It was predicted in the 1930s, dismissed as unobservable, and then discovered by accident by a graduate student checking her charts.

Chapter 01

"Little green men"

In 1967 Jocelyn Bell, a graduate student at the University of Cambridge, was analysing data from a new radio telescope she had helped build. The telescope produced about thirty metres of paper chart every day, and she read it by hand. Among the noise she noticed a small mark, which she called "a bit of scruff", that kept reappearing at the same position in the sky.

When she and her supervisor, Antony Hewish, recorded it at high speed, it turned out to be a series of sharp radio pulses arriving every 1.34 seconds with astonishing regularity. Nothing natural was known to tick like that. Half-jokingly, the team labelled the source "LGM-1", for little green men. Then Bell found a second source, in a different part of the sky, ticking at a different rate. Two alien civilisations signalling at once on the same frequency seemed unlikely. It had to be a new kind of natural object. The press named them pulsars.

The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics went partly to Hewish for the discovery. Bell was not included, which many astronomers then and since have seen as an injustice, though she has said she did not feel bitter. In 2018 she received a major science prize, the Breakthrough Prize, and gave the full 3 million US dollars to fund physics students from under-represented groups.

Chapter 02

An idea that came before the evidence

The neutron, a particle with no electric charge found in atomic nuclei, was discovered in 1932 by the British physicist James Chadwick. Within two years, the astronomers Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky proposed that a giant stellar explosion, for which they coined the term supernova, could turn an ordinary star into a "neutron star". In 1939 Robert Oppenheimer and George Volkoff calculated how such a star would hold itself up, and suggested there would be a maximum possible mass.

Most astronomers saw this as a curiosity. A star twenty kilometres wide would be far too small to see across interstellar distances. No one expected neutron stars to announce themselves with radio beams. When pulsars were found, theorists soon argued that a small, dense, spinning object was the likeliest source. The case closed when pulsars ticking many times a second turned up: a white dwarf, the next densest option, would tear itself apart spinning that fast. The thirty-year-old prediction was confirmed.

Astronomers estimate that the Milky Way contains hundreds of millions of neutron stars, nearly all of them invisible to us. More than 3,000 have been catalogued as pulsars, and more are found every year.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Collapse, spin and magnetism

A neutron star is born in less than a second, held up by a quantum effect, and then shaped for the rest of its life by two inheritances from its parent star: spin and magnetism, both hugely amplified by the collapse.

Chapter 04

Born in a fraction of a second

A star of more than about eight times the Sun's mass builds up layers of heavier and heavier elements by fusion, ending with a core of iron. Iron is a dead end: fusing it absorbs energy rather than releasing it. When the iron core grows to around 1.4 solar masses, nothing can hold it up any more.

The core collapses in well under a second, falling inward at a large fraction of the speed of light. The pressure is so great that electrons are forced into protons, turning them into neutrons and releasing a flood of neutrinos, ghostly particles that barely interact with anything. The core shrinks from a few thousand kilometres across to about twenty. Then it suddenly becomes stiff, and the infalling outer material rebounds off it. Together with the energy carried by the neutrinos, this drives the explosion outward as a supernova, as described in Supernovae. The neutron star is what is left at the centre.

Chapter 05

What holds it up, and the limit

An ordinary star is held up by heat, as Volume 29 explains (What a star actually is). A neutron star has no fusion and slowly cools, yet it does not collapse. What holds it up is a rule from quantum physics, the Pauli exclusion principle: no two neutrons can be in exactly the same state. When neutrons are packed together, they are forced into higher and higher energy states, and that resists further squeezing. This is called degeneracy pressure, helped at these densities by the strong nuclear force pushing neutrons apart.

There is a limit. Above a certain mass, gravity wins and nothing can stop the collapse to a black hole. The exact limit depends on how matter behaves at these densities, which is not fully known. The heaviest neutron stars measured so far are a little over two solar masses, and most estimates put the maximum somewhere between about 2 and 2.5. Measuring that limit precisely is one of the main goals of neutron-star research, because it reveals how the densest matter in the universe behaves.

Chapter 06

The lighthouse

When a spinning skater pulls in her arms, she spins faster. A star's core shrinking from thousands of kilometres to twenty does the same thing on an extreme scale. So newborn neutron stars spin fast, many times per second. The collapse also concentrates the star's magnetic field enormously.

Charged particles accelerated along that magnetic field send out beams of radiation from the magnetic poles. If the magnetic poles are tilted relative to the spin axis, as on Earth, the beams sweep around as the star turns. If a beam happens to sweep across Earth, we see a pulse once per turn, exactly like the flash of a lighthouse seen from a ship. That is what a pulsar is. Neutron stars whose beams miss us are still there, simply invisible to radio telescopes.

Pulsars slow down as their radiation carries energy away. The famous Crab pulsar, at the heart of the remnant of a supernova that Chinese astronomers recorded in the year 1054, spins about thirty times a second and is gradually slowing. Some old neutron stars in pairs, however, have been spun back up by gas pulled from a companion star, like a merry-go-round pushed by hand. These millisecond pulsars can turn hundreds of times a second, and they are the most stable of all.

Chapter 07

Magnetars: the strongest magnets known

A small fraction of neutron stars have magnetic fields about a thousand times stronger than ordinary pulsars. They are called magnetars. Their fields are so strong that they would distort atoms, and would wipe every credit card on Earth from halfway to the Moon. As the field shifts, it can crack the star's solid crust in a "starquake", releasing bursts of X-rays and gamma rays.

On 27 December 2004, a magnetar called SGR 1806-20, on the far side of the Milky Way, produced a giant flare. In a fraction of a second it released more energy than the Sun gives out in many thousands of years. The radiation was strong enough to disturb Earth's upper atmosphere measurably, despite travelling for tens of thousands of years to get here.

Magnetars may also explain one of astronomy's recent mysteries. Fast radio bursts are flashes of radio waves lasting a few thousandths of a second, mostly from other galaxies, first reported in 2007. In 2020 a magnetar in our own galaxy produced a burst very like a weak version of them. That strongly suggests magnetars cause at least some fast radio bursts, though perhaps not all.

Chapter 08

Inside a neutron star

Nobody can look inside a neutron star, but physics allows some confident statements and some educated guesses. The outer layer is thought to be a solid crust about a kilometre thick, made of atomic nuclei arranged in a crystal lattice, far harder than steel. Deeper down, theorists predict that nuclei are squeezed into strange shapes, sheets and tubes, a phase nicknamed nuclear pasta.

Below that, neutrons are expected to form a superfluid, a quantum fluid that flows with no friction. Evidence comes from glitches: some pulsars suddenly speed up very slightly, which is best explained by the inner superfluid transferring spin to the crust. What lies at the very centre is unknown. It may be neutrons and other particles, or possibly free quarks, the particles that normally live inside neutrons and protons. That question is taken up in Chapter 14.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Clocks, gold and extreme physics

Neutron stars confirmed Einstein's prediction of gravitational waves, revealed where much of the universe's gold is made, and let physicists test matter at densities no laboratory can reach.

Chapter 09

Nature's best clocks

Millisecond pulsars tick so steadily that, over years, their pulse arrival times can be predicted to within a millionth of a second or better. That makes them precision instruments scattered across the galaxy.

In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor discovered a pulsar orbiting another neutron star. Over the following years, they measured the orbit shrinking very slowly. The rate matched exactly what Einstein's theory of gravity predicts if the pair is losing energy by giving off gravitational waves. It was the first evidence that gravitational waves are real, decades before they were detected directly, and it earned Hulse and Taylor the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Today astronomers time dozens of millisecond pulsars together, in projects called pulsar timing arrays. If very long gravitational waves pass through the galaxy, they stretch and squeeze the distances to the pulsars, shifting the arrival times in a recognisable pattern. In 2023 several teams around the world, including NANOGrav in North America, reported evidence for a background hum of such waves. Its most likely source is pairs of supermassive black holes orbiting each other across the universe.

Chapter 10

Where gold comes from

On 17 August 2017, gravitational-wave detectors recorded the final spiral of two neutron stars colliding about 130 million light-years away. Telescopes around the world then saw the flash of the explosion, called a kilonova. Its light showed the signature of freshly made heavy elements. Physics · Gravitational waves tells this story in detail (Gravitational waves).

What it showed about neutron stars themselves is the point here. When two collide, neutron-rich material is flung out, and neutrons rapidly attach to atomic nuclei, building them up into the heaviest elements, including gold, platinum and uranium. This is called the r-process ("r" for rapid). Neutron star mergers are now considered one major source of the heaviest elements. Whether they are the main source, or whether some rare kinds of supernova contribute as much, is still debated. Either way, a gold ring very probably contains atoms forged in neutron-rich explosions billions of years ago.

Chapter 11

A laboratory nobody can build, and a map home

On Earth, physicists can create matter at nuclear density only in tiny fragments, for an instant, in particle colliders. A neutron star is a whole star of it, stable for billions of years. By measuring neutron stars' masses and sizes, astronomers test theories of the strong nuclear force under conditions no experiment can match. NASA's NICER instrument on the International Space Station, installed in 2017, has measured the sizes of several neutron stars by timing X-rays from hot spots on their surfaces.

Pulsars have practical uses too. The plaques carried by the Pioneer spacecraft and the golden records on the Voyagers include a map showing the Sun's position relative to fourteen pulsars, each identified by its pulse rate. In 2017 a NASA experiment used X-ray pulsars to calculate the position of the Space Station, a first demonstration of pulsar navigation, which could one day guide spacecraft far from Earth, where GPS does not reach.

Chapter 14

The question at the centre

The deepest open question about neutron stars is the equation of state: the rule that says how hard matter pushes back when it is squeezed to several times the density of an atomic nucleus. It decides how big a neutron star of a given mass is, what the maximum mass is, and what happens in the core.

Each kind of measurement pins it down from a different side. Heavy neutron stars rule out "soft" matter that would collapse too easily. NICER's size measurements rule out matter that would make the stars too big or too small. And when two neutron stars spiral together, the way they deform each other's shape in the last moments leaves a trace in the gravitational-wave signal. Future detectors planned for the 2030s, such as Europe's proposed Einstein Telescope, aim to catch many such mergers in detail.

If quarks turn out to roam free at the centre, neutron stars would contain a form of matter that last existed throughout the universe in its first millionth of a second. A dead star twenty kilometres across would then be a window on the beginning of time, which is a fitting end for something first mistaken for a signal from little green men.

The core idea

A neutron star is the collapsed core of a massive star, about 20 km across and more massive than the Sun, held up by neutron degeneracy pressure. Collapse makes it spin fast and concentrates its magnetic field, so many neutron stars act as pulsars, sweeping beams across space like lighthouses.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 20 km wide and roughly 1.2–2 solar masses.
  • Maximum mass: roughly 2 to 2.5 solar masses, not yet known precisely.
  • First pulsar found in 1967; first evidence of gravitational waves from a binary pulsar found in 1974.
The common mistake

Thinking a pulsar switches on and off. It shines steadily in a beam; the pulses are the beam sweeping past Earth as the star rotates.
