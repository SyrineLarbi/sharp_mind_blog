Mars exploration. A dead desert that used to have rivers, and the robots reading its past.

Mars is the planet humans have studied most closely after their own. More than a dozen spacecraft have reached its surface or orbit and worked there, and the result is a story with a twist. The planet is a frozen desert with air too thin to breathe or even to keep water liquid. Yet its rocks show clearly that rivers once flowed and lakes once stood. Something happened to Mars. Finding out what, and whether anything ever lived there, is the reason we keep going back.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of Mars by machines, and one day by people

Mars exploration means sending spacecraft to fly past, orbit, land on and drive across the fourth planet from the Sun. So far, every visitor has been a robot.

Chapter 01

The canals that were not there

In 1877 the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli drew maps of Mars showing long dark lines. He called them canali, Italian for "channels". In English it was often read as "canals", which suggested engineering. The American businessman and astronomer Percival Lowell built an observatory partly to study them, and wrote books describing a dying Martian civilisation moving water from the poles. The idea inspired a century of science fiction.

The lines were mostly an illusion, produced by the eye joining faint spots seen through a blurry telescope. In July 1965 NASA's Mariner 4 flew past Mars and sent back about twenty grainy pictures. They showed craters, like the Moon. The first close look replaced a living world with a dead one, and for a while Mars seemed less interesting. It took later missions to show that the truth was stranger than either picture.

The air pressure line is the most important one. At such low pressure, liquid water on the surface quickly boils away or freezes. Mars has plenty of water, but almost all of it is ice, in the polar caps and under the ground. The planet also has spectacular geology, including Olympus Mons, the tallest volcano known in the solar system at roughly 22 kilometres high, and a canyon system, Valles Marineris, that would stretch across the United States.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Getting there, getting down, and driving with a delay

Every Mars mission must solve three problems: crossing hundreds of millions of kilometres, landing through an atmosphere that is too thin to help much, and working on a planet minutes away by radio.

Chapter 03

The ladder of missions

Exploring a planet happens in steps, each harder than the last. A flyby takes pictures while passing. An orbiter circles the planet for years, mapping it. A lander sits in one place and studies it closely. A rover drives from place to place. A sample return brings rocks back to laboratories on Earth. Humanity has done the first four at Mars. The fifth has never been done.

Missions leave Earth in launch windows that open about every 26 months, when the two planets are lined up for the cheapest path. The trip takes roughly six to nine months. Once there, orbiters such as NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, which can photograph objects the size of a kitchen table, do two jobs. They study the planet, and they relay data from rovers back to Earth.

Chapter 04

Seven minutes of terror

Landing on Mars is notoriously hard. A spacecraft arrives at about 20,000 kilometres per hour and must reach the ground at walking pace. The Martian atmosphere is thick enough to make a heat shield glow, but too thin for a parachute alone to slow a heavy lander enough. Engineers call the descent, which takes about seven minutes, the "seven minutes of terror".

The reason it is terrifying is the radio delay. Depending on where the planets are, a signal takes between about 3 and 22 minutes to travel between Earth and Mars. By the time controllers hear that the landing has started, it is already over, one way or the other. Every step must happen automatically, with nobody able to intervene.

Different missions solved it differently. Pathfinder in 1997 and the twin rovers Spirit and Opportunity in 2004 used airbags and bounced to a stop. The car-sized rovers Curiosity (2012) and Perseverance (2021) were too heavy for that. They used a heat shield, a supersonic parachute and then a sky crane: a rocket-powered platform that hovered and lowered the rover on cables before flying away to crash at a safe distance. Perseverance also used cameras to recognise the terrain and steer clear of hazards on the way down.

Chapter 05

Driving a car from 200 million kilometres away

Nobody can steer a Mars rover with a joystick. With a delay of many minutes each way, a rover driven live would crash. Instead, a team on Earth plans each day's activities, sends them up in the morning Martian time, and the rover carries them out on its own. Modern rovers can also navigate short distances by themselves, picking a safe route over rocks.

Power is the other constraint. Spirit and Opportunity ran on solar panels. They were designed to last 90 sols. Spirit lasted about six years. Opportunity kept going for more than fourteen, driving about 45 kilometres, until a planet-wide dust storm in 2018 blocked its sunlight. Curiosity and Perseverance instead carry a nuclear battery that turns heat from decaying plutonium into electricity, so they are not at the mercy of dust or winter.

In April 2021 Perseverance's small companion, the Ingenuity helicopter, made the first powered, controlled flight on another planet. It was planned for five test flights and made 72 before damage to its blades ended the mission in January 2024. Its success means future missions can include aerial scouts.

Chapter 06

Why so many missions failed

Mars has a reputation for destroying spacecraft. Roughly half of all missions sent there have failed in some way, especially in the early decades. The Soviet Union's Mars 3 made the first soft landing in 1971 and stopped transmitting within seconds. In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter because one team's software used imperial units and another expected metric ones, so the spacecraft flew too low into the atmosphere. Britain's Beagle 2 lander went silent in 2003. Images taken in 2015 showed it had landed but failed to unfold fully. Europe's test lander Schiaparelli crashed in 2016 after a computer misread its altitude.

The lesson is not that Mars is cursed. Most failures were small human errors in very complex systems that could not be tested in real Martian conditions. Success rates have risen sharply as experience accumulated, and newer entrants have done well. India reached Mars orbit on its first attempt in 2014, the United Arab Emirates' Hope orbiter arrived in 2021, and China landed its Zhurong rover the same year, on its first landing attempt.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A second planet to compare, and the best chance of finding past life

Mars matters because it began life looking somewhat like early Earth and then went a different way. That makes it the best natural experiment we have on how planets change.

Chapter 07

Follow the water

For about two decades, NASA's Mars strategy had a slogan: follow the water. Life as we know it needs liquid water, so the first question was whether Mars ever had it. The answer is now a firm yes.

From orbit

Orbiters found dried river valleys, ancient deltas where rivers once entered lakes, and clay minerals that form only in water. Radar showed huge amounts of ice under the surface in many regions.

On the ground

Opportunity found small round grains of a mineral called hematite, nicknamed "blueberries", that formed in water. Curiosity drove through layered rock that was once the bed of a lake in Gale Crater, a lake that probably lasted for long periods. Perseverance landed in Jezero Crater, on the remains of an ancient river delta.

Billions of years ago, at least at times, Mars had lakes and rivers and probably a thicker atmosphere. For part of its early history, parts of Mars may have been habitable, meaning they had the conditions life as we know it would need. Habitable does not mean inhabited. That is a separate question.

Chapter 08

The search for life, and the claims that did not hold

The search has a history of excitement followed by caution. In 1976 the two Viking landers ran experiments designed to detect living microbes in the soil. One gave results that looked positive. Most scientists concluded the reactions were chemical, and in 2008 the Phoenix lander found perchlorates, reactive salts that could explain them. In 1996 NASA scientists reported possible fossil microbes inside a Martian meteorite found in Antarctica, called ALH84001. Most researchers now think the features formed without life.

Curiosity has detected organic molecules, carbon-based chemicals that life uses but that also form without life. It has also measured small amounts of methane that seem to rise and fall with the seasons. On Earth most methane comes from living things, but it can also come from rock chemistry. Europe's orbiter designed to measure it from above found far less than expected. The methane question is contested.

The most intriguing find so far came from Perseverance. In 2024 it drilled a rock, nicknamed Cheyava Falls, with small spots whose chemistry, on Earth, is often linked to microbes. In 2025 NASA described it as a potential biosignature, a feature that could have been made by life. Non-biological processes have not been ruled out, and the rock would need to be studied in laboratories on Earth to go further.

Chapter 09

How a planet loses its air

Why did Mars change? Part of the answer is size. A small planet cools faster inside. Mars lost its global magnetic field early, probably around four billion years ago. Without it, the solar wind, a constant stream of charged particles from the Sun, could strip gas from the upper atmosphere. NASA's MAVEN orbiter, at Mars since 2014, measured this loss happening today and concluded that it removed much of the early atmosphere over billions of years. Lower gravity also makes it easier for gas to escape.

This matters beyond Mars. It shows that a planet can start with water and lose it. Habitability is not permanent. Comparing Mars, Venus and Earth, three rocky planets that went three different ways, is one of the main ways scientists judge which planets around other stars might stay habitable (see Exoplanets).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Robots, people, and the risk of contamination

The arguments about Mars are about who should go, what it costs, and how to avoid ruining the very thing we are looking for.

Chapter 11

Don't bring germs, don't bring back germs

An international scientific body, COSPAR, sets rules called planetary protection. They have two sides. Forward contamination means carrying Earth microbes to Mars. If a rover's tiny passengers survived and spread, we might one day "discover" life on Mars that we brought ourselves. So landers are cleaned in special rooms, and some regions where liquid water might exist are treated with extra care.

Backward contamination is the reverse: bringing Martian material to Earth. The risk is thought to be very low, but the rule is to handle returned samples as if they might be hazardous until shown otherwise, in sealed laboratories. The tension is obvious. The exploration that would answer the life question best, sending people, is also the exploration most likely to spoil the answer.

Chapter 13

What a human mission would really take

Because of the 26-month windows, a crewed trip would likely last about two and a half to three years: around six to nine months each way and over a year waiting on the surface for the planets to line up again. No human has spent more than about 14 months in space in one go.

The obstacles are not only rockets. Radiation: measurements made inside Curiosity during its cruise suggest the journey there and back alone would give a crew a dose around the whole-career limit NASA adopted in 2022, before any time on the surface. Health: bone and muscle loss, eye problems and isolation over years. Soil: Martian dust is fine and contains toxic perchlorates. Supplies: carrying everything is too heavy, so crews would need to make oxygen, water and rocket fuel on site, an approach called in-situ resource use. An experiment on Perseverance, MOXIE, showed it could make oxygen from the Martian air, in small amounts.

"Terraforming" Mars, warming it to make an Earth-like world, appears in many plans. A 2018 NASA-funded study concluded that Mars does not have enough accessible carbon dioxide to thicken its atmosphere much with any foreseeable technology. A human visit is a hard engineering problem; a second Earth is not on the table.

The core idea

Mars is a cold, thin-aired desert that once had rivers and lakes. Robots climbed the ladder from flyby to orbiter to lander to rover, and proved the planet was habitable in the past. Whether it was ever inhabited is open, and returning samples is the next step.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Gravity about 38% of Earth's. Air pressure under 1%.
  • Radio delay: about 3 to 22 minutes one way.
  • Launch windows every 26 months. Trip of 6–9 months.
  • Roughly half of all Mars missions have failed in some way.
The common mistake

Hearing "water found on Mars" or "habitable" and taking it to mean "life found". Habitable means the conditions were right, not that anything lived there.

The words, in plain words
Sol
A Martian day, 24 hours and 37 minutes.
Orbiter
A spacecraft that circles a planet, mapping it and relaying signals.
Rover
A robot vehicle that drives across a planet's surface.
Sky crane
A hovering rocket platform that lowers a heavy rover on cables.
Radio delay
The minutes it takes a signal to cross between Earth and Mars.
Nuclear battery
A device that turns heat from decaying plutonium into electricity.
Habitable
Having the conditions life as we know it would need. Not the same as inhabited.
Perchlorate
A reactive, toxic salt common in Martian soil.
Biosignature
A feature that could be evidence of past or present life.
Solar wind
The stream of charged particles flowing out from the Sun.
Planetary protection
Rules to avoid carrying life between Earth and other worlds.
In-situ resource use
Making supplies such as oxygen or fuel from local materials.
Sample return
A mission that brings material from another world back to Earth.
