Sharp Mind · Astronomy & Space · Topic 05 of 20

Galaxies. Islands of stars, held together by something we cannot see.

A hundred years ago, many astronomers believed our galaxy was the entire universe. Today we know it is one of hundreds of billions. Galaxies are where stars are born, where the elements are recycled, and where planets like ours can form. They come in a few basic shapes, they collide and merge, most have a giant black hole at their centre, and almost all of them are wrapped in a halo of dark matter that outweighs their stars many times over.

Question 1 · What is it?

Cities of stars in a mostly empty universe

A galaxy is a huge system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter, bound together by its own gravity. Proving that galaxies exist outside our own took a stubborn argument and a careful woman counting stars on glass plates.

Chapter 01

The night the universe got bigger

For centuries, astronomers saw faint, fuzzy patches in the sky that they called nebulae, "clouds". Some had a spiral shape. Were they small clouds of gas inside our galaxy, or whole separate galaxies, unimaginably far away? In 1920 two American astronomers, Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis, argued the question in public, in what became known as the Great Debate. Nobody could settle it, because nobody could measure the distances.

The tool came from Henrietta Swan Leavitt, who worked at Harvard measuring stars on photographic plates. In 1912 she published a discovery about a type of pulsing star called a Cepheid variable. The slower a Cepheid pulses, the more light it truly gives out. So if you time its pulse, you know its real brightness. Compare that with how bright it looks, and you can work out how far away it is, the way you could judge the distance of a light bulb if you knew its wattage.

In 1923, using the largest telescope of the time, on Mount Wilson in California, Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid in the Andromeda "nebula". Its pulse showed it was hundreds of thousands of light-years away, far beyond the edge of our galaxy. Modern measurements put Andromeda about 2.5 million light-years away. Andromeda was not a cloud in our galaxy. It was another galaxy. Overnight, the known universe went from one galaxy to countless.

Chapter 02

What a galaxy contains

A large galaxy like ours has four main ingredients.

Stars

From a few thousand in the faintest dwarf galaxies to hundreds of billions in ours, and more in the giants. Stars are where most of a galaxy's light comes from.

Gas and dust

The raw material for new stars, mostly hydrogen. In spiral galaxies it forms a thin disc, laced with dark dust lanes.

A central black hole

Most large galaxies have a supermassive black hole at their centre, from millions to billions of times the Sun's mass.

Dark matter

An invisible halo that extends far beyond the stars and, in a galaxy like ours, outweighs all the stars and gas several times over.

The distances inside a galaxy are vast and the distances between galaxies are vaster still, but the ratio is different from the ratio between stars. Stars in our neighbourhood are separated by tens of millions of times their own width. Galaxies are separated by only about ten to a hundred times their own width. Stars almost never collide. Galaxies collide all the time. That difference shapes everything in Question 2.

How many galaxies are there? The honest answer is hundreds of billions in the observable universe, and possibly more. Estimates have ranged up to about two trillion, depending on how many faint dwarf galaxies are counted. In July 2022 the first deep image from the James Webb Space Telescope showed thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky about the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length.

Chapter 03

Hubble drew the types as a "tuning fork" and wondered whether galaxies evolved along it. They do not, at least not in the way he guessed. But the classification captured a real split. Most galaxies are either blue, gassy discs making new stars, or red, gas-poor balls that have largely stopped. Understanding why that split exists is one of the main goals of the subject.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Halos, arms, mergers and monsters

Four processes explain most of what galaxies do: dark matter holds them, spiral arms organise their star birth, collisions reshape them, and their central black holes can switch their star formation off.

Chapter 04

Held together by the invisible

In the planets of our solar system, the outer ones move more slowly than the inner ones, because most of the mass is concentrated in the Sun. Astronomers expected the same in spiral galaxies: stars at the edge should orbit more slowly than stars near the crowded centre.

In the 1970s the American astronomer Vera Rubin and her colleague Kent Ford measured how fast stars and gas orbit in spiral galaxies. The outer parts were moving just as fast as the inner parts. At those speeds the visible matter could not hold them, and the galaxies should have flown apart. Radio astronomers measuring hydrogen gas far beyond the visible discs found the same.

The accepted explanation is that each galaxy sits inside a much larger, roughly spherical halo of dark matter, matter that has gravity but gives off no light. Combined with other evidence, this suggests dark matter makes up about 85 per cent of all matter in the universe. A galaxy's stars are like the lights of a city seen at night: the bright part of something much larger. What dark matter actually is remains unknown, and some physicists argue gravity itself should be modified instead. The case is set out in Physics · Dark matter.

Chapter 05

Spiral arms are traffic jams

If a spiral galaxy's arms were fixed bands of stars, the inner parts would wind them up tightly within a few rotations, because the inner stars complete their orbits faster. Galaxies have rotated dozens of times, yet their arms are not wound tight. So the arms cannot be made of the same stars all the time.

The leading explanation, the density wave theory, treats arms as patterns, like a traffic jam on a motorway. Cars slow down as they enter the jam and speed up as they leave it. The jam stays roughly in place while individual cars pass through. In a galaxy, stars and gas crowd together as they pass through an arm. The crowding squeezes gas clouds, and squeezed clouds collapse into new stars.

Massive young stars are very bright and blue but live only a few million years. They die before they have travelled far from the arm where they were born. That is why spiral arms glow blue: they are lit up by short-lived newborn stars. Not every spiral fits this neat picture. Some arms seem to be temporary, constantly forming and breaking, and astronomers still argue about which mechanism dominates in which galaxies.

Chapter 06

Galaxies grow by swallowing each other

Because galaxies are close together compared with their size, gravity pulls neighbours into each other. In the standard picture of cosmic history, small galaxies formed first and big ones were built by repeated mergers, together with a steady inflow of fresh gas. Astronomers call this hierarchical growth: big from small.

When two galaxies collide, their stars almost never hit each other. The gaps between stars are too large. Instead, gravity scrambles the stars' orbits. Long tails of stars are flung out, and gas clouds slam together, often triggering bursts of star formation. A merger of two similar spirals can destroy both discs and leave a smooth elliptical galaxy. That is a leading explanation for why the largest galaxies are ellipticals.

Smaller mergers happen too. Large galaxies regularly swallow dwarfs, tearing them into streams of stars that slowly spread around the host. Our own galaxy is doing this now, as The Milky Way describes.

Chapter 07

The black hole at the heart

At the centre of almost every large galaxy sits a supermassive black hole. Most of the time it is quiet. But when gas falls towards it, the gas swirls into a disc and heats to enormous temperatures before it disappears. That disc can outshine all the stars in the galaxy. Such a feeding black hole is called an active galactic nucleus. The brightest kind, first identified in the 1960s as star-like points of light at huge distances, are called quasars.

A striking finding of the late 1990s and 2000s was that the mass of a galaxy's central black hole is closely linked to the mass of the galaxy's central bulge of stars, even though the black hole is tiny in comparison. The black hole and its galaxy seem to grow together. The evidence that these black holes exist, including the images of two of them, is in Physics · Black holes.

Chapter 08

Why galaxies stop making stars

A galaxy makes stars as long as it has cold gas. Astronomers call the shutdown of star formation quenching, and the result is a "red and dead" galaxy, full of old, cool stars.

Several things can do it. A feeding black hole can blow out jets and winds that heat or push away the gas. Researchers call this feedback, and most galaxy simulations need it to avoid producing far more large galaxies than are observed. A galaxy falling into a crowded cluster can have its gas stripped away by the hot gas of the cluster, like a scarf pulled off by the wind. And a galaxy can simply use up its gas faster than it receives new supplies. Which mechanism dominates in which galaxies is an active research question.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The building blocks of the universe, and our own origin

Galaxies trace the largest structures that exist, let us look back to the early universe, and are the factories that made the atoms we are built from.

Chapter 09

Galaxies map the cosmic web

Galaxies are not spread evenly. They gather in groups of a few dozen, such as our own Local Group, and in clusters of hundreds or thousands, such as the Virgo Cluster about 50 million light-years away. Groups and clusters, in turn, sit along vast filaments and sheets surrounding enormous, nearly empty regions called voids.

Maps of millions of galaxies show this pattern clearly. It is called the cosmic web, and it looks rather like the structure of a sponge or of foam. The pattern is not random. Computer simulations that start from the tiny ripples seen in the leftover glow of the Big Bang, and add gravity and dark matter, produce webs that look statistically very like the real thing. The map of galaxies is one of the strongest tests of our whole picture of the universe. The Big Bang and Cosmic evolution topics follow this story.

Chapter 10

Looking at baby pictures

Light takes time to travel, so distant galaxies are seen as they were when the light left them (Looking out is looking back). The most distant galaxies are seen as they were when the universe was young. Astronomers can line up galaxies by distance and watch, statistically, how galaxies grew up.

The results contain surprises. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021, has found galaxies shining when the universe was less than 300 million years old, about 2 per cent of its current age. Several are brighter, or appear more mature, than many models expected so early. Webb also found a puzzling population of compact red objects, nicknamed "little red dots", whose nature is still debated. Whether these findings require changes to the standard theory or only to how stars form in the early universe is an open question as of 2026.

Chapter 11

Factories of the elements

The universe began with almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. Every heavier element was made in stars and spread by stellar winds, supernovae and neutron-star collisions. But for that material to be used again, it needs to be caught. A galaxy's gravity holds on to the enriched gas, mixes it into new clouds, and makes new stars and planets from it.

A galaxy is therefore a recycling plant. Each generation of stars is richer in heavy elements than the last. Rocky planets need those elements: silicon, oxygen, iron, magnesium. So do living things, which need carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus. Without galaxies to trap and recycle the products of dying stars, there would be no rocky planets and no chemists to study them. This links to the stellar side of the story in Supernovae and to the planetary side in Planet formation.

The biggest limitation is time. A galaxy changes over hundreds of millions of years, and we see a single frame. It is like trying to understand how humans age from one photograph of a crowded street: possible, with enough faces and care, but full of traps. Much of the debate about galaxy evolution comes down to which faces in the crowd are related to which.

Chapter 14

The far future of galaxies

Over the very long term, galaxies are running down. The rate at which the universe forms new stars peaked roughly ten billion years ago and has been falling ever since, as gas is used up. Galaxies will keep merging within their groups. Our Milky Way and Andromeda are approaching each other, and a merger in the next several billion years is possible. Recent analyses suggest it is less certain than once thought, as The Milky Way explains.

Meanwhile the expansion of the universe, which is speeding up because of dark energy (Physics · Dark energy), carries the groups apart. In the extremely distant future, perhaps hundreds of billions of years from now, astronomers in a merged local galaxy might see no other galaxies at all. Every other galaxy would have receded beyond view, and they would have no direct evidence that the universe was ever more than one galaxy. We live at a time when the rest of the universe is still visible, which is worth appreciating.

The core idea

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter, usually with a supermassive black hole at its centre. Galaxies grow by gas inflow and mergers, form stars in their discs and spiral arms, and can be shut down (quenched) by their black holes or their surroundings.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe, possibly more.
  • Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, the nearest large galaxy.
  • Dark matter is about 85 per cent of all matter.
The common mistake

Imagining galaxy collisions as stars smashing together. Stars almost never touch; it is gravity that reshapes the galaxies and gas clouds that collide.

The words, in plain words
Galaxy
A huge system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by its own gravity.
Cepheid variable
A pulsing star whose pulse rate reveals its true brightness, and so its distance.
Light-year
The distance light travels in a year, about 9.5 trillion km.
Spiral galaxy
A flat, rotating disc galaxy with curving arms where new stars form.
Elliptical galaxy
A smooth, rounded galaxy of mostly old stars, with little gas.
Dark matter halo
The large, invisible cloud of dark matter that surrounds a galaxy.
Rotation curve
A graph of how fast material orbits at different distances from a galaxy's centre.
Density wave
A pattern of crowding that moves through a disc, forming spiral arms.
Merger
The collision and combination of two galaxies into one.
Quasar
An extremely bright galaxy centre powered by gas falling into a supermassive black hole.
Quenching
The shutdown of star formation in a galaxy.
Cosmic web
The large-scale pattern of filaments, clusters and voids that galaxies trace.
