The Sun. An ordinary star that happens to be eight light-minutes away.

The Sun is the only star we can see as a disc instead of a point. Everything we know about the billions of other stars was first worked out, and then checked, on this one. It is a ball of hot gas held together by its own weight and kept from collapsing by the energy of nuclear fusion in its core. It is also a magnetic machine that throws storms across the solar system, and in a world run on satellites and power grids, those storms now matter to everyone.

Question 1 · What is it?

A ball of gas that has been shining for 4.6 billion years

For most of history, nobody could say what the Sun was made of or why it kept burning. Both answers arrived only in the last 160 years, and the second one needed a new branch of physics.

Chapter 01

The fire that was too old

In the nineteenth century, physicists tried to calculate how long the Sun could shine. If it were a giant lump of burning coal, it would run out in a few thousand years. The British physicist Lord Kelvin proposed a better source: the Sun is slowly shrinking under its own weight, and the squeeze releases heat. Even that gave it only a few tens of millions of years.

Geologists and biologists were unhappy. Rock layers and the slow pace of evolution, as Charles Darwin argued, seemed to need hundreds of millions of years at least. Physics and geology flatly disagreed about the age of the Earth, and for decades physics seemed to have the stronger case.

The geologists turned out to be right. In 1920 the English astronomer Arthur Eddington suggested that the Sun gets its energy by fusing hydrogen into helium. In 1939 the physicist Hans Bethe worked out the detailed nuclear reactions. Fusion releases millions of times more energy per kilogram than any chemical fire, which is enough to keep the Sun shining for about ten billion years. Kelvin had not made an arithmetic mistake. He simply could not know about the atomic nucleus, which had not yet been discovered.

The Sun is made of plasma, gas so hot that electrons have been stripped from their atoms. A plasma conducts electricity, so it can carry electric currents and is pushed around by magnetic fields. That one fact explains most of the Sun's dramatic behaviour.

Chapter 03

An ordinary star, seen up close

Astronomers sort stars by temperature and colour. The Sun is a G-type star: a yellow-white star of middling temperature. It is in the long, stable middle phase of its life, called the main sequence, when a star quietly fuses hydrogen in its core.

The Sun is not small, though. Most stars in our galaxy are red dwarfs, dim and cool, with a fraction of the Sun's mass. The Sun is brighter and heavier than most of them. It is also alone. Many stars of its size have a companion star, but the Sun does not.

Why does a "typical" star matter so much? Because the Sun is the calibration point for all of astronomy. When astronomers measure a distant star's mass or brightness, they usually express it in solar units. When they model how a star works inside, they check the model on the Sun first, because it is the only star whose surface, interior vibrations and particle output we can measure in detail.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From the core to the solar wind

Energy is made in a small central core, takes a very long time to work its way out, and leaves as light. Along the way, the Sun's churning gas builds magnetic fields that tie everything else together.

Chapter 04

Fusion: four become one, and a little goes missing

At the core, the weight of the whole Sun presses the gas to more than a hundred times the density of water. Hydrogen nuclei, which are single protons, normally repel each other because they all carry positive charge. At 15 million °C they move fast enough that some get close enough to stick. Through a chain of steps, four hydrogen nuclei end up as one helium nucleus.

The helium weighs about 0.7 per cent less than the four protons that made it. That missing mass becomes energy, following Einstein's E = mc². The c² (the speed of light, squared) is a huge number, so a tiny loss of mass releases enormous energy. Every second, the Sun turns roughly 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium and converts about four million tonnes of matter into pure energy.

That sounds like a frantic rate, but the Sun is so large that it has used only a small part of its fuel in 4.6 billion years. It also explains why fusion is so hard to copy on Earth: the Sun relies on its own crushing gravity and enormous volume, which no machine can reproduce (Fusion, the honest version of the joke).

Chapter 05

A very slow escape

The energy leaves the core as high-energy light, but it does not fly straight out. The gas is so dense that light is absorbed and re-emitted again and again, each time in a random direction, like a person trying to leave a packed crowd. Estimates of how long this takes range from tens of thousands to more than a hundred thousand years.

In the outer third, the gas starts to boil. Hot blobs rise, cool at the top and sink again. Telescopes see this as a grainy pattern on the surface, each grain about the size of a country, appearing and vanishing in minutes. Then the light escapes and reaches Earth in about eight minutes. The sunlight on your skin was made in the core long before humans built cities, and spent only its last eight minutes crossing the gap to you.

Scientists can also see the interior directly, in a sense. The Sun rings like a bell, with countless sound waves bouncing through it. Measuring the surface rising and falling, a technique called helioseismology, lets researchers map the temperature and rotation deep inside, the way earthquake waves reveal Earth's interior.

Chapter 06

The ghost particles that checked the theory

Fusion also releases neutrinos, tiny particles that barely interact with anything. Trillions pass through your body every second without effect. Unlike light, they leave the core immediately, so they are a direct live signal from the Sun's furnace.

From the late 1960s the American chemist Ray Davis caught a few of them each month with a huge tank of cleaning fluid in a South Dakota gold mine. He found only about a third of the predicted number. For three decades this solar neutrino problem was a serious embarrassment. Either the model of the Sun was wrong, or the physics of neutrinos was.

It was the neutrinos. Experiments in Japan and Canada around 1998–2001 showed that neutrinos change between three types as they travel, and Davis's detector could sense only one type. Count all three and the total matches the solar model. A puzzle about the Sun ended up rewriting particle physics, and it produced Nobel Prizes in 2002 and 2015.

Chapter 07

Spots, cycles and flares: the magnetic Sun

The Sun does not rotate as a solid ball. Its equator turns once in about 25 days, its poles in about 35. That uneven spin, combined with boiling plasma, stretches and twists the Sun's magnetic field. This churning, self-sustaining generator of magnetism is called the solar dynamo.

Where twisted bundles of magnetic field burst through the surface, they block some of the heat rising from below. Those patches are roughly 1,500 to 2,000 °C cooler than their surroundings, so they look dark. These are sunspots. A large one can be several times wider than Earth.

In 1843 the German amateur astronomer Heinrich Schwabe, after years of counting spots, noticed that their number rises and falls on a cycle. It is now known to average about eleven years. At each peak, the solar maximum, the Sun's overall magnetic field flips its north and south poles. Between about 1645 and 1715, during a period called the Maunder Minimum, sunspots almost vanished. That shows the cycle is not fully regular.

When tangled magnetic fields above sunspots snap and reconnect, they release stored energy in seconds. A solar flare is a sudden flash of light and X-rays. A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a giant cloud of magnetised plasma, billions of tonnes of it, flung into space. Flares reach Earth at the speed of light. CMEs travel more slowly and usually take one to three days.

Chapter 08

The corona and the wind it blows

During a total solar eclipse, when the Moon covers the bright disc, a pale glow of streamers appears around it. That is the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere. It is the strangest part of the Sun, because it is at one to three million degrees while the surface below is only about 5,500 °C. It is as if the air got hotter the farther you moved from a fire.

The corona is so hot that it cannot be held in by gravity. It streams away in every direction as the solar wind, a flow of charged particles moving at roughly 300 to 800 kilometres per second. The wind carries the Sun's magnetic field with it and fills the heliosphere, the bubble described in The Solar System.

Why the corona is so hot remains one of the big open questions in solar physics. The two leading ideas both involve magnetism. One says magnetic waves carry energy up from the churning surface and release it high above. The other, proposed by the American physicist Eugene Parker, says countless tiny flares, called nanoflares, heat it in small bursts. Most researchers suspect both play a role.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Life, climate, technology, and the key to every other star

The Sun powers nearly everything alive. Its storms can now cost billions. And it is the reference against which every other star in the sky is read.

Chapter 09

The power behind almost everything

At the top of Earth's atmosphere, every square metre facing the Sun receives about 1,360 watts, roughly the output of a small electric heater. That sunlight drives the weather, evaporates water for rain, and feeds plants through photosynthesis. Coal, oil and gas are sunlight stored by ancient plants and plankton. Wind is sunlight too, since it is driven by uneven heating. With small exceptions such as geothermal and nuclear power, almost all the energy people use began as sunlight.

Because the Sun drives climate, people often ask whether it explains today's global warming. The measurements say no. Satellites have tracked the Sun's output since the late 1970s. It rises and falls by only about 0.1 per cent over each cycle and has shown no upward trend in that time, while Earth has warmed clearly. Scientific assessments, such as those of the UN's climate panel, conclude that the Sun's contribution to recent warming is negligible. Volume 30 sets out how the warming is measured (What is happening, and how it is known).

Safety

Never look directly at the Sun, even briefly and even when it is partly eclipsed. Ordinary sunglasses are not enough. Use certified eclipse glasses or project the image through a pinhole. The retina can be permanently damaged without pain.

Chapter 10

When the Sun's weather reaches Earth

On 1 September 1859 the English astronomer Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots when two brilliant patches of white light flared up and faded within minutes. He had seen the first recorded solar flare. About seventeen hours later a huge CME hit Earth. Auroras, the northern and southern lights, were seen as far south as the Caribbean. Telegraph lines threw sparks, and some operators found they could send messages with their batteries disconnected.

The Carrington Event did little harm in 1859, because there was little to harm. The modern world is more exposed. A CME's magnetic field shakes Earth's own field and drives electric currents through long conductors such as power lines and pipelines.

March 1989, Quebec

A geomagnetic storm overloaded the Hydro-Québec grid, which collapsed within about 90 seconds. Around six million people lost power for roughly nine hours.

February 2022, orbit

A modest storm warmed and swelled Earth's upper atmosphere. The extra drag pulled down about 38 newly launched Starlink satellites before they could climb to safety.

May 2024, everywhere

The strongest storm in about two decades produced auroras seen across much of Europe and the United States. GPS-guided farm tractors lost accuracy during planting season.

This field is called space weather, and several countries now run forecasting centres for it, much as they do for storms. Spacecraft parked about 1.5 million kilometres towards the Sun measure the solar wind before it arrives, giving tens of minutes of firm warning. Grid operators, airlines and satellite operators use the forecasts. Several governments, including the UK's, list severe space weather on their national risk registers.

Chapter 11

The Rosetta Stone for the stars

In 1868, astronomers studying the Sun's light during an eclipse saw a bright yellow line in its spectrum, the band of colours that light splits into. No known element produced it. The English astronomer Norman Lockyer concluded it came from a new element and named it after the Greek word for the Sun, helios. Helium was found in the Sun 27 years before it was found on Earth. Volume 29 explains how those colour lines work as fingerprints (Light carries a signature).

The same method, refined on the Sun, is now applied to every star. And the Sun sets the scale for the most important question in modern astronomy, whether other planets could host life. "Earth-like" planets are judged by how much starlight they receive compared with what Earth gets from the Sun.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A steady star, with a temper

As stars go, the Sun is a good one to live beside. But "steady over billions of years" still allows violent days, and our knowledge of it has real gaps.

One honest limit deserves its own line. Nobody can yet forecast a solar cycle with confidence years ahead, or a big flare more than hours ahead. The dynamo is understood in outline, but its details are complex enough that cycle predictions have repeatedly missed. The cycle that began in 2019 was forecast to be weak, and turned out stronger than expected, peaking around 2024–2025.

Chapter 14

The Sun's own future

On the scale of human history, the Sun will not change. On the scale of geology, it will. It is about 30 per cent brighter now than when it formed, and it will keep brightening. In about five billion years the hydrogen in its core will run low. The core will contract and heat up, the outer layers will swell to perhaps a hundred or more times their current size, and the Sun will become a red giant.

After that it will puff its outer layers into space as a glowing shell of gas, which astronomers confusingly call a planetary nebula. What remains will be a white dwarf: a core roughly the size of Earth, so dense that a teaspoon would weigh tonnes, slowly cooling over many billions of years. The Sun is not massive enough to explode as a supernova or to become a neutron star. Those fates belong to heavier stars, covered in Supernovae and Neutron stars.

The shorter-term future is more practical. As more of daily life depends on satellites, navigation and long power lines, solar storms are turning from a curiosity into an infrastructure risk. Studying the Sun has become, among other things, a form of insurance.

The core idea

The Sun is a ball of plasma held together by gravity and powered by fusion of hydrogen into helium in its core. Its uneven, boiling rotation builds a magnetic dynamo that drives sunspots, an eleven-year cycle, flares and the solar wind, which together make up space weather.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 4.6 billion years old, roughly halfway through its stable life.
  • Core about 15 million °C; surface about 5,500 °C; corona 1–3 million °C.
  • Converts about 4 million tonnes of matter to energy every second.
The common mistake

Thinking the Sun is "burning" like a fire, or that it is a small, insignificant star. It is nuclear fusion, not combustion, and the Sun is brighter than most stars in the galaxy.

The words, in plain words
Plasma
Gas so hot that atoms lose their electrons. It conducts electricity and follows magnetic fields.
Fusion
Light atomic nuclei joining to form heavier ones, releasing energy from a tiny loss of mass.
Main sequence
The long, stable phase of a star's life, when it fuses hydrogen in its core.
Photosphere
The Sun's visible "surface", the layer where light finally escapes.
Convection
Heat carried by moving gas or liquid: hot rises, cool sinks.
Corona
The Sun's thin, extremely hot outer atmosphere, visible during a total eclipse.
Solar wind
The stream of charged particles flowing out from the corona in all directions.
Sunspot
A cooler, darker patch where strong magnetic fields break through the surface.
Solar cycle
The roughly eleven-year rise and fall in sunspots and solar activity.
Solar flare
A sudden burst of light and X-rays from released magnetic energy.
Coronal mass ejection
A huge cloud of magnetised plasma thrown off the Sun into space.
Neutrino
A tiny, nearly massless particle that passes through almost anything.
Space weather
Conditions in space, driven by the Sun, that affect satellites, grids and radio.
