Planet formation. How dust around a newborn star becomes worlds, in a few million years.

Every planet, including the one under your feet, began as dust: grains smaller than a particle of smoke, drifting in a flat disc of gas around a young star. Somehow, those grains became rocks, the rocks became mountain-sized bodies, and those collided and grew into planets. Astronomers agree on the broad story. Several of the steps in the middle are still genuinely puzzling, and the planets found around other stars have forced the story to be rewritten more than once.

Question 1 · What is it?

The building of worlds from leftovers

Planet formation is what happens to the material a new star does not swallow. It is fast by cosmic standards, messy, and now something astronomers can actually watch.

Chapter 01

The picture that surprised everyone

In 2014 a new radio telescope array in Chile's Atacama Desert, called ALMA, released an image of a young star named HL Tauri, about 450 light-years away. The star is thought to be only around a million years old, an infant compared with the Sun's 4.6 billion. Around it was a flat orange disc marked with sharp, dark, concentric rings, like a target or the grooves of a record.

Astronomers had expected a smooth disc. The most natural explanation for the gaps is that young planets are already clearing paths through the material, sweeping up dust along their orbits. If so, planets start forming much earlier than many models had assumed. Later ALMA surveys found that rings and gaps are common in such discs. The picture changed planet formation from a subject studied mainly with equations and meteorites into one studied with photographs.

Chapter 02

An eighteenth-century idea that survived

The basic idea is old. In 1755 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant suggested that the solar system formed from a rotating cloud of material. Around 1796 the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace developed a similar proposal. This became known as the nebular hypothesis, from nebula, Latin for cloud or mist.

The hypothesis fell in and out of favour for two centuries, mainly because nobody could explain the details. Its broad outline is now accepted. A star and its planets form together from one collapsing cloud, and the planets are built in the disc around the star. That disc is called a protoplanetary disc: "proto" means early, so it is the disc that comes before the planets.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Six steps, from smoke to planets

The process climbs an enormous ladder of sizes, from a thousandth of a millimetre to tens of thousands of kilometres. Each rung works differently, and one of them nearly broke the theory.

Chapter 04

From cloud to disc

Stars are born in cold, dark clouds of gas and dust, mostly hydrogen. When a dense patch of such a cloud begins to collapse under its own gravity, it is always turning slightly. As it shrinks, it spins faster, for the same reason a figure skater spins faster by pulling in her arms. Physicists call this the conservation of angular momentum: a spinning thing keeps its total "amount of spin" unless something takes it away.

That spin stops most of the material from falling straight into the centre. It flattens into a disc, in the same way a spinning ball of pizza dough flattens. Most of the mass still ends up in the central star. A small fraction, perhaps around a hundredth of the star's mass in a typical case, remains in the disc. The planets are built from that small leftover. About 99 per cent of it is gas. Only about 1 per cent is dust, and the dust is what rocky planets are made from.

Where did the dust come from? Every element heavier than hydrogen and helium was made in earlier generations of stars and spread by their deaths, as Supernovae explains. Planets are recycled stars.

Chapter 05

Dust to pebbles: easy at first

The first step is gentle. Dust grains drift through the gas and bump into each other at walking speed or slower. Like dust bunnies under a bed, they stick together through weak electrical forces between their surfaces. Fluffy clumps grow to the size of sand, then to pebbles a few centimetres across.

The disc also has a temperature gradient. Close to the star it is hot. Farther out, beyond the frost line, water vapour freezes into ice. Ice is sticky and plentiful, so beyond the frost line there is several times more solid material to build with. This is the main reason the giant planets formed in the outer solar system, as The Solar System describes.

Chapter 06

The metre barrier, and how nature gets past it

The next step, from pebbles and boulders to bodies kilometres wide, is where the theory ran into trouble. Two problems appear at roughly the size of a boulder a metre across.

First, bigger objects collide faster, and at those speeds they tend to shatter or bounce rather than stick. Second, the gas in the disc orbits slightly slower than solid objects would on their own, because it is partly supported by its own pressure. So boulders feel a constant headwind. The drag robs them of speed and makes them spiral inward towards the star. Calculations suggested that a metre-sized body could fall into the star in a few hundred years or less, much faster than it could grow. This became known as the metre-size barrier.

The most popular solution today is called the streaming instability, proposed in the mid-2000s. Pebbles drifting through the gas tend to bunch up, a little like cyclists drafting behind each other in a race. Where they bunch, they feel less headwind, so more pebbles catch up and the clump grows denser. Eventually a clump becomes dense enough for its own gravity to pull it together into a single body, perhaps a hundred kilometres across, skipping the dangerous in-between sizes altogether.

In January 2019 NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past Arrokoth, a small, ancient object in the Kuiper belt. It turned out to be two rounded lobes stuck together so gently that there is no sign of a violent crash. Many researchers see it as support for gentle gravitational collapse. These bodies, from a few kilometres to a few hundred across, are called planetesimals, the seeds of planets.

Chapter 07

Giant planets: a race against the vanishing gas

Once there are planetesimals, gravity takes over. The biggest ones pull in neighbours and grow fastest, a runaway effect where the rich get richer. Within perhaps a million years or so, a region can contain a few dominant planetary embryos, bodies roughly the size of the Moon or Mars. Many researchers now think embryos also grow quickly by sweeping up pebbles still drifting through the gas, a process called pebble accretion.

The leading explanation for giant planets is core accretion. An embryo beyond the frost line grows into a solid core of roughly ten Earth masses. At that point its gravity is strong enough to start pulling in the surrounding hydrogen and helium. The more gas it gathers, the stronger its pull, until it runs away and builds a planet like Jupiter, hundreds of Earth masses, mostly gas.

But there is a deadline. Observations of young star clusters show that most discs lose their gas within a few million years, blown away by the young star's radiation. A giant planet must be finished before the gas is gone. Evidence from meteorites suggests Jupiter's core formed very early, perhaps within the first million or two years. A rival idea, disc instability, proposes that some giants form directly and very fast when part of a heavy disc collapses under its own gravity. It may explain some giant planets found very far from their stars, but most researchers see it as the less common route.

Chapter 08

Rocky planets: the age of giant impacts

In the inner solar system there was less solid material and no time to grab gas. Rocky planets grew more slowly and more violently. After the gas cleared, dozens of embryos crossed each other's orbits and smashed together over tens of millions of years. Each collision could melt a planet's surface into an ocean of magma.

The most famous of these collisions made our Moon. According to the giant-impact hypothesis, a body roughly the size of Mars, often called Theia, struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago. Debris thrown into orbit gathered into the Moon. The idea explains why the Moon has a small iron core and why Moon rocks brought back by Apollo are so similar to Earth's mantle. The details are still debated, especially why Earth and Moon rocks are so similar, which some versions of the impact struggle to reproduce.

Chapter 09

Planets that wander

For a long time, astronomers assumed planets stayed where they formed. Then, in 1995, the first planet found around a Sun-like star turned out to be a giant orbiting in four days, far too close to its star to have formed there. It must have moved.

It is now accepted that planets migrate. A young planet's gravity creates ripples in the gas disc, and the ripples push or pull back on the planet, slowly changing its orbit. After the gas is gone, planets can still shift by scattering planetesimals or by pulling on each other.

Our own giants probably moved too. In the Nice model, named after the French city where it was developed in the mid-2000s, the giant planets started closer together and later spread out, with Uranus and Neptune pushed outward through a disc of icy bodies. A related idea, the Grand Tack, proposes that Jupiter first drifted inward to roughly where Mars is now, then reversed outward with Saturn, like a sailing boat tacking. That would explain why Mars is so small: Jupiter cleared away much of the material it would have grown from. Both models are influential, and both remain debated.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Water, life, and the variety of worlds

How a planet forms decides what it is made of, where it ends up, and whether it has water. Those are the first questions to ask about any planet that might host life, including our own.

Chapter 10

Where did Earth's water come from?

Earth formed inside the frost line, where it was too hot for ice. So where did the oceans come from? This is one of the central open questions of planetary science, and the answer is genuinely contested.

The main tool is a chemical fingerprint. Water contains hydrogen, and a small share of hydrogen is a heavier form called deuterium. The ratio of deuterium to ordinary hydrogen differs between sources. Comets were long the favourite delivery service, but when Europe's Rosetta mission measured the comet 67P in 2014, its water had a ratio about three times Earth's ocean value. A certain class of water-rich asteroids, the parent bodies of carbonaceous chondrite meteorites, matches Earth much better. That makes asteroids the leading candidate today.

Other researchers argue that some of Earth's water was already in the rocks that built it, or was made when hydrogen gas from the early disc reacted with magma. The likely answer is a mix of sources. The share from each is still argued over.

Chapter 11

Why exoplanets look the way they do

Thousands of planets are now known around other stars, and formation theory has to explain all of them. The commonest kinds found so far are planets between Earth and Neptune in size, often orbiting very close to their stars. Our solar system has none of these. Migration, which can carry planets inward through the gas disc, is the usual explanation, but the details are under active debate.

Planet formation also explains who wins and who loses. Stars richer in heavy elements are more likely to have giant planets, which fits core accretion: more dust means faster-growing cores. And some planets are thrown out entirely. Gravitational encounters can eject a planet from its system to wander alone through the galaxy, a rogue planet. Surveys suggest such planets exist in large numbers, though estimates of how many vary widely. The Exoplanets topic covers the discoveries themselves.

Chapter 12

Meteorites: the solar system's own fossils

Most asteroids never became part of a planet. Fragments of them fall to Earth as meteorites, and some have barely changed since the solar system formed. They contain small white grains called CAIs (calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions), the oldest known solids, dated to about 4.567 billion years. That date is effectively the birthday of the solar system, and it is measured to within about a million years.

Meteorites also contain tiny melted droplets called chondrules, whose origin is still not fully understood, and traces of radioactive elements that decayed quickly. Those short-lived elements suggest the young solar system was seeded with fresh material from a nearby dying star, though how and when are debated.

Sample-return missions have made the fossils cleaner. Japan's Hayabusa2 brought material from the asteroid Ryugu in 2020, and NASA's OSIRIS-REx brought material from Bennu in 2023, both untouched by Earth's air. Analyses published in 2025 reported that the Bennu samples contain many of the amino acids used by life and all five of the bases used in DNA and RNA. That does not mean asteroids carried life. It shows that the raw ingredients of biology formed naturally in the early solar system and were delivered to young planets.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A theory that works in outline and wobbles in the middle

Read as strengths and weaknesses of what scientists currently understand: the big picture is solid, the middle steps are partly patched, and the history of the field is a lesson in overconfidence.

The deepest weakness is historical. For two centuries, the theory was designed to produce one system, ours, and it confidently predicted that other systems would look similar. They do not. The lesson the field took from this is to treat any single example, however familiar, as one possible outcome rather than a template.

Chapter 15

How common are Earths?

The question everyone eventually asks is whether planets like Earth, rocky, temperate and wet, form often. Formation theory gives mixed signals. Rocky planets seem to form easily: small planets are abundant around other stars. But the conditions that made Earth particular, its water supply, its large Moon and its giant neighbours, depend on chance events such as which collisions happened and where Jupiter wandered.

Answering it will take both better theory and better counting. Future space telescopes designed to study Earth-like planets around Sun-like stars, such as NASA's proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory, are aimed at exactly this. Until then, the fair summary is that planet formation is common, rocky planets are common, and whether true Earth twins are common is still open.

The core idea

A collapsing cloud spins into a star and a flat disc. In the disc, dust sticks into pebbles, pebbles clump into planetesimals, and those grow into embryos. Beyond the frost line, cores grab gas and become giants before the gas vanishes. Closer in, giant impacts build rocky planets. Planets can then migrate far from where they were born.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The solar system's oldest solids are about 4.567 billion years old.
  • Gas discs typically last only a few million years.
  • A giant planet's core needs roughly ten Earth masses to start pulling in gas.
The common mistake

Assuming planets form where they are now. Planets move, sometimes dramatically, and the arrangement we see today is the end of a long rearrangement.
