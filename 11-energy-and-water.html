Sharp Mind 11 · how the world works

Energy and water. Eight things people talk about, explained simply.

This file is written in short sentences and easy words. Every new word is explained before it is used. The goal is not to make you an engineer. The goal is that when someone says "desalination" or "green hydrogen" at a dinner or in a meeting, you know what it is, you know why it is in the news, and you have one good sentence to say.

Before you start

How to use this file

Every technology here has the same eight parts. Always the same. So it is easy to follow, and easy to skip the parts you do not need.

The partWhat it gives you
1 · In one sentenceThe short definition. Learn this first. If you learn nothing else, learn this.
2 · Think of it like…A simple thing from normal life that works the same way.
3 · How it worksThe steps, in order. No maths.
4 · The numbersThree or four real figures. One exact number is worth five adjectives.
5 · What goes wrongThe problems and the arguments. This is the interesting part, and it is the part most people do not know.
6 · Who controls itWhich countries and companies. This is where the politics is.
7 · Where you meet itWhere this already touches your life.
8 · Say thisTwo or three short lines you can say out loud, without stopping.
The most important instruction in this file

The green boxes are short on purpose. Say them out loud once. Not in your head — out loud. A sentence you have never said is not available to you when someone is looking at you.

This is the whole argument of file 06: reading is input, saying is stock. Ten seconds per technology. If you skip it, you will have read this file and kept almost nothing, which happens to nearly everyone who reads nearly everything.

A note on the numbers

Figures move. Costs fall, capacity grows, and anything here that is a price or a total will be a little out of date within a year or two. That is fine — the shape is the useful part. "About three to four kilowatt-hours per tonne of water, down from about ten in the 1990s" stays true in shape long after the exact number moves. Where a number is doing real work in an argument, the source is named.

Part I · Water

Making seawater drinkable

One technology, and it is the one you asked about. It is also the clearest example of a rule that runs through this whole file: most "technology" stories are really energy stories.

How it works 01

Desalination

In one sentence

Desalination means taking the salt out of seawater so people can drink it.

Think of it like…

A coffee filter — but for salt.

You push water through a filter with holes so small that water gets through and salt does not.

There is one problem, and the whole industry is built around it. Salt water does not want to go that way. Nature pulls fresh water toward salt, not away from it. That pull is called osmosis. So to go the other way you have to push. Hard.

That pushing is the cost. Everything else is plumbing.

How it works · seawater reverse osmosis, about 70% of the world's plants
① TAKE IN SEAWATER
   Screens keep out fish, seaweed and sand.

② PRE-TREATMENT — filter out the small stuff
   Sand, tiny organisms, oil. Skip this and the membranes
   clog. Clogged membranes are the number one cause of a
   plant running badly, and the number one reason a cheap
   plant turns out expensive.

③ THE HIGH-PRESSURE PUMP
   Push the water to about 55-80 bar. That is roughly 60
   to 80 times the pressure in a car tyre.
   ⇒ THIS PUMP IS WHERE ALMOST ALL THE ELECTRICITY GOES.

④ THE MEMBRANE
   A plastic sheet rolled up inside a tube. The holes are
   far smaller than a salt particle with its water coat.
   Water passes. Salt does not.
   About 40-45% of what you pushed in comes out fresh.
   That share is called the RECOVERY RATE.

⑤ ⭐ THE ENERGY RECOVERY DEVICE — the invention that
   changed everything
   The 55% that did not pass through leaves the membrane
   STILL AT HIGH PRESSURE. In old plants that pressure was
   simply thrown away.
   An energy recovery device catches it and uses it to
   help push the next batch of incoming seawater.
   ⇒ this one idea cut the energy from over 10 units to
     about 3 units for the same water. Not an
     improvement — a different industry.

⑥ POST-TREATMENT
   The water is now TOO pure. It would slowly eat the
   pipes, and it tastes flat. So minerals are added back.

⑦ THE BRINE GOES BACK TO THE SEA
   What is left is about twice as salty as the sea it
   came from. Where it goes is the real argument (below).
The numberWhat it means
~3.2 kWh per cubic metreA modern plant with energy recovery. A cubic metre is 1,000 litres — a tonne of drinking water. Roughly what a kettle uses in three hours.
~10 kWh in the 1990sThe same water used about three times more electricity thirty years ago. This fall is the whole story of the industry.
~1 kWh is the floorPhysics says you cannot go below roughly this. So we are at about three times the theoretical minimum — which means there is real room left, but not enormous room.
~91.5 million m³ per dayWorld installed capacity in 2024, with about 105 million contracted, across more than 16,000 plants in 177 countries. Capacity grew about 40% between 2020 and 2025. (IDRA / Global Water Intelligence)
What goes wrong
  • Energy is the whole economics. Desalination is really electricity turned into water. Where power is expensive, the water is expensive. Where power is cheap, it is cheap. That is almost the entire story of which countries do this and which do not.
  • The brine. Half of what you take in leaves twice as salty, often a little warm, and carrying the chemicals used to stop the membranes scaling. Dumped in one spot with no mixing, it sinks and kills life on the seabed near the outfall. Spread through diffusers into moving water, the effect is local and manageable. This is the main environmental fight, and it is a fight about engineering the outfall, not about whether to desalinate.
  • The intake kills sea life. Fish get trapped on the screens; eggs and larvae get pulled through. Taking water from under the seabed instead of from open water largely fixes this and costs more.
  • It does not fix leaks. A network losing a third of its water to broken pipes gains less from a new plant than from repairing the pipes — and the pipes are cheaper. New plants are announced at ribbon-cuttings; repaired pipes are not.

Who controls it

The Gulf states use the most — Saudi Arabia and the UAE run the largest plants in the world. Israel gets a large share of its household water this way. Spain, Australia and California all have big plants.

The membranes themselves come from a small number of makers (DuPont, Toray, Nitto, LG Chem), and the big builders are a short list too — Veolia, Acciona, IDE, Metito. It is a concentrated industry.

Where you meet it

If you have drunk tap water in Dubai, Malta or most of the Gulf, you have drunk seawater.

It is also why, in those places, the water bill follows the electricity price. Water and power stop being separate questions.

Tunisia: the stated goal is to go from about 6% of drinking water from desalination to about 30% by 2030. There is a full report on this already in this project — dessalement/rapport-dessalement-tunisie.html — with the local plants, the solar coupling maths and the regulatory limits.

Say this

"Desalination is really an energy story. Taking the salt out is easy. Paying for the pressure is the hard part."

"Half of what goes in comes back out twice as salty. Where that brine goes is the real argument."

"A country with a coastline and cheap solar has a completely different water problem from one without."

Part II · Making electricity

Three ways to make power

Sun, wind and splitting atoms. Each one has exactly one hard problem, and knowing which problem is which is most of understanding the energy news.

How it works 02

Solar panels

In one sentence

A solar panel turns sunlight straight into electricity, with nothing moving.

Think of it like…

A leaf. A leaf catches light and turns it into something it can use. A panel does the same job, but what comes out is electric current instead of sugar.

The important difference from every other power station: nothing spins. No fuel, no steam, no turbine, no moving parts. That is why a panel can sit on a roof for twenty-five years and need almost nothing done to it.

How it works
① A PANEL IS MOSTLY SILICON
   Which is sand, purified to an extreme degree. That
   purifying is the expensive, energy-hungry part of
   making one.

② THE SILICON IS IN TWO LAYERS, TREATED DIFFERENTLY
   One layer has a few extra loose electrons. The other
   is slightly short of them. Where the two meet, this
   creates a permanent one-way electric push.

③ LIGHT KNOCKS AN ELECTRON LOOSE
   Light arrives in small packets called photons. A photon
   hits the silicon and knocks one electron free.

④ THE ONE-WAY PUSH SENDS IT IN ONE DIRECTION
   Electrons moving in one direction IS electricity.
   Do this with a very large number of photons and you
   have a useful current.

⑤ AN INVERTER CONVERTS IT
   The panel makes direct current (DC). The grid and your
   plugs use alternating current (AC). The inverter
   changes one into the other, and it is usually the first
   thing in the system to fail.
The numberWhat it means
20-23% efficientA normal panel turns about a fifth of the light that hits it into electricity. That sounds poor and does not matter — sunlight is free and the roof is already there.
Cost fell roughly 90%In the decade to about 2020. One of the fastest cost falls of any technology in history. This single fact explains most of what has happened in energy since.
~1,650 kWh per kWp per yearWhat a kilowatt of panels actually produces in a year in Tunisia. Northern Europe gets roughly half that. This ratio is why the same panel is a good investment in one country and a poor one in another.
~1.3-1.5 hectares per MWGround-mounted land use. Useful when someone says "we'll just build solar" — it tells you how much land they are talking about.
What goes wrong
  • The sun sets. This is not a detail, it is the entire problem. A solar plant with no storage covers the middle of the day and nothing else.
  • The grid needs supply to match demand second by second. Electricity is almost the only product that must be made at the exact moment it is used. Solar does not care what time you need it.
  • The duck curve. Where there is a lot of solar, midday power becomes nearly worthless and evening power becomes expensive — because everyone's panels produce at the same moment and stop at the same moment. Plotted over a day, the shape looks like a duck. You will hear this word.
  • Land and grid connection are usually the real bottleneck, not panels. Panels are cheap and available. Permission and a cable to connect to are not.
  • Old panels. Recycling them at scale is not really solved yet. It is a small problem now and a large one in about fifteen years.

Who controls it

China. Not partly — overwhelmingly. A very large majority of the world's polysilicon, wafers, cells and finished panels are made there, and the share has grown rather than shrunk.

That concentration is itself a political story: Europe and the US have both tried to build domestic manufacturing and have found it very hard to compete on cost.

Where you meet it

It is why electricity in the middle of a sunny day is getting cheaper, and why some countries now have hours of negative power prices — moments when it is worth paying someone to take the electricity away.

Say this

"Solar is now the cheapest electricity ever built, in sunny places. The problem was never the panel. The problem is the night."

"Almost all of it is made in one country. That is a supply chain question as much as an energy one."

How it works 03

Wind turbines

In one sentence

A wind turbine lets moving air spin a shaft, and the spinning shaft makes electricity.

Think of it like…

A fan running backwards.

A fan uses electricity to move air. A turbine lets moving air make electricity. Same machine, opposite direction.

One surprise: the blades do not get pushed by the wind like a sail. They are shaped like aircraft wings, and they get pulled — the same lift that holds up a plane. That is why the blade tips can move much faster than the wind itself.

How it works
① THE BLADES ARE WINGS
   Air moving over the curved side has to travel further,
   which creates lift — a pull, not a push. The blade is
   pulled around.

② THE SLOW SHAFT TURNS
   Only about 10-20 turns a minute. Slower than it looks.

③ A GEARBOX SPEEDS IT UP
   The generator needs fast rotation. The gearbox raises
   the speed about a hundredfold — and it is the part
   most likely to break. Some modern turbines skip the
   gearbox entirely (direct drive) to avoid exactly this.

④ THE GENERATOR MAKES ELECTRICITY
   A magnet spinning inside coils of wire. Same principle
   as a bicycle dynamo, much larger.

⑤ ⭐ POWER GOES WITH THE CUBE OF WIND SPEED
   This is the single most important fact about wind.
   DOUBLE the wind speed → EIGHT TIMES the power.
   ⇒ so a site with slightly better wind is not slightly
     better. It is dramatically better. Where you put a
     turbine matters far more than which turbine you buy.

⑥ IT TURNS ITSELF TO FACE THE WIND, AND SHUTS DOWN IN STORMS
   Above roughly 25 metres per second the blades are
   feathered and it stops. Too much wind is as useless
   as none.
The numberWhat it means
3-6 MW onshore, 8-15 MW offshoreOne modern turbine. Offshore machines are far larger because there is nobody to object to the size and the wind is better.
Blades over 100 metresOn the largest offshore machines, one blade is longer than a football pitch. Moving them by road is its own industry.
Capacity factor 25-40% onshoreHow much it actually produces over a year against its maximum. Offshore reaches 40-55%. A number below 100% here is normal, not a failure — the wind is not always blowing.
What goes wrong
  • The wind is not on your schedule. Same problem as solar, different shape — wind often blows at night, which is useful, and sometimes does not blow for a week, which is not.
  • Offshore got expensive again. For years costs fell. Then interest rates rose and steel and cable prices rose, and several large projects were cancelled or renegotiated. Offshore wind is very capital-heavy, so it is unusually sensitive to the cost of borrowing. This is the current news story, and it is a finance story, not a technology story.
  • Grid connection queues. In several countries the wait to connect a finished project to the grid is now longer than the wait to build it.
  • Blades are hard to recycle. They are glued composite, made not to come apart.
  • People do not want to look at them, and that is a real constraint, not a frivolous one.

Who controls it

Vestas (Denmark), Siemens Gamesa (Germany/Spain) and GE have been the traditional big three. Chinese makers — Goldwind, Envision, Mingyang — now build a large share of the world's turbines and have started to win projects outside China.

Where you meet it

On a windy night in a country with a lot of wind, wholesale electricity prices can go to zero or below. If you have a tariff that follows the market, that is why.

Say this

"Power goes with the cube of the wind speed. Double the wind, eight times the power. That is why where you put it matters more than what you buy."

"Offshore wind's problem right now is not the technology. It is interest rates."

How it works 04

Nuclear power, and SMRs

In one sentence

A nuclear plant boils water using the heat from splitting atoms, and the steam spins a turbine.

Think of it like…

A kettle. A very expensive, very heavily guarded kettle.

This sounds like a joke and it is the most useful thing to know about nuclear power. Everything after the heat — the steam, the turbine, the generator — is completely ordinary power station equipment, the same as in a coal or gas plant. The only unusual part is the heat source.

Nuclear plants are not complicated because making the electricity is complicated. They are complicated because of what happens if the heat source misbehaves.

How it works
① THE FUEL IS URANIUM
   Natural uranium is mostly a type that will not split.
   ENRICHMENT increases the share of the type that will
   (U-235) from under 1% to about 3-5% for a power plant.
   ⚠ the same machines, run longer, produce weapons-grade
     material. THIS IS WHY ENRICHMENT IS A POLITICAL
     SUBJECT and not a technical one.

② A NEUTRON SPLITS AN ATOM
   A neutron is a particle inside the nucleus. Fire one at
   a U-235 atom and the atom splits in two. This releases
   heat AND two or three more neutrons.

③ THOSE NEUTRONS SPLIT MORE ATOMS — the chain reaction
   Left alone it grows. So it is not left alone.

④ CONTROL RODS KEEP IT STEADY
   Rods of a material that soaks up neutrons slide in and
   out. In = fewer splits. Out = more. This is the
   throttle, and it is the whole safety story in one
   sentence.
   A MODERATOR — usually ordinary water — slows the
   neutrons down, because slow neutrons split atoms more
   reliably than fast ones.

⑤ THE HEAT BOILS WATER · STEAM SPINS A TURBINE
   From here it is a normal power station.

⑥ THE USED FUEL STAYS HOT AND RADIOACTIVE
   It sits in a pool of water for years to cool, then goes
   into sealed dry casks of steel and concrete.

⭐ WHAT AN SMR IS
   SMR = Small Modular Reactor. Smaller (roughly 50-300 MW
   instead of 1,000-1,600), built in a factory, shipped in
   pieces, assembled on site.
   THE BET: that building the same thing a hundred times in
   a factory is cheaper than building one enormous
   bespoke thing on a muddy field.
   ⚠ it is a reasonable bet. It is also, so far, mostly a
     bet — very few are operating.
The numberWhat it means
Capacity factor above 90%The highest of any power source. A nuclear plant runs nearly all the time, which is exactly what solar and wind cannot do.
Very low CO2 per unitCounting mining, building and decommissioning, lifetime emissions are in the same low range as wind. This is not seriously disputed.
10+ years, and over budgetRecent Western megaprojects — Flamanville in France, Hinkley Point C in Britain, Vogtle in the US — all ran many years late and billions over. This is the fact that decides whether nuclear grows.
What goes wrong
  • Cost and schedule. This is the problem. Not safety, not physics — project management. The same reactor design has been built in South Korea and China for a fraction of the Western cost and time. The difference is building the same thing repeatedly with a team that stays together, versus building one every twenty years with a workforce that has to be reassembled.
  • The waste is technically manageable and politically stuck. The volume is small and the engineering is understood. Finding a country willing to host the final store is the hard part. Finland's Onkalo is the first deep repository in the world to actually be built.
  • Chernobyl and Fukushima. Whatever the statistics say about deaths per unit of energy — and they are favourable to nuclear — a serious accident makes an area uninhabitable for a long time, and people weight that differently from a spread-out harm. That is not irrational.
  • SMRs have almost no track record. Treat cost claims for something not yet built at scale as a forecast, not a fact.

Who controls it

Russia's Rosatom and China build most of the world's new reactors and export them aggressively, often with financing attached. Enrichment capacity is concentrated in few hands, and Russia holds a large share of it — which became a live problem for Europe and the US after 2022.

Where you meet it

France gets most of its electricity this way, which is why French power is unusually low-carbon and why a bad year for French reactor maintenance raises electricity prices across Europe.

Say this

"Nuclear's problem in the West is not physics. It is project management. The same design costs several times more here than in Korea."

"An SMR is a bet that factory-building beats one-off megaprojects. It is a sensible bet with almost no track record yet."

Part III · Storing and moving it

The night problem

Solar stops at sunset and wind stops when it stops. Everything in this part exists because of that one sentence.

How it works 05

Grid batteries

In one sentence

A grid battery stores electricity when there is too much and gives it back when there is too little.

Think of it like…

A water tank on a roof.

The pump fills it when water is available. You draw on it when you need it. The tank does not make any water — it only moves water in time, from a moment when there was plenty to a moment when there is not.

A battery does exactly this with electricity. And, like the tank, it holds a certain amount and then it is empty. Remember the size of the tank. It is the whole limitation.

How it works
① IT IS LITHIUM-ION, INCREASINGLY "LFP"
   LFP = lithium iron phosphate. Cheaper, safer, contains
   no cobalt, and slightly heavier for the same energy.
   ⇒ weight does not matter when the battery is sitting
     in a field, which is why LFP has taken over grid
     storage faster than it took over cars.

② CHARGING MOVES LITHIUM FROM ONE SIDE TO THE OTHER
   Two electrodes with a liquid between them. Charging
   pushes lithium ions across. Discharging lets them move
   back. That movement IS the current.
   Nothing is burned and nothing is used up — which is why
   it can be done thousands of times.

③ THE PHYSICAL THING IS A ROW OF CONTAINERS
   Cells in racks, plus inverters, plus cooling, plus
   control software. It looks like shipping containers in
   a field, because that is what it is.

④ IT HOLDS HOURS, NOT DAYS
   A typical grid battery stores 1 to 4 hours at full
   output. Remember this number. It decides everything
   below.

⭐ AND HERE IS THE PART ALMOST NOBODY KNOWS
   The main money is NOT "storing solar for the evening".
   It is:
     FREQUENCY RESPONSE · the grid must stay at exactly
       50 or 60 cycles per second. When a big generator
       trips, something must respond in under a second.
       A battery responds in milliseconds. A steam
       turbine cannot. Batteries are simply the fastest
       thing on the grid, and that speed is worth money.
     ARBITRAGE · charge when power is cheap, sell when
       it is expensive.
     AVOIDING NEW CABLES · a battery near a bottleneck
       can be cheaper than upgrading the line.
The numberWhat it means
Prices fell around 90%Battery pack prices, from about 2010 to the early 2020s. The same fall that made electric cars possible made grid storage possible as a side effect.
85-90% round tripPut 100 units in, get 85-90 back. Good — compare hydrogen below, which returns about a third.
2-4 hoursNormal storage duration. Not days. Not a week.
What goes wrong
  • Duration is the whole limitation. Lithium is excellent for hours, poor for days, and useless for seasons. A still, cloudy week in winter is not a battery problem — it is a completely different problem, and it is the honest weak point of a renewables-heavy grid. Anyone who tells you batteries solve it is describing a battery a hundred times bigger than any that exists.
  • Fire. A damaged or badly made lithium cell can go into thermal runaway — it heats itself, faster and faster, and is very hard to put out. Real, manageable, and much less likely with LFP.
  • The supply chain. Lithium is not actually rare. Refining it is concentrated, and heavily in China. The bottleneck is processing, not geology — which is true of almost every "critical mineral" story you will read.
  • It wears out. Every charge cycle costs a little capacity.
Say this

"A battery does not make energy. It moves energy in time — and only by a few hours."

"The real money in grid batteries is not storing solar. It is being the fastest thing on the grid."

"Lithium is not rare. Refining it is concentrated. That is a different problem with a different fix."

How it works 06

Green hydrogen

In one sentence

Green hydrogen is hydrogen gas made by using renewable electricity to split water.

Think of it like…

A rechargeable fuel. You put electricity in and you get a gas you can store, move and burn later.

But there is a catch, and it is large: you get back much less than you put in. Think of a bucket you carry across a yard that leaks the whole way. It still works — but only if there is no shorter route.

That is the entire hydrogen argument in one image. Wherever you could simply use the electricity directly, hydrogen is the worse answer. Where you cannot, it may be the only answer.

How it works, and the colours you will hear
① AN ELECTROLYSER SPLITS WATER
   Run electricity through water and it separates into
   hydrogen and oxygen. This is simply burning, run
   backwards.

② THERE ARE THREE MAIN KINDS
   ALKALINE      old, cheap, likes steady power
   PEM           more expensive, copes well with power
                 that goes up and down — which matters
                 if you are running it off solar
   SOLID OXIDE   very efficient, runs hot, still immature

③ THEN YOU HAVE TO MOVE IT, WHICH IS THE HARD PART
   Compress it to high pressure, or chill it to -253°C to
   make it liquid, or convert it into ammonia and move
   that instead. All three cost energy.

④ TO USE IT: burn it, or run it through a fuel cell to
   turn it back into electricity.

⭐ THE COLOURS — this is vocabulary, not chemistry.
  The gas is identical. The colour describes how it was
  made.
    GREY   made from natural gas, CO2 released to the
           air. ⚠ THIS IS ABOUT 95% OF ALL HYDROGEN USED
           TODAY. When someone says "we already use a lot
           of hydrogen", this is what they mean.
    BLUE   made from natural gas, CO2 captured instead
           of released
    GREEN  made by electrolysis using renewable power
The numberWhat it means
~30-40% round tripElectricity → hydrogen → electricity. You lose roughly two thirds. Compare a battery at 85-90%. This single comparison settles most hydrogen arguments.
~50-55 kWh per kilogramElectricity needed to make one kilo of hydrogen today. So the cost of green hydrogen is mostly the cost of electricity, multiplied.
Several times the price of greyGreen hydrogen currently costs a multiple of the gas-made kind. Closing that gap is the whole industry's project.
What goes wrong
  • The losses. For anything you can electrify directly — cars, buses, home heating — hydrogen loses to a battery or a heat pump and will keep losing, because the physics does not improve much.
  • It is the smallest molecule there is. It leaks through seals that hold other gases, and it makes some steels brittle over time. Existing gas pipes cannot simply be filled with it.
  • The hype problem. Hydrogen has been "five years away" for about fifty years. A great many announced projects quietly do not reach a final investment decision. When you read a hydrogen announcement, the question is whether anyone has committed money.
  • But there is a real case, and it is worth knowing: fertiliser, steel and refining already use enormous amounts of hydrogen, made from gas, emitting CO2. Replacing that is a huge, genuine, unglamorous market. Steel made with hydrogen instead of coal is a real thing being built now. The case for hydrogen is industry. It was never cars.
Say this

"Hydrogen is not an energy source. It is a way to carry energy, and you lose most of it on the way."

"It makes sense for steel and fertiliser. It does not make sense for cars — a battery beats it."

"Ninety-five percent of the hydrogen we use today is made from natural gas. The green part is still tiny."

Part IV · The carbon problem

Cleaning up, and heating up

One technology everybody argues about, and one that almost nobody understands but everybody will eventually own.

How it works 07

Carbon capture

In one sentence

Carbon capture means catching CO2 so it does not reach the air, and putting it somewhere it will stay.

Think of it like…

A filter on a chimney — versus a vacuum cleaner for the whole sky.

These are two completely different jobs, and they are constantly given the same name, which is why the subject is so confusing.

At a chimney, CO2 is about 10 to 15 out of every 100 parts of the gas. In open air it is about 4 parts in ten thousand. So pulling it from the air means moving hundreds of times more air for the same result. Same chemistry. Wildly different bill.

Three different things with one name
① POINT SOURCE CAPTURE (often written CCS)
   Catch it at the chimney of a power station, cement
   works or steel plant. The concentrated one. Doable.

② DIRECT AIR CAPTURE (DAC)
   Catch it from open air. Works anywhere, costs far more,
   currently exists at very small scale.

③ ⚠ ENHANCED OIL RECOVERY
   Pump CO2 into an old oil field to push more oil out.
   THIS IS HISTORICALLY THE MAJORITY OF ALL "CARBON
   CAPTURE" EVER DONE, and it produces more oil.
   ⇒ when you read a capture figure, the first question
     is which of these three it is. Very often nobody
     says.

HOW THE STANDARD METHOD WORKS — amine scrubbing
  ① the exhaust gas passes through a liquid (an amine)
    that chemically grabs CO2 and lets everything else
    through
  ② heat that liquid. It releases the CO2, now pure and
    concentrated. The liquid is reused
    ⇒ ⚠ THAT HEATING IS THE ENERGY PENALTY. You are
      burning more fuel in order to capture
  ③ compress the CO2 until it behaves almost like a
    liquid
  ④ pipe it and inject it a kilometre or more underground
    into porous rock with a solid sealing layer above it,
    which is the same geology that held oil and gas in
    place for millions of years
The numberWhat it means
15-30% energy penaltyThe share of a plant's output consumed by capturing its own CO2. You need a bigger plant to deliver the same power.
Chimney capture is far cheaper than air capturePoint-source costs are typically in the tens of dollars per tonne upward; direct air capture today is several hundred. The gap is the concentration difference, and it will not close entirely.
Global capture is tinyTotal capacity is in the low tens of millions of tonnes a year, against roughly 37 billion tonnes of CO2 emitted annually. It is about one part in a thousand. This is the number that most changes how people talk about it.
What goes wrong
  • It costs energy to save energy's emissions. That is not fatal but it is inescapable.
  • The track record is poor. Several flagship projects captured far less than promised or shut down. This matters when judging new announcements.
  • The moral hazard argument. Critics say the promise of future capture is used to justify emitting now. This is a fair criticism of how it is sometimes used politically, and it is not an argument about the chemistry.
  • But here is the honest case for it. In cement, the CO2 does not mainly come from the fuel — it comes out of the limestone itself as a chemical consequence of making cement. You cannot avoid it by switching to clean power, because it is not about the power. For cement, and to a degree steel, capture may be the only route there is. If you want one defensible thing to say about carbon capture, say that.
Say this

"Catching CO2 at a chimney is chemistry. Catching it from open air is chemistry plus an enormous fan bill."

"Most of the CO2 ever captured has been used to push more oil out of the ground."

"For cement it might be the only option, because the CO2 comes out of the rock, not the fuel."

How it works 08

Heat pumps

In one sentence

A heat pump moves heat from outside into your house, instead of making heat by burning something.

Think of it like…

A fridge running backwards. You already own one.

A fridge takes heat out of the food compartment and dumps it into the room — go and feel the warm grille at the back. It does not make cold. It moves heat.

A heat pump does the same thing to your house: it takes heat from the outside air and dumps it inside. The outside gets slightly colder. You get warm.

And yes — cold air still has heat in it. "Cold" only means less heat than you would like. There is heat in air all the way down to -273°C, which is where there genuinely is none left.

How it works
① A LIQUID THAT BOILS AT A VERY LOW TEMPERATURE
   flows through a coil outside. It is called a
   refrigerant. It can boil at temperatures well below
   freezing.

② IT ABSORBS HEAT FROM THE OUTSIDE AIR AND BOILS
   Even at 0°C outside, there is enough heat to boil this
   liquid into gas.

③ A COMPRESSOR SQUEEZES THE GAS
   Squeezing a gas makes it hot. This is not magic — it is
   why a bicycle pump gets warm while you use it. Now the
   gas is hotter than your house.

④ INSIDE, IT GIVES UP ITS HEAT
   The hot gas passes through a coil indoors, warms your
   radiators or your air, and condenses back to liquid.

⑤ IT EXPANDS, GETS COLD AGAIN, AND GOES ROUND
   Forever, or until something breaks.

⭐ WHY IT BEATS BURNING — the one fact to keep
  You are not MAKING heat. You are MOVING it. So the
  electricity is only paying for the pump, not for the
  heat itself.
  ⇒ THAT IS WHY YOU CAN GET 3 OR 4 UNITS OF HEAT FOR
    1 UNIT OF ELECTRICITY.
  ⇒ a gas boiler can never beat 1 unit of heat per unit
    of fuel. Ever. It is making the heat.
  This ratio has a name you will hear: COP, the
  coefficient of performance. A COP of 3.5 means 3.5
  units of heat per unit of electricity.
  ⚠ AND NO, "350% EFFICIENT" DOES NOT BREAK PHYSICS.
    Nothing is being created. It is being carried.
The numberWhat it means
COP of 3-4 in mild weatherThree to four units of heat per unit of electricity. This is the number the whole case rests on.
Falling toward 2 or below in hard coldThe colder outside, the less heat there is to move and the harder the compressor works. So performance drops exactly when you most need heat.
The gas-to-electricity price ratioWhether it saves you money is almost entirely this. If electricity costs four times what gas costs per unit, a COP of 3.5 barely breaks even. If it costs three times, you save. This is a policy and tax question, not an engineering one.
What goes wrong
  • It prefers to run warm-ish and steady, not hot and short. A gas boiler sends water round at 60-70°C in bursts. A heat pump is most efficient at 35-45°C, running gently for longer. Fitted to a house designed around a hot boiler, with small radiators, it will either underperform or run at a poor COP.
  • Which is why most complaints are about bad installations, not bad technology. The heat pump is usually fine; the radiators were never resized and nobody did the heat-loss calculation.
  • It costs a lot upfront, and the saving depends on the price ratio above.
  • The refrigerant itself is often a strong greenhouse gas if it leaks, which is why newer systems are moving to propane and CO2 as refrigerants.
Say this

"A heat pump is a fridge running backwards. It does not make heat, it moves it — that is why it can be three hundred percent efficient without breaking physics."

"Whether it saves you money depends almost entirely on the ratio between the electricity price and the gas price."

"Most heat pump complaints are bad installations, not bad heat pumps."

The words, in plain words

These are the terms you will hear. None of them is complicated once someone tells you what it means, and nobody ever does.

When they say…They mean
kWhA unit of energy. One kilowatt running for one hour. A kettle uses roughly one over an hour.
MW / GWA rate, not an amount. Megawatt = how fast, kilowatt-hour = how much. A tap versus a bucket.
Capacity factorWhat a plant actually produced over a year, divided by what it could have produced running flat out. Solar ~15-25%, wind ~25-50%, nuclear ~90%.
BaseloadPower that runs all the time. Traditionally coal, gas and nuclear.
IntermittentComes and goes on its own schedule. Solar and wind.
DispatchableYou can turn it on when you choose. The thing intermittent sources are not.
The duck curveThe daily shape of demand in a place with lots of solar — a dip at midday, a steep climb at sunset. Looks like a duck.
CurtailmentSwitching off a wind or solar farm that could be producing, because there is nowhere to put the power. Wasting free electricity, and increasingly common.
LCOELevelised cost of energy. Total lifetime cost divided by total lifetime output. Useful for comparison, and it hides the fact that midday solar and 6pm solar are not worth the same.
BrineThe very salty water left over from desalination.
ElectrolyserThe machine that splits water into hydrogen and oxygen.
COPHeat pumps. Units of heat out per unit of electricity in.
Grid inertiaBig spinning generators resist sudden change simply because they are heavy, which steadies the grid. Solar and batteries have no spinning mass, so this has to be replaced with fast electronics. A real and underdiscussed engineering problem.

Your ten sentences

If you learn nothing else, learn these
1"Desalination is really an energy story. Taking the salt out is easy — paying for the pressure is the hard part."

5"Offshore wind's problem right now isn't technology. It's interest rates."
6"Nuclear's problem in the West isn't physics. It's project management."
7"A battery doesn't make energy. It moves energy in time — and only by a few hours."

Pick three of those and say them out loud, once, now. Not in your head.
