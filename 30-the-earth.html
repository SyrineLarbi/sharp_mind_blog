This volume explains how the planet is built and how it behaves — including, plainly and without argument, what is happening to its climate and how that is known.

Part I

Solid ground that isn't

Chapter 01

The idea everyone laughed at

Look at a map of the Atlantic and the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together. Anyone can see it, and people noticed for centuries.

In 1912 Alfred Wegener assembled the case properly: matching coastlines, matching rock formations on both sides, matching fossils of animals that plainly could not swim an ocean, and evidence of ancient ice sheets in places now tropical.

He was dismissed, sometimes contemptuously, for a reasonable-sounding reason: he could not explain what would move a continent. Without a mechanism, a pattern is only a coincidence.

The mechanism arrived in the 1960s from an unexpected direction — mapping the sea floor. There is a mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic, and rock is emerging along it. Better still, the rock either side is arranged in matching magnetic stripes, mirrored on both sides, recording reversals of Earth's magnetic field over time. The sea floor was spreading, symmetrically, and had been for a very long time. Wegener was right and had been dead for thirty years.

Chapter 02

Why rock flows

The Earth's surface is a set of rigid plates floating on a hotter layer beneath, which is solid rock that behaves like something extremely thick and slow — it flows, over millions of years, the way glass does not and tar does.

Heat from deep inside drives slow circulation in that layer, and the plates ride along at roughly the speed your fingernails grow. Everything geological happens where they meet.

Where plates pull apart, molten rock rises to fill the gap and makes new crust — the Atlantic ridge, and Iceland, which sits on it and is being torn in two. Where they push together, one is forced under the other and melts, and the result is a line of volcanoes and deep earthquakes — the entire rim of the Pacific. Where two continents collide with neither willing to sink, the crust crumples upward, which is the Himalayas, still rising as India continues to push north.

And where plates slide past each other, they lock, strain accumulates for decades or centuries, and then releases suddenly. That is an earthquake: not something breaking, but something that had been stuck letting go.

Chapter 03

Why you cannot predict an earthquake

We know exactly where they will happen — the maps are excellent. We can say roughly how often a given fault produces a large one. What cannot be done is saying that a particular fault will go next Tuesday, and the reason is fundamental rather than technological.

The strain builds invisibly, deep underground, and the trigger is the failure of rock at a specific point under conditions nobody can observe. Small slips and large ones begin identically; what determines whether it stops after a metre or runs for hundreds of kilometres is decided as it propagates.

So the effort goes into the things that do work: building codes that keep structures standing, warning systems that use the fast harmless wave arriving seconds before the damaging one to stop trains and close valves, and tsunami networks of the kind Volume 23 described being absent in 2004.

It is a useful case of a question being answered by changing it. We cannot predict the event, so the work went into surviving it — and that has saved vastly more lives than prediction would have.

Part II

Air and water

Chapter 04

Weather is one engine

Sunlight arrives at the equator nearly straight on and at the poles at a glancing angle, spread over more surface. So the tropics receive far more heat than the poles.

Everything else follows from the planet trying to even that out. Warm air at the equator rises, travels towards the poles at height, cools, sinks, and returns along the surface. That circulation is the whole engine of the atmosphere, and its structure explains the map: rain belts where air rises and dumps its moisture, and the world's great deserts in the bands where dry air is descending.

The spin of the Earth deflects all this sideways, which is why winds curve rather than run straight north and south, and why storms rotate.

Clouds and rain are the same story in miniature. Warm air holds more moisture than cold air. Lift it — over a mountain, or by heating from below — and it cools, and at some point it cannot hold what it has, and the excess condenses into droplets. That is a cloud. When the droplets grow too heavy to stay up, it rains. Everything about weather is warm air rising, cooling, and letting go of water.

Chapter 05

The ocean is a conveyor belt

The sea is doing the same job as the air — moving heat from the equator towards the poles — more slowly and with far more capacity, because water holds heat enormously better than air.

Surface currents are pushed by prevailing winds. But there is a deeper circulation driven by density. Cold water is denser, and salty water is denser, so where warm salty water arrives in the far north and cools, it becomes dense enough to sink — and that sinking pulls more warm water up behind it. The sunken water creeps along the deep ocean floor for centuries before rising again elsewhere.

This matters far beyond oceanography. That circulation is why north-western Europe is much milder than its latitude deserves — Britain sits as far north as parts of Canada that are frozen for months. The heat is delivered by water.

It also means the ocean is the planet's thermal flywheel: it absorbs the overwhelming majority of extra heat and takes centuries to respond, which is why changes lag their causes and why what has already been set in motion cannot simply be stopped.

Part III

The climate

Chapter 06

Why the planet is habitable at all

Sunlight arrives, warms the ground, and the ground radiates heat back outward. Certain gases in the atmosphere — carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour — let the incoming sunlight through but absorb the outgoing heat and re-radiate some of it back down.

This is not a pollutant effect. It is why Earth is habitable: without it the average surface temperature would be far below freezing and the planet would be a ball of ice. The effect has been understood since the nineteenth century and is not controversial in any scientific sense.

The neighbours make the point. Venus has a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere and a surface hot enough to melt lead — far hotter than its distance from the Sun accounts for. Mars has almost no atmosphere and is frozen. The amount of these gases is the main thing setting a planet's temperature, and we have two worked examples next door.

Chapter 07

What is happening, and how it is known

Burning coal, oil and gas releases carbon that has been locked underground for hundreds of millions of years. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has risen substantially since industrialisation, and continues to rise, and the planet has warmed.

The evidence is not one measurement. Direct atmospheric readings have run continuously since the 1950s. Air bubbles trapped in ice cores give hundreds of thousands of years of history, so we know the present level is far outside the natural range of that whole period. Thermometer records, ocean heat measurements, satellite data, retreating glaciers, rising seas and shifting seasons all point the same way.

And the fingerprinting is what settles the cause. The carbon accumulating in the air has the chemical signature of ancient plant material rather than of volcanoes. The upper atmosphere is cooling while the lower warms, which is what trapped outgoing heat produces and is not what a brighter Sun would produce. Nights are warming faster than days. Each of these distinguishes between candidate explanations, and they converge.

The consequences are not evenly distributed and not linear. Warmer air holds more water, so wet places get wetter and downpours more intense; warmer conditions dry soil faster, so dry places get drier. Seas rise both because ice melts and because water expands as it warms. Reefs bleach. Species shift poleward and upward, and those with nowhere further to go do not.

The part that is genuinely uncertain

It is worth being precise, because vagueness helps nobody. That it is warming and that we are causing it are not in serious scientific dispute. What is genuinely uncertain is the size of some feedbacks, the exact timing of particular thresholds, and regional detail. Those are real uncertainties, and they cut in both directions — they are as likely to mean worse as better.

Chapter 08

Why this is a hard problem to solve

The physics is settled and the difficulty is structural, and it is worth naming because it explains thirty years of slow progress.

The gas mixes globally, so emissions anywhere affect everywhere — which means no country benefits fully from its own restraint, and every country benefits from others acting. That is the hardest shape of problem there is.

The costs are immediate and local; the benefits are distant and diffuse. The worst effects fall mostly on people who emitted least and are least able to adapt. Carbon dioxide persists for a very long time, so the effect is cumulative rather than annual — which makes historical emissions a live question rather than a historical one. And the ocean's lag means a delay between causing and observing, which is exactly the situation human institutions handle worst.

None of that makes it unsolvable, and a good deal has changed — renewable electricity has become the cheapest option in most of the world far faster than anyone predicted. But it explains why the difficulty was never the science.

Reference

What to carry forward

The ground moves

Wegener had the pattern and no mechanism, so he was dismissed for thirty years. The sea floor spreading settled it. Earthquakes are not something breaking but something stuck letting go — which is why we can map where and not say when, and why the work went into surviving them instead.

One engine

Sunlight hits the equator straight on and the poles at a glance, and everything about weather is the planet redistributing that. Warm air rises, cools, and lets go of its water — that is clouds, rain, deserts and storms.

Why Europe is warm

Water carries heat far better than air. Cold salty water sinking in the north pulls warm water up behind it, which is why Britain is mild at the latitude of frozen Canada — and why the ocean's centuries-long lag makes climate changes arrive late and persist.

The climate, plainly

The greenhouse effect is why Earth is habitable — Venus and Mars are the worked examples. That it is warming and that we cause it are settled; the fingerprints (ancient-plant carbon, upper atmosphere cooling while the lower warms, nights warming fastest) rule out the alternatives. The hard part was never the science.
