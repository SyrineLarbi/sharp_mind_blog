Space manufacturing. A factory where nothing sinks, nothing rises, and every kilogram costs a fortune to move.

For fifty years people have predicted factories in orbit, and for fifty years the factories have not arrived. The reason is simple arithmetic. Getting anything into space is expensive, and bringing it back safely is hard. Two things have changed since about 2015. Launch has become much cheaper, and small companies have started flying capsules that make a product in orbit and land it on Earth. The idea is no longer science fiction. Whether it becomes an industry depends on finding products that are worth more per kilogram than the trip costs.

Question 1 · What is it?

Making things where gravity no longer pulls them apart

Space manufacturing means producing materials or objects outside Earth's atmosphere. It covers three quite different ideas, and it helps to keep them apart.

Chapter 01

A capsule full of medicine lands in the desert

In June 2023 a small Californian company called Varda Space Industries put a capsule the size of a large kitchen oven into orbit. Inside was a tiny automated chemistry lab. Its job was to dissolve a drug, let the solution cool, and grow crystals of the drug while the whole spacecraft floated in free fall. The drug was ritonavir, an HIV medicine.

The chemistry worked within weeks. Getting home took far longer: regulators had to agree where and when the capsule could land, and Varda waited months for permission. In February 2024 the capsule finally came down under a parachute on a military test range in Utah. It was widely described as the first time a private company had made a product in orbit on purpose and returned it from a free-flying spacecraft.

Why ritonavir? Because it has a famous history. In 1998, years after the drug went on sale, a new crystal form appeared in the factory. That form dissolved less well in the body, and the original capsules had to be withdrawn and reformulated. The lesson was that the same molecule can stack into different crystals, and the stacking changes how a medicine works. If space lets you control the stacking more precisely, that could be worth paying for. That, in one story, is the whole promise of space manufacturing, and also its challenge.

The first kind is a business question: can a product earn enough on Earth to pay for the round trip? The second and third are logistics questions: is it cheaper to make something in space than to lift it out of Earth's gravity? Engineers call the third kind in-situ resource utilisation, usually shortened to ISRU. "In situ" is Latin for "in place". It means living off the land.

Chapter 03

What is actually different up there

People often say there is "no gravity" in orbit. That is wrong. At the height of the International Space Station, about 400 kilometres up, gravity is still about 90 per cent as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because the station and everything in it are falling together around the Earth, moving sideways so fast that they keep missing it. Inside a falling box, nothing presses on anything else. Scientists call this condition microgravity, because tiny forces remain from air drag and the spacecraft's own movements.

Space also offers a nearly perfect vacuum, strong sunlight for power, and room. It brings problems too: radiation that damages electronics and cells, huge temperature swings, and no easy way to fix a machine that breaks.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Remove the weight, and liquids start to behave differently

Most of the benefit comes from switching off three everyday effects that gravity causes. Then comes the hard part: launching the factory and getting the product home.

Chapter 04

No sinking, no rising, no stirring

On Earth, gravity quietly shapes every liquid process. Heat a pan of soup and the warm liquid at the bottom rises while cooler liquid sinks. That circulation is called convection. Mix sand into water and the sand settles to the bottom. That is sedimentation. And a bubble in a liquid floats up because of buoyancy. All three depend on weight. In free fall, none of them happens in the normal way.

Now think about a crystal growing in a solution. On Earth, the liquid around the crystal is constantly stirred by convection, and the growing crystal tends to sink. Molecules arrive unevenly, and defects build in. In microgravity, molecules reach the crystal mainly by slow, even diffusion, the random drifting of molecules from crowded areas to empty ones. The result can be larger, more regular crystals. Calm conditions give order a chance.

A fourth effect is called containerless processing. On Earth, molten metal or glass must sit in a container whose walls can contaminate it. In orbit, a blob of liquid can float freely, held in place by sound waves or electric fields, which allows unusually pure materials.

Chapter 05

The products people are betting on

Every candidate product shares one feature: it must be worth far more per kilogram than the cost of the trip. That rules out steel, cement and almost everything else. Here are the serious candidates.

Drug crystals

Protein drugs such as antibodies are often hard to crystallise. Better crystals help scientists see the molecule's shape and can make a medicine easier to store or inject. The drug maker Merck has run experiments on the space station with its cancer drug Keytruda and has said they helped it understand how to make more uniform crystals. Usually the space crystal guides the Earth process; it is not the product.

Special optical fibre

A glass called ZBLAN (named after the fluorides of zirconium, barium, lanthanum, aluminium and sodium) could in theory carry light with much less loss than ordinary silica fibre. On Earth, tiny crystals form while it cools and scatter the light. Experiments suggest fewer crystals form in microgravity. Several companies have drawn test fibre on the space station. Whether it can be made in useful lengths at a sensible price is still unproven.

Semiconductor crystals

Computer chips start as large, ultra-pure crystals. Some specialist materials used in power electronics and radio equipment are hard to grow without defects. A British start-up, Space Forge, aims to grow "seed" crystals in orbit that would then be used to grow larger crystals on Earth. Its first satellite was lost in 2023 when the rocket carrying it failed.

Living tissue

On Earth, soft printed tissue collapses under its own weight before it can mature. In microgravity, a printed structure can hold its shape while cells grow. Researchers have tested bioprinting, printing with living cells, on the space station. Making full transplantable organs this way is a long-range hope, not a near product. See Lab-grown organs.

Chapter 06

The round trip: up, work, down

Launch is the step that has changed most. In the Space Shuttle era, by common estimates, putting a kilogram into low orbit cost more than 50,000 US dollars in today's money. A reusable SpaceX Falcon 9, whose first stage lands and flies again, brought the advertised price down to a few thousand dollars per kilogram. Rideshare missions, where many small satellites share one rocket, made Varda possible.

Operating in orbit means the factory must run itself, by radio, with no worker to unblock a pipe. On the space station, astronauts can help, but astronaut time is one of the scarcest resources in existence, and experiments compete for it.

Return is the part outsiders forget. Coming back from orbit at about 28,000 kilometres per hour heats the outside of a capsule to well over a thousand degrees Celsius. The capsule needs a heat shield, a parachute, a landing zone and, as Varda learned, legal permission to come down. The inside must stay cool enough not to ruin the product.

Chapter 07

Building in space, for space

Every object launched from Earth has to survive launch and fit inside the rocket's nose cone, called the fairing. The James Webb Space Telescope's 6.5-metre mirror was too wide to fit, so it flew folded and opened in space in a long chain of risky steps. A structure assembled or printed in orbit would face neither limit, so it could be lighter and larger.

The first steps are modest. In 2014 a 3D printer on the International Space Station made its first plastic part, testing whether astronauts could print tools and spare parts instead of waiting months for a supply flight. In 2024 a European metal printer on the station produced what were reported as the first metal parts printed in orbit. These are demonstrations, not factories. But the logic is strong for long missions. A crew three years from home cannot carry a spare for every part that might break, but it can carry raw material and a printer.

Chapter 08

Living off the land: the Moon and Mars

The deepest reason to make things in space is a law of rocketry. To go faster, a rocket must throw mass backwards, and every extra kilogram of payload needs much more than a kilogram of extra fuel. It also needs fuel to lift that fuel. Climbing out of Earth's gravity is by far the most expensive part of any journey into the solar system. The Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's, so lifting something off the Moon takes far less energy.

That makes lunar materials interesting. Craters near the Moon's poles that never see sunlight hold water ice. Water can be drunk, split into oxygen for breathing, or turned into hydrogen and oxygen rocket fuel for a refuelling station. Moon dust, called regolith, can be heated and fused into bricks or used in printed walls. Engineers have done this with simulated lunar dust on Earth.

On Mars, NASA's Perseverance rover carried a toaster-sized device called MOXIE. Between 2021 and 2023 it pulled carbon dioxide from the thin Martian air and split off oxygen, producing about 120 grams in total. That is a tiny amount, but it was the first time anyone had made a useful resource from another planet's environment. It proved the chemistry, not the scale.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Better products, cheaper exploration, and a new kind of economy

Space manufacturing matters for three reasons: a few products may genuinely be better, exploration may become affordable, and governments see orbit as strategic territory.

Chapter 09

The value-per-kilogram test

The simplest way to judge any space manufacturing claim is to compare what the product is worth with what the trip costs. A rough sorting looks like this.

Notice the last row. Something cheap on Earth can be very valuable in orbit, because its real price includes the launch. A litre of water costs almost nothing at home and thousands of dollars delivered to orbit. That is why the long-term case for space manufacturing is mostly about supplying activity in space, not about selling to people on the ground.

Chapter 10

The end of the space station era, and what replaces it

Most microgravity research so far has happened on the International Space Station, run since 2000 by the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. It is ageing. NASA plans to retire it around 2030 and has contracted SpaceX to build a vehicle that will push it down to burn up over the ocean. China operates its own station, Tiangong, completed in 2022.

NASA's plan is to become a customer rather than an owner. Several American companies, including Axiom Space, Vast, and teams behind projects called Starlab and Orbital Reef, are designing private stations. If they fail to find enough customers, there could be a gap with no Western crewed laboratory in orbit. Space manufacturing is not just a hope for these stations; it is part of how they intend to pay their bills.

Chapter 11

The factory that never opened: a lesson from the 1980s

This is not the first wave of excitement. In the early 1980s, the American aerospace firm McDonnell Douglas developed a machine to purify biological substances on the Space Shuttle using electrophoresis, a method that separates molecules by pulling them through a liquid with an electric field. On Earth, convection spoils the separation; in orbit, it worked better. In 1984 one of the company's engineers, Charles Walker, flew on the Shuttle to run it, the first person from private industry to fly as a crew member for a company's own project.

Two things ended the plan. After the Challenger disaster in 1986, Shuttle flights stopped for more than two years. And on the ground, genetic engineering advanced, letting companies make the target hormone in bacteria and cell cultures cheaply. Earth caught up. That is the risk for every space product: a clever ground process may solve the same problem far more cheaply.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Unique conditions, brutal logistics

The physics of orbit offers real advantages. The difficulty is almost entirely practical: cost, access, repair and return.

Chapter 13

The costs that are not on the invoice

Every object in orbit adds to a crowded environment. Low Earth orbit already holds many thousands of satellites and a cloud of fast-moving debris, and one collision can create thousands of fragments. Scientists have long warned of a chain reaction, sometimes called the Kessler syndrome after the NASA scientist Donald Kessler, in which collisions breed more collisions. Responsible space factories must plan how their hardware will come down safely.

Return brings its own risks. Capsules must land in empty areas, and each landing needs legal approval. The rules were written for governments, not frequent commercial deliveries. And there is a question of fairness. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says no nation can claim the Moon or other bodies. American law since 2015 says companies can own resources they extract. Not every country agrees that these two positions fit together, and the question of who may mine the Moon is contested.

Chapter 15

How to read the next headline

Three questions cut through most announcements. First, what is the value per kilogram, and is it far above the cost of launch and return? Second, is the space product the thing being sold, or only a lesson for improving a process on Earth? Third, has anyone flown it, brought it back and sold it to a paying customer who is not a government research agency?

The most likely future is undramatic: small capsules of high-value samples, used mostly as a research tool. The bigger vision, factories that supply ships, stations and bases from material found in space, depends on whether people actually go and stay. Space manufacturing will grow as fast as space activity grows, and no faster. Its fate is tied to the wider future of exploration, which is covered in Space exploration and Moon exploration.

The core idea

In orbit, everything is falling together, so convection, sinking and buoyancy almost disappear. That helps a few processes, such as growing crystals. The question is always economic: is the product worth more per kilogram than the launch, operation and return?

The numbers worth knowing
  • Gravity at space-station height is still about 90 per cent of the ground value.
  • Launch fell from over 50,000 dollars per kilogram (Shuttle era, today's money) to a few thousand on a reusable rocket.
  • The ISS is due to retire around 2030.
The common mistake

Thinking cheap launch alone makes space factories inevitable. Return, repair and competition from improved ground processes are just as decisive, and they ended the first wave in the 1980s.

The words, in plain words
Microgravity
The near-weightless condition inside a spacecraft in free fall. Gravity is still there; nothing presses on anything else.
Convection
The circulation of a liquid or gas as warm parts rise and cool parts sink. It needs weight.
Sedimentation
Heavier particles settling to the bottom of a liquid.
Diffusion
The slow, random spreading of molecules from crowded areas to empty ones.
Crystal form (polymorph)
One of several ways the same molecule can stack into a solid. Different forms can dissolve differently.
Containerless processing
Melting or treating a material while it floats, so no container touches or contaminates it.
ZBLAN
A fluoride glass that might carry light with very low loss if made without tiny crystals.
Fairing
The nose cone that protects a payload during launch and limits its size.
Re-entry
Coming back through the atmosphere from orbit, which heats a capsule's outside enormously.
ISRU
In-situ resource utilisation: making what you need from materials found where you are.
Regolith
The loose dust and broken rock covering the Moon and other airless bodies.
Kessler syndrome
A feared chain reaction in which debris collisions create more debris and more collisions.
Outer Space Treaty
The 1967 agreement that no nation may claim the Moon or other celestial bodies.
