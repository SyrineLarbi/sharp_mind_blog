Synthetic biology. Treating living cells as factories you can program.

The insulin that keeps millions of people with diabetes alive is made by bacteria and yeast that were given a human gene. The "blood" that makes some plant-based burgers look like meat comes from engineered yeast. A malaria drug once extracted only from a plant has been brewed in vats. These are products of synthetic biology, a field that tries to make biology work like engineering: design a living system on a computer, build it from DNA, test it, and improve it. It is quieter than artificial intelligence and may matter as much. This file explains how it works, what it already makes, and why the same tools that could cure diseases also worry security experts.

Question 1 · What is it?

Biology, approached the way engineers approach machines

Synthetic biology is less a single technique than an attitude: the belief that living systems can be designed from standard parts, built reliably and improved step by step.

Chapter 01

From tinkering to designing

Humans have changed living things for thousands of years. Farmers bred wolves into dogs and wild grasses into wheat by choosing which ones reproduced. From the 1970s, genetic engineering let scientists move a single gene from one organism into another. The first great product was human insulin made by bacteria, approved in 1982, which replaced insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle. The file Genetic engineering tells that story.

Synthetic biology goes a step further. Instead of moving one gene, it aims to design whole systems: several genes working together in a chosen order, switched on and off by chosen signals, sometimes built from DNA sequences that do not exist in nature. The difference is like that between replacing a part in a car and designing a new car from a catalogue of standard parts. The ambition is to make biology predictable enough that a design on a screen behaves the same way when built in a cell.

A computer analogy is common and partly useful. DNA is a code written in four chemical letters, A, C, G and T. Cells read that code and make proteins, which do most of the work in living things. If you can write the code, you can in principle program the cell. The analogy breaks down in important ways, as Chapter 08 explains, but it captures why people call cells "programmable factories".

Question 2 · How does it work?

Read, write, design, test, learn

The field rests on three abilities: reading DNA cheaply, writing it cheaply, and predicting what a piece of DNA will do once it is inside a cell. The first two are now very good. The third is the hard one.

Chapter 03

Reading and writing the code

Sequencing means reading the order of letters in a piece of DNA. The first human genome took over a decade and cost billions of dollars, finishing in 2003. Today a human genome can be read in a day or two for a few hundred dollars. Volume 13 explains how (DNA sequencing). Cheap reading lets engineers check exactly what they have built.

DNA synthesis means writing: building a DNA strand letter by letter from chemicals, following a sequence typed into a computer. Companies now print genes to order and post them in small tubes, often within days. Writing is still slower and more expensive than reading, and errors grow with length, so long sequences are usually assembled from shorter pieces, like a long sentence built from words. Once you can both read and write DNA cheaply, you can treat genes as designs to be edited rather than as discoveries to be found. Editing an existing genome precisely, rather than writing a new one, is where CRISPR comes in.

Chapter 04

Parts, devices and circuits

Engineers like standard parts. A gene, from an engineer's point of view, is built from a few kinds of DNA segment. A promoter is the "on" switch where the cell's machinery starts reading. A coding sequence holds the recipe for a protein. A terminator marks where reading stops. Combine parts with different strengths and you can control how much of each protein a cell makes.

Proteins can in turn switch other genes on or off. That lets designers build genetic circuits, the biological equivalent of simple electronic logic. In 2000 two teams published landmark circuits in bacteria in the same issue of the journal Nature. One was a toggle switch: a cell that flips between two stable states, like a light switch, when given a chemical signal. The other, called the repressilator, was an oscillator that made bacteria glow on and off in a repeating cycle.

Chapter 05

The chassis: choosing which cell to build on

Engineers call the host organism the chassis, like the frame of a car onto which parts are bolted. The favourites are well understood and easy to grow. The gut bacterium Escherichia coli, usually shortened to E. coli, doubles roughly every twenty minutes in good conditions and has been studied for a century; the strains used in laboratories are harmless. Baker's yeast is a favourite for more complex products, because it is closer to human cells in how it folds and processes proteins, and because industry already knows how to grow it in huge vats for beer and bread.

Some researchers have tried to build a simpler, more predictable chassis. In 2016 the J. Craig Venter Institute reported a bacterium stripped down to 473 genes. Strikingly, the function of around a third of those essential genes was unknown. Even the simplest living cell still contains machinery nobody fully understands, which says a lot about how far "programming life" still has to go. An international project has also rebuilt the chromosomes of baker's yeast in redesigned, synthetic form, a much larger and more complex genome.

Chapter 06

Design, build, test, learn

Because cells are hard to predict, synthetic biology works in loops. Engineers design a set of DNA variants on a computer, build them, test how the cells behave, and learn from the results to make better designs. This cycle is the field's working method. Large automated laboratories called biofoundries run it at industrial scale, with robots that assemble DNA, grow thousands of strains in parallel and measure the results.

Artificial intelligence is speeding up the "design" and "learn" steps. Protein structure prediction, recognised by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to David Baker, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, lets researchers predict a protein's shape from its sequence and even design new proteins that nature never made. Models trained on DNA itself are beginning to suggest sequences with chosen properties. The bottleneck is shifting from "can we build it?" to "do we know what to build?"

Chapter 07

Why biology resists engineering

The computer analogy breaks down in at least four places, and they explain why synthetic biology took longer to deliver than its early champions expected.

Context changes everything

A DNA part that works well in one cell, or next to one gene, may behave differently somewhere else. Electronic components do not care what is wired beside them. Biological parts often do.

Cells are noisy

Two genetically identical cells in the same flask can make quite different amounts of a protein, because the molecules inside are few and move randomly. Circuits must tolerate this noise.

The host has its own plans

Making a foreign product costs the cell energy and can poison it. The cell's own regulation pushes back, and yields fall.

Evolution never stops

Any cell that breaks the engineered circuit and stops wasting energy on it grows faster and takes over the culture. Engineered designs tend to decay over many generations.

The last point is the deepest. Engineers build machines that stay as designed. Biologists work with machines that copy themselves and change with every copy. That is the source of biology's power, and the source of its unpredictability.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicines, food, materials and the environment

Synthetic biology is already part of daily life, mostly invisibly. Its potential reach is wide because almost anything made from carbon can, in principle, be made by a cell.

Chapter 08

Medicines made and delivered by cells

Many of the most important modern drugs are proteins made by engineered cells: insulin, many cancer treatments, and the monoclonal antibodies described in Volume 13. Synthetic biology makes these processes more efficient and opens new ones. The artemisinin project, led by Jay Keasling at the University of California, Berkeley, inserted a chain of plant and yeast genes into yeast so that it produced a precursor of the malaria drug. It reached commercial production in 2013. Its later history is instructive: when the price of plant-grown artemisinin fell, the brewed version struggled to compete. A technical success is not automatically an economic one.

Cells can also be the medicine. In CAR-T therapy, approved in the United States from 2017, a patient's own immune cells are given a designed gene that lets them recognise and kill a cancer, and are then returned to the patient. For some blood cancers, it has produced lasting remissions where nothing else worked. The mRNA vaccines described in Volume 13 (mRNA vaccines) are another example of designed genetic instructions turned into medicine at speed.

Chapter 09

Food, fabrics and fuels from fermentation tanks

The oldest biotechnology is fermentation: yeast making beer, bacteria making yoghurt. Synthetic biology upgrades it. In precision fermentation, a microbe is engineered to make one specific ingredient, such as a milk protein, an egg protein or the iron-containing molecule called heme that gives some plant-based burgers a meaty taste. Many cheeses are already made with chymosin, a curdling enzyme produced by engineered microbes instead of taken from calves' stomachs. Volume 15 covers the economics of this approach.

The same methods can make fragrances, dyes, enzymes for washing powders, and experimental materials such as spider-silk-like fibres. Biofuels were an early hope, but most have struggled to compete with cheap oil. The pattern is consistent: engineered microbes compete best on high-value, low-volume products and struggle with bulk commodities, because fermentation tanks are expensive and oil refineries are huge and efficient.

Chapter 10

Engineering the environment itself

The most ambitious and contested applications release engineered organisms into the wild. The British company Oxitec has released male mosquitoes carrying a gene that makes their female offspring die, so local populations of disease-carrying mosquitoes fall; trials have run in Brazil, the Cayman Islands and Florida.

Researchers are also studying so-called gene drives, inheritance systems designed to spread a chosen trait through a wild population much faster than normal breeding would. Projects aimed at malaria-carrying mosquitoes are the best known. Nothing of this kind has been released into the wild, and most scientists in the field argue that it should not be until there is international agreement, because a change that spreads by itself cannot easily be called back. The United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity has debated the issue repeatedly, and the discussion involves not only scientists but the communities who would live with the result.

Other environmental applications are less dramatic and closer to use: microbes engineered to break down plastics or pollutants, bacteria that supply nitrogen to crops so that less fertiliser is needed, and organisms that sense contamination in soil or water. Volume 12 explains why fertiliser matters so much (Fertiliser, the process that feeds half the world).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful, cheap, and hard to keep inside the laboratory

Synthetic biology's benefits are concrete and already arriving. Its risks come from the same features: low cost, wide availability, and living systems that reproduce.

Chapter 12

Dual use, and how the field tries to govern itself

Biology has a feature that most technologies lack: the same knowledge that lets you prevent a disease can tell you how diseases work. This is called the dual-use problem. As DNA synthesis became cheap, biosecurity experts began to worry that dangerous work would become easier for people outside established institutions, and more recently about AI tools that help design biological molecules.

The main responses are practical rather than prohibitions on knowledge. Companies that make DNA to order screen their orders and their customers, coordinated through an international consortium of synthesis companies, and several governments have issued screening guidance. Research that could create particular risks is reviewed before funding in a number of countries, and AI developers have begun testing their models for whether they could give meaningful uplift in this area and restricting what they will answer. None of these measures is complete, and the honest description is of a field trying to stay ahead of its own tools. The Biological Weapons Convention of 1972 bans such weapons but has no inspection system, which many governments regard as its central weakness.

The hopeful precedent is Asilomar in 1975, when scientists paused parts of their own research until safety rules were agreed. That tradition is why synthetic biology holds public discussions about limits earlier than most fields. It is not a guarantee, and self-governance works only when backed by law and by the practical choices of suppliers.

Chapter 14

What to watch, and how to read the claims

Three signals matter. The first is cost. Writing long DNA sequences is still expensive compared with reading them; if that price falls the way sequencing did, far more designs become worth trying. The second is prediction. The field's bottleneck is knowing what a design will do before building it, and AI models for proteins and genomes are the most promising attack on that problem. The third is scale-up. Many companies have shown a product in a flask and then failed to make it cheaply in a factory; the industry had a wave of financial failures in the mid-2020s for exactly this reason.

When you read a synthetic biology announcement, three questions sort hype from substance. Has this been done outside a single laboratory? Is the product cheaper than the existing way of making it, including the cost of the fermentation plant? And is the organism contained in a tank, or intended to live in the open? The gap between a cell that makes a molecule and a business that sells it is usually several years and a great deal of money.

Volume 50's judgement remains the fair one: synthetic biology is already working, attracts less attention than it deserves, and is likely to matter more than several things that attract more. It will probably reach you as an ingredient in a medicine, a cheese or a washing powder long before it reaches you as a headline. The core idea

Synthetic biology applies engineering method to living systems: standard DNA parts, assembled into circuits, placed in a well-understood host chassis, and improved through repeated design, build, test, learn cycles. Cheap DNA reading and writing made it possible; biology's noise, context-dependence and evolution make it hard.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Human insulin from engineered microbes was approved in 1982.
  • A human genome cost billions and took over a decade to 2003; today it is read in days for a few hundred dollars.
  • A stripped-down "minimal" bacterium needs about 473 genes, and the job of roughly a third of them was unknown.
The common mistake

Taking the computer analogy literally. Cells are noisy, context-sensitive and evolving, so a design that works in one setting may not work in another, and may decay over generations.

Test yourself

Can you explain why engineered microbes compete well for medicines and flavours but badly for fuels? If so, you understand why value per kilogram decides what gets brewed.

The words, in plain words
Synthetic biology
Designing and building living systems from standard genetic parts, using engineering methods.
Genetic engineering
Moving or changing genes in an organism; the older, narrower practice.
DNA sequencing
Reading the order of letters in DNA.
DNA synthesis
Writing DNA chemically from a sequence typed on a computer.
Promoter
The DNA switch where the cell starts reading a gene.
Genetic circuit
Several genes wired so they switch each other on and off, producing logic-like behaviour.
Chassis
The host organism that engineered parts are built into, often E. coli or yeast.
Biofoundry
An automated laboratory that builds and tests many genetic designs in parallel.
Precision fermentation
Using an engineered microbe to brew one specific ingredient.
Minimal genome
The smallest set of genes a free-living cell needs to survive and reproduce.
CAR-T therapy
A treatment in which a patient's own immune cells are engineered to attack their cancer.
Dual use
Knowledge or tools that can serve both beneficial and harmful purposes.
Containment
Keeping engineered organisms inside controlled equipment, and designing them so they cannot thrive outside it.
