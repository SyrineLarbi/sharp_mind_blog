Humanoid robots. A machine built in our shape, for a world built for our bodies.

For most of their history, useful robots looked nothing like people. They were arms bolted to factory floors, or boxes on wheels in warehouses. Humanoid robots, with two legs, two arms and a head, were research projects and trade-fair attractions. Since about 2022 that has changed. Dozens of companies, many in the United States and China, are raising large sums to build humanoids for real work, and the first paid trials are running in warehouses and car plants. This file explains why anyone would choose such a difficult shape, what makes a two-legged robot so hard to build, what artificial intelligence has changed, and how far the reality is behind the videos.

Question 1 · What is it?

A general-purpose body, on purpose

A humanoid robot is a machine with roughly human proportions, designed to move and handle objects in spaces made for people. The shape is a strategy, not a decoration.

Chapter 01

Why copy the human body at all?

Engineers usually avoid copying nature directly. Aeroplanes do not flap their wings, and cars roll on wheels instead of walking. So why build a robot with legs, which are slower, less stable and more expensive than wheels? The answer is about the world, not the robot. Almost every building, tool, vehicle and workstation on Earth was designed around the human body. Stairs assume legs. Door handles assume hands at a certain height. Shelves, kitchen counters, car assembly lines and hospital beds all assume a person of roughly our size.

A specialised robot needs the environment rebuilt around it. Modern warehouses do exactly that: they lay out floors, shelves and conveyors so that wheeled robots can do the work. A humanoid promises the opposite bargain. Keep the world as it is, and make the machine fit it. If that works, one kind of robot could in principle do many different jobs, the way a person can, and could learn new ones without anyone rebuilding the factory.

There is a second, newer argument. Modern robots learn partly by watching and copying people, either from video or from humans steering the robot remotely. That is easiest when the robot's body matches the teacher's body. A robot with two hands of roughly human size can copy a human demonstration far more directly than a robot with three suction grippers.

Chapter 02

Fifty years of falling over

The first full-scale humanoid is usually credited to Waseda University in Tokyo, whose WABOT-1 walked a few slow steps in the early 1970s. Japan led the field for decades. In 2000 Honda unveiled ASIMO, a child-sized robot that walked smoothly, climbed stairs and waved to crowds. It became the world's most famous robot and was a triumph of engineering, but it was never a product. Honda ended its development in 2018.

The hard limits became public in 2015 at the DARPA Robotics Challenge, a US military research competition held after the Fukushima nuclear disaster. Teams had to make robots drive a vehicle, open a door, turn a valve and climb steps in a mock disaster zone. Many of the best robots in the world moved painfully slowly, and a widely shared video showed robot after robot toppling over. It was a humbling reminder that tasks a child does without thinking were still beyond the best machines.

Boston Dynamics then showed how much was possible. Its Atlas robot, developed with DARPA funding, became famous from about 2017 onwards for running, jumping and backflips. Those videos were real but carefully rehearsed. In 2024 the company retired the original hydraulic Atlas and announced a new, fully electric version aimed at factory work, a sign of how the whole field was turning from research to products.

Chapter 03

The new wave

Around 2021–2023 the field changed character. Tesla announced its Optimus humanoid in 2021. Start-ups such as Figure AI, Agility Robotics, Apptronik and 1X in the US and Europe, and Unitree, UBTech and many others in China, raised money to build humanoids for commercial work. Several reached early paid trials: Agility's Digit moving tote boxes in logistics warehouses, Figure testing robots at a BMW plant in South Carolina, and Chinese firms placing robots in car factories.

Prices have also fallen sharply at the low end. In 2024 the Chinese firm Unitree announced a small humanoid at a starting price of about 16,000 US dollars, far below earlier research robots, though basic models like that are aimed at researchers and developers rather than at doing real jobs.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A body, a sense of balance, hands, and a brain that learns

A humanoid is several hard problems stacked on top of each other. Each layer has to work in real time, because a two-legged robot that stops thinking falls over.

Chapter 04

The body: joints, motors and power

Engineers describe a robot's flexibility by its degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways its joints can move. Your elbow bends in one way, which is one degree of freedom; your shoulder can swing in several directions. A modern humanoid typically has a few dozen degrees of freedom across its legs, arms, waist, neck and hands. Each one needs an actuator, the robot's equivalent of a muscle, which is usually an electric motor with a gearbox.

Actuators are where much of the cost sits. They must be strong enough to lift the robot and a load, precise enough to place a screw, light enough not to make the legs too heavy, and tough enough to survive falls. They must also be "back-drivable" or compliant, meaning they give way a little when pushed, so a robot that bumps into a person does not hit like a car door.

Then there is power. A humanoid carries its own battery, and walking, balancing and lifting are energy-hungry. Current commercial humanoids typically work for a few hours before recharging or swapping a battery. Every kilogram of battery makes the robot heavier, which means it needs stronger motors, which need more battery. That loop limits how long and how hard a humanoid can work.

Chapter 05

Balance: walking is controlled falling

A table with four legs stands still without effort. A person on two legs is constantly making tiny corrections, mostly without noticing. Each step is a small fall forward, caught by the next foot. A humanoid has to do the same thing in software, hundreds of times a second, using sensors that measure the angle of every joint, the forces on its feet and the tilt of its body, like the balance organ in your inner ear.

Older robots such as ASIMO used a careful mathematical approach. They planned each step so that the robot's weight always stayed over the area covered by its feet, which made them stable but stiff and cautious. They walked with bent knees on flat floors. The modern approach is different. Engineers build a detailed computer simulation of the robot and let thousands of virtual copies practise walking, falling and getting up, using reinforcement learning: trial and error, with rewards for staying upright and moving efficiently. The learned controller is then transferred to the real robot, a step called sim-to-real.

Simulation compresses years of practice into hours. That is the main reason humanoids went from stiff shuffling to walking over rough ground, recovering from shoves and even running in just a few years. In April 2025, Beijing hosted a half-marathon in which humanoid robots ran alongside human runners; a few finished, slowly and with help from human handlers. It was a stunt, but it showed how far locomotion had come.

Chapter 06

Hands: the hardest part

Walking is now largely solved for ordinary floors. Hands are not. The human hand is one of the most complex structures in the body, with more than twenty degrees of freedom and thousands of touch sensors in the skin. It can crack an egg, thread a needle and grip a hammer, adjusting its force without conscious thought.

Robot hands face three problems at once. They need many small, strong motors packed into a tight space. They need a sense of touch, which is still crude compared with skin. And they need software that knows how to handle objects it has never seen: a soft bag, a slippery bottle, a tangled cable. Grasping unfamiliar objects reliably is the central unsolved problem of the whole field. Many commercial humanoids today use simpler grippers and stick to tasks such as moving boxes and totes, which need strength and reach rather than dexterity.

Chapter 07

The brain: from programs to robot foundation models

Traditional industrial robots follow exact programs: move to this point, close the gripper, move to that point. That works only when everything is always in the same place. Humanoids are meant for messier settings, so they need to understand what they see and decide what to do.

The newest approach borrows from language models. A vision-language-action model takes in camera images and an instruction in ordinary words, such as "put the apple in the bowl", and outputs motor commands. Google DeepMind showed an early version, RT-2, in 2023, and several companies and labs have since built their own. These models are trained on huge amounts of internet text and images, which gives them general knowledge about objects, plus recordings of robots performing tasks. The hope is that, like language models, they will get broadly more capable as they grow.

There is a catch. Language models learned from trillions of words already written on the internet. No equivalent library of robot movements exists. Companies collect data in three main ways: people teleoperating robots (steering them remotely, often with virtual-reality headsets and gloves) while every movement is recorded; simulation; and videos of humans doing tasks. The lack of robot data is the bottleneck many researchers think matters most, and whoever solves it cheaply may lead the field.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Shrinking workforces, industrial rivalry, and care

The interest in humanoids is driven less by curiosity than by demographics and competition between countries.

Chapter 08

Too few workers

Many rich and middle-income countries face shrinking working-age populations. Japan, South Korea, China and much of Europe have low birth rates and ageing populations, and employers in manufacturing, logistics and care already struggle to fill jobs that are physically hard, repetitive or done at night. Ordinary industrial robots have helped for decades, but they only suit work that can be standardised.

The economic case for humanoids is that they could take on the remaining manual tasks that are too varied for a fixed robot arm. The promise is not replacing all workers but filling gaps that automation has so far been unable to reach. Whether that promise is met depends on cost. A humanoid needs to work reliably for years, at a total cost below what it replaces, which no company has yet demonstrated at scale.

Chapter 09

A race between countries

Humanoids have become part of industrial policy. In 2023 China's industry ministry issued guidance naming humanoid robots as a priority technology, and Chinese cities and provinces have backed many companies. China also builds much of the world's supply of motors, batteries and sensors, the same components humanoids need, which may let its firms produce them more cheaply. American firms, by contrast, have so far led in AI models and attracted the largest private investments.

This rivalry matters because the country that manufactures robots at scale may gain an advantage in manufacturing generally, in the way factory automation once helped Japan and Germany. It also raises security questions that resemble those around telecoms equipment: a robot is a networked machine with cameras and microphones, operating inside factories and homes. Who controls its software and data will matter.

Chapter 10

Care, companionship and the uncanny valley

The most emotionally charged use is care for elderly people. Japan has experimented with care robots for years, from lifting aids to a seal-shaped robot called Paro that comforts people with dementia. Studies of these deployments suggest that practical help, such as lifting a patient, is welcomed, while the idea of replacing human contact with machines is more controversial, among both carers and families.

Appearance matters here. In 1970 the Japanese roboticist Masahiro Mori proposed the uncanny valley: as a robot looks more human, people like it more, until it becomes nearly but not quite human, at which point it suddenly feels eerie. The idea is debated, but most commercial humanoids avoid realistic faces for this reason. The ethical question is not only whether robots can care, but whether a society should accept machines in roles that were valued partly because a person filled them. Different cultures are likely to answer this differently.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Generality against efficiency

The human shape buys flexibility. It pays for it in cost, complexity, energy and safety. For almost any single task, a specialised machine is better.

The table explains why humanoids make sense only if the generality is real. A humanoid that can do only one job is just an expensive, slow version of a specialised robot. Its value depends on doing many different jobs well enough, which is exactly what has not yet been proved.

Chapter 12

Safety: a machine that can fall on you

Factory robot arms are usually fenced off or slowed down when a person comes near. Collaborative robots, or cobots, are designed to work next to people at limited force and speed. Humanoids are harder. A robot weighing as much as an adult that is actively balancing cannot simply freeze when something goes wrong, because freezing may make it fall. Safety standards for such dynamically balancing machines are still being developed by international standards bodies.

Homes are hardest of all. They contain children, pets, stairs, liquids and fragile objects, and nobody supervises the robot. This is why most experts expect humanoids in factories and warehouses long before they appear in ordinary homes. Privacy is another concern: a household robot would map your rooms and see everything you do.

Chapter 14

How to watch a humanoid video

Humanoid companies rely heavily on video to raise money, and many videos are impressive. Some are also misleading in ways a careful viewer can check. At high-profile public events, some humanoids that appeared to act on their own were later reported to have been partly controlled by remote human operators. Teleoperation is a legitimate way to collect data, but it is not autonomy.

When you watch, ask five questions. Is the robot acting by itself, or is someone steering it? Is the video sped up? (Many are, and say so in small print.) How many attempts were filmed to get this one? Is the setting a real workplace or a prepared stage? And how long can it do this before a person must step in? The number that matters is not what a robot did once, but how many hours it works between human interventions.

The broader lesson is the one from Volume 50. Progress in humanoids is real and faster than most experts expected a decade ago, especially in walking and in learning from data. But the gap between a demonstration and a machine that works unsupervised for years is large, and it closes slowly. For the wider family of machines that act on their own, from drones to farm equipment, read the next file, Autonomous systems.

The core idea

Humanoids copy the human shape because the world is built for human bodies, and because a matching body makes it easier to learn from people. Walking has improved fast thanks to simulation and reinforcement learning. Dexterous hands, enough robot training data, cost and safety remain the hard problems.

The numbers worth knowing
  • ASIMO appeared in 2000; Honda stopped developing it in 2018.
  • A modern humanoid has a few dozen degrees of freedom; a human hand alone has more than twenty.
  • Commercial humanoids typically run for a few hours per battery charge (as of 2026).
The common mistake

Treating a polished video as proof of a product. Ask how long the robot works without a human stepping in, and whether anyone is steering it remotely.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a humanoid is worse than a specialised robot at any single task, yet companies still think it could be worth building? If so, you understand the generality bet.

The words, in plain words
Humanoid robot
A robot with roughly human body proportions: legs, arms, torso and head.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent ways a robot's joints can move.
Actuator
The part that produces movement, usually an electric motor with a gearbox: the robot's muscle.
Compliance
A joint's ability to give way slightly when pushed, which makes contact safer.
Reinforcement learning
Learning by trial and error, rewarding attempts that succeed.
Simulation
A detailed computer model of the robot and its world, used for practice.
Sim-to-real
Transferring a skill learned in simulation to the physical robot.
Teleoperation
A person steering a robot remotely, often to record training data.
Vision-language-action model
An AI model that takes images and spoken or written instructions and outputs motor commands.
Dexterity
Skill at handling objects precisely, especially with fingers.
Cobot
A collaborative robot designed to work safely next to people.
Uncanny valley
Masahiro Mori's idea that nearly-human robots feel eerie.
