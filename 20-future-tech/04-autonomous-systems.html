Sharp Mind · Future & Emerging Technologies · Topic 04 of 20

Autonomous systems. Machines that keep working when nobody is holding the controls.

On a rover on Mars, a signal from Earth can take twenty minutes to arrive. In a mine in Western Australia, giant trucks carry ore day and night with no one in the cab. Over the battlefields of Ukraine, small drones fly through radio jamming that cuts them off from their pilots. These machines have one thing in common: at some point they must decide what to do by themselves. That is what "autonomous" means. This file explains how such systems work, why they are spreading from space probes to farms and armies, and why the hardest questions they raise are about responsibility rather than engineering.

Question 1 · What is it?

Automatic is not the same as autonomous

A machine that follows a fixed sequence is automatic. A machine that chooses its own actions in a changing world, towards a goal, is autonomous. The line between them is where most of the difficulty lives.

Chapter 01

The washing machine and the drone

A washing machine runs a programme: fill, spin, drain, rinse. If you throw a brick in, it carries on regardless. It is automatic. Now picture a delivery drone crossing a town. Wind pushes it sideways, a crane appears that was not on the map, a bird flies close, its battery runs lower than expected. It must notice each change and choose what to do, without asking anyone. It is autonomous.

The distinction is not about cleverness but about who handles the unexpected. An automatic machine leaves surprises to humans; an autonomous one is expected to cope with them itself. Most real systems sit in between, handling routine surprises alone and calling for help with unusual ones.

People who design these systems describe the human's role in three ways. With a human in the loop, the machine proposes and a person must approve each important action. With a human on the loop, the machine acts by itself while a person watches and can step in. With a human out of the loop, nobody is watching in real time. The same drone can work in any of these modes, and the choice is often a policy decision rather than a technical one.

One reason runs through the whole table. Autonomy is needed wherever a human operator is too far, too slow, too expensive or too cut off to be in control all the time. Notice that intelligence is not the reason. Distance, delay and danger are.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sense, locate, decide, act, and check again

Every autonomous system runs the same basic cycle many times a second. What differs is how much of the world it must understand, and what happens when its links to the outside are cut.

Chapter 03

The basic cycle

How an autonomous machine runs, loop after loop
  SENSE    cameras, radar, lidar, sonar, GPS, inertial sensors
    |
  LOCATE   where am I? (map + position estimate)
    |
  PERCEIVE what is around me, and what will it do next?
    |
  PLAN     which route or action best meets the goal, within the rules?
    |
  ACT      motors, rudders, propellers, tools
    |
  CHECK    did it work? anything unexpected? -> back to SENSE
    |
  if confidence is low or a rule is hit: SLOW, STOP, RETURN HOME or CALL A HUMAN

The last line of the diagram is the part outsiders overlook. Good autonomous systems spend as much design effort on what to do when unsure as on normal operation. A mining truck stops if its sensors disagree. A drone that loses its link flies home or lands. The safe fallback is known in engineering as a fail-safe, and choosing it well is often the difference between a minor incident and a disaster. The underlying feedback idea, measure and correct, is explained in Control systems.

Chapter 04

Knowing where you are without GPS

Most outdoor autonomous machines rely on satellite navigation, such as GPS, to know where they are. GPS is wonderful and fragile. Its signals are weak and easy to drown out with a cheaper transmitter, called jamming, or to fake, called spoofing. Since 2022, widespread GPS interference around conflict zones in Eastern Europe and the Middle East has disrupted navigation for ordinary airliners as well as military drones.

So serious systems carry backups. An inertial measurement unit uses tiny accelerometers and gyroscopes to track movement from a known starting point, the way you can walk a few steps with your eyes closed. It drifts over time. Cameras can match what they see against maps or satellite photos, and some systems navigate by recognising landmarks. Combining several imperfect sources, a technique called sensor fusion, gives a position more reliable than any one of them. Underground and underwater, where satellite signals do not reach at all, machines rely entirely on such methods.

Chapter 05

Delay forces independence: the Mars example

Mars shows the logic most clearly. Depending on where the two planets are in their orbits, a radio signal takes roughly between 3 and 22 minutes to travel one way. Steering a rover with a joystick is impossible: by the time you see a rock, the rover has already hit it. Early rovers were given short, cautious instructions each day and moved slowly.

NASA's Perseverance rover, which landed in 2021, can plan its own routes over the terrain ahead, using its cameras to map hazards and choose a safe path. Engineers on Earth set the destination and the rules; the rover decides the steps. The greater the delay or the weaker the link, the more decisions must move onto the machine itself. The same principle explains underwater gliders, which surface only occasionally to report, and military drones designed to keep going when jammed.

Chapter 06

Swarms: many simple machines working together

One trend is to replace a single expensive machine with many cheap ones that coordinate. A swarm is a group of autonomous machines that share information and divide tasks among themselves, like ants or starlings, without one person controlling each. Light shows in which hundreds or thousands of drones form pictures in the night sky are a familiar, choreographed example, although these mostly fly pre-planned paths rather than deciding for themselves.

True swarms, in which machines decide together, are harder. They need reliable communication between members and rules that produce good group behaviour without central control. Armies are interested because a swarm can overwhelm defences built to stop a few large targets, and can keep working when some members are lost. Farmers and search teams are interested because many small machines can cover a wide area quickly.

Chapter 07

How do you prove it is safe?

Traditional engineering proves safety by testing and by analysing every possible state of a system. An autonomous machine that uses machine learning is harder to analyse, because its behaviour comes from training data rather than from rules a person wrote. It may behave well in a million tests and still fail on a situation nobody thought of, the "long tail" of rare events described in Autonomous vehicles.

Engineers respond in layers. They run vast numbers of simulated scenarios. They restrict the machine to an operating envelope, a defined set of conditions such as a fenced mine site, daylight or certain wind speeds. And they wrap the learned components in simpler, checkable rules that override them: "never fly above this height", "never enter this zone". Much of autonomy in practice is keeping the machine in a world small enough to be tested. That is why mining and farming moved faster than city streets.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cheaper work, safer jobs, and a revolution in war

Autonomous systems are already changing how food is grown, how minerals are mined and how wars are fought. The military change is the fastest and the most controversial.

Chapter 08

The quiet civilian revolution

Some of the most important autonomous systems are the least visible. In the iron-ore mines of the Pilbara, driverless haul trucks, each the size of a house, have operated for over a decade, controlled from centres hundreds or thousands of kilometres away. Mining companies report fewer accidents and more consistent operations, because trucks do not get tired and drive the same way each time.

In Rwanda, the company Zipline began delivering blood to hospitals by small autonomous aircraft in 2016, dropping packages by parachute. It later expanded to other countries and to medicines and other products. The case is often cited because it solved a real problem, slow roads to rural clinics, with a narrowly defined autonomous service. In farming, self-steering tractors guided by satellite positioning have been common for years, and fully driverless tractors and weeding robots are entering use. The common thread is a defined task in a defined place, where autonomy pays for itself quickly.

Chapter 09

The drone war

The war that followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 turned drones into a central weapon. Both sides use large numbers of cheap drones for spotting targets and for attacks, including small racing-style drones flown through a pilot's video goggles, known as first-person-view drones. Production reportedly reached millions of units a year. Ukraine also used uncrewed sea drones to strike Russian ships in the Black Sea.

The link to autonomy is jamming. Both sides use electronic warfare to cut the radio link between drone and pilot. Drone makers responded by adding terminal guidance: the pilot picks a target, and if the link is lost in the final seconds, software on the drone keeps tracking and hits it. Some drones also use fibre-optic cables, which cannot be jammed. The pressure of electronic warfare is pushing weapons towards more autonomy step by step, not through a single decision to build "killer robots". That makes the line between remote control and machine choice increasingly blurred.

Chapter 10

Should a machine decide to kill?

Lethal autonomous weapon systems are weapons that, once activated, can select and attack targets without further human intervention. Some defensive systems already work close to this, such as shipboard guns that shoot down incoming missiles faster than a person could react. The debate is about extending such autonomy to attacks on people and vehicles in complex settings.

The case for restrictions or a ban

Machines cannot reliably tell a soldier surrendering from one fighting, or a civilian from a combatant. Delegating the decision to kill removes human moral judgement and creates a "responsibility gap": nobody clearly answers for a wrongful death. Cheap autonomous weapons could spread to terrorists and make wars easier to start. The International Committee of the Red Cross and many states call for new legally binding rules.

The case against a ban

Existing international humanitarian law already applies to any weapon. Machines do not panic, seek revenge or tire, and might in some cases be more discriminating than stressed soldiers. A ban would be hard to define and verify, and would bind only countries that respect it. Several major military powers have opposed a binding ban, preferring guidelines.

States have discussed the issue at the United Nations, under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, since 2014, without agreeing a treaty. In 2023 the UN General Assembly passed its first resolution on the subject, expressing concern and asking for states' views. Many proposals centre on the idea of meaningful human control: a person must understand the situation well enough to take responsibility for each use of force. What counts as "meaningful" is exactly what is contested.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Removing people from danger, and from responsibility

The same feature, no human at the controls, is both the main benefit and the main risk.

Chapter 12

The human problems that autonomy creates

Two human effects deserve attention. The first is automation bias: people tend to trust a machine's judgement too much, especially when it is usually right. A supervisor watching ten drones on a screen may approve a recommendation without truly checking it. That turns a "human in the loop" into a rubber stamp.

The second is skill fade. When machines handle routine work, the people who must take over in an emergency get less practice. Aviation learned this lesson with autopilots: pilots who rarely fly by hand can struggle when automation suddenly hands control back. The more reliable an autonomous system is, the less prepared its human backup tends to be. Good designs plan for this with training, clear alerts and handovers that give people time to understand the situation.

Chapter 14

What to watch

Three signals will show where autonomous systems are heading. The first is regulation of shared spaces. Machines in fenced mines need few rules; machines over cities, on roads and at sea near other vessels need many. The speed at which regulators approve flights and voyages without a person watching each machine will set the pace of civilian growth.

The second is the lessons of war. Military technology often spreads into civilian life, as GPS and the internet once did. The cheap, jam-resistant navigation and targeting developed in current conflicts will spread too, for good uses such as search and rescue and for harmful ones. The third is the AI inside. As the models described in AI agents gain the ability to understand scenes and instructions, autonomous machines could take on less structured work. That would make the questions of control and accountability even more pressing.

The useful summary is that autonomy is a dial, not a switch, and society is choosing its setting domain by domain. In a mine, the dial can go far. Over a crowded city, it moves slowly. In decisions over life and death, the argument about where it should stop has only just begun.

The core idea

An autonomous system senses, decides and acts towards a goal without a person controlling each step, and must handle surprises itself. Autonomy is needed where humans are too far, too slow or too cut off. Engineers keep it safe with fail-safes, sensor fusion and limited operating envelopes. The hardest questions are about responsibility, especially in weapons.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A radio signal takes about 3 to 22 minutes one way between Earth and Mars.
  • Zipline's medical drone deliveries in Rwanda began in 2016.
  • UN talks on autonomous weapons have run since 2014; the first General Assembly resolution came in 2023.
The common mistake

Picturing autonomy as a single leap to a fully independent machine. In reality it grows one function at a time, often pushed by practical problems like jamming or delay.

Fail-safe
