Human-AI collaboration. A good team is not the best human plus the best machine. It is the best way of joining them.

Most talk about AI asks whether a machine can do a job instead of a person. The more useful question for the next decade is how people and machines do a job together. The answer is not obvious. Teams of humans and AI sometimes beat either one alone by a wide margin. Sometimes they do worse than the machine would on its own, because the human trusts it at the wrong moments or ignores it at the right ones. This file is about what makes the difference.

Question 1 · What is it?

People and machines sharing one task

Human-AI collaboration means a person and an AI system working on the same task, each doing part of it. The interesting questions are who does which part, and who has the last word.

Chapter 01

The amateurs who beat the grandmasters

In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue computer beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a match. Instead of giving up, Kasparov helped promote a new form of the game called "advanced chess", in which each human player could consult a computer during the game. Players with this support were sometimes called centaurs, after the creature from Greek myth that is half human and half horse.

In 2005 an online "freestyle" tournament allowed any mix of people and computers to enter. Strong grandmasters with computers took part, as did powerful chess programs alone. The winner, to general surprise, was a team of two American amateur players using ordinary computers and several programs. Kasparov later drew the lesson in a much-quoted line: a weak human with a machine and a better process could beat a strong human with a machine and a worse process.

Chess engines have since become so strong that the human now adds little. But the story's lesson has travelled far beyond chess. The quality of a human-machine team depends heavily on how the work is divided and how disagreements are handled, not just on how clever each member is.

Chapter 02

A ladder from tool to boss

In 1978 the engineers Thomas Sheridan and William Verplank described a ladder of ten levels of automation, from a computer that offers no help at all to one that acts entirely on its own and ignores the human. Their ladder is still used, in simplified forms, to describe AI today.

Role of the AIWho decidesEveryday example
ToolThe human does the task; the AI handles one step when askedSpell check, translation of one sentence
AdviserThe AI suggests; the human choosesA program flagging a suspicious area on a scan
Co-workerBoth produce parts of the work; the human edits and approvesDrafting a report or code with an assistant
Supervised agentThe AI acts; the human monitors and can stop itAn autopilot; an AI agent booking travel with approval steps
Autonomous systemThe AI acts alone within set limitsA spam filter; automatic fraud blocking

Collaboration lives mainly in the middle three rows. The further down the ladder you go, the less the human does, but the more it matters that they can step in correctly when needed. That is a harder job than it looks, as Chapter 10 shows.

Chapter 03

Complementary strengths

Teams work best when members are good at different things. Humans and today's AI systems are, broadly, strong in different places.

Where AI is usually stronger

Speed and stamina. Searching or summarising huge amounts of text. Spotting statistical patterns across millions of examples. Producing many drafts or options quickly. Consistency: it does not get tired at four in the afternoon.

Where people are usually stronger

Knowing what actually matters in this situation. Common sense about the physical and social world. Noticing that something is odd or that the question is wrong. Taking responsibility. Building trust with other people. Handling the truly new case.

The list is changing as systems improve, and it is not tidy. AI is sometimes excellent at tasks that look hard and fails at ones that look easy. The boundary between what it does well and badly is uneven and hard to see in advance, which is the central practical problem of collaboration.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Dividing the work, placing the human, and calibrating trust

A working partnership has three design choices: how tasks are split, where the human sits in the process, and how much the human trusts the machine. Getting the third one right is the hardest.

Chapter 04

Centaurs and cyborgs: two ways to split a task

In a 2023 study, researchers from Harvard, the Wharton School and other universities worked with the consulting firm Boston Consulting Group. They watched hundreds of consultants do realistic tasks with and without an AI model. Among those who used it well, they saw two styles, and named them.

Centaurs divide the work cleanly. The human decides which sub-tasks to give the AI, such as a first draft or a summary, and keeps others, such as the final judgment, entirely for themselves. There is a clear line between the horse and the human. Cyborgs blend the two continuously. They work sentence by sentence with the AI, asking, editing, pushing back and asking again, so that it is hard to say afterwards who did what.

Neither style is always better. Centaur working makes it easier to check the AI's part and keep responsibility clear. Cyborg working can be faster and more creative, but it is easier to absorb the machine's errors without noticing. The skill is choosing the style to fit the task and the stakes.

Chapter 05

In the loop, on the loop, out of the loop

Where the human sits
  IN THE LOOP       AI proposes  -->  HUMAN approves each one  -->  action
                    (slow, careful; human sees everything)

  ON THE LOOP       AI acts  -->  action          HUMAN watches, can stop it
                    (fast; human sees summaries and alarms)

  OUT OF THE LOOP   AI acts  -->  action          human reviews later, if at all
                    (fastest; errors found after the fact)

Systems that make decisions about people, such as credit, hiring, medical treatment or policing, usually keep a human in the loop. The European Union's AI Act, adopted in 2024, requires that high-risk AI systems be designed so that people can oversee them effectively, understand their limits and override them.

The danger is a human who is in the loop only on paper. If a person must approve hundreds of recommendations an hour, they stop thinking and start clicking. Researchers call this a rubber stamp. Oversight only counts if the human has the time, the information and the authority to say no.

Chapter 06

Too much trust, and too little

Two opposite mistakes spoil most partnerships. The first is automation bias: the tendency to accept a machine's answer even when other evidence says it is wrong. Drivers have followed navigation apps into lakes and onto closed roads. In offices, people copy AI-generated text with invented facts into reports, as Volume 49 warns.

The second is algorithm aversion. In experiments published in 2015, the psychologists Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons and Cade Massey found that people who saw a forecasting algorithm make a mistake lost faith in it much faster than in a human forecaster who made similar mistakes. They then chose the human, even though the algorithm was more accurate overall. We forgive people for errors more easily than machines.

The goal is calibrated trust: trusting the system exactly as much as its track record deserves, on this kind of task. That means relying on it heavily where it is reliable, and checking it carefully where it is not. Calibration is task by task, not a single feeling about "AI".

Chapter 07

Design that helps people judge

How a system presents its output changes how people use it. Three design choices make the biggest difference. First, showing uncertainty: a system that says "I am fairly unsure about this one" helps people decide where to look. Second, showing reasons or sources, so the human can check the basis of an answer rather than just the answer. Third, adding the right friction: asking the human to form their own view before seeing the AI's suggestion, at least for important decisions, reduces blind copying.

Explanations are not a cure-all. Studies have found that people sometimes trust a system more simply because it gives an explanation, even a poor one. An explanation is useful only if the human can actually test it. A good collaboration is designed so that checking is easy and doing nothing is not automatic.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The evidence from hospitals, offices and cockpits

Collaboration matters because it is how AI is actually being used: not replacing whole jobs, but joining people in them. The results so far are real, uneven and full of lessons.

Chapter 08

A second reader for breast cancer screening

In many European countries, every screening mammogram is read by two radiologists, because a single reader misses some cancers. Doctors are scarce, so researchers have tested AI as one of the readers. In a large Swedish trial reported in 2023, AI sorted scans by risk and marked suspicious areas, and radiologists read them with that support. Early results found the AI-supported approach detected somewhat more cancers than standard double reading, while cutting the radiologists' reading workload by around two-fifths.

The design is what made this work. The AI did not decide alone. It decided which scans needed one reader and which needed two, and pointed to areas worth a second look. Other studies are more mixed. Some found that doctors ignored correct AI advice, or followed wrong advice, depending on how it was shown. The same algorithm can help or harm depending on the workflow around it. See also Computer vision and AI in healthcare.

Chapter 09

The jagged frontier in office work

The consulting study from Chapter 04 produced the clearest picture yet of AI in knowledge work. On tasks that the AI handled well, such as generating product ideas and writing up plans, consultants with the AI finished more tasks, worked faster, and produced work that graders rated as substantially better. The weakest performers gained the most, which matches a separate study of call-centre agents described in Productivity.

But the researchers also set a task that looked similar and that the AI tended to get wrong: a business problem that required careful reading of interview notes and figures. On that task, consultants using AI were noticeably less likely to reach the correct answer than those without it. They trusted a fluent, plausible and wrong analysis. The authors described AI's abilities as a "jagged frontier": some tasks that seem hard lie inside it, and some that seem easy lie outside. The danger is not that the frontier exists, but that you cannot see it from where you stand.

Chapter 10

The irony of automation

In 1983 the British psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper called "Ironies of Automation". Her argument has become more relevant every year. When a machine handles the routine work, the human is left with two jobs: watching the machine, and taking over when it fails. But people are bad at watching for long periods with nothing happening. And when the machine fails, it is usually in an unusual, difficult situation, exactly when the out-of-practice human is least ready.

Aviation has lived this for decades. In 2009 Air France flight 447 was flying over the Atlantic when ice briefly blocked the sensors that measure airspeed. The autopilot switched off. The pilots, suddenly flying by hand at high altitude in the dark, misread the situation, and the aircraft stalled and fell into the ocean, killing all 228 people on board. Investigators pointed to many causes, including how pilots were trained to handle such events. Airlines and regulators have since put more emphasis on manual flying practice. The more reliable the automation, the more deliberately humans must practise the skills it replaces.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A real multiplier, with hidden costs

Well-designed teams catch more errors and work faster. Badly designed ones combine the weaknesses of both partners.

Chapter 12

Deskilling, sameness and the blame gap

Deskilling is the worry that people who lean on a tool lose the ability to work without it. Many people who use satellite navigation every day find it harder to find their way without it. Early studies in medicine have raised the same concern: one study published in 2025 reported that doctors who had grown used to an AI helper for spotting growths during colonoscopy became less good at spotting them when the helper was switched off. The evidence is still young, but the pattern is familiar from aviation.

Sameness is subtler. When many people ask the same model for ideas, the ideas tend to resemble each other. Each person's work may improve while the variety of work across a whole group shrinks. For creative fields and for decision-making, where diverse views catch errors, that is a real cost.

The blame gap is about responsibility. When a human-AI team makes a mistake, it is easy for the user to blame the system, the vendor to blame the user, and the organisation to blame "the process". Good collaboration needs a named person who owns the outcome, and who has the power to reject the machine's advice.

Chapter 14

What makes a good collaborator

If the research so far points anywhere, it is that the most valuable human skills in a partnership with AI are not technical tricks for writing requests. They are older skills. Knowing your field well enough to spot a wrong answer matters most, because you cannot check what you do not understand. Breaking a problem into parts and deciding which to delegate matters next. So does the habit of asking "how would I know if this were wrong?" before accepting a result.

There is a paradox here. AI helps beginners most, yet the people best placed to use it safely are experts. Organisations will need to find ways for juniors to build real expertise even when a machine could do much of their early work for them. Whether they manage that is one of the open questions in How work may change because of AI. The partner worth being is the one who knows when the machine is wrong.

The core idea

Human-AI teams can beat either partner alone, but only with a good process: a sensible division of tasks, a human placed where they can really oversee, and calibrated trust, high where the system is reliable and low where it is not. AI's abilities form a jagged frontier that is hard to see.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Sheridan and Verplank's ladder had ten levels of automation (1978).
  • Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" dates from 1983.
  • The 2023 Swedish screening trial cut radiologists' reading workload by about two-fifths while finding somewhat more cancers.
The common mistake

Treating trust in AI as one dial set high or low. Trust should be calibrated task by task, and a human "in the loop" who cannot realistically say no is not overseeing anything.
