Smart cities. A city that measures itself, and the argument over who reads the measurements.

A "smart city" uses sensors, networks and software to see what is happening in its streets, pipes and buses, and to react faster. Some of this is quietly brilliant: traffic lights that adapt, leaks found before the road collapses, buses that tell you when they will arrive. Some of it has been expensive theatre. And all of it raises the same question. When a city can watch itself in detail, who decides what it watches, and who owns what it sees?

Question 1 · What is it?

A city with a digital nervous system

There is no official definition. In practice, a smart city is one that collects data about itself in real time and uses it to run services better. The arguments start with who builds it and for whom.

Chapter 01

The neighbourhood that was never built

In 2017 the city of Toronto chose Sidewalk Labs, a sister company of Google, to plan a new neighbourhood on a disused stretch of waterfront called Quayside. The vision was dazzling. Heated pavements would melt snow. Traffic lights would give pedestrians more time when they needed it. Robots would carry rubbish through underground tunnels. Sensors would measure noise, air and the flow of people, and the whole district would be designed around that data.

Then residents began asking questions. Who would own the data collected on public streets? Could a company whose business was advertising be trusted with it? A well-known privacy expert who had been advising the project resigned in 2018, saying she could not be sure the data would stay anonymous. Critics argued that a private firm was effectively writing the rules for a piece of the city. In May 2020 Sidewalk Labs withdrew, citing economic uncertainty during the pandemic.

The project failed, but it taught the field its main lesson. The hard part of a smart city is not the technology. It is trust, ownership and democratic control.

Chapter 02

What "smart" actually means

The phrase took off around 2008, when IBM ran a big marketing campaign called "Smarter Planet" and other technology firms followed. It has been used for almost anything with a chip in it. A more useful definition has three parts. A smart city senses what is happening, through meters, cameras, phones and ticket gates. It connects that information so it can be combined. And it acts on it, by changing a traffic light, sending a repair crew, or giving people better information.

Most real smart-city work is unglamorous. It is the bus-arrival screen, the parking app, the water meter that reports itself, the streetlight that dims when the street is empty. The best smart-city projects are usually invisible; you notice only that something works better.

Chapter 03

Two ways to build one: from scratch or by retrofit

Built from scratch

Some cities were designed as smart from the start. Songdo in South Korea, built on reclaimed land from the 2000s onward, was planned with sensors and networks in its infrastructure. Masdar City in Abu Dhabi aimed to be a model of clean, connected living. Saudi Arabia's NEOM proposed a linear city called The Line. Such projects often grew more slowly than planned, and some critics found them sterile. The Line was reported in 2024 to have been sharply scaled back.

Retrofitted

Most smart-city work happens in old cities. Barcelona, Singapore, Amsterdam, Copenhagen, London and Seoul have added sensors, data platforms and digital services to streets that already existed. This is slower and messier, but it is where nearly all people live. The world's smart cities are mostly ordinary cities with a digital layer added, not gleaming new ones.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four layers, from the sensor to the decision

Every smart-city system, whatever it does, has the same structure: something measures, something carries the measurement, something stores and combines it, and someone or something acts.

Sensing is the bottom layer. Some sensors are new, such as air-quality monitors on lamp posts. Many are old: induction loops buried under the road at traffic lights have counted cars for decades. Phones are the biggest sensor of all. Anonymised location data from mobile networks or apps can show how crowds move through a city.

The network layer carries the readings. Fibre and mobile networks handle cameras and heavy data. Battery sensors that send a few bytes a day use special low-power radio networks, so they can run for years without a battery change. The Internet of Things file explains these in detail.

The platform layer is where the value is made, and where most arguments happen. Data from different departments, which have usually never shared anything, is combined. A water leak, a traffic jam and a burst of complaints from residents might turn out to be the same event. The top layer is action: a human or an automatic rule does something with what the data shows. Data that nobody acts on is just an expensive hobby.

Chapter 05

Traffic: the oldest smart-city application

Long before the phrase existed, cities used computers to time traffic lights. Britain developed an adaptive system called SCOOT in the early 1980s. It uses detectors to measure traffic and adjusts the timing of green lights every few seconds across a whole network of junctions. Newer systems use cameras and artificial intelligence to do the same with more detail. Studies of adaptive signals have generally reported shorter journeys and fewer stops, though the size of the gain varies a great deal by city.

A more powerful tool is road pricing, charging drivers to use busy roads at busy times. Singapore began charging for driving into its centre in 1975 and introduced fully electronic pricing in 1998, with charges that vary by place and time of day. London followed with a congestion charge in 2003 and Stockholm in 2007. These schemes depend on technology, cameras that read number plates or tags in cars, but the real work is done by the price, not the sensor. Data shows where the congestion is; the charge changes behaviour.

Chapter 06

Water, light and waste: the quiet savings

Many cities lose a large share of their treated water through leaking pipes before it reaches any customer. In some places the figure is a fifth or more. Engineers call this non-revenue water. Pressure sensors and acoustic listening devices on pipes can find leaks early, and smart meters show unusual use in a single home. Finding a leak a month early saves water, energy and sometimes a collapsed road.

Street lighting is another example. Replacing old lamps with LEDs cuts electricity use sharply on its own. Connecting them lets a city dim them when streets are empty and report broken ones automatically. In waste collection, sensors in bins report when they are full, so trucks can skip half empty ones. These are the smart-city projects with the clearest payback, and they get almost no headlines.

Chapter 07

The control room

After deadly floods and landslides in 2010, Rio de Janeiro opened a city operations centre, built with IBM. Dozens of agencies, from traffic to weather to emergency services, shared one room and one wall of screens. When heavy rain was forecast, the centre could warn neighbourhoods, close roads and send crews before disaster struck. It became a famous image of the smart city: officials watching the whole city at once.

Control rooms work best for emergencies and coordination, where getting many departments to see the same picture at the same moment saves lives. They are less useful for slow problems such as poverty or bad housing, which no dashboard can fix. Critics also point out that a room that sees everything is, by design, a tool of central control. That can be reassuring or alarming, depending on who sits in it.

Chapter 08

The citizen's side: services and voice

For most residents, the smart city is a phone. They pay for parking, report a broken streetlight, renew a permit or check the next tram. Many American cities use a single number or app, often called 311, for non-emergency requests, and the requests themselves become data about where problems cluster.

Some cities have used digital tools to give people a voice, not just a service. Barcelona built an open-source platform called Decidim ("we decide" in Catalan) in 2016 for residents to propose and debate city projects, and other cities have since adopted it. The idea is that a smart city should also be a city that listens, not only one that measures. Participation is uneven, however. The people who use such platforms tend to be the better educated and better connected.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

More people, old infrastructure, and the power to watch

Smart cities matter because cities are where most people live, because their systems are under strain, and because the same tools that make services better also make surveillance easy.

Chapter 09

The pressure on cities

The United Nations estimated in 2018 that about 55 per cent of the world's people lived in urban areas and projected about 68 per cent by 2050. Most of that growth will happen in Africa and Asia (see Urbanization). At the same time, many rich cities run on water pipes, bridges and power lines built a century ago.

A smart city promises to get more out of what already exists. Building a new road or water main costs a fortune and takes years. Squeezing ten per cent more capacity out of the existing one through better timing, early repair or pricing can be far cheaper. That is the economic case in one sentence. It also helps cities adapt to heat waves and floods, by showing where they hit hardest.

Chapter 10

Safety, or surveillance?

Cameras are the most controversial sensor. Modern video analysis can track vehicles, count crowds and, with facial recognition, identify individuals. Supporters say it helps find missing children and catch violent criminals. Opponents say it ends the ordinary ability to move through a city without being recorded, and that the systems have been shown in tests to make more errors for some groups than others.

Societies have answered differently. Many Chinese cities have deployed dense camera networks under "safe city" programmes, closely linked to policing. San Francisco in 2019 banned its own city departments from using facial recognition, and several other American cities followed. The European Union's AI Act, adopted in 2024, sharply restricts police use of live facial recognition in public places, with limited exceptions such as searching for victims of serious crimes. The same camera means different things under different laws, which is why the rules matter as much as the hardware.

Chapter 11

Who owns the city's data?

When a technology company installs a city's sensors and runs its platform, it often controls the data format, the software and the contract. Switching to a rival later can be so costly that the city is effectively stuck. Economists call this vendor lock-in. It shifts power from elected officials to suppliers.

In response, some cities have insisted on open standards and on owning their data. Barcelona in the late 2010s made "technological sovereignty" an explicit policy: city contracts required data collected in public to remain a public resource. Many cities also publish non-personal data as open data, so that researchers and start-ups can build services. The journey-planning apps on your phone often depend on transit data that cities chose to release.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Real efficiency, real risks

The benefits are concrete and measurable. The risks are about power, privacy and fragility, and they are harder to measure.

Chapter 13

Three ways it goes wrong

Fragility. In March 2018 a ransomware attack, software that locks a victim's files until a ransom is paid, hit the city of Atlanta. Many online services stopped for days, and some residents had to pay bills and fines in person. The more a city depends on connected systems, the more a single attack can stop it. Security is often the item cut first from a tight budget.

Solutionism. This is the habit of assuming every problem has a technical fix. Congestion is often a problem of land use and prices, not of information. An app that shows the traffic jam does not remove it. Sensors cannot build affordable housing.

Pilot-itis. Many cities have run eye-catching trials paid for by a vendor or a grant, then quietly stopped when the money ran out. A sensor that is not maintained gives wrong readings, and wrong data can be worse than none. The test of a smart-city project is whether it is still running, and still trusted, five years later.

Chapter 15

What a good smart city looks like

The direction of travel since Toronto is clear. Cities are moving away from big all-in-one packages sold by a single company, towards smaller projects aimed at specific problems, owned and understood by the city itself. Self-driving vehicles, if they spread, will add a huge new source of city data and new decisions about streets (see Autonomous vehicles).

A good smart city starts with a problem, not a technology. It collects the least personal data that does the job. It publishes what it collects and why. It keeps the ability to switch suppliers. And it measures success by what residents experience, not by the number of sensors installed. Smart is not the opposite of old; it is the opposite of blind.

The core idea

A smart city senses, connects and acts. The biggest gains come from quiet improvements to existing systems: traffic, water, light and waste. The biggest risks are surveillance, lock-in and fragility, which are questions of law and governance more than engineering.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 55 per cent of people lived in cities in 2018; the UN projected about 68 per cent by 2050.
  • Singapore has charged for central driving since 1975; London since 2003.
  • Toronto's Sidewalk Labs project ended in 2020, after three years of debate.
The common mistake

Judging a smart city by its gadgets. Data changes nothing unless someone acts on it, and the most effective tools, such as road pricing, are policies that technology merely enables.
