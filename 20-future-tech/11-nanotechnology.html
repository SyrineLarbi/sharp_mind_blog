Nanotechnology. The quiet revolution that arrived without the tiny robots.

In the 1990s, nanotechnology was sold to the public as a future of microscopic machines swimming through your blood and assemblers that could build anything from dirt. Thirty years later, almost none of that exists, and yet nanotechnology is everywhere. It is in the transistors of the phone in your pocket, in the sunscreen on your skin, in the vaccines that were rolled out in 2021, in the coating on your spectacles and in the electrodes of your car battery. This file explains what really changes when you build at the scale of atoms, which applications work today, and why the most famous prediction about the field never came true.

Question 1 · What is it?

A scale, not a single technology

Nanotechnology is any deliberate engineering of matter at roughly 1 to 100 nanometres, where familiar materials start behaving in unfamiliar ways.

Chapter 01

How small is a nanometre?

A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. The numbers are hard to feel, so use comparisons. A human hair is about 80,000 to 100,000 nanometres thick. A sheet of paper is about 100,000 nanometres. A single water molecule is about 0.3 nanometres across, and a strand of DNA is about 2 nanometres wide. A typical virus is in the range of tens to a hundred nanometres, which is why virus particles and engineered nanoparticles are often roughly the same size.

Another way to picture it: if a nanometre were the size of a marble, a metre would be about the diameter of the Earth. The nanoscale is the size range where you are working with tens to thousands of atoms at a time, rather than with bulk material. That is exactly the range where the ordinary rules of materials start to bend.

Chapter 02

Why small things behave differently

Three effects explain nearly everything in the field.

EffectWhat happensEveryday consequence
Surface area explodesCut a block into smaller and smaller pieces and the total surface grows enormously, while the amount of material stays the same. At the nanoscale, a large share of the atoms sit on the surface.Faster chemical reactions, better catalysts, more active drug particles
Quantum effects appearAt these sizes, the rules of quantum physics start to show. Confining electrons in a smaller space changes the energy they can absorb and emit.Quantum dots that glow in a colour set purely by their size, used in televisions and in medical imaging
Different forces dominateGravity becomes irrelevant; surface forces, stickiness and the constant jostling of molecules take over.Nanoparticles clump together; small objects stick to surfaces rather than falling off

The striking result is that the same chemical substance can have different properties purely because of the size of its pieces. Bulk gold is yellow and famously unreactive. Gold nanoparticles scatter light so that a suspension looks red, and they can act as catalysts. Medieval glassmakers used this effect without knowing it: the deep reds in some stained-glass windows come from gold particles a few tens of nanometres across.

Chapter 03

Feynman, Drexler and the word itself

The usual starting point is a 1959 lecture by the physicist Richard Feynman called "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom". He pointed out that nothing in the laws of physics forbids arranging atoms individually, and offered prizes for shrinking machinery and writing. He did not use the word nanotechnology; it was coined in 1974 by the Japanese engineer Norio Taniguchi.

The public idea of the field came from Eric Drexler, whose 1986 book Engines of Creation described molecular assemblers: machines that would build objects atom by atom, including copies of themselves. He also raised an alarming scenario in which self-copying machines consumed everything around them, which journalists named "grey goo". That image dominated popular coverage for twenty years.

The chemist Richard Smalley, who shared a Nobel Prize for discovering a football-shaped carbon molecule, argued publicly against Drexler in the early 2000s. His objections, often summarised as the "fat fingers" and "sticky fingers" problems, were that a molecular assembler would need manipulators made of atoms, which would be too bulky to reach into tight spaces and too sticky to let go of what they grabbed. Chemistry, he argued, does not work like a tiny workshop. Most working scientists sided with Smalley, and the field moved on to what the tools could actually do. Drexler later said grey goo had been a distraction from real work. The debate is worth knowing because it explains the gap between what people expected and what arrived.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Seeing atoms, and two ways of arranging them

Nanotechnology became possible when instruments could see and touch individual atoms. The making is done either by carving material down or by letting molecules assemble themselves.

Chapter 04

The instruments that opened the door

You cannot see a nanoparticle with an ordinary microscope, because visible light has waves hundreds of nanometres long and simply flows around objects smaller than that. Electron microscopes, which use beams of electrons instead of light, pushed the limit far lower from the 1930s onward.

The decisive invention came in 1981 at IBM in Zurich, where Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer built the scanning tunnelling microscope. A needle sharpened to a single atom at its tip moves just above a surface. A tiny electrical current flows across the gap, and because it changes sharply with distance, tracking it maps the surface atom by atom. Binnig and Rohrer won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986, and a related instrument, the atomic force microscope, followed.

These instruments could also push atoms about. In 1989 Don Eigler and Erhard Schweizer at IBM arranged 35 xenon atoms on a metal surface to spell "IBM". The image became famous. It proved the principle and also showed its limits: the work was done in a vacuum at extremely low temperature, and moving atoms one at a time is far too slow for making anything useful. The field's instruments let us see and touch atoms, but not manufacture with them one by one.

Chapter 05

Top-down: carving the world smaller

The top-down approach starts with a block of material and removes or patterns parts of it, the way a sculptor works. The supreme example is making computer chips. Patterns are projected onto a silicon wafer coated with light-sensitive chemicals, then etched, in a process repeated dozens of times. The most advanced machines use extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres, generated by vaporising droplets of tin with lasers, and are built by a single company in the Netherlands. Volume 12 tells that story (Computer chips).

Chip features are now measured in a few nanometres, although the "3 nanometre" and "2 nanometre" labels used in marketing no longer describe any single physical dimension. The honest summary is that the semiconductor industry is the largest and most precise nanotechnology operation in human history, and most people never think of it as nanotechnology at all.

Chapter 06

Bottom-up: letting molecules arrange themselves

The bottom-up approach does the opposite. Instead of carving, you choose molecules whose shapes and charges make them snap into the structure you want, and then let chemistry do the assembly. This is called self-assembly, and nature does it constantly: soap molecules form bubbles, fats form cell membranes, proteins fold into precise shapes.

A spectacular example is DNA origami, developed by Paul Rothemund and published in 2006. DNA's letters pair in a strict way, A with T and C with G. By designing short strands that bind to chosen places on one long strand, you can fold it into a planned two- or three-dimensional shape: a smiley face, a box with a lid, a tiny frame. Researchers use such structures to position other molecules precisely, which is hard to do any other way. Bottom-up methods make enormous numbers of identical structures cheaply, but only the structures that chemistry is willing to form.

Chapter 07

The material stars: carbon in new shapes

Carbon has provided the field's most celebrated materials. In 1985 Harold Kroto, Richard Smalley and Robert Curl discovered a hollow sphere of 60 carbon atoms, nicknamed the buckyball, which won them the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In 1991 Sumio Iijima drew attention to carbon nanotubes, tubes of carbon atoms a few nanometres wide that are extraordinarily strong and conduct electricity well.

In 2004 Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov in Manchester isolated graphene, a single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb pattern, using adhesive tape to peel flakes from graphite. It is the thinnest material known, stronger than steel for its weight and an excellent conductor of heat and electricity. They won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010.

These materials were predicted to transform everything. Reality has been slower. Nanotubes and graphene are hard to make at consistent quality and in large amounts, and hard to connect to ordinary components. Their main uses today are as additives: small quantities mixed into batteries, composites, coatings and tyres to improve strength or conductivity. That is genuinely valuable and much less dramatic than the predictions. It is a good illustration of a general rule: a wonderful material in a laboratory is a manufacturing problem for the next twenty years.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

It is already in your phone, your medicine cabinet and your sunscreen

Nanotechnology turned out to be an enabling layer inside other industries rather than an industry of its own. That is why it is both everywhere and invisible.

Chapter 08

Medicine: getting a drug to the right place

The biggest medical use is drug delivery. A drug swallowed or injected spreads through the whole body, which is why chemotherapy damages healthy tissue. Packaging a drug inside a nanoparticle changes how it travels: the particle protects the drug from being destroyed, carries it in the bloodstream, and can be coated so it is taken up by particular cells.

The clearest example reached billions of people. The mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 use lipid nanoparticles: fatty bubbles roughly a hundred nanometres across that carry fragile genetic instructions into cells. Without them the mRNA would be destroyed within minutes. Volume 13 explains the vaccine itself (mRNA vaccines). Similar particles have carried cancer drugs for years, and nanoparticle-based contrast agents help medical scanners see tissues more clearly.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Nanomedicine is a real and regulated field, but products sold online with "nano" in the name are not thereby safe or effective.

Chapter 09

Energy, water and everyday products

In batteries, nanoscale materials increase the surface where chemical reactions happen and shorten the distance ions must travel, which allows faster charging and higher capacity. Silicon nanostructures in battery electrodes are one of the most active areas, and the file Advanced batteries covers that in detail. In solar panels and catalysts, nanostructured surfaces capture more light or expose more active sites.

Water treatment uses membranes with pores a few nanometres across, which separate salt and contaminants from water: this is how reverse-osmosis desalination works, as Volume 11 explains. Everyday products use nanoscale coatings for scratch resistance on spectacle lenses, water repellence on fabrics, and anti-reflective layers on screens. Modern sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide particles small enough that the cream looks transparent rather than white, while still blocking ultraviolet light. Silver nanoparticles are added to some textiles and surfaces to limit bacteria, a use that has itself raised environmental questions.

The pattern is consistent: nanotechnology rarely creates a new product category; it improves an existing one from the inside. That is why most people use it daily and never notice.

Chapter 10

The economics, and the labelling problem

Governments invested heavily and early. The United States launched its National Nanotechnology Initiative in 2000, and the European Union, Japan, China and others followed with large programmes. The result was a generation of instruments, laboratories and trained researchers, which paid off across chemistry, materials science, electronics and medicine.

It also produced a labelling problem. Because "nano" attracted funding, a great deal of ordinary chemistry and materials science was relabelled, and because nanotechnology is now embedded in other industries, nobody can say exactly how large the field is. Market figures quoted for "the nanotechnology market" vary wildly depending on whether chips, batteries and medicines are counted. The honest way to judge the field is by its products, not by its market estimates.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Better materials, and particles whose effects we are still learning

The benefits are proven and widespread. The risks are not the ones the public was warned about; they concern health, the environment and the limits of our testing.

Chapter 12

Nanotoxicology: the real safety question

Very small particles can go where larger ones cannot. Inhaled nanoparticles can reach the deepest parts of the lungs, and some can pass into the bloodstream. Shape matters as much as size: studies in the late 2000s found that long, thin, rigid carbon nanotubes could cause inflammation in the lining of the lung in mice in a way that resembled the behaviour of asbestos fibres, while short or tangled nanotubes did not. That finding shaped workplace safety rules for handling them, and it is a reminder that "nanomaterial" is not one substance with one risk profile.

The study of these effects is called nanotoxicology. Its conclusions so far are mixed and specific. Titanium dioxide and zinc oxide particles in sunscreen have been examined extensively, and regulators in Europe and Australia have concluded that they do not penetrate healthy skin in meaningful amounts, although inhaling them in spray form is advised against. Silver nanoparticles released from textiles into waste water are an environmental concern. Regulators have responded with definitions and labelling rules: the European Union requires "nano" to be declared for certain ingredients in cosmetics and food.

The honest position is that the risk is real, manageable and material-specific, and that the dangerous thing was never self-replicating machines; it is ordinary industrial chemistry at a scale where our old safety tests may not apply.

Chapter 14

Why the hype failed, and what that teaches

Nanotechnology is one of the clearest case studies in how technology predictions go wrong. The forecasters were right that control at the atomic scale would be transformative. They were wrong about the form it would take. The transformation came through materials, instruments and processes folded into existing industries, not through autonomous miniature machines.

Three lessons travel well to other fields. First, the dramatic version of a technology is rarely the useful one; the useful one is usually a better component inside something that already exists. Second, laboratory success and manufacturing success are separated by years and by enormous cost, which is why graphene is in your battery additives rather than in a space elevator. Third, the risks that materialise are usually mundane: not grey goo, but questions about what a particle does in a lung or a river.

Applied to today's announcements, those lessons suggest asking of any "nano" claim: which specific material, at what cost per kilogram, inside which existing product, and tested how? The core idea

Nanotechnology is engineering matter at roughly 1 to 100 nanometres, where surface area, quantum effects and different dominant forces give familiar substances new properties. It is made either top-down by carving, as in computer chips, or bottom-up by self-assembly. It arrived as an invisible layer inside other industries, not as tiny robots.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A nanometre is one billionth of a metre; a human hair is about 80,000–100,000 of them thick.
  • The scanning tunnelling microscope, which let us see single atoms, was built in 1981 (Nobel Prize 1986).
  • Graphene was isolated in 2004 using adhesive tape (Nobel Prize 2010).
The common mistake

Thinking nanotechnology failed because the nanobots never came. It succeeded in a different form, inside chips, vaccines, batteries and coatings.

The words, in plain words
Nanometre
One billionth of a metre; about the width of a few atoms.
Nanoparticle
A particle roughly 1 to 100 nanometres across, which may behave differently from the bulk material.
Surface-area-to-volume ratio
How much surface a piece of material has for its size; it rises sharply as pieces get smaller.
Quantum dot
A nanocrystal whose colour of emitted light depends on its size.

Making small structures by patterning and removing material, as in chip making.
Bottom-up
Making structures by letting molecules assemble themselves.
Self-assembly
