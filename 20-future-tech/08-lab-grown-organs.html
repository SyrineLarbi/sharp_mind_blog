Lab-grown organs. Building a body part from a few cells, and why plumbing is the hardest part.

Every year, many thousands of people die waiting for a kidney, liver, heart or lung that never comes. Those who do receive one must take drugs for the rest of their lives to stop their immune system destroying it. Growing a new organ from a patient's own cells would solve both problems at once. The idea has produced some of medicine's most exciting results, a few genuine successes in patients, and one of its worst scandals. This file explains what has really been built, how it is done, why a whole kidney is so much harder than a patch of skin, and what to expect in the coming decades.

Question 1 · What is it?

Growing body parts instead of borrowing them

"Lab-grown organs" is a headline phrase for a whole field, usually called tissue engineering or regenerative medicine. It ranges from simple sheets of skin, already in hospitals, to whole hearts, which remain far away.

Chapter 01

The queue that never gets shorter

In the United States alone, more than 100,000 people are on the national waiting list for an organ, and most of them need a kidney. Similar shortages exist in every country with a transplant programme. Each donated organ depends on someone dying in the right circumstances, usually in an intensive-care unit, and on their family agreeing. Supply is limited by tragedy, so it can never be planned to meet demand.

Even a successful transplant comes with a cost. Because a donor's cells carry different identity markers from the recipient's, the recipient's immune system sees the organ as an invader. To stop it attacking, the patient takes immunosuppressant drugs for life. These raise the risk of infections and some cancers, and many transplanted organs still fail after ten or twenty years.

Tissue engineering aims at both problems. If an organ could be grown to order, there would be no queue. If it were grown from the patient's own cells, the immune system would recognise it as "self", and the lifelong drugs might not be needed. That is the dream. The rest of this file is about how close it is.

Chapter 02

A ladder of difficulty

The single most useful idea in the field is that tissues differ hugely in how hard they are to build. Engineers often describe four levels.

LevelExamplesWhy it is easier or harderStatus (2026)
1 · Flat sheetsSkin, cornea surfaceThin enough that nutrients soak in from belowUsed in patients for decades, for example for severe burns
2 · TubesBlood vessels, windpipe sections, urethrasSimple shape, few cell types, flow through the middleSome approved products and successful trials
3 · Hollow organsBladder, oesophagusA bag shape with a few layers of cellsA small number of patients treated in trials
4 · Solid organsKidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreasDozens of cell types, thick tissue, dense networks of blood vesselsNot yet built for patients; research in animals and dishes

When you read a headline about a "lab-grown organ", the first question is which level it belongs to. A lab-grown bladder and a lab-grown kidney are not the same achievement at different stages; they are different problems.

Chapter 03

A real success and a real scandal

The field's best-known early success came from Anthony Atala and colleagues, then in Boston and later at Wake Forest University in the United States. They took a small sample of bladder tissue from young patients with a serious bladder condition, grew the cells in the laboratory, layered them onto a bladder-shaped scaffold, and implanted the result. They reported the outcome for seven patients in 2006. The new bladders worked for years. It was proof that a hollow organ grown from a patient's own cells could function inside the body.

The warning came a few years later. The surgeon Paolo Macchiarini became famous for implanting artificial windpipes seeded with patients' cells, first in Spain and then at the Karolinska Institute in Sweden. His results were presented as breakthroughs. In reality, most of the patients died, and investigations found that the work had been rushed into people without adequate animal testing and that published reports misrepresented outcomes. He was later convicted by a Swedish court of harming patients. The lesson the field took is that excitement, prestige and desperate patients are a dangerous combination, and that evidence must come before hope.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Cells, scaffolds, signals, and a blood supply

Growing a tissue needs four things: the right living cells, a structure to hold them, the chemical and physical signals that tell them what to become, and a way to feed them once the tissue is thick.

Chapter 04

The raw material: stem cells

Most cells in your body are specialised. A skin cell makes skin, and a liver cell makes liver. A stem cell is one that can divide to make more of itself and can also turn into specialised cells. Embryos contain the most flexible kind, which can become any cell type. Adults keep more limited stem cells in some tissues, such as bone marrow and the lining of the gut.

The big change came in 2006, when the Japanese scientist Shinya Yamanaka showed that ordinary adult cells could be turned back into embryo-like stem cells by switching on just four genes. These are called induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPS cells; "pluripotent" means able to become almost any cell type. The method was repeated with human cells in 2007, and Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. It meant that, in principle, a patient's own skin or blood cells could be turned into the raw material for any tissue they need, without using embryos.

Turning stem cells into the right specialised cells is the next step, called differentiation. Researchers guide it by adding growth signals in a carefully timed order, copying the sequence an embryo goes through. Recipes now exist for heart muscle cells, retina cells, insulin cells, neurons and many more. The cells made this way are often less mature than adult ones, more like fetal cells, which is one of the field's ongoing difficulties.

Chapter 05

The frame: scaffolds, including ghost organs

Cells in a dish spread out in a flat layer. To form a tissue with shape and strength, they need a scaffold: a three-dimensional frame they can attach to and grow through. Some scaffolds are made from materials that slowly dissolve in the body, leaving only the new tissue behind. Others are made from natural proteins such as collagen, the main protein in skin and tendons.

A striking alternative is to borrow nature's own frame. In 2008 a team in Minnesota led by Doris Taylor took a rat heart and washed it with detergent until every cell was gone. What remained was a pale, translucent "ghost heart": the protein skeleton, including the branching channels where blood vessels had been. When they added new heart cells, some of the structure began to beat. This is called decellularisation. It has been tried with hearts, lungs, livers and kidneys from animals and from human organs unsuitable for transplant. The attraction is that the ghost organ keeps the architecture nobody yet knows how to build from scratch. The difficulty is recolonising it with the right cells in the right places, in the billions.

Chapter 06

Bioprinting: a printer that uses cells as ink

A 3D bioprinter works like a normal 3D printer, laying down material layer by layer from a digital design. Its ink, called bioink, is a gel carrying living cells. By printing different cell types in different positions, researchers can build structures with a planned layout, such as skin with several layers, cartilage in the shape of an ear, or small pieces of liver tissue.

Bioprinting is genuinely useful and frequently overhyped. Printing a shape is not the same as making a working organ. After printing, the cells must survive, connect with each other, mature and start doing their jobs, which can take weeks of growth in a bioreactor, a container that supplies nutrients, oxygen and sometimes movement or pressure to mimic the body. The printer places the cells; biology has to do everything else.

Chapter 07

The great barrier: blood vessels

Here is the reason skin was easy and kidneys are not. Cells need oxygen and nutrients, and in still tissue oxygen can only spread a short distance, roughly a fraction of a millimetre. That is why almost every cell in your body sits very close to a tiny blood vessel, a capillary. A thin sheet of skin can survive by soaking up nutrients from the wound bed beneath. A block of tissue as thick as a sugar cube cannot: the cells in the middle starve and die.

A real kidney contains an enormous branching tree of vessels, from arteries the width of a pencil down to capillaries thinner than a hair, all connected without leaks, plus around a million tiny filtering units. Building that network, and connecting it to the patient's circulation, is widely regarded as the single biggest unsolved problem in growing solid organs. Researchers are attacking it by printing channels into gels, by using decellularised scaffolds that already contain the vessel paths, and by encouraging vessels to grow in from the body after implantation.

Chapter 08

Organoids: letting cells build themselves

One of the great surprises of the last two decades is how much cells can organise themselves. In 2009 the Dutch scientist Hans Clevers and colleagues showed that a single stem cell from a mouse gut, given the right gel and signals, would grow into a tiny structure with the folds and pits of real intestine. These self-assembled mini-tissues are called organoids. Within a few years researchers had grown organoids resembling parts of the brain, kidney, liver, lung, stomach and retina.

Organoids are usually no bigger than a grain of rice or a lentil, and they lack blood vessels, immune cells and the full structure of a real organ. They will not be transplanted as replacement kidneys any time soon. But they are extraordinarily useful. An organoid grown from a patient's own cells lets doctors test how that patient's disease, or that patient's tumour, responds to a drug, in a dish. They are also a window into human development and disease that was previously impossible to study.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

No waiting lists, no rejection, and fewer animal tests

The big prize is replacement organs. Long before that arrives, the same techniques are already changing how drugs are tested and how some conditions are treated.

Chapter 09

What already reaches patients

Away from the headlines, a steady stream of engineered tissues has reached clinics. Grown sheets of a patient's own skin cells have been used for severe burns since the 1980s. Cartilage cells taken from a patient's knee, grown in the laboratory and returned on a membrane, are an approved treatment for some joint injuries. In December 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration approved a lab-grown blood vessel, made from human cells that are then removed so the patient's body does not reject the frame, for repairing injured arteries.

Cell therapies built from stem cells are also advancing. In 2014 Japanese researchers transplanted retina cells made from iPS cells into a patient with an eye disease, the first such procedure. Early trials in Japan and the United States have transplanted lab-made dopamine-producing neurons into people with Parkinson's disease, with results reported in 2025 suggesting the cells were safe and survived; whether they reliably help is still being tested. These are not whole organs, but each one solves a piece of the organ problem in real patients.

Chapter 10

Testing medicines on human tissue instead of animals

Most drugs that work in animals fail in human trials, often because a mouse's liver or heart is not a human one. Organoids and organs-on-chips offer an alternative. An organ-on-a-chip is a clear plastic device about the size of a USB stick, with tiny channels lined with human cells. A team at Harvard's Wyss Institute, led by Donald Ingber, described a "lung on a chip" in 2010 that even mimicked breathing movements. Chips now exist for liver, kidney, gut and other tissues, and some link several organs together.

Regulators have taken notice. A US law passed in late 2022 removed the old requirement that new drugs be tested on animals before human trials, allowing other methods instead, and in 2025 the FDA announced plans to reduce animal testing for some types of drug. Human tissue grown in the laboratory may change drug development years before it changes transplant surgery. Animal testing is still widely used, and nobody expects it to vanish quickly, because no dish yet captures a whole body.

Chapter 11

Three routes to a new organ

Lab-grown organs are not the only answer to the shortage. Two other routes are moving fast, and it is worth seeing them side by side.

These routes complement each other. One hybrid idea is to grow human organs inside animals: in experiments, human stem cells injected into animal embryos lacking the genes for a particular organ have started to form tissue of that organ. That work is at a very early stage and raises sharp ethical questions, discussed in Chapter 15.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A cure for the shortage, at a price nobody can yet name

The benefits would be enormous if the technology worked at scale. The costs are partly technical, partly financial and partly about the damage done by overpromising.

Chapter 12

Weighing the promise

Advantages

  • No waiting list. Organs made on demand, not dependent on deaths.
  • No rejection, in principle. Organs from a patient's own cells could avoid lifelong immunosuppression.
  • Children. Engineered tissue could be designed to grow, which donated adult organs do not fit.
  • Better research. Organoids and chips allow testing on human tissue and personalised drug choice.
  • No ethical dilemma over donors, including the illegal organ trade.

Disadvantages

  • Unsolved biology. Blood vessels, maturity and scale are not yet solved for solid organs.
  • Cost and time. Growing cells for one patient takes weeks and is expensive; "off the shelf" donor-cell versions bring back rejection risk.
  • Safety. Stem cells that do not fully differentiate can form tumours.
  • Unequal access. Early treatments are likely to be available only in rich health systems.
  • Hype and harm. Overpromising has led to scandals and to clinics selling unproven treatments.
Chapter 13

The shadow industry of "stem cell clinics"

The genuine excitement about stem cells created an opening for businesses selling unproven treatments directly to patients. Clinics in many countries have offered injections of "stem cells", often from a patient's own fat or blood, for conditions ranging from arthritis to autism to multiple sclerosis. Most of these treatments have no good evidence behind them. Some have caused serious harm, including blindness after injections into the eye and tumours after unregulated procedures.

Regulators such as the US FDA have warned the public and taken action against some clinics, and the International Society for Stem Cell Research publishes guidance for patients. A useful rule is that a genuine trial does not charge patients large sums to take part and will explain exactly what is being tested. The gap between what the science has proved and what is being sold is one of the real costs of this field.

Chapter 15

The ethical questions coming into view

As the techniques improve, they raise questions that did not exist before. Brain organoids show coordinated electrical activity, and some researchers have connected them to electrodes for simple computing experiments. Nobody thinks a lentil-sized organoid is conscious today, but scientists and ethicists have begun to discuss where the limits should be if they grow more complex. The neighbouring file Consciousness explains why that question is so hard to answer.

Growing human tissue inside animals raises questions about how much human tissue is acceptable, and in which organs. Access is a third issue. If personalised organs become possible but cost as much as a house, they could widen health inequalities. These questions are being discussed now, while the technology is still early, which is the right moment.

The fair summary is this. Lab-grown organs are real at the simple end, promising in the middle, and still a long-term goal at the solid-organ end. The shortage of organs will probably be eased first by better use of human donors and by pig organs, while the cells-and-scaffolds approach keeps solving one piece of the puzzle at a time. The tools that make that possible, reading and writing genes, are in CRISPR and in Synthetic biology.

The core idea

Tissue engineering combines stem cells, a scaffold and growth signals to build living tissue. Thin and hollow tissues already work in patients. Solid organs are blocked mainly by the need for a dense network of blood vessels. Organoids, small self-built mini-organs, are already transforming research.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than 100,000 people are on the US organ waiting list, most needing a kidney.
  • Yamanaka's reprogramming of adult cells into stem cells: 2006 (Nobel Prize 2012).
  • Lab-grown bladders worked in seven patients reported in 2006.
The common mistake

Treating every "lab-grown organ" headline as the same achievement. A printed patch of tissue is not a working kidney; ask which level of the ladder it is on.
