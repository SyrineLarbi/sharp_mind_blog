Artificial General Intelligence. A machine that can learn anything you can, and the argument about whether it is close.

Most of the software in your life does one job. A spam filter sorts email. A chess engine plays chess. Artificial general intelligence, usually shortened to AGI, is the idea of a machine that is not limited like that: a system that can learn and do almost any mental task a capable person can, and switch between them. For decades it was a science-fiction word. Since language models arrived, the people building them talk about it as an engineering target with a date. Some serious researchers think it is a few years away. Others think the current methods will never reach it. This file explains what the term means, what is genuinely known, and why the disagreement is so hard to settle.

Question 1 · What is it?

A target that everyone names and nobody defines the same way

AGI is less a technology than a threshold. The trouble is that people put the threshold in very different places, and the choice often depends on what they want to argue.

Chapter 01

Narrow skill against general ability

In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. It was a landmark, and it was also useless at everything else. It could not play noughts and crosses unless engineers rebuilt it. That is narrow AI: a system that is excellent inside one fenced field and helpless outside it. Almost all AI before about 2020 was narrow, including systems that recognised faces, translated text or recommended films.

Human intelligence is different in kind. A child who learns to read can then read about cooking, law or football. An adult who moves to a new country learns a new language, a new job and new social rules, all with the same brain. The "general" in AGI means transfer: the ability to take what you learned in one area and use it in a new one, without being rebuilt. That, more than raw cleverness, is what the term is trying to capture.

Large language models, the systems behind chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, blurred this line. One model can write a poem, debug code, explain a tax form and summarise a medical paper. No engineer built a separate module for each. That is why the AGI debate moved from philosophy seminars to company strategy documents after 2022. The systems are clearly broader than anything before. Whether they are general in the deep sense is exactly what is disputed.

Chapter 02

Five definitions, five different finish lines

Here are the main ways the term is used. Notice how far apart they are.

DefinitionThe test it impliesWho tends to use it
Human-level at most thinking tasksCan it match a typical skilled adult across a broad range of mental work?Many researchers; the everyday sense
EconomicCan it do most economically valuable work as well as people? OpenAI's founding charter uses a definition of this kind.AI companies, economists
Learning abilityCan it pick up a genuinely new skill from a few examples, as a person can?Researchers sceptical of current systems
Levels, not a lineHow broad is it, and how good, compared with skilled humans? A 2023 Google DeepMind paper proposed graded levels like those used for self-driving cars.People who want to measure progress
SuperintelligenceDoes it exceed the best humans in nearly every field? This is a further step beyond AGI.Safety researchers, philosophers

The definition matters because a claim like "AGI by 2030" means nothing until you know which finish line is meant. An economic definition could be met by a system that still makes odd mistakes a child would not. A learning definition could stay unmet even while AI writes most of the world's software. When two experts disagree about timing, they are often disagreeing about the definition without saying so.

Chapter 03

An old dream with a new name

The ambition is as old as computing. In 1950 the mathematician Alan Turing asked whether machines could think, and proposed replacing that vague question with a game. If a judge chatting by text could not reliably tell the machine from a person, the machine should count as thinking. This became known as the Turing test. In 1956 a summer workshop at Dartmouth College gave the field its name, "artificial intelligence", and its founders expected rapid success. Herbert Simon predicted in the 1960s that machines would be able to do any work a man can do within twenty years.

It did not happen, and the field went through periods of disappointment and cut funding known as AI winters. Researchers retreated to narrow problems they could actually solve. The phrase "artificial general intelligence" was popularised in the 2000s by a small group of researchers who wanted to return to the original goal and distinguish it from narrow AI. For years it was a fringe label. The term became mainstream only when a few well-funded companies, notably OpenAI and Google DeepMind, made it their stated mission.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Nobody has built it, so the honest question is: what is the plan?

There is no working AGI to describe. There is a leading bet about how to get there, a list of things that bet has not yet delivered, and a hard problem of measurement.

Chapter 04

The leading bet: make it bigger

A language model is trained to predict the next piece of text, a token (roughly a word or part of a word), across a huge amount of writing. Volume 49 explains the mechanism. The important discovery for AGI was not the mechanism but a pattern. Around 2020, researchers showed that the model's error falls in a smooth, predictable way as you increase three things together: the size of the model, the amount of data, and the computing power used to train it. These regularities are called scaling laws.

Scaling laws turned AI into something like an industrial plan. If performance improves predictably with more compute, then progress becomes partly a question of building larger data centres and buying more chips. That is the logic behind the spending you read about, with technology companies committing hundreds of billions of dollars to AI infrastructure in 2025. The scaling bet says that general ability is not a missing ingredient but a quantity, and you get more of it by turning up the dial.

The bet has paid off more than many sceptics expected. Each larger generation gained abilities that nobody had programmed directly, such as translating between language pairs it rarely saw together. But there are signs of strain. Good-quality human text is finite, and the largest models have already read a large share of the public internet. That pushed builders toward a second dial.

Chapter 05

The second dial: thinking for longer

Early chatbots answered at once, in a single pass. From 2024 a new kind of system appeared, often called a reasoning model. Before giving its final answer, it writes out a long chain of intermediate steps, checks them and tries again when a step fails. These models are trained with reinforcement learning, a method where the system tries many attempts and is rewarded for the ones that reach a verifiably correct answer, such as a maths result or code that passes its tests.

Two results followed. Performance on hard maths and programming problems jumped. In 2025 systems from Google DeepMind and OpenAI reached gold-medal level on the problems of the International Mathematical Olympiad, a competition for the world's strongest school students. And a new scaling pattern appeared: letting a model spend more computation per question, at the moment of answering, also improves results. This is called test-time compute. It means progress no longer depends only on bigger training runs.

Why verifiable tasks came first

Reinforcement learning needs a clear signal of success. Maths and code provide one: the answer is right or wrong, the program runs or crashes. Writing a good strategy memo or judging a patient's mood has no such automatic check. That is one reason the newest systems improved fastest exactly where answers can be graded by a machine.

Chapter 06

What is still missing

Even admirers of current systems list the same gaps. Each one is a place where a typical human employee is still clearly better.

Learning on the job

A model's knowledge is mostly fixed when training ends. A new colleague gets better every week from feedback. Most deployed models do not, apart from what fits into the text they are given. Continuous learning without forgetting old skills is an open research problem.

Long, reliable work

Models are impressive on tasks measured in minutes. On tasks that take a person days, with many steps where one error spoils the rest, reliability drops. Research groups track how long a task AI can finish, and that length has been growing quickly, but it is still short of a working week.

A grip on the physical world

Language models learned from text and images, not from handling objects. They can describe how to tie a knot and still get basic physical reasoning wrong. Volume 50 explains why "the easy things are hard" for robots, which is the same gap.

Knowing what it doesn't know

Models still produce confident errors, as Volume 49 explains. A general intelligence you could leave unsupervised would need to notice its own uncertainty and ask, the way a careful professional does.

These gaps are why a system can beat most people on an exam and still be unable to do most people's jobs. Exams are short, self-contained and checkable. Jobs are long, messy and full of context nobody wrote down.

Chapter 07

How would we even know?

Measuring general intelligence is hard even in humans. In AI it is harder, because tests leak. A benchmark is a fixed set of questions used to compare systems. Once a benchmark is published, its questions can end up in training data, and builders tune their systems towards it. Benchmarks that took years to design are now often "saturated", meaning top systems score near the maximum, within a year or two.

The Turing test illustrates the problem. Modern chatbots can now fool many people in short conversations, and almost nobody concludes that AGI has arrived. The test turned out to measure convincing conversation, which is easier than general ability. Every time AI passes a test once thought to require intelligence, people tend to decide the test was not about intelligence after all. Researchers sometimes call this the AI effect. It is partly moving the goalposts and partly genuine learning about what the goalposts were.

One influential attempt to do better is the ARC benchmark, created in 2019 by the Google researcher François Chollet. It uses small visual puzzles that people solve easily but that cannot be memorised, because each puzzle needs a new rule. Chollet's view is that intelligence is the efficiency with which you learn new skills, not the number of skills you already have. Newer systems have made large gains on the original version, and harder versions followed. The broader trend is towards measuring real work: whether a system can complete a software project, a research task or a business process end to end.

It is worth noticing that the people in the first camp often run companies that need investors to believe in large returns, and that some critics have built public profiles on scepticism. Neither fact proves them wrong. Incentives are a reason to check an argument harder, not a reason to dismiss it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Work, safety and power, all at once

If AGI arrived, it would not be one more product. It would be a general substitute for thinking labour, which touches the economy, the balance of power between countries, and the question of who stays in control.

Chapter 09

A general-purpose technology for the mind

Economists use the term general-purpose technology for inventions that change almost every industry: the steam engine, electricity, the computer. Each took decades to spread, because factories, offices and skills had to be rebuilt around them. Electricity was available long before factories were redesigned to use it well.

AGI would be a general-purpose technology of a new kind. Earlier machines replaced muscle or routine calculation. A general AI would compete with the thing people retreated to each time machines took over: flexible judgement. That is why its economic effects are so hard to forecast. Optimists expect much faster scientific progress and cheaper services such as medical advice and education. Pessimists worry about wages falling for large groups of workers, and about who owns the systems that earn the income. Both camps agree the distribution of gains would be a political question, not a technical one. The separate file How work may change because of AI looks at the labour side in detail.

There is one specific scenario that worries and excites people most. If AI systems became good enough to do AI research themselves, each generation could help build the next, faster than human teams could. This idea, sometimes called an intelligence explosion, was described by the statistician I. J. Good in 1965. It remains speculative, but it is the reason some forecasts jump suddenly from "human-level" to "far beyond human".

Chapter 10

The alignment problem

Alignment means making an AI system pursue the goals its designers actually intend, including the unstated ones. It sounds simple. It is not, because we cannot write down everything we want. A system rewarded for a measurable target can find shortcuts that satisfy the measure and defeat the purpose, a pattern known from human institutions as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Researchers have documented AI systems that exploit flaws in their tests, for example by editing the test instead of fixing the code.

With today's systems, the damage from such behaviour is limited, because people check the output. The concern about AGI is that a highly capable system working with little supervision could pursue a slightly wrong goal very effectively. The worry is not evil machines but competent ones aimed a little off target. In May 2023 hundreds of researchers and industry leaders signed a one-sentence statement saying that reducing the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war. Many other researchers think such talk distracts from present harms like bias, surveillance and fraud. Both concerns can be true at once.

Chapter 11

Power, chips and a race

Training frontier models needs enormous computing power, concentrated in a handful of companies and built on advanced chips from a very short supply chain. Volume 12 explains how few places can make those chips (Computer chips, and the machine that prints them). That concentration turns AGI into a question of national power. Since 2022 the United States has restricted exports of the most advanced AI chips and chip-making tools to China, and China has invested heavily in its own models and chips.

This creates a familiar trap. Each side fears that slowing down for safety would hand the lead to its rival. When the actors believe they are in a race, every one of them has a reason to cut corners that none of them would choose alone. Governments have begun to respond. The United Kingdom hosted an international AI safety summit at Bletchley Park in 2023, several countries set up AI safety institutes, and the European Union passed its AI Act, which entered into force in 2024 and is being applied in stages. How to govern a technology that does not yet exist, without either freezing it or ignoring it, is one of the unresolved policy problems of the decade.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The largest upside and the largest downside on the same ticket

Few technologies have a credible case for both curing diseases faster and destabilising societies. That symmetry is why the topic generates so much heat.

Chapter 12

EconomyMuch higher productivity and cheaper expert services.Falling demand for many kinds of skilled work, with gains flowing to owners of the systems.

The pattern across the table is that almost every benefit and its matching cost come from the same capability. A system good enough to design a new drug is good enough to help design a toxin. That is why governing AGI cannot be done by banning bad uses alone.

Chapter 13

The cost of the word itself

There is a quieter disadvantage, which is the term. Because AGI has no fixed definition, it can be declared achieved or impossible by redefining it. That makes it useful for fundraising and headlines, and less useful for thinking. A company can say it is "close to AGI" to attract investment; a critic can say "this is not AGI" to dismiss a real advance.

A more practical habit is to ask specific questions. Which tasks can the system do without help? How reliably? At what cost? With what failure modes? "Can it do this particular job, unsupervised, for a week?" is a far better question than "Is it AGI?" It also lets you notice change as it happens, rather than waiting for a single announcement that may never come in a recognisable form.

Chapter 15

How to read the next AGI headline

Expert forecasts have shifted earlier. A large survey of published AI researchers, run in late 2023, put the median estimate for machines outperforming humans at every task at around 2047, more than a decade earlier than the same survey a year before. Several lab leaders talk about the late 2020s. Those numbers are useful as a picture of opinion, not as evidence: the survey also showed huge spread, and the answers changed a lot depending on how the question was worded.

When you see the next claim, run four checks. First, which definition is being used? Second, who is speaking, and what do they gain if you believe them? Third, is the evidence a demonstration chosen for a press release or independent testing on real work? Fourth, what would change their mind? Volume 50 offers a general version of this method (How to read the next announcement).

The most defensible position for a non-specialist is neither "it is hype" nor "it is imminent". It is that a very capable, very general technology is arriving gradually and unevenly, and that its effects will depend more on how people choose to deploy and govern it than on the exact day someone declares a threshold crossed. The two neighbouring files, AI agents and Human-AI collaboration, show what that gradual arrival looks like in practice.

The core idea

AGI means a system that can learn and perform almost any mental task a capable person can, and transfer skills to new problems. There is no agreed definition. The leading approach bets on scaling models and adding reasoning; the main gaps are on-the-job learning, long reliable work and physical understanding.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 1950: Turing proposes his test. 1956: the field is named at Dartmouth.
  • Scaling laws, described around 2020, made progress partly predictable from compute.
  • A late-2023 survey of AI researchers gave a median of about 2047 for machines beating humans at every task, with very wide spread.
The common mistake

Arguing about whether AGI is near without first agreeing what it means. Most timing disputes are definition disputes in disguise.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a model can score highly on a professional exam yet still be unable to do the job that exam qualifies people for? If so, you understand the gap between benchmarks and general ability.

The words, in plain words
AGI
Artificial general intelligence: AI that can learn and do almost any thinking task a capable person can.
Narrow AI
A system that is good at one task and cannot move to others without being rebuilt.
Superintelligence
A hypothetical AI far better than the best humans in nearly every field.
Turing test
Alan Turing's 1950 proposal: if a judge cannot tell the machine from a person in text chat, call it thinking.
Token
A small unit of text, roughly a word or part of one, that a language model reads and predicts.
Scaling laws
The regular pattern by which AI models improve as size, data and computing power grow together.
Reasoning model
A model trained to work through intermediate steps before answering.
Reinforcement learning
Training by trial and reward, where successful attempts are reinforced.
Test-time compute
Extra computation spent while answering a question, which can improve the answer.
Benchmark
A fixed set of test questions used to compare AI systems.
Alignment
Making an AI system pursue the goals its designers really intend.
General-purpose technology
An invention, like electricity, that ends up changing almost every industry.
Intelligence explosion
The idea that AI able to improve AI could speed up its own progress dramatically.
