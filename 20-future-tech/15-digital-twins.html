Digital twins. A copy of the real thing that lives in a computer, and is only as honest as the data that feeds it.

A digital twin is a computer model of a real object or system that is kept up to date with data from the real one. A jet engine, a wind farm, a factory, a port or a city can each have a twin. Engineers use it to see what is happening inside without opening anything up, to predict what will break, and to try out a change on the copy before risking it on the original. The idea is powerful and real. The phrase has also become one of the most overused labels in technology marketing.

Question 1 · What is it?

A model that stays connected to the thing it copies

Engineers have built models for centuries. What makes a twin different is the live link: data flows from the real object into the model, and decisions flow back.

Chapter 01

A spacecraft in trouble, and its copies on the ground

In April 1970 an oxygen tank exploded on Apollo 13, more than 300,000 kilometres from Earth. The three astronauts were alive, but their spacecraft was crippled, short of power, water and breathable air. Nobody on the ground could look at it. What NASA did have were the simulators that astronauts trained in, and engineers who knew every switch and circuit.

Over the following days, crews on the ground used those simulators, and spare hardware, to work through problems before the astronauts tried them for real: how to power up the dead command module for re-entry with very little electricity, and how to make square air-cleaning canisters fit a system built for round ones. Engineers updated the simulators with what the crew reported, so the copies on the ground matched the damaged ship as closely as possible. The astronauts came home.

Apollo 13 is often called the first digital twin, although the simulators were partly physical and the phrase did not yet exist. The story contains the central idea. When you cannot touch the real thing, a copy that is kept in step with it lets you test before you act.

Chapter 02

Three ingredients

The term itself came later. The American engineer Michael Grieves described the concept around 2002 in courses on managing a product through its life, and NASA used the phrase "digital twin" in a technology roadmap around 2010. Most definitions now agree on three ingredients.

A physical thing

An engine, a pump, a bridge, a power grid, a city district or a human organ. It is called the physical asset.

A virtual model

Software that represents how that thing is built and how it behaves: its shape, materials, physics and history.

A data link

A flow of measurements from the real thing to the model, and of decisions or settings back again, repeated over the asset's whole life.

The third ingredient is the one that matters. A beautiful 3D drawing of a building is not a twin. A twin knows what the real building is doing today: how warm each room is, how much the structure has moved, which pump is vibrating more than last month.

Chapter 03

Model, shadow, twin: a spectrum

Because the label is popular, it is stuck on many things that are not quite twins. Some engineers use a three-step scale to keep the language honest.

LevelData from the real thingAction back to the real thingExample
Digital modelNone, or added by handNoneA design simulation of a bridge before it is built
Digital shadowAutomatic and continuousNone; humans read it and decideA dashboard model of a factory showing live machine data
Digital twinAutomatic and continuousYes, settings or decisions flow backA model that adjusts a wind turbine's blades to reduce wear

In everyday speech, people use "digital twin" for all three. That is fine, as long as you ask which one is meant. The more of the loop that is closed, the more useful the twin, and the more dangerous a wrong one becomes.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Build a model, feed it data, ask it questions

A twin is built in layers: a model of how the thing behaves, a stream of measurements that keeps the model honest, and tools that use the result to answer "what if?"

Chapter 04

Two ways to build the model

The first way is physics-based. Engineers write down the laws that govern the object: how heat flows, how metal bends, how air moves over a wing. The computer solves those equations for the object's exact shape and materials. One common method, called finite element analysis, splits the object into thousands or millions of tiny pieces and calculates the forces on each. Physics models are trustworthy when conditions are well understood, but they can be slow and need expert work to set up.

The second way is data-driven. Instead of equations, the model learns patterns from history: when the vibration looked like this, the bearing failed three weeks later. This is machine learning. It can capture effects that nobody wrote an equation for, but it only knows what it has seen. Faced with something new, it can be confidently wrong.

Most serious twins are hybrids. Physics provides the skeleton, and data corrects it for the quirks of this particular machine. Two pumps of the same model, installed in different buildings, wear differently. The physics says how pumps behave; the data says how this pump behaves.

Sensors on the real asset measure things like temperature, pressure, vibration, flow and position. The readings travel over a network, often through the kinds of low-power radio described in the Internet of Things file. Before the model can use them, the data must be cleaned, because sensors drift, fail and occasionally report nonsense.

The model is then adjusted to fit the measurements, a step called calibration. If the model predicted the bearing would be at 60 degrees and the sensor says 68, either the model is wrong or something has changed in the machine. Both are worth knowing. The gap between what the twin expects and what the sensor reports is often the most valuable signal of all. It is how early faults are caught.

Chapter 06

Asking the twin questions

Once the twin matches reality, it can answer three kinds of question.

What is happening now? Some quantities cannot be measured directly. You cannot put a thermometer inside the hottest part of a turbine blade. But a model fed with readings from nearby can estimate it. Engineers call this a virtual sensor.

What will happen? The twin can run forward in time. A common goal is to estimate the remaining useful life of a part, meaning how long it can safely keep working. That allows predictive maintenance: replacing a part shortly before it would fail, rather than on a fixed schedule (too early, wasting money) or after it breaks (too late, causing damage).

What if we change something? Operators can test a new speed, a new route or a new layout on the twin first. A factory can see whether a new production line will create a bottleneck before moving any machines. The twin turns expensive real-world experiments into cheap virtual ones.

Chapter 07

How detailed is detailed enough?

The Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges wrote a one-paragraph story about an empire whose map-makers became so precise that they drew a map the same size as the empire itself. It was useless, and later generations left it to rot in the desert. Digital twins face the same temptation.

A twin's level of detail is called its fidelity. A higher-fidelity twin includes more parts, more physics and more data. It is also slower to run, more expensive to build and harder to keep accurate. The right fidelity depends on the question. To plan when to service a fleet of trucks, a simple model of wear is enough. To understand why one engine blade cracked, you need a detailed physics simulation. A good twin is as simple as the question allows, not as detailed as the budget allows.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

From fixing things after they break to knowing before

Twins matter because breakdowns, downtime and bad decisions about expensive systems cost enormous sums. Seeing inside a system, and rehearsing changes, changes how it is run.

Chapter 08

Jet engines that report on themselves

Large jet engines are among the most closely watched machines on Earth. Engine makers such as Rolls-Royce and GE collect performance data from engines in service and compare it against models of how each engine should behave. When a reading drifts, specialists can plan an inspection at the next convenient stop rather than wait for a fault.

This changed the business. Airlines can pay engine makers for each hour an engine flies, with the maker responsible for keeping it working, an arrangement often called "power by the hour". Under such contracts, the maker earns more when the engine spends less time in the workshop, so it has every reason to invest in monitoring and modelling. The twin is not just a technical tool. It is the basis of a different kind of contract.

Chapter 09

Factories, grids and ports

In manufacturing, twins of production lines let engineers balance the flow of work, spot machines that are about to fail, and design new lines virtually before buying equipment. The wider movement of connected, data-driven factories is often called Industry 4.0, covered in Manufacturing.

Electricity grids are a natural fit. As more power comes from wind and sun, which rise and fall with the weather, grid operators need to predict flows minute by minute and test how the network would cope if a line or a power station failed. A grid twin can run thousands of such scenarios. Wind farm operators use twins to adjust turbines so that the wake of one does not slow the one behind it. Large ports, including Rotterdam, have built twins of their harbours to plan ship movements and berths.

Chapter 10

Cities and the whole planet

Singapore built a detailed 3D model of the entire city-state, known as Virtual Singapore, combining buildings, terrain and infrastructure. Planners can test how a new tower would shade a park, how wind would flow between buildings, or where floodwater would go. Most such city models are closer to a digital model or shadow than a full twin, because live data covers only parts of the city. See Smart cities for the wider debate.

The most ambitious project is the European Union's Destination Earth, launched in 2022. It aims to build highly detailed digital replicas of the Earth system, starting with extreme weather and climate adaptation, so that governments can test how floods, droughts and heat waves would play out under different choices. Weather forecasting itself has worked like a twin for decades: millions of observations are fed into a physics model of the atmosphere several times a day. Your weather forecast is the oldest planetary-scale digital twin.

Chapter 11

A twin of the human body

The boldest version is a twin of a person, or of one organ. Researchers have built detailed computer models of the heart that simulate its electrical signals and the pumping of its chambers. A collaboration called the Living Heart Project, started in 2014, brought companies, doctors and regulators together to develop such models. Some are used to test how medical devices such as stents and pacemakers might behave before they are tried in patients.

Personalised twins remain mostly a research goal. Cardiologists have explored models built from a patient's own scans to plan treatment for heart rhythm problems. A full twin of a whole person, on which doctors could test every drug before giving it, is far away. The body is not a machine with a parts list; it adapts, varies and is still only partly understood. See Personalized medicine.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Cheap experiments, expensive upkeep

A twin can save far more than it costs, but only if it is kept accurate, used to make real decisions, and trusted for the right reasons.

Chapter 13

When the twin drifts from reality

A twin can quietly go out of date. Someone replaces a valve with a different model and nobody updates the software. A sensor is knocked out of position and starts reporting the wrong temperature. Over months, the copy and the original drift apart while the screens still look perfectly confident. This is the twin's version of the old computing rule: garbage in, garbage out.

The deeper risk is automation bias, the human habit of trusting a computer's answer over one's own eyes. If operators believe the twin rather than the alarm bell, a wrong model becomes dangerous. Good practice is to treat the twin as a well-informed adviser: check it against reality regularly, record its known limits, and keep people who understand the physical system. A twin should sharpen human judgment, not replace the people who know the machine.

There is also a commercial trap. Much of the value of a twin comes from data owned by the operator, but the software often belongs to a vendor. As with smart cities, switching later can be expensive. Market forecasts for "digital twin" spending vary enormously between consultancies, which is a sign that the label covers very different things.

Chapter 15

Where AI fits in

Two trends are pushing twins forward together. First, AI models trained on physics simulations, sometimes called surrogate models, can imitate a slow simulation in a fraction of the time. AI weather models from several companies and research centres have shown that this works at global scale. Second, language-based AI makes twins easier to question. An engineer can ask in plain words why a line slowed down yesterday, rather than writing a query.

Both bring a familiar caution. A surrogate is only reliable inside the range of situations it was trained on, and the rare extreme event is exactly what operators most need to get right. The likely future is a twin built on physics, sped up by AI, checked against sensors, and questioned by people who know the real system. The copy is getting faster and easier to talk to; it still has to be kept honest. How people and these systems divide the work is the subject of Human-AI collaboration.

The core idea

A digital twin is a model plus a live data link to a real asset. Sensors keep the copy in step; the copy estimates what cannot be measured, predicts failures, and lets people test changes virtually. It is only as good as its model, its data and the people checking it.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The concept was described around 2002; NASA used the name around 2010.
  • Apollo 13 (1970) is the classic precursor story.
  • The EU's Destination Earth twin of the planet began in 2022.
The common mistake

Calling any 3D model a twin. Without a continuous link to the real thing, it is a model, and with a link but no feedback, it is a shadow.
