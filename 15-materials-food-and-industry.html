Materials, food and industry. How the physical world is actually made.

One idea to carry through this file

In files 11 and 12 the recurring answer was energy and the processing step. Here it is something else, and it is the reason these industries are called "hard to abate":

The CO2 often comes from the chemistry, not the fuel.

You can run a steelworks or a cement kiln on perfectly clean electricity and still emit enormous amounts of carbon dioxide — because the carbon is not coming from what you burn. It is coming from the reaction itself. Once you see that, you understand why these two industries are treated completely differently from power generation.

Part I · The two hard materials

Steel, cement, and plastic

The first two are about fifteen percent of global emissions together. The third is the one everybody thinks they understand.

How it works 01

Steel

In one sentence

Steel is iron with a little carbon in it, and making it is one of the largest single sources of CO2 in the world.

Think of it like…

Un-rusting.

Iron in the ground is already rusted. Iron ore is iron bonded to oxygen — that is what the red colour is. So making iron is not really about heat. It is about taking the oxygen off.

And to pull oxygen away from iron, you need something that wants oxygen more than iron does. For four thousand years, that something has been carbon.

Carbon takes the oxygen. Carbon plus oxygen is carbon dioxide. The CO2 is not a side effect of the fuel. It is the reaction.

How it works
THE TRADITIONAL ROUTE — blast furnace
  ① IRON ORE + COKE + LIMESTONE go into the furnace
    Coke is coal, baked to drive off everything but the
    carbon.
  ② HOT AIR IS BLASTED IN
    The coke burns to carbon monoxide.
  ③ ⭐ THE CARBON MONOXIDE STRIPS THE OXYGEN OFF THE ORE
    iron oxide + CO → iron + CO2
    ⇒ THIS IS THE STEP. The CO2 here is unavoidable in
      this route, no matter how clean your electricity
      is, because carbon is doing the work.
  ④ OUT COMES MOLTEN "PIG IRON"
    With about 4% carbon dissolved in it, which makes it
    hard and brittle and nearly useless.
  ⑤ BLOW OXYGEN THROUGH IT
    To burn most of that carbon back out, down to
    roughly 0.05-2%. THAT is steel. The whole difference
    between brittle iron and structural steel is a
    fraction of a percent of carbon.
  ⑥ ALLOY, CAST, ROLL.

THE SECOND ROUTE — electric arc furnace (EAF)
  Melt SCRAP steel with an enormous electric arc.
  ✓ far lower emissions, and it uses steel that already
    exists
  ⚠ limited by how much scrap there is, and scrap
    accumulates contaminants it is nearly impossible to
    remove — especially COPPER from wiring, which makes
    steel crack when hot. You cannot boil copper out of
    iron.
  ⇒ so recycled steel slowly drifts down the quality
    ladder. It is a real limit, and it is rarely
    mentioned.

⭐ AND THE GREEN ROUTE — swap the carbon for hydrogen
  Use hydrogen to take the oxygen off instead of carbon.
    hydrogen + oxygen → WATER
  ⇒ you get steam instead of carbon dioxide. Then melt
    the resulting iron in an electric furnace.
  ⇒ THIS IS THE STRONGEST GENUINE CASE FOR HYDROGEN
    ANYWHERE (file 11). Not cars. This.
The numberWhat it means
~7-8% of global CO2Steel alone. If the steel industry were a country it would be one of the largest emitters on earth.
~1.8-2 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of steelBy the blast furnace route. Roughly two tonnes of carbon dioxide for every tonne of steel beam.
15-20 years without stoppingA blast furnace runs continuously for that long between relinings. You cannot switch it off and on. Which means the replacement cycle for this industry is measured in decades, not years, and every furnace built today locks in its emissions until the 2040s.
What goes wrong
  • Green steel needs enormous amounts of cheap hydrogen, which does not yet exist at that scale. The constraint is entirely upstream — it is file 11's problem, arriving here.
  • The assets last decades, so change is slow and very capital-heavy. Nobody writes off a working blast furnace early.
  • Steel is a commodity bought on price. Green steel costs more, and a beam is a beam. Which is why the policy instruments are "green premiums" from buyers who choose to pay, and border carbon adjustments to stop cheap high-carbon imports undercutting cleaner domestic producers. That is a trade fight dressed as a climate policy, and both descriptions are accurate.
  • Scrap is finite. You cannot recycle your way to global steel demand, because the steel in existence is smaller than the steel still to be built.
Say this

"Making steel isn't really about heat. It's about taking oxygen off rust — and we use carbon to do it. That's where the CO2 comes from. The chemistry, not the fuel."

"Green steel swaps carbon for hydrogen, so you get steam instead of CO2. This is the strongest case for hydrogen anywhere — not cars."

"The difference between brittle iron and structural steel is about one percent of carbon."

How it works 02

Cement

In one sentence

Cement is the glue in concrete, and making it releases CO2 twice — once from the fuel, and once from the rock itself.

Think of it like…

Cooking limestone. And limestone is carbon dioxide, locked into stone.

Millions of years ago, sea creatures pulled CO2 out of the water to build their shells. Those shells became limestone. The carbon has been sitting there, safely stored, ever since.

Heating it lets it back out. So even with a perfectly clean, zero-emission furnace, making cement still emits — because you are un-doing what the shells did.

How it works
① QUARRY AND CRUSH LIMESTONE
   Calcium carbonate. Mix with some clay and sand.

② INTO THE KILN, AT ABOUT 1,450°C
   An enormous rotating tube, slightly tilted, that the
   material tumbles slowly down.

③ ⭐ CALCINATION — the step that matters
   calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + CARBON DIOXIDE
   The rock gives up its CO2.
   ⇒ THIS IS ABOUT 60% OF CEMENT'S EMISSIONS, AND NO
     CHANGE OF FUEL TOUCHES IT. You could heat the kiln
     with sunlight and this CO2 would still come out.

④ THE OTHER ~40% IS THE FUEL
   Getting to 1,450°C. That part CAN be decarbonised —
   electrically, or with hydrogen, or with waste heat.

⑤ OUT COMES "CLINKER"
   Little grey nodules. Grind them with a bit of gypsum
   and you have cement.

⑥ CEMENT IS NOT CONCRETE
   Concrete = cement + sand + gravel + water.
   Cement is only about 10-15% of concrete by weight —
   and very nearly all of its emissions.
   ⇒ so "low-carbon concrete" almost always means
     "less clinker", not a different rock.

⚠ AND IT DOES NOT DRY. IT REACTS.
  Concrete sets by a chemical reaction with water, not
  by water evaporating. Which is why concrete sets
  perfectly well underwater, and why you keep it damp
  while it cures rather than letting it dry out.
The numberWhat it means
~7-8% of global CO2Cement. Roughly the same as steel. Together the two are around fifteen percent of everything.
~4 billion tonnes a yearConcrete is the second most-used substance on earth, after water. More is poured every year than almost anything else humans do.
Desert sand does not workConcrete needs angular sand that interlocks. Desert sand has been rounded by wind. This is why Dubai imports sand. And sand mining from rivers and coasts is a genuine environmental problem with organised crime attached to it.
What goes wrong
  • You cannot fuel-switch away sixty percent of it. Which makes cement the single strongest case for carbon capture anywhere (file 11) — not as an excuse to keep burning things, but because there is genuinely no other route for that portion.
  • Clinker substitution is the realistic lever — replacing some clinker with fly ash from coal plants, slag from steelmaking, or calcined clay. It works, it is deployed today, and it is limited by supply: fly ash comes from coal plants, which are closing, and slag comes from blast furnaces, which green steel would eliminate. Decarbonising one industry removes the ingredient another was using to decarbonise. That is a genuinely awkward coupling and it is rarely mentioned.
  • New cements face a brutal certification problem. Buildings must stand for a century. No engineer will approve a novel binder without decades of evidence, and you cannot get decades of evidence without deploying it. This is not conservatism for its own sake — it is what happens when the failure mode is a collapsed building.
  • Concrete does re-absorb some CO2 over its life as it slowly carbonates. It is real, and it is nowhere near enough to offset what was released.
Say this

"Cement emits twice: once from the fuel, once from the rock. Limestone is carbon dioxide locked in stone, and heating it lets it out."

"You can't fix that half by switching fuel. Which is why cement is the best argument for carbon capture there is."

"Dubai imports sand. Desert sand is too round — concrete needs grains that interlock."

How it works 03

Plastics, and why most recycling is not

In one sentence

Plastics are long chains of carbon made from oil or gas — and most "recycled" plastic is not recycled.

Think of it like…

A chain made of identical links.

Different plastics are different links. Polyethylene, polypropylene, PET — each is a different unit, repeated thousands of times.

Melt two different chains together and you do not get a blend. You get something weaker than either. Which is why sorting matters so enormously, and why a bale of mixed plastic is worth close to nothing.

How it works, and what recycling actually does
MAKING IT
  ① crack oil or gas into small molecules
  ② link them into long chains — polymerise
  ③ THERMOPLASTICS (most packaging) can be melted and
    reformed. THERMOSETS (epoxy, tyres, most
    electronics) are chemically locked and CANNOT be
    remelted, ever. Different problem entirely.

RECYCLING IT — two routes
  MECHANICAL — what almost all recycling is
    sort → wash → shred → melt → re-pellet
    ⚠ EACH CYCLE SHORTENS THE CHAINS A LITTLE. The
      plastic gets weaker every time. So it is usually
      DOWNCYCLED into something less demanding — a
      bottle becomes fibre, fibre becomes filler,
      filler becomes waste.
    ⇒ it is a delay, not a loop.

  CHEMICAL — break the chain back into its links
    Genuinely closes the loop. Also energy-hungry,
    expensive, and currently a very small share of
    anything.

⭐ THE THING TO KNOW AS A CONSUMER
  THE NUMBER IN THE TRIANGLE IS NOT A RECYCLING PROMISE.
  It is a RESIN IDENTIFICATION CODE — it tells a sorting
  facility which plastic this is. Nothing more.
  ⇒ 1 (PET bottles) and 2 (HDPE) genuinely get recycled
    at scale, because they are valuable and easy to sort
  ⇒ films, pouches, and black plastic mostly do not.
    Black plastic is invisible to the infrared sorters,
    so it goes to landfill regardless of what it is
    made of.
What goes wrong
  • Virgin plastic is usually cheaper than recycled, because it tracks the oil price. When oil is cheap, recycled plastic simply cannot compete, and recyclers go out of business. That is the real reason recycling rates are low — not consumer laziness. The system is downstream of the oil price, and it always has been.
  • Multi-layer packaging is unrecyclable by design. A crisp packet is several different plastics and often aluminium, bonded together to keep the contents fresh. It is genuinely brilliant engineering and it can never be separated economically. The same is true of most pouches.
  • One wrong item can spoil a whole bale. Contamination is the industry's constant complaint and it is a real economic cost, not a moral one.
  • Microplastics are now essentially everywhere — in soil, in rain, in the deep ocean, and in human tissue. The honest position on health effects is that we do not yet know, and "we do not know" is not the same as "it is fine". It is also not the same as alarm.
  • And the honest hierarchy is the one nobody repeats: reduce, then reuse, then recycle. Recycling is last on that list for a reason — it is the weakest of the three, and it received most of the public attention because it required the least change from anyone.
Say this

"The number in the triangle isn't a recycling promise. It just identifies which plastic it is."

"Recycled plastic competes with the oil price. When oil is cheap, recycling loses money — that's the real reason the rates are low."

"A crisp packet is several plastics bonded together. Brilliant engineering, unrecyclable by design."

Part II · Food

Three ways of making and moving what you eat

One that is older than people think, one that is oversold, and one that is invisible and enormous.

How it works 04

Precision fermentation

In one sentence

Using microbes as tiny factories to make one specific protein, instead of getting it from an animal.

Think of it like…

Brewing beer — except you have reprogrammed the yeast to make something else.

Yeast already takes sugar and turns it into alcohol. That is its job and it has been doing it for humans for thousands of years. Give the yeast a different instruction, and it takes sugar and makes a milk protein instead.

The tank, the sugar and the yeast are all the same. Only the instruction changed.

How it works · and why it is far less new than it sounds
① TAKE THE GENE FOR THE PROTEIN YOU WANT
   For example the gene a cow uses to make whey or
   casein.

② PUT IT INTO A MICROBE
   Yeast or a fungus.

③ FEED IT SUGAR IN A FERMENTATION TANK
   Warm, stirred, oxygenated. A brewery, essentially.

④ IT MAKES THE PROTEIN AND SECRETES IT

⑤ FILTER, PURIFY, DRY
   You now have the protein — molecularly identical to
   the animal version — and no animal was involved.
   ⚠ note: the microbe is REMOVED. It is not in the
     final product. This matters for how people react
     to it, and it is almost never explained.

⭐ AND HERE IS THE PART THAT CHANGES THE CONVERSATION
  THIS IS NOT NEW.
  · INSULIN has been made this way since the 1980s.
    Before that it was extracted from PIG PANCREASES,
    which required an animal per patient per year and
    caused allergic reactions.
  · RENNET, the enzyme that curdles milk into cheese,
    used to come from the stomach lining of calves.
    Most hard cheese in the world is now made with
    fermentation-produced rennet.
  ⇒ THAT SECOND ONE IS WORTH SAYING OUT LOUD. Most
    people eating cheese have been eating the product
    of precision fermentation for decades and have
    never once been told.
What goes wrong — and what it is not
  • Cost at scale. Fermentation capacity is expensive and limited, and it is competing with commodity dairy, which is one of the most ruthlessly optimised supply chains on earth. For high-value proteins it already wins. For bulk, not yet.
  • The feedstock is sugar, which has its own land, water and fertiliser footprint. It is better than a cow, and it is not nothing.
  • Labelling fights. Is it milk? The dairy industry says firmly not, and the argument is commercial rather than scientific.
  • And do not confuse three different things, because the press constantly does:
    • Plant-based — plant protein shaped and flavoured to resemble meat. Available now, ordinary technology. Though the Impossible burger's heme, the thing that makes it taste bloody, is precision fermentation.
    • Precision fermentation — this entry. A microbe makes one animal protein. Commercially real today.
    • Cultivated meat — actual animal cells grown in a bioreactor into actual muscle. Vastly harder, vastly more expensive per kilogram, and genuinely years away at any scale. Most breathless "lab-grown meat" headlines are about this one.
Say this

"It's brewing. You've just reprogrammed the yeast to make a milk protein instead of alcohol."

"This isn't new — insulin has been made this way since the eighties. Before that it came from pig pancreases."

"Most cheese already uses fermentation-made rennet rather than calf stomach. Nobody noticed, and nobody objected."

How it works 05

Vertical farming

In one sentence

Growing crops indoors, in stacked layers, under LED light.

Think of it like…

A warehouse of shelves, where each shelf is a field.

You control everything: light, temperature, humidity, nutrients, even the carbon dioxide in the air. No weather. No seasons. No pests, so no pesticides. No soil.

And one enormous trade, which is the whole story: you have replaced free sunlight with electricity you have to buy.

How it works
① STACKED TRAYS, NO SOIL
   HYDROPONICS  roots sit in nutrient-rich water
   AEROPONICS   roots hang in air and are misted

② LEDs TUNED TO WHAT PLANTS ACTUALLY USE
   Plants mostly use red and blue light. Green light is
   largely reflected — which is exactly why leaves look
   green to you.
   ⇒ so the lights are red and blue, which is why every
     photograph of a vertical farm is purple. It is not
     a design choice. It is not wasting energy on
     colours the plant ignores.

③ A CLOSED WATER LOOP
   Water that is not absorbed is captured and
   recirculated. Uses roughly 90-95% less water than
   field farming.

④ CLIMATE CONTROL MEANS NO SEASONS
   10-15 or more harvests a year instead of 2 or 3.
   Combined with stacking, yield per square metre of
   FLOOR can be tens of times field farming.
What goes wrong — and this is a case study in a good technology oversold
  • Electricity is the entire economics. Exactly like desalination in file 11. When energy prices spiked in 2022, several of the best-funded vertical farming companies in the world went bankrupt within months. Not because the growing failed — because the arithmetic did.
  • It only works for high-value, fast-growing, low-light crops. Leafy greens, herbs, microgreens. Possibly some berries. That is the list.
  • ⭐ And it will never work for calories. Wheat, rice, maize and potatoes need far more light per kilogram and sell for far less. The light cost per calorie is hopeless and no improvement in LED efficiency closes a gap that large. You will not see vertical wheat. Anyone telling you vertical farms will feed cities is describing salad and calling it food security.
  • The honest claim is still good: fresh salad and herbs, grown next to the city that eats them, all year round, with no pesticides, almost no water, and no air freight. That is a genuinely worthwhile thing. It is just much smaller than what was sold to investors.
Say this

"Vertical farming replaces free sunlight with electricity you pay for. That's the whole economics, and it's why they went bankrupt when energy prices spiked."

"It works for salad. It will never work for wheat — the light cost per calorie is hopeless."

"The lights are purple because plants don't use green light. That's also why leaves look green."

How it works 06

The cold chain

In one sentence

Keeping something cold from the farm or factory all the way to you, without a single gap.

Think of it like…

A chain — and the metaphor is exact, which is rare.

It is only as strong as its weakest link. Every handover is a risk: truck to warehouse, warehouse to van, van to shop, shop to your bag.

Break it once, for an hour, and the product may be ruined while looking completely fine. That is what makes it hard. The failure is invisible, and it happened three days ago, two countries away.

How it works
① THE REFRIGERATION ITSELF
   Exactly the same vapour-compression cycle as the heat
   pump in file 11 — just pointed the normal way round.
   Compress a gas and it gets hot. Let it expand and it
   gets cold. Move the heat from inside to outside.
   ⇒ a fridge and a heat pump are the same machine. One
     is sold for what it takes away, the other for what
     it delivers.

② THE CHAIN, LINK BY LINK
   chilled store → refrigerated truck → distribution
   centre → van → shop chiller → your fridge
   Plus "reefer" containers for sea freight, which are
   plugged into ship's power for weeks.

③ THE TEMPERATURE BANDS
   ambient · chilled (about 2-8°C) · frozen (-18°C) ·
   ultra-cold (-70°C, for some vaccines — see the mRNA
   entry in file 13)

④ MONITORING, AND WHY THE RECORD MATTERS
   Data loggers travel with the load; increasingly they
   report continuously.
   ⚠ AND THE RECORD MATTERS AS MUCH AS THE TEMPERATURE.
     A pallet with a gap in its temperature log is
     rejected even if the food is perfect — because
     nobody can prove it is. In pharmaceuticals this is
     absolute.
The numberWhat it means
Roughly a third of all food producedIs lost or wasted, on the most-cited estimates. In lower-income countries a large share of that is cold chain failure between the farm and the market — food that was grown, harvested, and then rotted before it could be sold.
The weak link is the handoverNot the big refrigerated warehouse, which is monitored and reliable. It is the loading bay, the last mile, the van that waited.
Refrigerant leaks are potentMany refrigerants are greenhouse gases thousands of times stronger than CO2 per kilogram. The Kigali Amendment is phasing the worst of them down — and this is one of the quiet climate wins nobody talks about.
What goes wrong
  • Power cuts in countries with unreliable grids destroy both food and vaccines. The unglamorous fix — solar-powered fridges for clinics — has done an enormous amount of good and receives almost no attention.
  • Vaccine wastage from cold chain breaks is a large global health problem, and it is logistics, not medicine.
  • And it is why some fruit tastes worse than it used to. Produce is bred and picked to survive the journey — firm enough to handle, ripening on the way. Flavour is traded for transportability. That is not a conspiracy; it is an optimisation, and you are eating the result of it.
Say this

"It's called a chain because it's only as strong as the weakest link — and the weak link is almost always a handover, not the warehouse."

"About a third of the world's food is lost or wasted, and a lot of that is a refrigeration gap between the farm and the market."

"A fridge and a heat pump are the same machine pointed in opposite directions."

Part III · The invisible utilities

Two systems you never think about

One saved more lives than any medicine. The other barely exists yet and is about to matter enormously.

How it works 07

Water treatment

In one sentence

Making water safe to drink, and making used water safe to give back.

Think of it like…

Two opposite journeys, run by the same people.

On the way in: take river or ground water and remove everything that could hurt you.

On the way out: take sewage and remove enough that a river can cope with what is left.

The second one is not about making sewage clean. It is about making it survivable for the river, which is a lower and much more achievable bar.

How it works · both directions
DRINKING WATER — on the way in
  ① SCREEN, then COAGULATE
    Add a chemical (usually alum) that makes tiny
    suspended particles clump together into bigger ones.
    Too small to remove becomes big enough to remove.
  ② SETTLE
    The clumps sink.
  ③ FILTER
    Through sand and gravel, or through membranes.
  ④ ⭐ DISINFECT — chlorine, UV, or ozone
    THIS IS THE STEP.
    ⇒ Chlorinating public drinking water is plausibly
      the single largest life-saving intervention in
      human history. Not a medicine. Not a vaccine. A
      chemical added to water at the start of the
      twentieth century, which collapsed death rates
      from cholera, typhoid and dysentery in every city
      that adopted it.
  ⑤ AND LEAVE SOME CHLORINE IN THE PIPES ON PURPOSE
    A small residual travels with the water, so that if
    something contaminates a leaky pipe two kilometres
    later, it is still killed.
    ⇒ that is why tap water smells faintly of chlorine.
      It is not a failure of treatment. It is the
      treatment, still working.

WASTEWATER — on the way out
  ① PRIMARY   screen out solids, let heavy matter settle
  ② ⭐ SECONDARY — and this is the interesting part
    YOU ARE FARMING BACTERIA.
    Aerate the water and let colonies of microbes eat
    the dissolved organic matter. They multiply, clump
    together, and settle out — and you recycle some of
    them back to keep the population going.
    ⇒ a sewage works is a livestock operation. The
      livestock is invisible and it is doing all the
      work.
  ③ TERTIARY  remove nitrogen and phosphorus, so the
    river does not get an algal bloom (see the
    fertiliser entry, file 12), then disinfect
What goes wrong
  • Leaks, and they are enormous. Many networks lose 20-40% of treated water between the plant and the tap. The cheapest new water supply available to most cities is fixing the pipes they already have — and it is almost never funded, because a repaired pipe has no ribbon to cut and a new plant does. This is the same pattern as file 11's desalination entry, and it is worth noticing how often it recurs.
  • PFAS — "forever chemicals". Conventional treatment does not remove them, because they were engineered not to break down. Removing them requires activated carbon or ion exchange at large scale, and it is becoming one of the biggest cost items in the water industry in several countries.
  • Combined sewers. In older cities, rainwater and sewage share the same pipes. Heavy rain overwhelms the works, and the overflow — raw — goes into the river by design, because the alternative is it coming up through the floor. Fixing this means digging up cities.
  • Energy. Treating and pumping water is often among the largest electricity consumers a municipality has.
Say this

"Chlorinating drinking water probably saved more lives than any medicine ever invented."

"Sewage treatment is farming bacteria. You aerate the water and let microbes eat the waste — that's the heart of it."

"The cheapest new water supply in most cities is fixing the leaks. It just doesn't get a ribbon-cutting."

How it works 08

Battery recycling

In one sentence

Getting the lithium, nickel and cobalt back out of dead batteries.

Think of it like…

An urban mine.

The metals in a used battery sit at far higher concentration than in any ore in the ground. A tonne of old battery packs is a richer source of nickel and cobalt than a tonne of rock from a mine — by a large margin.

So the question was never whether it is worth doing. It is whether there is anything to do it to.

How it works
① DISCHARGE AND DISMANTLE
   Carefully. A charged battery that gets short-circuited
   during disassembly catches fire, and a lithium fire
   is very hard to put out.

② SHRED INTO "BLACK MASS"
   A dark powder containing all the valuable metals
   mixed together. This is the traded intermediate — you
   will see the term in industry news.

③ THEN ONE OF THREE ROUTES
   PYROMETALLURGY — smelt it
     simple, robust, high energy
     ⚠ and you LOSE the lithium and aluminium into the
       slag. It works and it throws away the thing the
       battery was named after
   HYDROMETALLURGY — dissolve in acid, separate
     chemically
     more complex, lower energy, recovers far more
     INCLUDING the lithium
     ⇒ this is where the industry is going
   DIRECT RECYCLING — recover the cathode material
     intact, without breaking it down to elements
     best in theory, hardest in practice, mostly still
     research
What goes wrong — and the fact almost everyone gets wrong
  • ⭐ There is not much to recycle yet. This is the single most misunderstood thing about the sector. The first large wave of electric cars is still on the road. Most of what recyclers actually process today is manufacturing scrap from battery factories, not end-of-life packs. When you read that a recycler is running at capacity, that is usually what they are running on.
  • And "second life" delays it further. A car battery down to 70-80% capacity is finished for a car — and perfectly good for grid storage, where weight and volume do not matter. That is an excellent outcome for the world and it pushes the recycling feedstock out by roughly another decade, which is awkward for anyone who built a recycling plant on a ten-year plan.
  • The chemistry keeps moving. A plant designed around one cathode chemistry faces a different dominant chemistry ten years later. Designing a process flexible enough to handle what has not been invented yet is genuinely difficult.
  • Collection is the boring bottleneck. Dead batteries are heavy, classified as dangerous goods, and expensive to transport. Much of the cost is logistics, not chemistry.
  • Regulation is driving this more than economics — the EU battery regulation sets mandatory recycled content and collection targets. That is a case where the rule arrived before the market, deliberately, to make sure the capacity exists when the wave finally lands.
Say this

"A dead battery is a richer ore than anything in the ground. That's why they call it urban mining."

"There isn't much to recycle yet. Most of what recyclers process is factory scrap — the first big wave of EVs is still driving around."

"Before recycling there's second life. A battery that's finished for a car is fine for grid storage, and that delays everything by another decade."

Reference

The words, in plain words

When they say…They mean
Hard to abateIndustries where the CO2 comes from the chemistry, not just the fuel — so clean electricity alone does not fix them. Steel, cement, chemicals, aviation, shipping.
Process emissionsCO2 released by the reaction itself rather than by burning something. The 60% of cement's emissions you cannot fuel-switch away.
ClinkerThe grey nodules that come out of a cement kiln. Grind them and you have cement. "Low-clinker" is the main realistic lever on cement emissions.
Reduction (in metals)Removing oxygen. The opposite of oxidation. Making iron from ore is a reduction — you are un-rusting it.
Scope 1, 2, 3Emissions you make directly / from the electricity you buy / from your whole supply chain and your products in use. Scope 3 is usually by far the largest and by far the least reliably measured.
Circular economyDesigning so materials keep being used rather than discarded. A good aim, and mostly still an aim.
DowncyclingRecycling into something of lower quality. What most plastic recycling actually is — a delay rather than a loop.
FeedstockThe raw material going into a process. Sugar for fermentation, oil for plastics, scrap for an arc furnace.
Hydroponics / aeroponicsRoots in nutrient water / roots in mist. Both mean no soil.
ReeferA refrigerated shipping container. Plugged into the ship's power for the whole voyage.
Black massThe shredded powder from dead batteries, containing all the valuable metals. Now traded internationally as a commodity.
PFAS"Forever chemicals" — engineered not to break down, which is exactly why they are useful and exactly why they are a problem. Conventional water treatment does not remove them.

Three threads running through all eight
⭐ ① THE CO2 OFTEN COMES FROM THE CHEMISTRY, NOT THE FUEL
  steel   carbon strips the oxygen off the ore
  cement  the limestone gives up its own CO2
  ⇒ which is why these are called HARD TO ABATE, and why
    clean electricity alone does not solve them. It is
    also the honest case for carbon capture, which is a
    much narrower case than its advocates make and a
    much stronger one than its critics allow.

⭐ ② THE UNGLAMOROUS FIX IS USUALLY THE BEST ONE
  fixing water leaks  > building a new plant
  reduce and reuse    > recycling
  second life         > recycling
  clinker substitution> novel cement
  ⇒ AND IT NEVER GETS FUNDED, because there is nothing
    to open, no ribbon, no photograph. Watch how often
    this pattern appears once you know to look for it.

⭐ ③ ECONOMICS DECIDES, NOT TECHNOLOGY
  plastic recycling   loses to the oil price
  vertical farming    lost to the electricity price
  green steel         loses to ordinary steel on price
  battery recycling   waits for feedstock that does not
                      exist yet
  ⇒ in almost every case here the technology works and
    the arithmetic does not. That is a different kind of
    problem and it needs a different kind of fix —
    usually a rule, a price, or a guaranteed buyer.

⇒ SO WHEN YOU MEET A NEW INDUSTRIAL STORY, ASK:
  ① WHERE EXACTLY DOES THE CO2 COME FROM?
  ② WHAT IS THE BORING VERSION OF THIS FIX, AND WHY IS
    NOBODY DOING IT?
  ③ DOES THIS WORK AT TODAY'S PRICES, OR ONLY AT
    HOPED-FOR ONES?

On the figures. Emissions shares, recycling rates and food waste estimates here are the commonly cited ranges and they are genuinely uncertain — different bodies measure them differently, and the "one third of food is wasted" figure in particular covers very different things in different studies. They are here for order of magnitude. The chemistry, which is the part that matters most in this file, does not move.
