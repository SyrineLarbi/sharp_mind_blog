Sharp Mind 01 · the foundation

Thinking fast without thinking badly. Speed is retrieval, not processing.

The person who answers well in three seconds is not running a faster processor than you. They are pulling a pre-built answer off a shelf you have not stocked, and using a small number of moves to buy themselves the time to find it. This handbook takes that apart into mechanisms you can build: what expertise physically is, why you blank, what to do in the three seconds after a hard question, and how to stock the shelf on purpose.

Why this handbook exists

Every other handbook in this library assumes you can produce something in the moment. File 02 gives you better questions, 03 gives you better structure, 04 gives you better delivery — and all three are useless if your mind goes white the second attention lands on you.

So this one comes first, and it is the only one that deals with the raw mechanics: what is actually happening in the seconds between a question and your answer, and which parts of that you can control.

What this does not cover

The psychology of fast thinking is taught next door and is not repeated here. System 1 and System 2, the cognitive biases that fast thinking is prone to, and how to debias a decision are in master-yourself/phase-1, file 01. The anxiety version of going blank — the racing heart, the self-monitoring, the safety behaviours — is a different mechanism with a different fix, and it lives in phase-11, file 01. Section 08 here draws the line between the two carefully, because treating one with the other's remedy is why people stay stuck.

Part I · Where speed comes from

The machinery

Four sections on what quick thinking physically is. If you only take one idea from this handbook, take the one in section 02 — everything else in the library is downstream of it.

Thinking Fast 01

The myth of the quick mind

Sit in a meeting with someone who is obviously sharp and you will see the same thing every time. Someone finishes a rambling six-minute update. There is a pause. The sharp person says: "So the launch slips two weeks unless the vendor confirms by Friday — is that the whole of it, or is there a second dependency?"

What you conclude, watching that, is that they thought faster than you. They did not. Break the sentence into its parts and nothing in it required speed:

What they didWhat it actually required
Compressed six minutes into one clauseRecognising the shape of the update — "this is a dependency-and-date problem" — which they had seen forty times before
Named the real constraintKnowing that in this kind of problem the constraint is nearly always a date or an approval, so knowing where to look
Asked whether there was a second oneA stock question they ask in every dependency conversation, held ready, not invented on the spot

None of that is processing speed. It is recognition plus a stocked shelf. They recognised the category of the situation, and they had a question already built for that category. The three seconds they took were not spent computing — they were spent retrieving.

This matters enormously, because the two explanations lead to completely different lives. If quick people are quick because their minds run faster, there is nothing you can do and you should stop reading. If they are quick because they have recognised this shape before and pre-built the response, then quickness is a stock problem, and stock can be built deliberately.

The reframe this whole library runs on

  the bottleneck is not effort — it is what is already built.

There is a second reason the "fast mind" story is worth killing. It quietly tells you that when you freeze, it is because your mind is not good enough. That is both false and paralysing. You freeze for mechanical reasons, covered in section 08, and each of them has a fix.

The question is never "how do I think faster?" It is always "what should I have already had ready?"
Thinking Fast 02

Chunking — what an expert physically has that you do not

The clearest evidence for all of this comes from chess, and it is worth knowing properly because it changes how you practise.

In the 1940s the Dutch psychologist and chess master Adriaan de Groot showed positions from real games to players of different strengths for a few seconds, then took the board away and asked them to reconstruct it. Grandmasters reconstructed the position almost perfectly. Ordinary club players managed a fraction of it. The obvious conclusion — grandmasters have extraordinary memories — turned out to be wrong.

In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon ran the decisive follow-up. They repeated the experiment, but with a second condition: some boards held real positions from real games, and some held pieces scattered at random. On real positions, the masters crushed the novices, exactly as before. On random positions, the masters' advantage almost completely disappeared.

The chunking experiment, and what it means for you
REAL GAME POSITION           RANDOM SCATTER OF PIECES
  master  ████████████ ~93%    master  ██ ~small edge
  novice  ███ ~25%             novice  ██ ~the same

⭐ THE MASTER'S MEMORY IS NOT BIGGER. IT IS BETTER ORGANISED.

  the novice sees:  24 separate pieces
                    → 24 things to remember
                    → working memory holds maybe 4
                    → recalls ~25%

  the master sees:  "castled kingside, standard pawn chain,
                     knight outpost on d5, rook doubling"
                    → 4 or 5 MEANINGFUL UNITS
                    → working memory holds 4 or 5
                    → recalls almost all of it

⇒ A CHUNK IS A PATTERN THAT HAS BECOME A SINGLE UNIT.
  Same brain, same memory limit. Bigger units.

⚠ SCRAMBLE THE BOARD AND THE ADVANTAGE VANISHES, because
  random pieces form no patterns, so there is nothing to
  chunk, and the master is back to holding 24 things too.

Working memory — the small amount you can actively hold at once — is famously limited. George Miller's 1956 paper put it at "seven, plus or minus two"; later work by Nelson Cowan and others narrowed it to roughly four chunks for most material. Either way, the limit is small, it is roughly fixed, and no amount of practice raises it.

What practice does instead is change what counts as one item. This is the whole game. You cannot expand the shelf, so you make each thing on it bigger.

What a beginner holds

Reading a supplier contract: this clause, that clause, this date, that penalty, this counterparty, that jurisdiction. Twelve separate items competing for four slots. Nothing is left over to think about them with.

What someone experienced holds

"Standard framework agreement, aggressive termination clause, penalties are the negotiable part." Three chunks. Nine slots free — which is exactly the free capacity that looks, from the outside, like fast thinking.

This is why the sharp person in the meeting had room to ask a good question. They were not spending their working memory holding the update together. The update had collapsed into one chunk on arrival, leaving them free to inspect it.

The consequence people miss

Chunks are domain-specific. The chess masters were only masters on chess boards; put them in front of a scattered board, or a supplier contract, and their advantage evaporated. There is no such thing as a generally quick mind — there are people who have chunked the situations they keep finding themselves in. Which means the practice that pays is not "brain training". It is building chunks in the specific situations you actually face, which is what section 10 and the program in file 10 are for.

Thinking Fast 03

How experts actually decide — the first option, not the best one

The natural assumption about good decision-makers is that they generate several options and compare them. The psychologist Gary Klein spent years watching people decide under real time pressure — fireground commanders, ICU nurses, military officers — expecting to document that comparison process. He found something else.

Experienced fireground commanders, asked how they chose between options, frequently said they had not chosen at all. They looked at the fire, recognised what kind of fire it was, and one course of action simply presented itself. They then ran it forward in their head — if we go in through the back, what happens? — and if the simulation showed no disaster, they did it. If it showed a problem, they modified it or moved to the next option that came to mind. Klein called this the recognition-primed decision model.

Recognition-primed decision (Klein) — the loop under expert speed
THE TEXTBOOK MODEL                THE ACTUAL MODEL
  generate options A, B, C          ① SIZE UP THE SITUATION
  score each on criteria               "this is a ____ kind of problem"
  compare the scores                ② ONE OPTION SURFACES
  select the winner                    typical for that kind of problem
                                    ③ MENTALLY SIMULATE IT
  slow · deliberate · rare              "if I do this, then what?"
  under time pressure               ④ WORKABLE? → DO IT
                                       PROBLEM? → tweak it, or take
                                                  the next option

⭐ EXPERTS DO NOT PICK THE BEST OPTION.
  THEY TAKE THE FIRST WORKABLE ONE AND TEST IT IN THEIR HEAD.

⇒ THE EXPERTISE IS ALL IN STEP ①.
  Recognising the situation type is what makes the option that
  surfaces a good one. A novice runs the same loop — but the
  option that surfaces for them is bad, because the size-up was.

⚠ WHICH IS WHY "CONSIDER MORE OPTIONS" IS BAD ADVICE UNDER
  TIME PRESSURE. The fix is a better size-up, not a longer list.

Two things follow from this, and both are directly usable.

First: the leverage is in the size-up. If you correctly identify what kind of situation you are in, a decent response tends to present itself. If you misidentify it, no amount of subsequent cleverness recovers. This is why section 05 — naming the category before answering — is the central move in this handbook.

Second: mental simulation is the quality check that makes speed safe. The step between "an option surfaced" and "I said it out loud" is where the whole difference between fast and reckless sits. It takes about two seconds: run it forward one step and see if it breaks.

The two-second check, in words

Before you say the thing that just came to you, ask: "And then what happens?" One step forward, no more. If the next step is fine, say it. If the next step is obviously bad, you have just saved yourself in-flight, and nobody in the room ever knew there was a first draft.

Where this connects

Klein's other famous contribution — the pre-mortem, where you imagine the project has already failed and work backwards to why — is a slow-thinking cousin of the same mental simulation, and it is taught in master-yourself/phase-8, file 02, along with decision journals and probabilistic thinking. That file is about deciding well over days. This one is about deciding well over seconds.

Thinking Fast 04

Pre-loading versus computing live

Here is the single most practical consequence of everything above. In almost any meeting, interview, or conversation that matters, most of what you will need to say could have been prepared. Not scripted — prepared. The questions people ask are far less varied than they feel in the moment.

Watch what actually gets asked in a status meeting over a month and you will find the same six questions in rotation: where are we, what is blocked, what do you need, when will it be done, what changed since last time, what is the risk. An interview loop is even more predictable. A quarterly review is nearly a script.

THE TWO WAYS TO ARRIVE AT AN ANSWER COMPUTING LIVE PRE-LOADED ────────────── ────────── question lands question lands │ │ ▼ ▼ parse it recognise it │ │ ▼ ▼ search memory ─── slow, and retrieve the answer under pressure, unreliable you already built │ │ ▼ ▼ assemble a structure deliver it │ ( ~1 second ) ▼ find the words spare capacity left over │ to read the room, adjust, ▼ and add the thing that deliver it makes it land ( 6-12 seconds, visibly ) ⇒ SAME PERSON. SAME BRAIN. THE ONLY DIFFERENCE IS WHETHER THE WORK HAPPENED BEFORE OR DURING.

The uncomfortable part is that pre-loading is boring, unglamorous, and done alone the night before. It has none of the appeal of "being quick on your feet". But it is what being quick on your feet is made of, and the people you admire for it are almost all doing it — they are simply not doing it in front of you.

The 80/20 of pre-loading

You do not need to prepare for everything. Before anything that matters, prepare exactly four things. This takes about ten minutes and it is the highest-return ten minutes in this entire library.

The ten-minute pre-load · run before any meeting that matters
① THE THREE QUESTIONS THEY WILL ASK ME
   Not ten. Three. The ones you would ask if you were them.
   Write a 30-second answer to each, out loud, once.
   → this is where 80% of your "quick thinking" will come from

② THE THREE QUESTIONS I WILL ASK THEM
   Pulled from your bank (file 02). Having these ready means
   you are never the person with "no, nothing from me".
   → also: an asked question buys you 40 seconds of thinking

③ ONE NUMBER I KNOW COLD
   A single figure, with its source and its date.
   "Returns ran 4.2% of shipped units last quarter, from the
    November ops report."
   → one exact number does more for credibility than five
     adjectives. It also anchors the discussion on your ground.

④ THE ONE THING I WANT THEM TO REMEMBER
   One sentence. If they forget everything else, this survives.
   → and it goes FIRST, not last (file 03)

⚠ NOTE WHAT IS NOT ON THIS LIST: a script, slides, or a
  rehearsed monologue. Those make you rigid. These four make
  you loaded.
Do this once, this week

Take the next meeting in your calendar that you are slightly nervous about. Spend ten minutes on the four items above. Then notice, during the meeting, how much of what you say comes off the shelf rather than out of the air — and how much attention that frees up for actually listening. It is the cheapest demonstration of this handbook's whole argument.

Part II · The moves

What to actually do in the room

Five techniques for the seconds between a question and your answer. Each is small enough to use tomorrow, and they compound: the category tells you the question, the pause gives you room to find it, the skeleton gets it out of your mouth in order.

Thinking Fast 05

Name the category before you answer — the central move

This is the technique that does the most work in this handbook, and it follows directly from Klein: the expertise is in the size-up. So make the size-up explicit.

When a hard question or a messy situation lands, do not reach for an answer. Reach for a label. Almost everything that gets discussed in working life is one of five kinds of problem, and once you know which one you are in, the right response narrows dramatically.

CategoryYou are in it whenThe move it calls for
Definition People are using the same word for different things. The argument keeps restarting. Nobody can say what "done" is. Stop and define. "Before we go further — when we say 'late', do we mean past the promised date or past the internal target? I think we mean different things."
Data The disagreement is about what is true, and it is answerable. Two people have different numbers. Do not argue. Establish what would settle it. "This is checkable. What number would change your mind, and where would we get it?"
Trade-off Everyone agrees on the facts and still disagrees. Both options are defensible. Someone says "why can't we do both?" Name the axis being traded. "We're trading speed against reversibility here. Which one are we optimising for this quarter?"
Decision rights The discussion has gone round twice. Nobody in the room can actually say yes. The phrase "we should probably..." keeps appearing. Surface the owner. "Who signs off on this? If it isn't anyone here, the useful output of this meeting is a recommendation, not a decision."
Sequencing Everyone agrees on what to do and it still is not happening. Multiple things are "in progress". Nothing finishes. Force an order. "I think we agree on all four. What has to be true before we can start the second one — is there an order here we're not saying?"
The category move, compressed to a card
⭐ WHEN A HARD QUESTION LANDS, DO NOT REACH FOR AN ANSWER.
  REACH FOR A LABEL.

  ① DEFINITION      · same word, different meanings
                    → define it out loud
  ② DATA            · answerable, and unanswered
                    → name what would settle it
  ③ TRADE-OFF       · agreed facts, unresolved values
                    → name the axis
  ④ DECISION RIGHTS · no one here can say yes
                    → find who can
  ⑤ SEQUENCING      · agreed actions, no order
                    → force the order

⇒ NAMING THE CATEGORY OUT LOUD IS ITSELF THE CONTRIBUTION.
  Half the time you do not need to solve anything. Saying
  "I think we're stuck on a definition, not a decision" ends
  a twenty-minute loop, and it is the thing people remember.

⚠ AND IT BUYS YOU TIME HONESTLY. While you say the label,
  you are thinking about the content. That is the trick
  hiding inside the technique.

Why this works even when you know nothing about the subject

This is the part worth dwelling on. You can be the least knowledgeable person in a meeting and still name the category correctly, because categories are about the shape of the disagreement, not its content. You do not need to understand the vendor's pricing model to notice that two people are using "cost" to mean different things.

That makes this the fastest route to being useful in an unfamiliar room — and it is the exact opposite of the usual instinct, which is to stay quiet until you understand the domain.

Field-tested phrasings
  • "Can I check something — are we disagreeing about what's true, or about what to do about it?" (separates data from trade-off in one sentence)
  • "I might be behind, but I think we're using 'pilot' two different ways." (definition, with a face-saving preface)
  • "Is this ours to decide, or ours to recommend?" (decision rights, without accusing anyone)
  • "We agree on all of it. Do we agree on the order?" (sequencing — often the whole unlock)
Thinking Fast 06

The three-second pause, and what happens inside it

Almost everyone answers too fast. Not because they are quick, but because silence is uncomfortable and filling it feels like competence. It is the reverse. The rushed answer is the one that comes out unstructured, hedged, and longer than it needed to be.

Three seconds of silence before answering does two separate jobs, and it is worth being clear that they are separate.

Job one · it buys retrieval time

Three seconds is genuinely enough to run the size-up from section 05 and let a response surface. Not to construct an argument from nothing — but you are not constructing from nothing, you are retrieving. Three seconds is a lot of retrieval.

Job two · it reads as authority

A pause before answering signals that the question was taken seriously and that you are not anxious to please. The person who answers instantly reads as reactive; the person who pauses reads as considered. This is unfair, and it is consistent.

The problem is that three seconds of silence feels, from the inside, like thirty. So make the pause audible. A pause you narrate is a pause nobody experiences as a gap:

Filling three seconds without filling them with "um"
THE PAUSE, MADE AUDIBLE — pick one, mean it

  "Let me think about that for a second."
     → the strongest. Almost nobody says it. It is read as
       confidence every single time, because only someone
       unworried about the silence would announce it.

  "Good question — give me a moment to get it right."
     → warm version. Slight risk of sounding like a stall if
       overused; keep it for genuinely hard questions.

  "Two things — " ... (then think while you say the first)
     → the structural stall (file 03). Announcing the shape
       commits you to nothing and buys the whole first clause.

  "Just so I answer the right question: do you mean X or Y?"
     → the best one when you genuinely are not sure. Buys
       ~8 seconds AND makes your answer more accurate.

⚠ WHAT NOT TO DO
   · "um", "so", "yeah, I mean" — these fill the same time
     and cost credibility instead of building it (file 04)
   · starting the sentence before you know how it ends, then
     rescuing it mid-flight. Everyone can hear that.
   · answering the question you wish they had asked
A caution on the pause

Three seconds, not ten. There is a real threshold past which a pause stops reading as considered and starts reading as stuck, and it is somewhere around four or five seconds of pure silence. If you need longer than that, you need one of the honest stalls in section 09 — which buy you thirty seconds legitimately — rather than a longer silence.

Thinking Fast 07

The five-second answer skeleton

You have paused, you have named the category to yourself, and something has surfaced. Now it has to come out of your mouth in an order that makes sense. Under pressure this is where most people lose it — not for lack of an answer, but because the answer arrives as a pile rather than a sequence.

The fix is to have one skeleton, always the same, that you can pour any content into. This is a compressed preview of the Minto structure taught properly in file 03 — here it is only the emergency version, the one you can execute in the two seconds after the pause.

The skeleton · learn this one shape and use it for everything
⭐ POSITION · REASON · QUALIFIER · HANDBACK

  ① POSITION    the answer, in one sentence, first
                "Yes — I'd push the launch."

  ② REASON      one or two, no more, said as a count
                "Two reasons. The vendor hasn't confirmed,
                 and we'd be launching into the holiday freeze."

  ③ QUALIFIER   the honest boundary of what you know
                "That's assuming the vendor date is real —
                 I haven't verified it myself."

  ④ HANDBACK    return the floor deliberately
                "What's your read on the vendor?"

⇒ TOTAL: about 15 seconds of speech.
  And the QUALIFIER is the part that makes it credible, not
  the part that weakens it — it is the difference between
  confident and overclaiming.

WORKED, UNDER PRESSURE
  Q: "Do you think we should switch suppliers?"
  ① "Not yet."
  ② "One reason: we've only got one bad quarter of data,
      and the last two were fine."
  ③ "If the next month looks the same, I'd change my answer."
  ④ "What's driving the timing on the question?"

  ⇒ fifteen seconds, a clear position, an honest boundary,
    and the floor handed back. Nothing about it required
    you to be fast.
Why the handback matters more than it looks

Ending on a question does three things at once: it stops you over-explaining, which is the single most common way good answers get ruined; it moves the burden of the next thirty seconds to someone else while you recover; and it makes you look interested rather than performing. Ending an answer cleanly is a skill in itself, and it is covered further in file 04.

The one-reason rule

Give one strong reason, or two. Never four. This is counterintuitive — surely more support is more convincing? — but stacking reasons has a well-known effect: your argument is judged near the strength of its weakest member, not its strongest, because that is the one people attack. Four reasons gives your listener four choices of where to push, and they will pick the softest.

What over-supporting sounds like

"I think we should delay, because the vendor hasn't confirmed, and also the holiday freeze is coming, and honestly the QA team is stretched, and I'm not sure marketing is ready either, and there was that issue last time..."

Every clause after the second one weakens the case. The listener now argues about marketing's readiness — your weakest point — and the vendor confirmation, which was decisive, never gets discussed.

Thinking Fast 08

When you go blank — the mechanics, and which fix applies

Going blank is not one thing. It has at least three distinct causes, they feel almost identical from the inside, and they need different responses. Applying the wrong fix is why people conclude they are simply "bad under pressure" and stop trying.

CauseHow to tell it is this oneThe fix
Overload The question had four parts, or the preceding update was long and you were still holding it. You feel scattered rather than afraid. You could answer any one part if asked separately. Dump the load. "There are three things in that — can I take them one at a time?" You have just converted four chunks into one, and freed your working memory to actually work.
Nothing stored You genuinely have not thought about this before. It is not that you cannot find the answer — there is no answer to find. Calm but empty. Say so, with a date. The move in section 09. Trying to generate expertise live is the failure mode here, and everyone can tell.
Threat response Physical: heart, heat, tunnel vision, hearing your own voice from outside. You know the answer and cannot reach it. Afterwards it comes back instantly — l'esprit de l'escalier. This one is not a thinking problem and thinking techniques will not touch it. See the note below.
The important distinction in this handbook

The third row is anxiety, not cognition. Under a threat response, attention turns inward onto self-monitoring — how am I doing, how do I look, they can tell — and inward attention is precisely what makes retrieval fail, because the retrieval needs the attention that self-monitoring is consuming.

No amount of skeleton-learning fixes that, and the honest thing to say is that this handbook is the wrong tool for it. The right tools — the cognitive model of social anxiety, safety behaviours, graded exposure, reappraising arousal as excitement rather than fear — are taught properly in master-yourself/phase-11, file 01 and file 03. If your blanking is mostly this, go there first and come back. The two bodies of work reinforce each other; neither substitutes for the other.

The one thing that helps all three

There is a single move that reduces every version of blanking, and it is not a mental technique at all. It is writing during the meeting.

Keeping a running note — the claim, the number, the question you want to ask, in three or four words each — offloads working memory onto paper. You are no longer holding the update; the paper is. That is capacity returned to you directly, in the moment, and it works whether you are overloaded or anxious. It also gives you somewhere to look during the three-second pause, which is worth more than it sounds.

THE RUNNING NOTE — what it actually looks like ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ vendor conf → Fri? ← the fact │ │ 2wk slip ← the number │ │ ?? who owns the date ← the question │ │ !! holiday freeze ← the thing to raise │ └─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ ? = a question I want to ask !! = a point I want to make → = an open dependency ⇒ four symbols, three words a line. Not minutes — a working memory extension. When the room turns to you, you are reading, not searching.
Thinking Fast 09

The four honest stalls

Sometimes three seconds is not enough and you need real time. There are exactly four ways to buy it that cost you nothing in credibility, and each buys a different amount.

Buying time without paying for it
① THE CLARIFIER            buys ~8 seconds
   "Just so I answer the right thing — do you mean the
    cost to us, or the cost to the customer?"
   → and half the time the clarification changes the answer,
     so this is not only a stall, it is better work.

② THE SCOPE CHECK          buys ~10 seconds
   "Do you want the short version or the full picture?"
   → they almost always say short. You now have permission
     to give three sentences instead of three minutes.

③ THE REFLECT-BACK         buys ~12 seconds
   "So the concern is that if the vendor slips, we've
    committed the marketing spend with nothing to launch."
   → you restate their question in your words. They confirm
     or correct. You have thought for twelve seconds AND
     demonstrated you were listening (see file 05).

④ THE DEFERRAL WITH A DATE  buys as long as you need
   "I don't know. Let me check and come back to you
    Thursday morning."
   → the strongest of the four, and the most avoided.
     Covered in full in file 04, because the reason people
     will not use it is a status fear, not a thinking one.

⭐ THE RULE THAT MAKES ALL FOUR WORK:
  A STALL YOU EXECUTE CONFIDENTLY IS NOT READ AS A STALL.
  Delivered with a downward inflection and no apology, every
  one of these reads as rigour. Delivered apologetically,
  with a rising tone, all four read as floundering. The words
  are only half of it.

⚠ AND THE ONE THAT IS NOT HONEST:
  restating the question slowly to fill air while your face
  says you are searching. Everyone can see it. It costs more
  than silence would have.
The compounding effect

The clarifier and the reflect-back are unusual in that they buy time and improve the output. You answer a better-specified question, and the other person feels heard. Over a year of meetings, someone who habitually clarifies before answering ends up with a reputation for precision that they did not exactly set out to build.

Part III · Building it

Making it stick

The moves in Part II work immediately. Part I's mechanism — chunking — takes months. These three sections are how you get the second one, which is the one that actually changes you.

Thinking Fast 10

Building chunks on purpose

Chunks form from repeated exposure with feedback. Exposure alone does not do it — plenty of people sit through hundreds of meetings and chunk nothing, because nothing ever tells them whether their read of a situation was right.

The mechanism that does work is the one taught in master-yourself/phase-8, file 01 as deliberate practice: work at the edge of your ability, with immediate feedback, repeated with refinement. That file covers the general principle. What follows is the version specific to this skill.

The chunk-building loop · one meeting, five minutes
BEFORE — 60 seconds, on paper
  ① "What kind of conversation is this about to be?"
     → pick one of the five categories from section 05
  ② "What will the hard question be?"
     → one guess, written down

DURING — the running note
  ③ keep the four-symbol note from section 08

AFTER — 3 minutes, same day
  ④ WAS MY CATEGORY RIGHT?
     If no: what was it actually, and what was the tell I
     missed? ← this line is where the chunk forms
  ⑤ DID THE HARD QUESTION COME?
     If a different one came, write it into your bank.
  ⑥ WHAT DID I SAY THAT I WOULD SAY DIFFERENTLY?
     One sentence. Write the better version out in full,
     in the words you would actually use.

⭐ STEP ④ IS THE WHOLE EXERCISE.
  Predicting the category and then checking is what turns
  raw exposure into a pattern. Without the check, you can
  sit through a thousand meetings and chunk nothing.

⇒ TIMESCALE, HONESTLY: you will notice sharper reads in
  four to six weeks. The reflex — categorising without
  deciding to — takes months. It does arrive.
Set expectations honestly

Sections 05 to 09 are techniques and they work the first week. Chunking is not a technique, it is an accumulation, and nothing accelerates it past a certain point. The reason to start the loop above now rather than later is simply that the clock only runs when you are doing it.

Thinking Fast 11

The pre-brief, and the answer bank behind it

Section 04 gave you the ten-minute pre-load. Do it for six months and something better happens: the answers start repeating. The same three questions come up, and you are writing the same answer for the fourth time.

At that point, stop writing them fresh. Keep them.

An answer bank is a single document of the twenty questions you actually face, each with a written answer of about forty-five seconds. Not a script to recite — a rehearsed core you adapt. Its whole purpose is to move work from live to pre-loaded, permanently.

The rule that makes a bank work

Write every answer out loud, then transcribe — never the other way round. Written language and spoken language have different rhythms, and an answer composed on paper comes out of your mouth sounding composed on paper. The full construction, with the recording drill that goes with it, is file 10.

Thinking Fast 12

The drills

Five drills. Each is short, each targets one mechanism from this handbook, and each fails if you only read it.

1 · The category call · daily, 60 seconds

Before any meeting, write down which of the five categories it will be. Afterwards, write what it actually was. This is the chunk-builder from section 10 and it is the highest-value drill in the handbook. Do only this one if you do only one.

2 · The forced pause · daily, in one conversation

Pick one conversation a day. In it, pause three full seconds before every answer. It will feel absurd. Nobody will notice — that is the finding. Run it for a week and the pause stops feeling like a gap.

3 · Skeleton reps · 5 minutes, three times a week

Take a question from the news or your work. Answer it out loud in the position–reason–qualifier–handback shape. Time yourself: under twenty seconds. Do three. The goal is for the shape to arrive without being summoned.

4 · The one-number habit · weekly

Each week, learn one exact figure about your own work — with its source and date. After three months you have twelve, which is more than almost anyone in the room, and it changes how your opinions are received.

5 · The recording · weekly, 2 minutes

Record yourself answering one hard question on your phone. Watch it. This is the most uncomfortable and most useful drill in the entire library — you will hear hedges you did not know you used, and hear them once is enough to start losing them. Full protocol in file 10.

6 · The stall, deliberately · once a week

Use one of the four honest stalls on purpose, in a real conversation, when you did not strictly need it. The point is to find out that nothing bad happens. That discovery is what makes the stall available to you when you do need it.

The minimum viable version

If you will not do six drills — and most people will not — do drill 1 and drill 5. The category call builds the underlying chunks; the weekly recording tells you the truth about how you actually come across. Everything else is acceleration.

Reference

Term bank

The vocabulary from this handbook
chunk
A pattern that has been learned well enough to be held as a single unit in working memory. Expertise is largely the accumulation of chunks in one domain. Chunks do not transfer between domains.
working memory
The small amount of information you can actively hold and manipulate at once — roughly four chunks for most material. Roughly fixed. Practice changes chunk size, never slot count.
size-up
Klein's term for the initial reading of a situation. The step where expertise mostly lives — get it right and a workable option surfaces on its own; get it wrong and nothing downstream recovers.
recognition-primed decision
Klein's model of expert decision-making under pressure: recognise the situation type, let one option surface, simulate it forward, act if it holds. Not comparison between options.
mental simulation
Running a candidate action forward one step to see whether it breaks. The two-second quality check that separates fast from reckless.
the five categories
Definition · data · trade-off · decision rights · sequencing. The five shapes almost any working disagreement takes. Naming the category out loud is itself a contribution.
pre-loading
Doing before a conversation the work that would otherwise have to be done during it. The main source of what looks like quick thinking.
the answer skeleton
Position · reason · qualifier · handback. One reusable shape for delivering any answer under pressure in about fifteen seconds.
the honest stalls
Clarifier · scope check · reflect-back · deferral with a date. Four ways to buy time that cost nothing in credibility when delivered without apology.
answer bank
A written set of about twenty questions you actually face, each with a rehearsed forty-five-second answer. The permanent form of pre-loading.
the running note
A four-symbol working-memory extension kept on paper during a meeting. Not minutes. The single most reliable defence against going blank.

On the research cited here. The chunking work (de Groot 1946; Chase & Simon 1973) and the working-memory limit (Miller 1956; Cowan 2001) are among the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology and are safe to rely on. Klein's recognition-primed decision model comes from field observation of experts rather than controlled experiment — it is a well-supported description of how experienced people report deciding, not a proof that this produces better outcomes than deliberate comparison. The techniques in Part II are craft, not science: they are field-tested and widely used, and you should judge them by whether they work for you. Where a claim in this library rests on contested or failed-replication research, it is flagged where it appears — see the don't-bother panels in files 07–09.
