Part I

Food, and why it goes off

Chapter 01

Three different kinds of spoiling

Food deteriorates by three separate routes, and confusing them is why people throw away perfectly good food and eat genuinely dangerous food.

Microbes multiply. Bacteria, yeasts and moulds arrive, find moisture and warmth, and grow. Some of them merely spoil the food — sour milk, slimy meat, fuzzy bread. Others produce illness, and the crucial, counter-intuitive point is that the dangerous ones often change nothing you can detect. Food poisoning rarely smells. A dish that looks and smells fine can make you very ill, and a dish that smells sour is usually just unpleasant.

Fats go rancid. Oxygen attacks fat and breaks it down, producing that stale, waxy, crayon-like smell in old nuts, old oil and old crisps. Light and heat speed it up. This is not dangerous in the short term and it is genuinely unpleasant — it is the reason oils belong in a cupboard rather than beside the hob, and why nuts keep far longer in the fridge.

Enzymes keep working. A cut apple browns, a banana ripens on the counter, a potato sprouts. The food is still alive in a chemical sense, and its own machinery continues after harvest. Cold slows it, heat stops it permanently — which is why vegetables are briefly boiled before freezing.

Knowing which process you are looking at tells you what to do: microbes mean cold, dry, acidic or sealed; rancidity means dark and cool; enzymes mean cold or a brief cooking.

Chapter 02

The dangerous middle

Bacteria have a comfortable range, and it is roughly the range of a warm room up to the temperature of a hot bath. In that band they multiply fast — a small population can become a large one within a few hours. Below it they slow dramatically; above it they die.

Everything in food safety follows from that single fact.

Cool cooked food quickly and get it into the fridge rather than leaving it on the counter to reach room temperature slowly, which is the most common domestic mistake there is. Divide large quantities into smaller containers so they cool faster.

Defrost in the fridge, not on the worktop, where the outside sits in the danger band for hours while the middle is still frozen.

Reheat properly, all the way through, and be careful with microwaves, which heat unevenly — stir halfway and let it stand. Rice deserves a specific mention: it can carry a spore that survives cooking, and leaving cooked rice warm for hours lets it germinate and produce a toxin that reheating will not destroy. Cool it fast, refrigerate it, use it soon.

Keep raw meat below everything else in the fridge so nothing drips onto food that will not be cooked, and keep its board and knife separate. Washing raw chicken is actively harmful — it spreads bacteria around the sink in a fine spray and kills nothing.

The label that actually matters

"Use by" is a safety date. "Best before" is a quality date. They are not interchangeable and a great deal of food is wasted because people treat them as the same. Past "best before", food may be stale or less good and is generally safe. Past "use by" — on meat, fish, dairy, prepared foods — do not eat it, regardless of how it looks or smells, for exactly the reason in the previous chapter.

Chapter 03

How preservation has always worked

Every traditional method of keeping food is an attack on what microbes need — water, warmth, a neutral environment, or air. Once you see that, the whole history of food preservation becomes one idea applied six ways.

Remove the water. Drying, and also salting and sugaring, which work by pulling water out of the microbes themselves. This is why jam, honey, cured ham and dried fruit keep almost indefinitely.

Make it acidic. Vinegar directly, or fermentation, where friendly bacteria produce acid themselves and crowd out everything else. Yoghurt, sauerkraut, kimchi, sourdough and pickles are all the same trick.

Remove the air. Canning, vacuum packing, sealing under oil or fat.

Change the temperature. Cold slows, freezing halts, heat sterilises. Freezing does not kill most microbes; it pauses them, which is why thawed food spoils quickly and why refreezing thawed food is a poor idea.

Add smoke, which both dries and deposits preservative compounds. And add a preservative directly, which is the modern version of the same intention and the subject of the next chapter.

Part II

Reading a label

Chapter 04

The order is the information

The single most useful thing about an ingredients list is that it is ordered by weight, most first. Almost everything you need is in that ordering rather than in the individual words.

If sugar appears in the first three ingredients, it is a sugary product whatever the front of the packet says. If the named ingredient — the chicken in the chicken soup, the fruit in the fruit yoghurt — appears late, there is little of it.

Sugar has a particular trick worth knowing. It appears under many names at once — syrups, juices concentrated, anything ending in the standard sugar suffixes, dextrose, molasses, malt extract. Split across five entries, each one sits lower in the list than the single combined total would. The nutrition table is the honest version, and the "of which sugars" line cannot be split.

Two further reading habits. Check the portion the numbers refer to, which is frequently smaller than anything a person would actually eat, and compare products using the per-hundred column instead. And know what the front-of-pack claims legally mean: "light" is relative to the same maker's standard version and may still be substantial; "no added sugar" says nothing about the sugar already there; "natural" is close to meaningless; and a product can be free of one thing while being high in another, which is the entire basis of that style of marketing.

Chapter 05

The frightening words

The long chemical names and the numbered additives cause more anxiety than anything else on a packet, so here is the honest picture.

A numbered additive code is simply a substance that has been assessed and approved for use, with a number instead of a long name. Many of them are utterly ordinary things: vitamin C is one, as is the colour in paprika, as is the citric acid in a lemon. The number does not indicate danger; it indicates that a regulator has a file on it.

The governing principle is the one from Volume 26 and it is worth stating again because almost all the confusion dissolves once it is held firmly: the dose makes the poison. There is no such thing as a toxic substance, only a toxic quantity. Water will kill you in sufficient amount; the frightening-sounding compound in your shampoo may be present in a quantity far below any level at which it does anything at all. "Contains chemicals" is true of everything, including an apple, whose full ingredients list would look alarming if printed.

That said, the reassurance has limits and it is dishonest to pretend otherwise. Long-term effects of small exposures are genuinely hard to study. Combinations are studied less than individual substances. And approvals do get revised when evidence accumulates, which means the current list is a best estimate rather than a guarantee.

So the reasonable posture is neither panic nor dismissal. A long ingredients list is not evidence of harm; it is usually evidence of processing — and the sensible heuristic is about how much of your diet is heavily processed rather than about any particular word on any particular packet.

Chapter 06

What preservatives and the rest are doing

Every category on a label has a job, and knowing the job takes the mystery out.

Preservatives stop microbes growing — the modern version of salting and pickling. Their alternative is not an unpreserved product; it is a product that spoils fast, and spoilage causes far more illness than preservatives ever have. The nitrites in cured meats are the one genuinely contested case, and the honest position is that processed meat is best eaten in moderation for reasons that go beyond the nitrites.

Antioxidants stop fats going rancid — the same oxidation from Chapter 01, blocked chemically.

Emulsifiers hold together things that do not naturally mix, which is oil and water in essentially every sauce, dressing and ice cream. Egg yolk is the traditional one and does exactly the same job as the industrial versions.

Stabilisers and thickeners control texture, and a surprising number are simply extracts from seaweed, seeds and trees.

Flavourings, colourings and sweeteners are the cosmetic layer. Worth knowing that "natural flavouring" means derived from a natural source and tells you nothing about how processed it is or how much of it there is, and that the molecule is identical either way — a plant's version of a compound and a factory's version are the same substance.

Part III

Around the house

Chapter 07

Cleaning, and the mixtures that are genuinely dangerous

Most cleaning products are one of three things, and matching the type to the mess does more than buying a specialist product for every surface.

Soaps and detergents work because their molecules have one end that likes water and one that likes grease, so they surround grease and carry it away. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why they handle oily dirt and do little against limescale.

Acids dissolve mineral deposits — limescale, rust, hard-water film. Vinegar and lemon are the mild household versions; descalers are the strong ones.

Alkalis dissolve grease and organic matter. Bicarbonate is the mild version, oven cleaner and drain unblockers the aggressive ones.

Which immediately explains why mixing vinegar and bicarbonate, a fixture of internet cleaning advice, mostly produces an impressive fizz and a jar of salty water — the acid and the alkali cancel each other out, and you have used both to make neither.

Never mix these — this is the important part of the volume

Bleach and ammonia (ammonia is in many glass and multi-surface cleaners) release a toxic gas that has killed people in bathrooms. Bleach and any acid — vinegar, limescale remover, some toilet cleaners — release chlorine gas, which does the same. Bleach and rubbing alcohol is also bad news. The general rule that requires no memory at all: never mix cleaning products, and never use a second product on a surface before rinsing off the first. Ventilate. And never decant anything into an unlabelled bottle.

Chapter 08

Water, plastics and the things on your skin

Three short topics that people ask about constantly.

Hard and soft water. Hard water has dissolved minerals from the rock it passed through. It is harmless to drink — arguably slightly beneficial — and it is what furs up kettles, leaves films on glasses and makes soap lather poorly. Soft water lathers easily and feels slippery, which is the soap rinsing rather than the water being dirty. A filter jug mostly improves taste by removing chlorine; it does not make unsafe water safe.

On bottled versus tap: in countries with reliable mains supply, the tap water is tested far more frequently than the bottled and is generally indistinguishable once chilled. Where the supply is not reliable, that reasoning does not transfer, and boiling remains the most effective domestic treatment against microbes — though it does nothing about dissolved chemicals, and in fact concentrates them slightly.

Plastics. Different plastics behave differently and the relevant variable is almost always heat. Heat is what encourages substances to migrate out of a container and into what it holds, which is why heating food in containers not intended for it is the practice worth avoiding, and why scratched or ageing plastic is worth replacing. Cold storage is far less of an issue. Containers marked as suitable for food and for microwave use have been tested for that use; the ones that were never intended for it — takeaway tubs, old margarine pots — have not been.

Cosmetics and personal care. The ingredient list on these follows the same weight-ordered rule, and for most products the first ingredient is water. Beyond that: sunscreens work either by absorbing or by reflecting light and both work, provided enough is applied — which almost nobody does, and under-application is by far the most common reason a sunscreen underperforms. "Hypoallergenic" is not a regulated promise. Fragrance is the most common cause of reactions, and "unscented" sometimes means a masking fragrance was added. And natural does not mean gentle: essential oils, citrus juice and many plant extracts are genuine irritants, which is the dose principle again pointing in the unfashionable direction.

Reference

What to carry forward

Food safety

Dangerous bacteria usually change nothing you can smell or see. Cool cooked food fast, defrost in the fridge, reheat thoroughly, keep raw meat low and separate, never wash raw chicken. "Use by" is safety; "best before" is quality.

Labels

The list is ordered by weight, and that ordering is the information. Sugar hides by appearing under several names at once — check the nutrition table instead, and compare per hundred rather than per portion.

The additives

The dose makes the poison. A number is a regulator's file, not a warning, and "contains chemicals" is true of an apple. But the long-term and combined effects are genuinely less studied — so the sensible measure is how processed your diet is overall, not any single word.

The safety rule

Never mix cleaning products — bleach with ammonia or with any acid produces gases that have killed people at home. Rinse between products, ventilate, never decant into unlabelled bottles. And with plastics, heat is the variable that matters.
