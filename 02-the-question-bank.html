Sharp Mind 02 · the core skill

The question bank. You cannot always have the best answer. You can always have the best question.

In most rooms the best answer belongs to whoever has been there longest. That competition is not open to you. The best question is open to anyone — it requires no seniority, no domain history, and no certainty, and it changes the direction of a conversation more reliably than any answer does. This handbook is the seven shapes a good question takes, four moves built on them, and forty questions written out so you can use them cold.

File 01 ended on the size-up: name the category of the problem and the right response narrows. This handbook is the other half of that sentence. So the five categories from file 01 and the seven question types here are designed to click together. Section 02 shows the mapping.

What this does not cover

Questions used to build rapport — openers, small talk, the FORD topics, the escalation ladder from facts to feelings — are a different craft with a different purpose, and they are taught in master-yourself/phase-11, file 02. Questions used to negotiate — Chris Voss's calibrated "how" and "what" questions, labelling, mirroring — are in phase-9, file 02. This handbook is about a third thing: questions that find out what is actually true and what is actually at stake. Different purpose, different shapes.

Part I · The types

The seven shapes

Almost every genuinely useful question is one of seven kinds. Two of them are strong enough to get their own sections.

Question Bank 01

Why a question beats an answer

There is an asymmetry in meetings that most people never exploit. Offering an answer is expensive: you have to be right, you have to have standing to say it, and if it lands badly it is attached to you. Offering a question costs almost nothing, and it does more.

Giving an answerAsking a question
RequiresDomain knowledge you may not haveNoticing what is missing — available to anyone
Risk if wrongAttached to you permanentlyAlmost none — you asked, you did not claim
Effect on the roomOne more opinion in a pile of opinionsRedirects everyone's attention at once
What it signalsWhat you knowHow you think — which is the thing people actually judge
Time it buys youNone. You are speakingThirty to sixty seconds while somebody else answers

That last row is worth sitting with. A well-placed question is the only move in a conversation that simultaneously advances the discussion and gives you time to think. Nothing else does both.

The most useful line in this handbook is the one about signalling. People assess your intelligence far more from the questions you ask than from the answers you give, because an answer only reveals whether you happened to know something, while a question reveals the model you are using to look at the situation. Someone who asks "is this reversible?" has told you more about how they think than someone who recites five facts.

The reason this is not a free lunch

Bad questions cost more than silence. The question asked to look clever, the question whose answer was in the first slide, the question that reopens something already decided — each of these is legible, and each is expensive. Section 09 is entirely about when not to ask, and it should be read as seriously as the rest.

Question Bank 02

The seven types, sorted by purpose

Most advice about questions sorts them by grammar — open versus closed, leading versus neutral. That is not useful in the moment, because you do not choose a question by its grammar. You choose it by what is missing from the conversation.

So here they are sorted by purpose. Learn the seven headings; the phrasings underneath are interchangeable.

TypeUse it whenThe forms
① Goal Everyone is discussing how and nobody has said why. The most commonly missing question in working life. "What does good look like here?" · "If this works, what's different in three months?" · "What are we actually trying to achieve — is it the cost or the speed?"
② Constraint A plan is being described as though nothing is in the way. Something always is. "What's actually blocking this — money, people, time, or approval?" · "If we had twice the budget, would this still be hard?" · "What has stopped us doing this before?"
③ Assumption The plan rests on something unexamined. Section 03 — the strongest question in this handbook. "What would have to be true for this to work?" · "What are we taking for granted?" · "Which part of this are we least sure about?"
④ Evidence A claim is being made confidently and its basis has not been stated. "How do we know that?" · "What would change your mind?" · "Is that measured or estimated?" · "Can you give me an example?"
⑤ Ownership The conversation has looped and nothing is landing. Section 07. "Who decides this?" · "Who's doing the work?" · "Is this ours to decide or ours to recommend?"
⑥ Consequence A decision is being taken without its downstream being examined. The cheapest question in the list. "And then what happens?" · "What happens if we do nothing?" · "Is this reversible?" · "Who else does this land on?"
⑦ Trade-off Someone is describing a plan with no cost. Every real plan has a cost. "What are we giving up?" · "If we could only have one — speed or certainty — which?" · "What's the version of this that we'd regret?"

Choosing the type in three seconds

The five categories from file 01 map onto these almost directly. That mapping is the whole reason the two handbooks are in this order.

Category → question type · the lookup
YOU DIAGNOSED (file 01)        SO YOU ASK (this file)

  ① DEFINITION          →       ④ EVIDENCE / ① GOAL
     same word, two meanings      "what do we mean by ___ ?"
                                  "what does good look like?"

  ② DATA                →       ④ EVIDENCE
     answerable, unanswered       "how do we know?"
                                  "what would change your mind?"

  ③ TRADE-OFF           →       ⑦ TRADE-OFF / ① GOAL

                                  "which are we optimising for?"

⭐ AND ONE THAT FITS EVERYWHERE:
  ③ ASSUMPTION — "what would have to be true?"
  There is no category where this is the wrong question.
  If you learn one thing from this handbook, learn that one.

⇒ THE DIAGNOSTIC, IN ONE LINE:
  listen for which of the seven has NOT been said, and say it.
  Usually it is GOAL or TRADE-OFF. Almost nobody asks those.
Question Bank 03

"What would have to be true?"

This is the highest-leverage question in working life, and it is worth building out properly. It is strongly associated with Roger Martin, who used it at Monitor and wrote about it with A.G. Lafley in Playing to Win as part of what he called assertive inquiry — but its power does not depend on the pedigree. It depends on a structural trick.

The trick

An ordinary disagreement is a contest between two positions: I think we should do A, you think we should do B. Each side defends. Neither can concede without losing.

"What would have to be true?" converts that contest into a shared list. Instead of arguing about whether B is right, both of you now describe the conditions under which B would be right. That list is a factual object. It can be inspected, and — this is the part that does the work — individual items on it can be checked.

THE CONVERSION BEFORE AFTER ────── ───── "We should switch suppliers." "For switching to be right, "No, we shouldn't." what would have to be true?" │ │ ▼ ▼ two positions ① the quality problem is defended structural, not a bad month │ ② the new supplier can hold ▼ our volume nobody can concede ③ switching cost is under without losing one quarter of the saving ④ we can exit the current contract without penalty │ ▼ "Which of these are we least sure about?" → ① and ④ │ ▼ NOW IT IS RESEARCH, NOT AN ARGUMENT. And nobody lost.

Notice what happened to the disagreement. It did not get resolved by persuasion; it got decomposed into checkable pieces, and the two least certain pieces became the work. The person who was against switching never had to back down — they simply agreed to find out about item ①.

The four steps

Running the question properly
① STATE THE OPTION NEUTRALLY
   Not "your idea", not "the risky option". Just the option.
   "OK — option B is that we switch suppliers this quarter."

② ASK FOR CONDITIONS, NOT DEFENCE
   "What would have to be true for that to be the right call?"
   ⚠ NOT "why do you think that?" — that invites defence.
     The conditions framing invites construction instead.

③ LIST THEM OUT LOUD, IN THE OTHER PERSON'S WORDS
   Four to six items. Write them where people can see them.
   Take their phrasing, not your improved version of it.

④ RANK BY UNCERTAINTY — THE STEP EVERYONE SKIPS
   "Which of these are we least sure about?"
   → the two least certain items ARE the decision.
   → everything else can be assumed and revisited.

⭐ THE THREE VARIANTS WORTH KNOWING

  FOR YOUR OWN IDEA — the most useful and least used
    "What would have to be true for me to be wrong here?"
    Said out loud, this buys you more credibility than any
    amount of confidence. It is also genuinely useful.

  FOR SOMEONE ELSE'S IDEA YOU DOUBT
    "What would have to be true for this to work?"
    Kills the idea gently if the conditions are absurd —
    and they hear it themselves rather than from you.

  FOR A DECISION ALREADY TAKEN
    "What would have to change for us to revisit this?"
    Sets a tripwire instead of relitigating. Very strong.
The failure mode

Asked with an edge, this question is a weapon and everybody hears it: "Right — so what would have to be true for that to work?" is not inquiry, it is a public demolition wearing inquiry's clothes.

It only works when you are genuinely willing to find that the conditions hold. If you have already decided the other person is wrong and you are using the question to walk them into it, do not use it — say what you think instead. That is more honest and costs you less.

Where this exists next door

A single line of this appears in master-yourself/phase-8, file 02, in the debiasing toolkit, as "what would have to be true for the other option to be better?" It is listed there as one item among several and never named as a technique. This section is that line built out — the conversion mechanism, the four steps, the three variants and the failure mode.

Question Bank 04

Inversion — asking the question backwards

The mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice to his students was "invert, always invert". Charlie Munger made it famous in business: when a problem resists you from the front, turn it round and attack the opposite.

The forward questionThe inverted question
How do we make this project succeed?How would we guarantee this project fails?
How do I become good at my job?What are the three things that would get me quietly written off?
What should we do this quarter?What are we doing now that we would not start today?
How do I make a good impression?What does the person who annoys everyone in meetings do?
Should we hire this candidate?What would make us regret this hire in six months?

The reason this works is not mystical. It is that failure modes are more concretely imaginable than success paths. Asked how a project succeeds, you produce vague aspirations — good communication, strong execution. Asked how it fails, you produce specifics immediately: the vendor slips, the one person who understands the system leaves, nobody owns the data migration. Those specifics are actionable in a way the aspirations never were.

The single most useful inverted question

"What would make us regret this in six months?"

Deploy it before any decision that is hard to undo. It is short, it is not adversarial, it invites everyone to contribute rather than just the sceptics, and it surfaces the specific risk that the optimistic framing was hiding. If you take one sentence from this handbook into your next meeting, take this one.

Inversion versus the pre-mortem

These are relatives and it is worth not confusing them. The pre-mortem — Gary Klein's technique where a team imagines the project has already failed and writes the story of why — is a structured group exercise, run once, taking thirty to sixty minutes, and it is taught in master-yourself/phase-8, file 02. Inversion is the underlying habit of mind, deployable in a single sentence, in any conversation, with no setup. Munger is also mentioned in one clause of phase-10, file 03. Use the pre-mortem for a launch; use inversion forty times a week.

Part II · The moves

Five habits built on the seven types

These are not question types — they are patterns of use. Each one is a small change in behaviour with a disproportionate effect on how you are read.

Question Bank 05

What → So what → Now what

Here is the most common failure in professional communication, and it is so widespread that fixing it alone will distinguish you: people report facts and stop.

There are three rungs on the ladder, and almost everyone lives on the first.

The ladder · the same fact at three heights
THE FACT: "Returns were 6% last month."

  ① WHAT      — the fact
     "Returns were 6% last month."
     ⇒ true, and useless. The listener has to do all the work.
       This is where 80% of updates stop.

  ② SO WHAT   — the implication for something we care about
     "Returns were 6% last month, up from 4%. At this volume
      that's about 40k of margin, and it's concentrated in
      one category."
     ⇒ now it means something. You have connected the number
       to the goal. This is where you start sounding senior.

  ③ NOW WHAT  — the decision it forces
     "...so either we pull that category or we accept the
      margin hit for the quarter. I'd pull it, but it's
      your call and I need an answer by Thursday."
     ⇒ now it is actionable, with a recommendation and a
       deadline. This is where you sound like the person
       who should be running it.

⭐ THE RULE: NEVER DELIVER A "WHAT" WITHOUT A "SO WHAT".
  If you cannot say why a fact matters, you are not ready
  to say the fact.

⇒ AS A QUESTION, THIS IS THE MOST USEFUL THING YOU CAN ASK
  WHEN SOMEONE ELSE STOPS AT RUNG ①:
    "So what does that mean for the launch date?"
    "OK — and what does that force us to decide?"
  Said without aggression, it is a gift. You are moving the
  conversation to where the value is.

The reason this is such reliable leverage is that rung one requires no judgement, and rung two requires all of it. Anyone can read a number off a report. Connecting it to what the organisation cares about is the actual work, and doing it out loud is the fastest way to be perceived as someone who thinks rather than someone who reports.

The overcorrection

Do not jump to rung three when you have not earned rung two. Recommending an action before you have established why the fact matters reads as impulsive, and it invites the response "hang on, what are we even talking about?" The ladder is a ladder. Climb it in order, quickly.

Question Bank 06

Forcing an order of magnitude

Vague quantity words are where disagreement hides. Significant. A lot. Soon. Risky. Most customers. A big chunk. Two people can use the same word, mean numbers ten times apart, and leave the meeting believing they agreed.

The move is to convert the vague word into a number. But asking "how much exactly?" often fails, because the person genuinely does not know and now feels caught out. The better form is the bracket.

The bracket question · why it works better than "how much?"
❌ "How much is it costing us?"
   → "I'd have to look into it."   [conversation over]

✓  "Roughly what order — closer to 10k or closer to 100k?"
   → "Oh, more like 100k."         [you now have a number]

WHY THE BRACKET WORKS
  · it asks for a magnitude, not a figure, so it can be
    answered from intuition without anyone being wrong
  · it is easy to answer, so it does not feel like a test
  · it exposes, harmlessly, whether ANYONE in the room knows
  · a factor-of-ten answer is usually all the decision needs

⭐ THE FORMS
  "Are we talking days, weeks, or months?"
  "Is that most customers or a handful?"
  "Ballpark — 5% or 50%?"
  "If you had to bet, where would you put it?"

⇒ THE LAST ONE IS THE STRONGEST. "What would you bet?"
  moves someone from stating an opinion to pricing it, and
  people are markedly more careful when they are pricing.

⚠ AND THE TELL WORTH WATCHING FOR:
  when nobody can bracket the number, that IS the finding.
  "It sounds like nobody actually knows the size of this —
   should that be the first thing we go and find out?"
  is often the most valuable sentence in the meeting.
Why this compounds

Do this consistently and two things happen. First, decisions in your orbit start being made on magnitudes rather than adjectives, which is straightforwardly better. Second, you acquire a reputation for precision without ever having to be the person who knows the exact figure — you are the person who asks for it. That reputation is cheaper to build than it looks.

Question Bank 07

Who actually decides

A large share of wasted meeting time comes from one unexamined fact: nobody in the room has the authority to decide the thing being discussed. The discussion is therefore unresolvable, and it loops. People mistake the looping for complexity.

The symptoms are specific, and once you can spot them the diagnosis takes seconds:

  • The same three points have been made twice each, in the same order
  • The phrase "we should probably..." keeps appearing and nothing follows it
  • Someone says "I think Finance would want..." and Finance is not present
  • The meeting is scheduled to happen again next week with the same agenda
Four roles that get collapsed into one word
WHEN PEOPLE SAY "WHO OWNS THIS?" THEY ARE USUALLY
CONFLATING FOUR DIFFERENT THINGS:

  DECIDES     · can say yes and make it stick
  CONSULTED   · must be asked first, can block informally
  DOES        · actually performs the work
  INFORMED    · needs to know afterwards, has no say

⇒ MOST STUCK DISCUSSIONS ARE A MISMATCH BETWEEN THE FIRST
  TWO. Everyone is deferring to a person who is CONSULTED,
  believing they DECIDE. So nothing moves and nobody knows why.

THE QUESTIONS, IN ORDER OF DIRECTNESS

  SOFTEST — for when you are junior or new
    "Just so I know where this goes next — who signs this off?"

  NEUTRAL — the workhorse
    "Is this ours to decide, or ours to recommend?"
     ⭐ this one sentence has ended more circular meetings
       than any other in this handbook

  DIRECT — when the loop is obvious and costly
    "We've been round this twice. Can anyone here actually
     say yes? If not, what's the recommendation we send up?"

  AFTERWARDS — the one that prevents the repeat
    "Before we close — who's doing what by when?"
Why people avoid this question

Asking who decides feels political, as though you are challenging someone's authority or angling for your own. In practice the opposite is true: it is read as wanting the thing to actually happen, which is rare enough to be noticed. The genuinely political move is the one everybody else is making — sitting in a meeting that cannot conclude, saying nothing, and letting it be rescheduled.

The one situation to be careful in: if the person who should decide is in the room and visibly avoiding it, the public version of this question puts them on the spot. Take that one afterwards, privately. Reading which situation you are in is file 05.

Question Bank 08

The second question

Watch conversations for a week with this one thing in mind and you will not be able to unsee it: almost nobody asks a second question about the same answer. The pattern is question, answer, new topic. Question, answer, new topic.

This matters because of what the first answer is. The first answer is the prepared one — the version the person has said before, smoothed by repetition, and often not quite true any more. The second question is what gets you the real one.

WHAT THE SECOND QUESTION FINDS Q1 "How's the migration going?" A1 "Yeah, good — we're on track." ← the prepared answer Q2 "What's the least-finished part of it?" A2 "Honestly? The data mapping. We haven't really started that." ← the real answer ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────── the entire content of that exchange is in the second question. Ninety percent of conversations stop at A1 and the participants believe they have been informed.

There are five second questions worth having memorised. They work in almost any context — work, interviews, and across a dinner table.

The second questionWhat it does
"Can you give me an example?"The single strongest one. Vague claims cannot survive it. A real thing produces a specific story immediately; a rehearsed abstraction produces hesitation, and that hesitation is information.
"What do you mean by ___ specifically?"Attacks the one word doing all the work in their sentence — "scalable", "aligned", "on track", "difficult". Pick the vaguest word and hand it back.
"What happened the last time?"Converts opinion into track record. Enormously useful when someone is confident about a prediction.
"What's the least-finished part?"Gets past "on track" without accusing anyone of anything. The phrasing assumes something is unfinished, which makes admitting it easy.
Nothing — just silenceWait three seconds after they stop. Most people continue, and what comes after the pause is more candid than what came before it. This is the cheapest technique in the library and the hardest to do.
The social version

Outside work, the second question is the strongest signal of genuine listening that exists, and almost nobody sends it. Someone mentions they have taken up sailing; the standard response is either a new topic or a story about your own sailing. The second question — "what got you into it?", then "what's the part you didn't expect?" — is so unusual that people remember the conversation. This is covered from the rapport side in master-yourself/phase-11, file 02, and put to work in file 06.

Question Bank 09

When not to ask

A question is not free. Six situations where the right move is to say nothing, or to say something that is not a question.

Do not ask whenBecauseDo this instead
You are asking to look clever Everyone can tell. The tell is that the question is longer than it needs to be and contains a small speech. Nothing. Or ask the short version of the same question with the speech removed.
The answer was in the material It says you did not read it, which costs far more than the answer is worth. Look it up afterwards. If you must ask, name it: "I may have missed this in the deck —"
The decision is already made Reopening a settled decision reads as not accepting it, regardless of how the question is phrased. Ask the tripwire version instead: "What would have to change for us to revisit this?"
The room needs a decision, not more exploration At minute fifty of a sixty-minute meeting, a good question is a bad contribution. Opening is not always the goal. Close instead: "I think we have enough. My read is X — does anyone disagree?"
Someone is upset Questions to someone who is flooded feel like interrogation, however gently meant. They cannot process them. Listen and reflect. This is a different skill — phase-9, file 01 and file 05.
It is a challenge wearing a question's clothes "And how exactly is that going to work?" is not a question. Everyone knows it is not a question. Say the objection plainly. It is more honest and, oddly, better received.
Two tests, applied silently, before you speak
① THE OPEN/CLOSE TEST
   "Does this room need opening or closing right now?"

   OPENING   = more possibilities, more information
               → early in a discussion, at a kickoff, when
                 a plan is thin, when one voice dominates
   CLOSING   = fewer possibilities, a decision
               → late in a meeting, when the same points
                 are repeating, when a deadline is near

   ⇒ a brilliant opening question asked during a closing
     phase is experienced as obstruction. Same question,
     wrong moment, opposite effect.

② THE BUDGET TEST — for when you are new or junior
   You have roughly TWO questions of credit in a meeting
   where you are not established. Spend them on the two
   highest-value ones, not the first two that occur to you.

   ⇒ so hold your first question. Write it in your running
     note (file 01 §08) and see whether a better one arrives
     by minute twenty. It usually does.
Part III · The bank

Forty questions, ready to use

The point of a bank is that you do not compose under pressure. Read these once, choose the six you would actually say in your own voice, and put those in your answer bank.

The bank, by situation

  has not been made yet — it has only been wished.

⑧   What's the least-finished part of this?

⑩   What's blocked, and on whom?

⭐ ⑬ IS THE MANAGER'S BEST QUESTION. It converts a status

⑭   What do we know for certain, and what are we assuming?
⑮   Is it still getting worse, or has it stopped?
⑯   Who else is affected who doesn't know yet?
⑰   What do you need from me in the next hour?
⑱   What's the earliest we could have caught this?

⚠ ORDER MATTERS ENORMOUSLY HERE. ⑮ FIRST — stop the bleeding

  in the moment it is heard as blame, and you will not be
  told the next thing early.
Someone's proposal — they want a yes and you are not sure
⑲   What would have to be true for this to work?
⑳   What's the version of this that fails?
㉑   What are we giving up by doing it?
㉒   Is this reversible? How expensive is it to undo?
㉓   What would make us regret this in six months?
㉔   If we did nothing, what happens?
㉕   Who has done this before that we could ask?

⭐ ㉒ IS THE MOST EFFICIENT QUESTION IN THIS ENTIRE BANK.
  Reversible decisions should be made fast and cheaply;
  irreversible ones deserve all the scrutiny. Knowing which
  you are in is most of good judgement, and one question
  settles it.
Interview — the questions you ask them
㉖   What does someone who's brilliant in this role do that
    someone merely good doesn't?
㉗   What's the hardest part of this job that isn't in the
    description?
㉘   Why is this role open?
㉙   What would you want me to have accomplished in ninety days?
㉚   What's changed about this team in the last year?
㉛   What's the thing about working here that people are
    surprised by?

⭐ ㉖ AND ㉙ DO DOUBLE DUTY. They tell you what the job really
  is, and they signal that you are already thinking about
  performing it rather than getting it. Ask ㉘ always — the
  answer, and the ease of the answer, are both data.
One-on-one — with your manager, and with someone who reports to you
UPWARDS — asking your manager
  ㉜  What's the most useful thing I could take off your plate?
  ㉝  What am I doing that you'd want me to do less of?
  ㉞  What's on your mind this month that I might not know about?

DOWNWARDS — asking someone who reports to you
  ㉟  What's frustrating you that I could fix?
  ㊱  What are you working on that you're not sure matters?
  ㊲  What would you do if this were entirely yours?

⭐ ㉝ IS THE HARDEST TO ASK AND WORTH THE MOST. Asked plainly,
  without a nervous laugh, it gets you the correction you
  would otherwise not hear until a review. Ask it once a
  quarter, and do not defend the answer — say thank you,
  write it down, and change something.
When you are lost — the meeting has left you behind
㊳   Can I check I'm following — is the issue X, or is it Y?
㊴   Can you back up one step? I lost the thread at ___.
㊵   Who is this for, again?

⭐ THE COUNTERINTUITIVE FINDING: admitting you are lost, once,
  without apology, raises your standing rather than lowering
  it. Half the room was also lost. The person who says so is
  read as confident enough not to need to pretend — and
  everyone else is quietly grateful.

⚠ ONCE, THOUGH. Twice is a pattern; three times and the
  reading changes. If you are lost more often than that,
  the fix is preparation before the meeting, not questions
  during it.
How to use this bank

Do not memorise forty questions. Pick six — the ones you would actually say out loud in your own voice, without them sounding borrowed. Rewrite those six in your own phrasing and put them in your answer bank (file 01, §11). Six questions you will genuinely use beat forty you admire.

If you want a starting six: ㉒ is this reversible? · ⑲ what would have to be true? · ㉓ what would make us regret this? · ⑧ what's the least-finished part? · can you give me an example? · is this ours to decide or ours to recommend?

Question Bank 11

The drills

1 · The missing type · daily, during any meeting

In your running note, write which of the seven types has not been asked. Do not necessarily ask it — just notice. After two weeks you will find the same one or two are always missing in your organisation, and that is a standing opportunity.

2 · The second question · daily, one conversation

Pick one conversation a day and force yourself to ask a second question about the same answer before changing topic. Track how often the second answer differs materially from the first. The number will surprise you.

3 · Inversion reps · weekly, 5 minutes

Take a decision you are facing and write down five ways it could go wrong, specifically. Not "it might fail" — five named mechanisms. Then ask which two you can cheaply reduce.

4 · The bracket habit · daily

Every time you hear a vague quantity word, silently ask yourself what number you would bracket it at. Say it out loud once a day. This one becomes automatic faster than any other drill here.

5 · The rung check · on your own updates

Before sending any status update or writing any Slack message that contains a fact, check which rung it stops at. If it stops at what, add the so what before you send. This single habit changes how your written communication is read within about a month.

6 · The prepared three · before every meeting that matters

From the pre-load in file 01 §04: three questions you will ask, written down. Not invented in the room. This is the drill that makes all the others usable under pressure.

The minimum viable version

Drill 2 and drill 5. The second question changes what you learn from other people; the rung check changes what other people learn from you. Between them they cover both directions of every conversation you have.

Reference

Term bank

The vocabulary from this handbook
the seven types
Goal · constraint · assumption · evidence · ownership · consequence · trade-off. Questions sorted by purpose rather than grammar. Choose by what is missing from the conversation.
assertive inquiry
Roger Martin's term for advocating your own view while genuinely investigating the other person's. "What would have to be true?" is its central move.
the conversion
What "what would have to be true?" does: turns a contest between two positions into a shared list of checkable conditions, so nobody has to concede in order for the disagreement to resolve.
inversion
Jacobi's "invert, always invert", popularised by Munger. Attack a problem from its opposite. Works because failure modes are more concretely imaginable than success paths.
the regret question
"What would make us regret this in six months?" The most portable form of inversion. Non-adversarial, invites everyone, surfaces the specific risk the optimistic framing hid.
the ladder
What → so what → now what. Most communication stops on the first rung. Climbing to the second is where judgement becomes visible.
the bracket
Asking for an order of magnitude between two anchors — "closer to 10k or 100k?" — rather than an exact figure. Easier to answer, harder to dodge, and usually sufficient.
decision rights
Who decides · who is consulted · who does · who is informed. Most stuck discussions are a confusion between the first two.
the second question
A follow-up on the same answer rather than a move to a new topic. The first answer is the rehearsed one; the second question gets the real one.
the open/close test
Asking silently whether the room needs more possibilities or fewer, before contributing. A good opening question asked in a closing phase is experienced as obstruction.
the question budget
Roughly two questions of credit in a room where you are not established. Hold the first one and see whether a better arrives.
