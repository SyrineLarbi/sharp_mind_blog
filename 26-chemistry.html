Chemistry. What everything is made of, and why it does what it does.

Chemistry is the study of one question: what happens when atoms meet other atoms. That is rust, cooking, digestion, batteries, plastic, medicine, soap and you. This volume explains what is actually going on in each case, in ordinary language, with no equations and no periodic table to memorise.

Part I

Why atoms bother

Chapter 01

The only thing you need to know about an atom

An atom has a nucleus and electrons around it, and for chemistry the nucleus barely matters. Almost everything chemical is about the outermost electrons — how many there are, and whether the atom would rather have more or fewer.

Electrons sit in layers, and an atom is contented when its outer layer is full. Most atoms are not. Some have one or two spare that they hold loosely. Others are one or two short of full and would very much like to borrow.

That is the whole engine of chemistry. Atoms react in order to end up with a full outer layer — by giving electrons away, by taking them, or by sharing. Everything that follows is detail.

It also explains the one group of elements that does almost nothing. Helium, neon and argon already have full outer layers. They are already contented, so they do not react, which is why you can put helium in a balloon near a flame and nothing happens, and why old light bulbs were filled with argon — to surround the hot filament with something that refuses to burn it.

Chapter 02

The periodic table is a seating plan

It looks like something to memorise. It is actually a chart arranged so that the useful information is in the position.

Elements are laid out in order of how many protons they have. The rows break where a new electron layer starts. And the consequence is that everything in a column has the same number of outer electrons, which is why a column behaves alike.

So the far-left column all have a single loose electron they are eager to lose, and are all violently reactive metals — drop any of them in water and you get a reaction, more violent the further down you go. The column near the right are all one electron short and are all aggressive, grabby elements: fluorine, chlorine, bromine. And the last column are the contented ones from the previous chapter.

Mendeleev arranged it before anyone knew electrons existed, purely from how elements behaved, and left gaps where the pattern demanded an element nobody had found. He predicted their properties. They were found, and they matched. That is about as convincing as a scientific prediction gets.

Chapter 03

Three ways to hold atoms together

Giving and taking. A sodium atom has one loose electron; a chlorine atom is one short. Sodium hands it over. Now both have full outer layers — but sodium is short one negative charge and chlorine has one extra, so they are oppositely charged and stick together. That is table salt, and it explains why salt is a hard crystal with a very high melting point: the whole thing is a rigid grid of alternating charges.

It also explains why salt dissolves. Water molecules are slightly positive at one end and slightly negative at the other, so they crowd around each charged piece and pull the grid apart.

Sharing. Two atoms that both want more electrons can pool them and both count the shared pair. That is how water, oxygen, plastic, sugar and essentially all of life are held together. Shared bonds make discrete molecules rather than endless grids, which is why these substances tend to melt and boil at far lower temperatures.

Pooling. Metals do something else: all the atoms release their loose outer electrons into a shared sea that flows through the whole lump. That single picture explains every property of metals at once. They conduct electricity, because the electrons are free to move. They conduct heat, for the same reason. They are shiny, because the free electrons interact with light. And they bend rather than shatter, because the atoms can slide past each other while the electron sea holds everything together — which is exactly what a blacksmith is exploiting.

Part II

Reactions you meet every day

Chapter 04

Burning, rusting and breathing are the same reaction

They look nothing alike and they are the same process at different speeds: something combining with oxygen and releasing energy.

Burning is fast — fast enough to produce heat and light. Rusting is the same reaction running over years, which is why a rusty nail is not hot. And the respiration happening in every cell of your body right now is the same thing again, run slowly and in careful stages so the energy can be captured rather than wasted as a flame.

You are, in a real and unmetaphorical sense, burning your food. Very slowly, at body temperature, with the energy released in small useful instalments. The waste products are the same as a fire's: carbon dioxide and water, which is what you breathe out and part of why you exhale visible mist on a cold morning.

Understanding this explains fire extinguishers. A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat; remove any one and it stops. Water removes heat. A blanket or foam removes oxygen. Turning off the gas removes fuel. There is no fourth option.

Chapter 05

Acids, bases, and why they cancel

An acid is a substance that releases a particular fragment — a hydrogen atom stripped of its electron — when dissolved in water. A base is something that mops them up. That is the whole definition, and everything else follows.

Strong acids release a great many and are corrosive; weak ones release few, which is why vinegar and lemon juice are acids you can put on food. Strong bases are equally corrosive in the other direction — drain cleaner is not gentle.

Mix them and they neutralise, producing salt and water. That is why an indigestion tablet, which is a mild base, settles an over-acidic stomach; why bicarbonate takes the sting from a sting; and why baking involves an acid and a base meeting in a wet mixture to produce carbon dioxide bubbles that raise the dough.

The strength scale runs from strongly acidic through neutral in the middle to strongly basic, and each step on it is a tenfold change rather than a single one — which is why small-sounding differences in soil or water acidity matter enormously to living things.

Chapter 06

Why soap works

Oil and water do not mix because water molecules are charged at their ends and stick to each other preferentially, squeezing the uncharged oil out into blobs. Washing greasy hands in water alone achieves nothing, as everyone has discovered.

A soap molecule is built as a compromise: one end is charged and loves water, the other is a long uncharged tail that is happy in oil. Put soap in greasy water and the molecules arrange themselves with their tails buried in the grease and their heads sticking out into the water.

The result is a droplet of grease wrapped in a ball of soap molecules, presenting a water-friendly surface to the world. The water can now carry it away. Soap does not dissolve grease; it disguises it as something water is willing to pick up.

Detergent does the same. So does bile in your intestine, for the fat in your dinner. And the reason handwashing destroys many viruses is that they are wrapped in an oily coat, and soap takes that coat apart.

Chapter 07

What a battery actually is

A battery is two different metals and a reaction that wants to happen, arranged so that the only route is through your device.

One metal gives up electrons more readily than the other. Connect them directly and electrons flood across and the energy is wasted as heat. Separate them with a substance that lets charge move but blocks electrons, and the electrons are forced to travel the long way round — through the wire, through your phone, doing work on the journey.

A flat battery is not empty. It has simply finished its reaction — the materials have converted into their end state and there is nothing left that wants to happen. A rechargeable battery is one where pushing electricity backwards through it reverses the reaction and restores the starting materials, which works for a few thousand cycles before the physical structure degrades.

This is also why batteries go flat in the cold: reactions slow when particles move sluggishly, so less current is available, and why a car may not start on a frosty morning and then start fine at noon.

Part III

The chemistry of things that matter

Chapter 08

Carbon, and why life chose it

One element has an entire branch of chemistry to itself. Carbon has four outer electrons — exactly half a full layer — which means it is equally happy sharing in four directions.

That makes it uniquely good at building. It can form chains of unlimited length, rings, branches and three-dimensional frameworks, and bond stably with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and itself. No other element comes close for versatility.

So every substance in a living thing is built on carbon skeletons — proteins, fats, sugars, DNA. So is plastic, petrol, alcohol and most medicines. When people speculate about life elsewhere being based on something else, the shortlist is extremely short, because nothing else has carbon's combination of four-way bonding and stability.

And the same element is graphite in your pencil and diamond on a ring. Identical atoms; different arrangement. In graphite they lie in flat sheets that slide over each other, which is why it marks paper and works as a lubricant. In diamond every atom is locked to four others in a rigid framework, which is why it is the hardest natural substance. Arrangement matters as much as composition, and that is one of chemistry's central lessons.

Chapter 09

Plastics, and the problem with a good idea

A plastic is an enormously long chain made by hooking small carbon-based units together over and over. Change the unit or the way the chains pack and you get everything from cling film to a crash helmet.

They were a genuine triumph. Light, cheap, waterproof, mouldable into any shape, and chemically inert — which is exactly what you want in a food container or a medical device or a water pipe.

And chemically inert is precisely the problem. Nothing in the natural world evolved to break these chains, because they did not exist until a century ago. So bacteria that cheerfully digest wood, bone and leather have no equipment for polyethylene, and it persists — grinding down into ever smaller fragments rather than decomposing.

Recycling helps less than people assume, because most plastics degrade slightly each cycle and mixed types cannot easily be separated. The genuine solutions are using less, designing for actual recyclability, and developing plastics that something can eat. All three are in progress and none is close to sufficient.

Chapter 10

How a medicine knows where to go

A tablet does not contain instructions. It contains molecules of a particular shape, and the shape is the whole mechanism.

Your cells are covered in receptors — structures with specific shapes that a particular natural molecule fits into, like a key in a lock. When it fits, something happens in the cell.

A drug is a molecule shaped to interact with one of those locks. Some are keys that fit and turn, producing the effect. Others are shaped to fit and not turn, blocking the lock so the natural key cannot get in — which is how a great many blood pressure and allergy medicines work.

Side effects follow immediately from this picture. If a similar lock exists on a different kind of cell, the same drug will fit there too, and do something nobody wanted. Most of the difficulty in developing a medicine is not finding something that works but finding something that works only where you want it to.

Antibiotic resistance also follows. Kill most bacteria and the few that happen to have a slightly different lock survive and reproduce — and you have selected for exactly the ones your drug cannot touch. Taking a partial course is the ideal way to breed them, which is what Fleming warned about in Volume 24 and what we have spent eighty years doing anyway.

Reference

What to carry forward

The one idea

Atoms react to fill their outer electron layer — by giving, taking or sharing. Elements that are already full do nothing at all. The periodic table is arranged so that a column shares outer electrons, which is why a column behaves alike.

Three bonds, and what they explain

Giving and taking makes rigid charged grids — salt, high melting point, dissolves in water. Sharing makes discrete molecules — water, sugar, plastic, life. Pooling makes the metallic electron sea, which explains conduction, shine and bendiness all at once.

The everyday ones

Burning, rusting and breathing are one reaction at three speeds — you are slowly burning your food. Soap has a water-loving head and a grease-loving tail, so it disguises grease as something water will carry. A battery forces electrons the long way round, and a flat one has simply finished reacting.

Shape and arrangement

Graphite and diamond are the same atoms arranged differently. A medicine works by shape fitting a receptor — which is also exactly why side effects exist, and why an unfinished course of antibiotics breeds the bacteria your drug cannot touch.
