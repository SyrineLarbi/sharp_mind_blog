Exercise physiology. The body rebuilds itself to match the demands you put on it.

Most people know that exercise is good for them. Far fewer know why. Exercise physiology is the science of what actually happens when you move: how muscles get their energy, how the heart and lungs deliver oxygen, and how the body responds to repeated effort by becoming a different machine. The central idea is simple. The body adapts to exactly the stress it meets, and it slowly takes away what it does not use. That one rule explains marathon runners, weightlifters, astronauts, and the decline of a person who spends a month in bed.

Please note

This is general education, not medical advice. If you have heart disease, chest pain on effort or another serious condition, ask a doctor before starting hard training.

Question 1 · What is it?

The science of the body under load

Exercise physiology studies the short-term response to effort, such as a faster heartbeat, and the long-term adaptations that follow weeks of training, such as a bigger heart.

Chapter 01

The conductors and the drivers

In the early 1950s, the British epidemiologist Jerry Morris studied the staff of London's double-decker buses. Each bus had two workers. The driver sat all day. The conductor climbed the stairs hundreds of times a shift, collecting fares. The two men were similar in class, income and work hours. Yet the conductors had clearly lower rates of heart disease, and when they did have heart attacks, they were less often fatal. Morris found a similar pattern among postmen, who walked, compared with office clerks.

Before this work, published in 1953, heart disease was widely seen as an unavoidable part of ageing or of stress. Morris helped show that physical activity in everyday life is linked to heart health. His studies were observational, so they could not prove cause on their own, but decades of later research, including experiments on what training does to the heart, filled in the mechanism. Exercise physiology is the study of that mechanism.

Chapter 02

What "fitness" actually means

"Fit" is not one quality. Physiologists separate several, and each responds to a different kind of training.

ComponentWhat it isHow it is trained
Cardiorespiratory fitnessHow well heart, lungs and muscles use oxygen over sustained effortWalking, running, cycling, swimming; intervals
Muscular strengthThe most force a muscle can produceLifting heavy loads for few repetitions
Muscular enduranceHow long a muscle can keep workingLighter loads, many repetitions
PowerForce produced quicklyJumping, sprinting, fast lifts
Flexibility and balanceRange of movement and control of postureStretching, yoga, balance practice; especially important in old age

The single most studied measure is VO2max, the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in and use per minute. It is usually expressed per kilogram of body weight. A typical untrained adult might be somewhere around 30 to 40 millilitres per kilogram per minute; elite endurance athletes can reach 70 to 90. It is a summary of the whole oxygen chain, from lungs to muscle.

Chapter 03

The currency: ATP and three ways to make it

Every muscle contraction is paid for with one molecule: ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Think of it as the cell's cash. Muscles store only enough for a few seconds of hard work, so they must keep making more. They have three systems, which differ in speed and capacity, like a wallet, a cash machine and a bank.

The three energy systems, roughly

The aerobic system is slow but enormously efficient. It takes place inside mitochondria, small structures in cells that burn fuel with oxygen. It can use fat, and even a lean person carries enough fat for many hours of exercise. The quick systems rely on carbohydrate. Muscles and liver store carbohydrate as glycogen, a few hundred grams in total. When marathon runners "hit the wall" around 30 kilometres, it is largely because glycogen is running out and the body must shift to slower fat burning.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Stress, recovery, and a rebuilt machine

Training works by disturbing the body's balance, then letting it recover. During recovery the body overbuilds a little, so the same stress is easier next time.

Chapter 04

The oxygen chain

To work for more than a minute or two, muscles need oxygen. It travels through a chain: air into the lungs, oxygen into the blood, the heart pumping blood to muscles, and muscles pulling oxygen out of the blood. A simple formula from physiology, the Fick principle, describes it: oxygen used = blood pumped per minute × oxygen extracted from each litre.

At rest, a typical heart pumps about 5 litres of blood a minute. During maximal effort, an untrained adult might reach around 20 litres, and an elite endurance athlete considerably more. Heart rate rises, but the biggest difference between people is stroke volume, the amount of blood pushed out with each beat. In healthy people, the lungs are rarely the limit. The heart's pumping capacity is usually what sets VO2max, with the muscles' ability to use oxygen as the second factor.

Chapter 05

What endurance training changes

Weeks of regular aerobic exercise change the body at every link of the chain.

The heart and blood

The heart's main pumping chamber enlarges and fills more fully, so each beat moves more blood. Resting heart rate falls; many endurance athletes rest in the 40s. Blood volume increases. Doctors call these normal changes "athlete's heart".

The muscles

Muscles grow more tiny blood vessels (capillaries) and many more mitochondria. They store more glycogen and get better at burning fat, which saves glycogen for later. Signalling molecules inside the cell, such as one called PGC-1α, switch on these changes.

Many of these changes begin within days to weeks. They also reverse quickly. Stop training and blood volume falls within days, and much of the gain in VO2max is lost within a couple of months. This is the "use it or lose it" rule in its plainest form. Fitness is not something you have; it is something you keep doing.

Chapter 06

Strength: nerves first, then muscle

When beginners start lifting weights, they often get noticeably stronger in a few weeks, before their muscles have grown much. The early gains come largely from the nervous system. The brain learns to activate more muscle fibres at once, to fire them in better coordination, and to relax opposing muscles. Strength is partly a skill.

Growth follows. Muscle fibres come in two broad types. Slow-twitch (type I) fibres are resistant to fatigue and rich in mitochondria; they dominate in endurance athletes. Fast-twitch (type II) fibres produce more force and tire quickly; they dominate in sprinters. Heavy loads put mechanical tension on fibres, which triggers signalling inside the cell (a pathway involving a protein called mTOR) that builds new muscle protein. Over months the fibres thicken, a process called hypertrophy. Eating enough protein supports this, but training, not protein powder, is the trigger.

Chapter 07

Lactate, fatigue and soreness: three myths

The first myth is that lactic acid is a waste product that poisons muscles. When glycolysis runs fast, muscles produce lactate. Far from being waste, lactate is a fuel. The heart, other muscles and the liver take it up and use it or rebuild it into glucose. The American physiologist George Brooks spent decades showing this, calling it the "lactate shuttle". The burning feeling during hard effort comes mainly from the build-up of acid and other by-products together, not lactate alone.

The second myth is that lactate causes the soreness you feel a day or two after exercise. It cannot: lactate levels return to normal within an hour or so. That later ache, called delayed-onset muscle soreness, comes from small-scale damage and inflammation in muscle fibres, especially after unfamiliar movements where the muscle lengthens under load, such as running downhill or lowering a weight slowly.

The third is that fatigue is purely a muscle running out of fuel. Fatigue happens at many levels, including the brain. The nervous system appears to hold back effort to protect the body, which is partly why encouragement, music or a rival can make you go faster. Fatigue is partly a decision the brain makes on your behalf. How large that brain role is remains debated.

Chapter 08

Muscle as a gland

Muscle is not only for movement. When it contracts, it pulls sugar out of the blood through a route that does not depend on insulin: contraction itself moves glucose transporters (called GLUT4) to the surface of muscle cells. This is why a short walk after a meal lowers blood sugar, and why exercise works even in people whose insulin works poorly, the problem behind type 2 diabetes.

Working muscle also releases signalling molecules into the blood. Researchers, including the Danish physiologist Bente Klarlund Pedersen, called these myokines. They act on fat, liver, bone, the immune system and the brain. One example is interleukin-6, which, when released by muscle during exercise, appears to have anti-inflammatory effects. Some myokines are well established; others, such as one called irisin, have been more controversial. The overall idea is solid: exercising muscle sends chemical messages to the whole body, which helps explain why exercise improves conditions that seem to have nothing to do with muscles, from depression to some cancers.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fitness is one of the strongest predictors of a long life

The physiology matters because it explains a striking public-health fact. Low fitness is linked to early death as strongly as many well-known risk factors, and almost anyone can improve it.

Chapter 09

The dose and the curve

In large studies that follow people for years, those with the highest cardiorespiratory fitness have much lower death rates than those with the lowest, even after accounting for smoking, weight and blood pressure. The benefit is not a straight line. It is steepest at the bottom: moving from completely inactive to a little active brings the largest gain.

The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines recommend that adults do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on at least two days. "Moderate" means you can talk but not sing; "vigorous" means you can say only a few words. The guidelines also stress that any activity is better than none, and that long periods of sitting should be broken up.

And the famous 10,000 steps? The figure came from the name of a Japanese pedometer sold in the 1960s, not from research. Later studies suggest that the risk of death falls as daily steps rise, and then levels off, at lower counts in older adults than in younger ones. More steps help, but there is nothing magic about 10,000.

Chapter 10

Inactivity is the real experiment

In 1966 researchers in Dallas put five healthy young men in bed for three weeks. Their VO2max fell sharply, and their hearts became smaller and less efficient. Thirty years later, the same men were tested again. The decline caused by three weeks in bed had been larger than the decline caused by three decades of ageing. Astronauts show similar losses in weightlessness, which is why they exercise for hours a day on the space station.

This turns the usual question around. Exercise is not an extra; the human body is built to expect movement, and much of what we call "normal ageing" is partly disuse. Muscle mass and strength decline from middle age, and faster in later life, a process called sarcopenia when it becomes severe. Strength training slows this at any age. Studies in people in their eighties and nineties have shown meaningful strength gains within weeks. For older people, leg strength and balance are what keep them out of care homes, because they prevent falls.

Chapter 11

Why two people respond differently

Put a group of people on the same training plan and their improvements will vary widely. The HERITAGE Family Study, led by the Canadian geneticist Claude Bouchard from the 1990s, put hundreds of previously sedentary people from families on an identical 20-week cycling programme. On average VO2max rose, but some people improved a great deal and a minority barely at all. Responses clustered in families, which pointed to a strong genetic influence.

That finding has a hopeful side. Later studies suggest that many "non-responders" do improve when the amount or intensity of training is increased, or that they improve on other measures, such as blood pressure or blood sugar, even if VO2max stays flat. A poor response on one measure rarely means exercise is useless for that person.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful medicine with real side effects

Exercise has the broadest benefits of any health intervention. It also has limits, risks and costs that are worth seeing clearly.

The heart risk deserves a sentence of context. Vigorous exercise briefly raises the chance of a heart attack or sudden cardiac arrest, but regular exercise lowers it overall, so that the most active people have the lowest risk across a whole week. The danger is concentrated in people who are usually inactive and suddenly do something strenuous. Building up gradually is the main safety rule.

Chapter 13

The weight-loss puzzle

Many people start exercising to lose weight and are disappointed. There are good physiological reasons. A 30-minute run burns perhaps 300 kilocalories, about the same as a large muffin. People often eat a little more afterwards. And, as research by the anthropologist Herman Pontzer suggests, total daily energy use may rise less than expected with more activity, because the body saves energy elsewhere.

The fair conclusion is not that exercise is useless for weight. It helps keep weight off after loss, reduces dangerous fat around the organs and protects muscle during dieting, which matters even more with the new weight-loss drugs described in Obesity. But exercise is best thought of as a treatment for health, not mainly for weight. Its benefits for the heart, blood sugar and mood appear even when the scale does not move.

Chapter 15

Why the pill is so hard to make

Researchers have found drugs that, in mice, switch on some of the same signals that endurance training does, making sedentary mice run longer. These results gave rise to the phrase "exercise in a pill". The idea is attractive for people who are paralysed, very frail or bedridden. But exercise works through many systems at once: the heart, blood vessels, muscle, bone, brain, immune system and fat all respond, partly through the myokines described in Chapter 08. A single drug can copy one signal; it cannot easily copy the whole conversation.

The more realistic future is less dramatic. Better wearables will make it easier to measure fitness and progress. Health systems may prescribe activity and fund supervised exercise, as some already do for heart patients and people with knee arthritis. And research into the molecules released by exercise may explain why it protects against so many diseases, which could lead to new drugs for specific conditions. Meanwhile the oldest finding in the field still holds: the best results come from regular movement, some of it hard, and some of it against resistance, kept up for life. For the population-level picture, see Public health.

The core idea

Muscles run on ATP, made by three energy systems. Sustained effort depends on an oxygen chain led by the heart. Training is stress plus recovery: the body overbuilds what is used (heart, mitochondria, muscle, nerves) and removes what is not. Fitness is one of the strongest predictors of long, healthy life.

The numbers worth knowing
  • WHO: 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus strength work twice.
  • Phosphocreatine lasts about 10 seconds at full effort.
  • VO2max: roughly 30–40 ml/kg/min untrained, 70–90 in elite endurance athletes.
  • Three weeks in bed cut fitness more than 30 years of ageing (Dallas study).
The common mistake

Judging exercise only by weight loss. Its biggest benefits, for the heart, blood sugar, mood and strength, show up even when the scale does not move.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a walk after dinner lowers blood sugar even in someone with insulin resistance? If so, you understand that muscle is a metabolic organ, not just a motor.

The words, in plain words
ATP
The molecule that carries energy inside cells. Muscles spend it to contract.
Phosphocreatine
A quick energy store in muscle that refills ATP for a few seconds.
Glycolysis
Breaking down glucose quickly without oxygen. Fast, but limited.
Mitochondria
Cell structures that burn fuel with oxygen to make ATP. Training multiplies them.
Glycogen
The stored form of carbohydrate in muscle and liver.
VO2max
The most oxygen your body can use per minute. The standard measure of aerobic fitness.
Stroke volume
The amount of blood the heart pumps with each beat.
Lactate
A product of fast glucose breakdown. A fuel, not the cause of next-day soreness.
Hypertrophy
Growth of muscle fibres in response to load.
Slow- and fast-twitch fibres
Muscle fibre types built for endurance (slow) or force and speed (fast).
Myokines
Signalling molecules released by working muscle that act on other organs.
Sarcopenia
Serious loss of muscle mass and strength with age.
DOMS
Delayed-onset muscle soreness: the ache a day or two after unfamiliar exercise.
