Public health. Medicine whose patient is everyone, and whose successes are invisible.

A doctor treats the person in front of them. Public health treats the population: the city, the country, the planet. Its tools are less dramatic than surgery. They include clean water, vaccination programmes, seatbelt laws, tobacco taxes and the quiet counting of who gets sick and why. Those tools have done more to lengthen human life than any hospital. The catch is that when public health works, nothing happens, and people rarely notice the epidemic that did not arrive. This file is an overview. Question 1 · What is it?

The organised effort to keep whole populations well

A classic definition, from the American public-health pioneer Charles-Edward Winslow in 1920, calls it "the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organized efforts" of society. The key words are preventing and organized.

Chapter 01

The last case of smallpox

Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people in the twentieth century alone. It disfigured and blinded many of those who survived. In 1967 the World Health Organization launched an intensified campaign to wipe it out. Vaccinating everyone on Earth was impossible, so the teams used a smarter method called ring vaccination: find every case quickly, then vaccinate the people around it, and the people around them, so the virus has nowhere to go.

In October 1977 a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, called Ali Maow Maalin developed the last case of naturally occurring smallpox. He recovered, and later worked as a polio vaccinator. In 1980 the WHO declared smallpox eradicated. It remains the only human disease ever eliminated from the planet.

Notice what the victory required. It needed a vaccine, but also a global surveillance network, thousands of field workers, cooperation between rival Cold War governments, and patient detective work to trace every chain of infection. That combination of science, organisation and politics is what public health is.

The two depend on each other. Hospitals need public health to prevent epidemics that would overwhelm them, and public health needs clinics to deliver vaccines and screening. But the difference in mindset is real. A doctor asks, "Why did this patient get sick?" Public health also asks, "Why does this population have so much of this disease?" The answers are often different.

Chapter 03

Three levels of prevention

Public health organises prevention into three levels, depending on when it acts.

Primary

Stop the disease starting. Vaccines, clean water, smoke-free laws, seatbelts, iodised salt. See Disease prevention.

Secondary

Catch it early, before symptoms, when treatment works best. Screening for cervical or bowel cancer, blood pressure checks. See Screening, and why more is not always better.

Tertiary

Limit the damage once disease exists. Rehabilitation after a stroke, foot care in diabetes to prevent amputations.

Primary prevention usually gives the biggest return, because it stops suffering and cost before they start. It is also the hardest to fund, because the benefit is spread across many people and many years, and nobody can point to the individual who was saved.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Count, explain, intervene, and check again

Public health runs a loop. It measures who is getting sick, works out why, chooses a way to intervene, and measures again to see whether it worked.

Chapter 04

Counting: epidemiology

Epidemiology is the study of how disease is distributed in populations and what causes it. Its most famous modern discovery came from two British researchers, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill. In 1950 they compared people with lung cancer in London hospitals with similar patients who did not have it, and asked about their habits. The people with lung cancer were far more likely to be heavy smokers. They then followed tens of thousands of British doctors for decades, and confirmed that smokers died much younger.

Those two designs are still the backbone of the field. A case-control study starts with sick people and looks back for causes. A cohort study follows a group forward in time; the Framingham Heart Study, begun in a Massachusetts town in 1948, identified high blood pressure and cholesterol as heart risks this way. Hill later set out a list of questions for judging whether an association is likely to be causal, such as how strong it is, whether the cause comes before the effect, and whether more exposure brings more disease. Those questions are still taught. See Epidemiology for the full toolkit.

Chapter 05

The prevention paradox

In the 1980s the British epidemiologist Geoffrey Rose pointed out something counter-intuitive. Most heart attacks do not happen to the small group of people with very high blood pressure. They happen to the much larger group with slightly raised blood pressure, simply because there are so many more of them. A large number of people at small risk can produce more cases than a small number at high risk.

This leads to Rose's paradox. A measure that shifts everyone a little, such as reducing salt in bread, can prevent many deaths across the population while offering each individual almost no noticeable benefit. That makes such measures powerful and unpopular at the same time. Many people ask, reasonably, why they should change anything when their own risk is low. The answer is that the population benefit is built from millions of tiny individual ones.

Chapter 06

The causes of the causes

Smoking causes lung cancer. But why do some groups smoke more than others? In the Whitehall studies, which began in 1967 and followed British civil servants, researchers led by Michael Marmot found a striking pattern. Even in this group of office workers, all with secure jobs and access to the same health service, death rates rose step by step from the top grade down to the bottom. Smoking, blood pressure and cholesterol explained only part of the gap. Control over one's work and life seemed to matter too.

Public health calls these the social determinants of health: income, education, housing, work, neighbourhood and discrimination. They are called the "causes of the causes" because they shape the behaviours and exposures that cause disease. This is why life expectancy can differ by many years between rich and poor areas of the same city. See Health inequality.

Chapter 07

The levers: from information to law

Once a cause is known, how can a society change it? In 2007 the Nuffield Council on Bioethics in Britain described an "intervention ladder". The higher the rung, the more it restricts freedom, and the stronger the justification it needs.

Rung (least to most intrusive)Example
Monitor the situationTracking flu cases or obesity rates
Provide informationCampaigns about handwashing; food labels
Enable choiceFree vaccination clinics; bike lanes; free school meals
Change the defaultHealthier side dish by default; opt-out organ donation
Use incentives or taxesTobacco and sugary-drink taxes; payments for checkups
Restrict choiceBanning trans fats; banning smoking in pubs
Eliminate choiceCompulsory quarantine of an infected person in a dangerous outbreak

The middle rungs, defaults and incentives, overlap with the ideas in Behavioral economics. In practice the most effective programmes use several rungs together. Tobacco control is the textbook case: information on packs, taxes, advertising bans and smoke-free laws, all at once.

Chapter 08

Who does the work

At the global level, the World Health Organization, founded in 1948 as a United Nations agency, sets standards, coordinates responses to outbreaks and declares international emergencies. It has no power to force countries to act; it relies on agreement. Large funders such as the Global Fund, Gavi (the vaccine alliance) and private foundations pay for much of the work in poorer countries.

Most public health is national and local, though. Countries have agencies such as the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention or national public-health institutes, plus local health departments that inspect restaurants, test water, trace contacts and run vaccination clinics. The details vary greatly between countries, as do the links with the wider health system described in How healthcare systems work.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The largest gain in human well-being in history

Measured in years of life, public health has a record that few other human endeavours can match. Its unfinished work is just as large.

Chapter 09

The record

Global life expectancy at birth was around 30 years in 1900. By the early 2020s it was over 70. Much of that gain came from children surviving: the number of deaths of children under five fell from about 12.8 million in 1990 to about 4.8 million in 2023, according to UN estimates, even as the world's population grew.

In 1999 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published a list of ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century in the United States. It included vaccination, safer workplaces, control of infectious diseases, fewer deaths from heart disease and stroke, safer and healthier foods, healthier mothers and babies, family planning, fluoridation of drinking water, recognition of tobacco as a health hazard, and motor-vehicle safety. Almost none of these is a hospital treatment. They are laws, programmes, engineering and habits.

Some successes are hardly known. Removing lead from petrol, a process that took decades and finished worldwide around 2021, cut exposure to a poison that harms children's brains. Adding iodine to salt prevents a deficiency that is a leading preventable cause of learning disability. Polio, once present in over 120 countries, now survives in its wild form in only a couple.

Chapter 10

The burden that remains

The pattern of disease has shifted. In most countries, the main killers are now non-communicable diseases, such as heart disease, stroke, cancer, diabetes and chronic lung disease, rather than infections. Tobacco alone still kills about 8 million people a year, according to the WHO. Road crashes kill around 1.2 million. Unhealthy diets, alcohol, air pollution and physical inactivity add many more.

Meanwhile infectious threats have not gone away. Tuberculosis, malaria and HIV still kill millions, mainly in poorer countries. Measles has returned where vaccination rates have fallen. And antibiotic resistance, explained in Volume 13, threatens to make common infections dangerous again. The gaps between countries are huge: a baby born in Japan can expect to live over 80 years, while in several sub-Saharan African countries life expectancy is still in the low 60s or below. See Healthcare access in developing countries.

Chapter 11

Pandemics: the stress test

COVID-19 showed both the power and the limits of public health. The WHO estimated that about 15 million more people died in 2020 and 2021 than would normally have been expected, a measure called excess mortality. Vaccines were developed in under a year, which saved millions of lives. But countries with similar wealth had very different outcomes, depending on how early they acted, how well they tested and traced, how much people trusted the government, and how unequal their societies were.

The pandemic also showed how fragile public trust is. Measures such as school closures, mask rules and vaccine requirements became political battles in many countries, and debates about which measures were worth their costs continue. Public health depends on cooperation, and cooperation depends on trust. The full story is in Pandemics and Vaccination programs.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Cheap, powerful, and politically difficult

The strengths of public health are its reach and value for money. Its weaknesses are invisibility, slow results, and the tension between the common good and individual freedom.

Chapter 13

The oldest argument: the individual and the group

Every public-health measure raises the same question: when may society limit a person's choices for the sake of health? The nineteenth-century philosopher John Stuart Mill argued that the only legitimate reason to restrict someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others. That principle fits infectious disease and second-hand smoke well. It fits poorly with measures that protect people from themselves, such as sugar taxes or motorcycle helmet laws.

The case for stronger action

Choices are shaped by the environment, marketing and poverty, so "free choice" is often not very free. Individual illness also costs others, through taxes and family care. Many accepted measures, such as seatbelt laws, were controversial at first and are now widely supported.

The case for restraint

Adults have the right to take risks with their own lives. Paternalism can be patronising and can hit the poor hardest. Measures that go too far can damage trust in public health itself, making future cooperation harder. Some coercive measures in history, such as forced sterilisation, were justified in the name of public health.

Countries draw this line differently, depending on their legal traditions and cultures, and the same country can move it over time. Good public health practice uses the least intrusive measure that works, explains its reasoning openly, and reviews whether restrictions are still needed.

Chapter 15

The money and trust problem

The biggest uncertainty is not scientific. In 2025 international health aid fell sharply, as the United States, long the largest funder, cut its global health programmes and announced its withdrawal from the WHO, and several European donors also reduced aid. How much this sets back programmes against HIV, tuberculosis, malaria and childhood illness will depend on whether other governments fill the gap. At the same time, misinformation spreads faster than ever, and trust in health authorities has fallen in many countries since the pandemic.

The likely future is therefore mixed. The tools are better than ever: cheap sequencing, new vaccines, digital data and decades of evidence on what works. Whether they are used depends on funding, trust and political will. Public health has always been a political achievement as much as a scientific one, and that is unlikely to change. For where health systems themselves are heading, see Future healthcare systems.

The core idea

Public health is the organised effort to prevent disease across populations. It measures disease (epidemiology), finds causes, including the social determinants, and acts through a ladder of levers from information to law. It has added decades to human life, mostly through prevention rather than treatment.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Smallpox: last natural case 1977, declared eradicated 1980.
  • Under-five deaths: about 12.8 million (1990) to 4.8 million (2023).
  • Tobacco kills about 8 million people a year (WHO).
  • COVID-19 excess deaths 2020–21: about 15 million (WHO estimate).
The common mistake

Judging public health by what it does visibly. Its best work is the disease that never happened, which is why it is so easy to underfund.

Test yourself

Can you explain why cutting salt in everyone's bread might prevent more strokes than treating only people with very high blood pressure? If so, you understand Rose's prevention paradox.

The words, in plain words
Public health
The organised effort of society to prevent disease and promote health in populations.
Epidemiology
The study of how disease is spread across populations and what causes it.
Cohort study
A study that follows a group forward in time to see who develops disease.
Case-control study
A study that compares sick and healthy people and looks back for causes.
Primary prevention
Stopping a disease from starting, for example by vaccination.
Screening
Testing people without symptoms to catch disease early (secondary prevention).
Ring vaccination
Vaccinating the contacts of each case, and their contacts, to box in an outbreak.
Prevention paradox
A measure that helps the population a lot may help each individual very little.
Social determinants of health
The conditions in which people live and work, such as income and housing, that shape health.
Non-communicable disease
A disease not passed between people, such as heart disease or diabetes.
Excess mortality
Deaths above the number normally expected for that time and place.
Herd immunity
Protection for the whole group when enough people are immune that a disease cannot spread.
