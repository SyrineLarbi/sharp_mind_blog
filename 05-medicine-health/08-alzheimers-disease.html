Alzheimer's disease. A twenty-year illness that only shows itself in the last ten.

Most people picture Alzheimer's as something that arrives in old age: a parent who starts repeating questions, then forgets the way home, then forgets your name. That is the part families see. Underneath, the disease has usually been working for fifteen or twenty years before anyone notices. That long hidden phase explains why it has been so hard to treat, why the first drugs that slow it only arrived in the 2020s, and why the future of Alzheimer's care is mostly about catching it early.

Question 1 · What is it?

The most common cause of dementia, and not the same word

Alzheimer's is a specific disease of the brain with a specific physical signature. Dementia is the name for what that disease, and several others, do to a person.

Chapter 01

The woman who said "I have lost myself"

In 1901 a woman in her early fifties called Auguste Deter was admitted to a psychiatric hospital in Frankfurt. She was confused, suspicious and unable to remember what she had just been told. When the young doctor examining her asked her to write her name, she could not finish it. She reportedly said, "I have lost myself." The doctor was Alois Alzheimer.

Auguste died in 1906. Alzheimer examined her brain under a microscope using new staining methods, and he saw two things nobody had described together before. Between the nerve cells there were clumps of an odd material, which we now call plaques. Inside many nerve cells there were twisted bundles of fibres, which we now call tangles. The brain had also shrunk. He presented the case at a meeting in 1906, and a few years later his senior colleague Emil Kraepelin put Alzheimer's name on the disease in a textbook.

The disease was defined from the start by what the brain looks like, not only by how the person behaves. For most of the twentieth century that signature could be confirmed only after death. Doctors can now see it in living people.

Chapter 02

Dementia is the symptom, Alzheimer's is one cause

Dementia means a decline in memory, thinking or reasoning that is serious enough to interfere with daily life. It is a description, like "fever", not a diagnosis. Many different diseases can cause it. Alzheimer's is the most common, responsible for roughly 60 to 70 percent of cases according to the World Health Organization.

Chapter 03

Forgetting a name is not the same thing

Everyone forgets where they put the keys. Healthy ageing does slow some things: recalling names, finding words, doing two tasks at once. The difference with Alzheimer's is the pattern. A healthy person forgets a detail and remembers it later, or remembers that they forgot. A person with early Alzheimer's forgets the whole event, such as the phone call this morning, and the forgetting does not come back with a hint.

Doctors describe a middle stage called mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Here the person and their family notice a real decline, and tests confirm it, but the person still manages everyday life on their own. Not everyone with MCI develops dementia; some improve, for example when the cause was depression, a medicine side effect or poor sleep. But many people with MCI do have early Alzheimer's, and they are exactly the group the newest drugs are meant for.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Two misfolded proteins, a slow fire, and a brain that fights back

The disease is a chain of events lasting decades. Scientists agree on most of the links. They still argue about which link starts the chain, and which links matter most for treatment.

Chapter 04

Plaques and tangles, explained

Start with the plaques. Nerve cells carry a protein in their outer membrane called amyloid precursor protein, or APP. Enzymes cut it up as part of normal maintenance. One way of cutting produces a short sticky fragment called amyloid-beta. In a healthy brain, amyloid-beta is made and cleared all the time. When production outpaces clearance, the fragments stick together, first in small clusters and then into large deposits between the cells. Those deposits are the plaques Alzheimer saw.

Now the tangles. Nerve cells have long arms, and they move material along those arms on internal tracks, a bit like a railway. A protein called tau holds the railway sleepers in place. In Alzheimer's, tau gets too many chemical tags attached (phosphate groups), lets go of the tracks and twists into tangles inside the cell. The railway falls apart, supplies stop arriving, and the cell eventually dies.

The two proteins behave differently, and the difference is clinically important. Amyloid plaques can be spread widely across the brain in people who seem perfectly well. Tau tangles track the symptoms much more closely: where the tangles are, the brain shrinks, and that region's job fails. Tangles typically appear first in a small area next to the hippocampus, the memory hub described in Psychology · Memory. That is why recent memories are the first casualty. Then tangles spread outward along connected pathways, a sequence described by the German anatomists Heiko and Eva Braak in the early 1990s.

Three lessons follow. First, by the time memory is clearly failing, a large amount of brain tissue is already lost, and lost nerve cells do not grow back. Second, a drug that clears amyloid might work best if given before tau has started spreading, which means before symptoms. Third, the disease has a long, quiet window in which prevention or early treatment could in principle make a big difference.

Chapter 06

Genes: a few certainties and one big risk factor

A small number of families carry a mutation in one of three genes: APP itself, or PSEN1 or PSEN2, which make parts of the enzyme that cuts APP. In these families, inheriting the mutation means developing Alzheimer's, often in one's forties or fifties. These families account for well under one percent of all cases. They matter far beyond their numbers, because almost every one of these mutations increases amyloid-beta production. That was strong early evidence that amyloid is involved in starting the disease.

People with Down syndrome have an extra copy of chromosome 21, which carries the APP gene. Most of them develop Alzheimer's changes by middle age. Again, more APP means more amyloid, and more amyloid means earlier disease.

For everyone else, the most important gene is APOE, which helps carry fats and cholesterol around the brain. It comes in three common versions, called e2, e3 and e4. Carrying one copy of e4 raises the risk of late-onset Alzheimer's roughly three to four times; carrying two raises it much more, by some estimates around ten times. The e2 version seems mildly protective. But e4 is a risk factor, not a sentence. Many people with e4 never develop the disease, and many people without it do.

A family that taught science

A large extended family in Antioquia, Colombia, carries a PSEN1 mutation that causes Alzheimer's around age 45. In 2019 researchers described one member who carried the mutation and stayed sharp into her seventies. She also had two copies of a rare APOE variant nicknamed "Christchurch". Her brain was full of amyloid, but tau had spread very little. One person proves nothing, but her case pushed researchers to study how APOE and tau interact.

Chapter 07

The other actors: immune cells, blood vessels and sleep

Plaques and tangles are not the whole story. The brain has its own immune cells called microglia. Their job is to clean up debris and fight infection. In Alzheimer's they cluster around plaques and try to clear them. Sometimes they help. Sometimes they seem to get stuck in an inflamed state and damage healthy connections. Several risk genes are active mainly in microglia, which has turned inflammation from a side effect into a suspected driver.

Blood vessels matter too. The brain uses about a fifth of the body's energy, and it depends on a steady blood supply. High blood pressure, diabetes and smoking damage small vessels, which reduces blood flow and may also weaken the clearance of amyloid. This is why heart health and brain health are so closely linked, and why the risk factors in The shared roots keep appearing in dementia research.

Then there is sleep. Around 2013, the Danish-American neuroscientist Maiken Nedergaard and colleagues described in mice a flushing system in which fluid flows through the brain during sleep and washes waste out. They called it the glymphatic system. Whether years of poor sleep help cause Alzheimer's, or early Alzheimer's simply damages sleep, is unsettled. Probably both.

Chapter 08

Why some brains cope: cognitive reserve

One of the most surprising findings in this field is that the amount of damage in the brain does not match the amount of dementia very closely. Autopsy studies have found people with plenty of plaques and tangles who had no obvious symptoms in life. A well-known long-term study of elderly American nuns, led by the epidemiologist David Snowdon from the 1980s onward, reported cases like this, and also found that how rich and complex a nun's writing was in her early twenties predicted her risk decades later.

The explanation researchers use is cognitive reserve. A brain with many alternative pathways, built through education, mentally demanding work, languages and social life, can lose some connections and still find another route. Reserve does not stop the disease. It delays the moment when the damage shows. Once symptoms do appear in someone with high reserve, the disease is usually further along, so decline can then be faster.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A disease of families, budgets and ageing societies

Alzheimer's takes away the thing people value most about themselves. Because the world is ageing, it is also turning into one of the largest health costs any society carries.

Chapter 09

The scale

The WHO estimated that about 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide in 2021, with around 10 million new cases a year. The biggest risk factor is age itself. Risk roughly doubles every five years after 65. As the share of people over 80 rises in nearly every country, the number of people with dementia is expected to keep rising for decades, with the fastest growth in low- and middle-income countries.

Women make up around two thirds of people with Alzheimer's, largely because they live longer. In England and Wales, dementia and Alzheimer's together became the leading recorded cause of death in the mid-2010s, overtaking heart disease, partly because heart disease deaths fell so much.

The money is enormous. The WHO put the global cost of dementia at about 1.3 trillion US dollars in 2019. More than half of that is not medical spending at all. It is the unpaid work of family members.

Chapter 10

The hidden patients: the carers

From diagnosis, people with Alzheimer's typically live for several years, often somewhere between four and ten, and some for twenty. In the later stages they need help with washing, eating and moving, and they may not recognise their closest relatives. Most of that care is given at home by spouses and adult children, more often women, who pay for it in depression, poor sleep and lost work.

Good dementia care is mostly not about drugs. It is about a clear diagnosis, planning while the person can still take part in decisions, safety at home, respite for carers, and treating the things that make confusion worse, such as pain, infections, hearing loss and constipation.

Chapter 11

How it is diagnosed now

Until recently, diagnosis meant interviews, memory tests and ruling out other causes such as thyroid problems, vitamin B12 deficiency or depression. A doctor could say "probable Alzheimer's" but could not see the disease. Three tools changed that.

Brain scans (PET)

A PET scan uses a radioactive tracer that sticks to amyloid or to tau, so the deposits light up on the image. Accurate, but expensive and available mainly in large hospitals.

Spinal fluid

A lumbar puncture collects a little of the fluid around the brain and spinal cord. Levels of amyloid and tau in it reflect what is happening in the brain. Cheaper than PET, but more invasive.

Blood tests

The newest tool. Tests measuring a form of tau called p-tau217 can detect Alzheimer's changes with high accuracy in specialist settings. The first such test was cleared for clinical use in the United States in 2025.

Blood tests could change the system, because any clinic can take blood. But a positive result in someone with no symptoms tells them they have the biology of the disease, not when or whether they will become ill. Knowing early is only useful if there is something to do with that knowledge, which is why testing and treatment are developing together.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The first drugs that change the course, and what they cost

For decades Alzheimer's medicine could only ease symptoms. The 2020s brought drugs that slow the disease itself. The improvement is real, modest and expensive, and doctors disagree about how much it is worth.

The antibody story deserves a closer look. Lecanemab was approved in the United States in 2023, donanemab in 2024. In their main trials, both cleared most of the amyloid from patients' brains, and the people treated declined more slowly than those on placebo. The slowing worked out at a few months of extra time over the trial period. Supporters call it the first proof that the disease can be changed, and expect more benefit from earlier, longer treatment.

Critics say a difference that size may be hard for a family to notice, and the side effects are real: people with two copies of APOE e4 have the highest risk of brain swelling and bleeding, and some deaths have been reported. Treatment needs repeated hospital visits and MRI monitoring, which few health systems can provide at scale. Regulators have split. The European regulator approved lecanemab in 2025 only for patients without two copies of APOE e4, while England's cost-effectiveness body, NICE, judged that the benefit was too small for the price to justify routine NHS funding. The disagreement is not about whether the drugs work at all. It is about whether a modest benefit justifies the risk, the cost and the strain on services.

Chapter 13

The amyloid hypothesis: right, wrong or incomplete?

In 1992 the British geneticist John Hardy and his colleague Gerald Higgins set out the amyloid cascade hypothesis: amyloid-beta builds up first, and everything else, tau, inflammation and cell death, follows from it. For thirty years it guided most drug research. It produced a long series of failed trials, and critics argued that the field had put too many eggs in one basket. In 2022 an investigation also raised serious concerns about images in an influential 2006 amyloid study, which was later retracted.

The case for

Every known inherited form of the disease changes amyloid production. Amyloid appears first in the timeline. And the antibody trials showed that removing amyloid does slow decline, which is what the hypothesis predicted. The retracted paper concerned one specific form of amyloid and did not underpin the whole theory.

The case against

Many people have heavy amyloid and no symptoms. Removing almost all of it slows decline only modestly. Tau, inflammation and blood vessels seem to carry the disease forward once it starts. Decades of focus on amyloid may have starved other ideas of money.

Most researchers now hold a middle position. Amyloid is probably the trigger, but not the whole fire. Once tau is spreading and microglia are inflamed, the disease may continue even without much amyloid. That is why the next generation of trials combines approaches, treats earlier, or targets tau directly.

Chapter 15

Prevention, the part already within reach

The most hopeful number in the field does not come from a drug. In 2024 the Lancet Commission on dementia, a large international expert group, estimated that around 45 percent of dementia cases could in theory be prevented or delayed by dealing with 14 risk factors across life. The list includes less education in childhood, hearing loss, high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, obesity, diabetes, physical inactivity, heavy drinking, depression, social isolation, head injury, air pollution and untreated vision loss.

That estimate is a model, not a promise. Some links may run backwards: early disease can cause isolation and inactivity. But other findings support it. In some wealthy countries, the share of older people with dementia at a given age appears to have fallen over recent decades, probably because of better education and better control of blood pressure. Natural experiments are adding new clues. A 2025 study of a shingles vaccination programme in Wales, for example, suggested that vaccinated people were somewhat less likely to be diagnosed with dementia, an intriguing result that still needs confirmation.

The practical message is unglamorous and solid. What protects the heart largely protects the brain: blood pressure control, movement, not smoking, and treating hearing loss. The future of Alzheimer's will likely combine early blood tests, a few targeted drugs, and a public-health effort that starts in midlife, not at the memory clinic.

The core idea

Alzheimer's is a brain disease marked by amyloid plaques outside nerve cells and tau tangles inside them. It begins 15 to 20 years before symptoms, spreads from memory areas outward, and is the most common cause of dementia. New drugs slow it modestly; prevention through vascular health may matter as much.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 57 million people lived with dementia worldwide in 2021 (WHO).
  • Alzheimer's causes roughly 60–70% of dementia.
  • Anti-amyloid antibodies slowed decline by roughly a quarter to a third over 18 months.
  • Up to about 45% of dementia may be preventable or delayable (Lancet Commission, 2024).
The common mistake

Treating Alzheimer's as an old-age event that starts when memory fails. It is a long process that starts in midlife, which is why both prevention and treatment are moving earlier.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a drug that removes almost all amyloid only slows the disease a little? If you can, you understand the timeline, the role of tau, and the debate over the amyloid hypothesis.

The words, in plain words
Dementia
A decline in memory and thinking serious enough to disrupt daily life. A description, with many possible causes.
Amyloid-beta
A sticky protein fragment cut from a larger protein (APP). It clumps into plaques.
Plaque
A deposit of amyloid-beta between nerve cells.
Tau
A protein that stabilises the transport tracks inside nerve cells. In Alzheimer's it twists into tangles.
Tangle
A twisted bundle of tau inside a nerve cell. Tangles track symptoms closely.
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)
A measurable decline that does not yet stop a person living independently.
APOE e4
A common version of a fat-carrying gene that raises the risk of late-onset Alzheimer's.
Microglia
The brain's resident immune cells, which clear debris and can drive inflammation.
Glymphatic system
A fluid-flushing system that clears waste from the brain, mostly during sleep.
Cognitive reserve
The brain's ability to keep working despite damage, built through education and mental activity.
Anti-amyloid antibody
A lab-made immune protein that marks amyloid for removal. Lecanemab and donanemab are examples.
ARIA
Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities: brain swelling or small bleeds seen on MRI in people taking these drugs.
