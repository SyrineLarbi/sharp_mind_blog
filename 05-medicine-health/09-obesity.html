Obesity. Not a failure of willpower, but a thermostat set in the wrong world.

The usual story about obesity is simple: some people eat too much and move too little, and they should try harder. The arithmetic in that story is true. Almost everything else in it is misleading. Body weight is controlled by the brain, much like body temperature, and the brain fights back when weight falls. Genes decide how strongly. The modern food environment pushes everyone in the same direction. Once you see obesity this way, it becomes much easier to understand why diets so often fail and why the new weight-loss drugs work.

Question 1 · What is it?

Too much body fat, or fat that harms you

Obesity is usually defined by a simple ratio of weight to height. That ratio is useful for populations and crude for individuals, and experts are now redrawing the definition.

Chapter 01

The mice that could not stop eating

In 1950, scientists at the Jackson Laboratory in Maine noticed a strain of mice that became enormously fat. They ate constantly and seemed never to feel full. The gene responsible was called "obese", and for over forty years nobody knew what it did. In 1994 the American geneticist Jeffrey Friedman and his team found out. The gene makes a hormone, released by fat cells, that tells the brain how much fat the body is carrying. They named it leptin, from the Greek for "thin".

Mice without leptin behave as if they are starving, however fat they become. Their brain never receives the message "there is enough stored". A handful of children have since been found with the same rare defect. They are ravenously hungry from infancy, and leptin injections transform them.

Leptin did not turn out to be a cure for ordinary obesity. Most people with obesity have plenty of leptin; their brains have become less responsive to it. But the discovery changed the science for good. Body fat is not just a passive store. It talks to the brain, and the brain regulates it.

Chapter 02

How it is measured, and why that is crude

The standard measure is the body mass index (BMI): weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. Someone 1.75 m tall and weighing 92 kg has a BMI of about 30. The ratio goes back to the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s, who wanted to describe the "average man", not diagnose anyone.

BMI's problems are well known. It cannot tell muscle from fat. It ignores where fat sits, which matters a lot. And the same BMI carries different risks in different populations: people of South Asian and East Asian ancestry tend to develop diabetes at lower BMIs, so several countries use lower thresholds for them. A simple extra check is waist size. UK guidance suggests keeping your waist below half your height.

Chapter 03

A disease, a risk factor, or both?

Whether obesity is a "disease" has been argued for years. The American Medical Association recognised it as one in 2013, and the World Health Organization treats it as a chronic disease. Critics worried the label would turn millions of healthy people into patients.

In January 2025 a commission of experts convened by The Lancet proposed a middle path. It suggested splitting the idea in two. Clinical obesity means excess fat that is already harming how organs work or limiting daily life, for example causing breathlessness, joint damage or raised blood sugar. Preclinical obesity means excess fat with no harm yet, but a higher risk for the future. The commission also argued that BMI alone should not be enough to diagnose anyone. The proposal is influential but still debated, and how countries will apply it is not yet settled.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A regulated system, pushed off balance

Weight follows a simple equation, but the brain controls both sides of it, and the environment keeps nudging the controls.

Chapter 04

Energy in, energy out: true and not very useful

Physics is not negotiable. If you take in more energy from food than you use, the extra is stored, mostly as fat. A kilogram of body fat holds roughly 7,000 to 8,000 kilocalories. So weight gain is always, in a narrow sense, about calories.

The trouble is that "eat less, move more" treats both sides of the equation as dials you can set freely. They are not. When you eat less, your body responds by making you hungrier and by burning less. When you move more, you tend to eat more and, as research on daily energy use suggests, the body partly compensates by spending less on other things. Saying obesity is "about calories" is like saying a crowded room is about more people coming in than going out. True, but it does not explain why.

Chapter 05

The brain's weight thermostat

Deep in the brain, a small region called the hypothalamus acts as a control centre for hunger. It receives signals from all over the body. Leptin reports on long-term fat stores. Hormones from the gut, such as GLP-1, report on the meal you just ate. A hormone called ghrelin, made in the stomach, rises before meals and drives hunger. The hypothalamus combines these signals and adjusts appetite and energy use.

How the system defends weight
   fat cells ---- leptin ("how much is stored") ------+
   gut ---------- GLP-1, PYY ("a meal just arrived") -+-->  HYPOTHALAMUS
   stomach ------ ghrelin ("time to eat") ------------+         |
                                                                 v
                      weight falls  -->  hunger UP, energy use DOWN  -->  weight drifts back
                      weight rises  -->  weak push back (the system resists loss more than gain)

The last line of that diagram is the key. The system is lopsided. It fights hard against weight loss, because for most of human history losing fat meant danger. It pushes back only weakly against weight gain, because surplus fat was rarely a problem in a world without supermarkets. Scientists sometimes describe a "set point" or a "settling point": a weight range the body tends to return to. That range can creep upward over years, and once it has, the brain defends the new, higher weight.

Chapter 06

Genes load the gun

Studies of twins and of adopted children show that body weight is strongly heritable. Adopted children's weight tends to resemble that of their biological parents more than that of the parents who raised them. Most estimates put the heritability of BMI somewhere between 40 and 70 percent.

That does not mean there is "a fat gene". For most people, hundreds of gene variants each nudge the risk a tiny amount. Strikingly, most of them act in the brain, on appetite and the response to food, not on metabolism in the muscles or liver. A minority of people have a single strong mutation, most commonly in a gene called MC4R that sits in the leptin pathway, and for them hunger is much harder to resist.

Heritability also needs care. It measures how much of the variation between people in a given environment comes from genes. It does not mean that weight is fixed. Human genes did not change between 1980 and 2020, yet obesity rates more than doubled. Genes decide who is most vulnerable; the environment decides how many become ill.

Chapter 07

The environment pulls the trigger

Two experiments changed how researchers think about the modern environment. In 2019 Kevin Hall and colleagues at the US National Institutes of Health had 20 adults live in a research unit for four weeks. For two weeks they were offered ultra-processed food; for two weeks, minimally processed food. The meals were matched for calories, sugar, fat and fibre on the plate, and people could eat as much as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 500 kilocalories a day more, and gained weight. It was a small study, but a carefully controlled one.

The second comes from the anthropologist Herman Pontzer, who measured the daily energy use of the Hadza, hunter-gatherers in Tanzania who walk many kilometres a day. Their total energy use, adjusted for body size, was similar to that of sedentary Westerners. Exercise is excellent for health, as Exercise physiology explains, but the obesity epidemic seems to be driven more by what and how much we eat than by a collapse in calories burned.

Other pressures add up: cheap energy-dense food everywhere, large portions, sugary drinks, short sleep, stress, some medicines, and marketing aimed at children. None of these is a choice in the simple sense. They are the water everyone swims in.

Chapter 08

Why most diets fail in the long run

Nearly any diet works for a few months. The problem is keeping the weight off. In many trials, a large share of the lost weight returns within two to five years. The reason is that the brain treats weight loss as a threat.

The clearest example is a 2016 study of contestants from the American television show The Biggest Loser, who had lost very large amounts of weight through extreme diet and exercise. Six years later most had regained much of it. More surprising, their resting metabolism was still burning around 500 kilocalories a day less than expected for their size. This slowdown is called metabolic adaptation. Hunger hormones stay raised too. A person who has lost weight is not in the same position as a person who was always that weight: they need to eat less, while feeling hungrier.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A global shift with consequences for every organ

Obesity raises the risk of many of the most common serious diseases. It is also one of the fastest health changes in human history.

Chapter 09

The scale

A large global analysis published in The Lancet in 2024 estimated that in 2022 more than one billion people worldwide were living with obesity, about one in eight people. Adult obesity rates had more than doubled since 1990, and rates among children and teenagers had roughly quadrupled. In the United States, around 40 percent of adults have obesity. Rates are rising fastest in many low- and middle-income countries, some of which now face undernutrition and obesity at the same time, sometimes in the same household.

Chapter 10

Fat is an organ, and where it sits matters

Fat tissue is active. It releases hormones and inflammatory signals. Fat under the skin, on the hips and thighs, is relatively harmless. Fat packed around the organs in the abdomen, called visceral fat, and fat that builds up inside the liver are much more dangerous. They drive insulin resistance, the mechanism behind type 2 diabetes described in Diabetes, and what insulin resistance means.

Metabolic harms

Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol, fatty liver disease, heart attack and stroke. Obesity is also linked to a higher risk of at least a dozen cancers, including bowel, womb, kidney and oesophageal cancer.

Mechanical harms

Extra weight strains knees and hips, causing osteoarthritis. Fat around the neck and chest causes sleep apnoea, where breathing stops repeatedly at night. These harms often affect daily life more than the metabolic ones.

Not everyone with a high BMI is unwell, and some thin people have dangerous visceral fat. But across populations the link between obesity and disease is strong and consistent. Losing even 5 to 10 percent of body weight improves blood sugar, blood pressure and liver fat measurably in people who are carrying harmful fat.

Chapter 11

The stigma, and why it backfires

People with obesity face open discrimination in jobs, schools and even clinics. Surveys repeatedly find that health professionals hold negative views about patients with obesity, and patients who feel judged avoid seeing doctors. Research on weight stigma finds it is linked to stress, comfort eating and further weight gain. In other words, shaming people about their weight tends to make the problem worse.

This is where the biology from Q2 matters socially. If weight is mainly about character, blame makes sense. If weight is a regulated trait shaped by genes and environment, blame is both unfair and useless. Many medical bodies now recommend "people-first" language, such as "a person with obesity", and treating obesity like other chronic conditions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Four ways to treat it, and none is free

For individuals, the options are lifestyle change, medicines and surgery. For whole populations, the option is changing the environment. Each has a different balance of benefit, risk and cost.

Two points stand out. First, surgery works mainly through biology, not just a smaller stomach: it changes gut hormones, including GLP-1, which is part of why the drugs were developed. A long-running Swedish study that began in the 1980s, the Swedish Obese Subjects study, found that people who had surgery lived longer on average than similar people who did not. Second, the drugs behave like blood-pressure tablets, not like antibiotics. They control the condition while you take them. Volume 13 covers that point in more detail.

Chapter 13

Policies for whole populations, weighed

What policy can do

Taxes on sugary drinks have reduced purchases where they were introduced, including Mexico from 2014. The UK's levy, from 2018, was designed to push manufacturers to reformulate, and many cut sugar to avoid it. Front-of-pack labels, limits on junk-food advertising to children, and healthier school meals all aim to change the default rather than rely on willpower.

The limits and objections

Evidence that any single policy has reversed obesity rates is weak, partly because the effects are small and slow to show. Food taxes hit poorer households hardest as a share of income. Some people see them as the state overruling personal choice. Industry lobbying shapes what gets passed.

The honest summary is that no country has yet reversed an obesity epidemic. The best-supported approach combines many small changes to the food environment, because the environment is what changed in the first place.

Chapter 15

The harder question the drugs raise

If a weekly injection can quiet the brain's hunger signals, does society still need to change the food environment? Some argue that the drugs finally make obesity a treatable medical condition, like high blood pressure, and that this is simply progress. Others worry that relying on lifelong medicine for a problem largely created by the environment is expensive and treats the symptom, not the cause. There are also questions about fairness: when supply is short and prices are high, who gets the drugs first?

Both sides agree on one thing. Prevention is cheaper than treatment, and it matters most in childhood, before the body's defended weight has crept upward. The likely future combines both: medicines for people who already have harmful obesity, and slow, unglamorous changes to food, cities and schools for everyone else. For the public-health side of that, see Nutrition policy.

The core idea

Body weight is regulated by the brain, which uses signals such as leptin and gut hormones to defend a weight range. Genes set how vulnerable each person is; the modern food environment pushes everyone upward. The system resists weight loss far more than gain, which is why diets usually fail and why drugs acting on the brain work.

The numbers worth knowing
  • More than 1 billion people lived with obesity in 2022, about 1 in 8.
  • BMI of 30 or more is the usual threshold; keep your waist below half your height.
  • Heritability of BMI: roughly 40–70%.
  • GLP-1 drugs: about 15–20% average loss in trials; surgery about 25–30%.
The common mistake

Thinking that because weight obeys "calories in, calories out", obesity is simply a choice. The brain controls both sides of the equation, and it fights back.

Can you explain why someone who has lost 20 kg needs to eat less than someone who has always weighed the same as them? If so, you understand metabolic adaptation and the lopsided thermostat.

BMI
Body mass index: weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. A rough population measure.

A hormone made by fat cells that tells the brain how much fat is stored.

A hormone from the stomach that rises before meals and drives hunger.

The drop in energy use, beyond what size predicts, that follows weight loss.

Fat stored around the abdominal organs. More harmful than fat under the skin.

When cells respond weakly to insulin, so blood sugar stays high.
