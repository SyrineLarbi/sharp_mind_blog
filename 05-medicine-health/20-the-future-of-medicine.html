The future of medicine. From repairing damage late to reading trouble early.

For most of its history, medicine has waited for people to fall ill and then tried to fix them. The next few decades are likely to push it in the other direction: detecting disease years before symptoms, designing drugs on computers, editing faulty genes inside the body, and treating long-term conditions with medicines that act on many organs at once. Some of this is already happening. Some is a decade away. Some may never arrive. And medicine also faces heavy pressures: ageing populations, drug-resistant bacteria, rising costs and too few health workers. This file sorts the likely from the hopeful, and closes the Medicine & Health category.

Question 1 · What is it?

A set of shifts already under way

"The future of medicine" is not one invention. It is several changes in how medicine finds, designs and delivers treatment, each moving at its own speed.

Chapter 01

A fifty-year puzzle, solved by software

Proteins are the working machines of the body. Each is a chain of building blocks that folds into a precise three-dimensional shape, and the shape decides what it does. For decades, finding a protein's shape meant months or years of difficult lab work, and predicting it from the chain alone was one of biology's great unsolved problems. Since 1994, researchers had held a contest every two years to see who could predict shapes best.

In 2020 a program called AlphaFold, from the company Google DeepMind, won that contest by a huge margin, predicting many shapes almost as accurately as lab experiments. Its makers then released predicted structures for nearly every known protein, free. In 2024 Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of DeepMind shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work, together with the American biochemist David Baker, who designs entirely new proteins.

AlphaFold did not cure any disease by itself. But it shows the shape of the future well. The biggest changes may come from tools that speed up discovery, rather than from a single miracle cure. Many of the changes in this file follow that pattern.

None of these shifts is complete, and none will replace the old way entirely. Most medicine in 2040 will still be checking blood pressure, prescribing cheap generic drugs and setting broken bones. The shifts change the edges first, then slowly move inward.

Chapter 03

How to read a medical prediction

Medical news is full of breakthroughs that never reach patients. A useful habit is to ask where an idea is in the long pipeline from lab to clinic. Roughly nine in ten drugs that enter human trials never get approved, and taking a new drug from discovery to approval commonly takes ten years or more.

Two more rules help. First, approval is not access: a drug can be approved and still be too expensive, or too complex to deliver, for most patients. Second, results in mice or cells tell you what is possible, not what will happen in people. Volume 44's checklist in Reading a health headline applies to every claim in this file.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The engines of change

Five technologies drive most of the shifts. Each turns biology into something closer to engineering: designed, programmed and tested faster.

Chapter 04

Programmable medicines: mRNA and beyond

An mRNA vaccine delivers a written instruction that tells cells to make a harmless piece of a virus, training the immune system. Volume 13 explains how it works. The important point for the future is that mRNA is a platform. To target a different disease, you change the instruction, not the whole manufacturing process. That is why COVID-19 vaccines could be designed within days of the virus's genetic code being published. In 2023 Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discoveries that made those vaccines possible.

The same platform is now being tested against cancer. In personalized cancer vaccines, a patient's tumour is sequenced, the mutations unique to it are identified, and an mRNA vaccine is written to teach the immune system to recognise those exact mutations. A mid-stage trial in melanoma, reported in 2022 and 2023, found fewer relapses when such a vaccine was added to immunotherapy. Larger trials are under way. Other "programmable" drugs work on similar principles: short strands of genetic material that switch off a single faulty gene, such as drugs that lower cholesterol with an injection twice a year.

Chapter 05

Gene editing moves inside the body

The first approved CRISPR therapy, from 2023, works by taking a patient's blood stem cells out, editing them in a lab and putting them back. It works, but it is costly and requires harsh chemotherapy to make room for the edited cells. The next step, already in trials, is to deliver the editing tools directly into the body, usually wrapped in tiny fat bubbles that travel to the liver.

Newer tools are also more precise. Standard CRISPR cuts both strands of DNA, as Volume 13 describes. Base editing changes a single letter without a full cut, and prime editing can write short new sequences. These lower the risk of unwanted changes. In 2025 a baby in Philadelphia with a rare liver disorder received a base-editing treatment designed just for him, described in Personalized medicine. The challenge now is reaching organs other than the liver and blood, such as the brain and muscles, and bringing costs down. Editing embryos, so that changes pass to future generations, remains widely rejected; see Volume 50.

Chapter 06

AI in discovery and diagnosis

Artificial intelligence is entering medicine at two ends. At the discovery end, programs predict protein shapes, suggest molecules likely to bind a target, and design new proteins. Several drugs whose design was helped by AI have entered human trials. Whether AI shortens the long and failure-prone trial stage, the most expensive part, is not yet proven.

At the clinical end, AI reads images. By 2025 the US regulator had authorised many hundreds of AI-based medical devices, most of them in radiology: tools that flag possible strokes on brain scans, spot signs of diabetic eye disease in retinal photos, or act as a second reader in breast screening. Large language models, explained in Volume 49, are being used to draft clinical notes and answer patient questions. They also invent things with confidence, which is dangerous in medicine. The likely future is AI as an assistant that a clinician checks, not a replacement. The detailed picture is in AI in healthcare.

Chapter 07

Catching disease earlier

Much of the future is not new treatment but earlier warning. Blood tests can now detect Alzheimer's changes years before symptoms, as Alzheimer's disease describes. Tests for tumour DNA in blood are being studied for cancer screening. Smart watches can detect an irregular heart rhythm called atrial fibrillation, a major cause of stroke. Continuous glucose monitors, small sensors worn on the arm, became available without a prescription in the United States in 2024.

Earlier detection is powerful only if two conditions hold: there must be a treatment that works better when started early, and the test must not raise too many false alarms. Otherwise it creates anxiety, extra tests and overtreatment. More information is not automatically better health, a point made carefully in Screening, and why more is not always better.

Chapter 08

Drugs that change whole diseases

Every so often a single class of drugs reshapes several diseases at once. Statins did this for heart disease. The GLP-1 drugs, explained in Volume 13 and in Obesity, may be the next example. Trials have shown benefits not only for weight and diabetes but also for heart attacks, kidney disease and sleep apnoea, and they are being tested in many other conditions. Not all such tests succeed, and some will fail.

Long-acting medicines are a second example. In 2024 a trial in Africa found that an injection of an HIV drug called lenacapavir, given only twice a year, prevented infection in all the women who received it. It was approved for HIV prevention in the United States in 2025. In malaria, two vaccines were recommended by the WHO in 2021 and 2023, the first ever against a parasite. Simpler dosing can matter as much as a new mechanism, because a drug that is easy to take is a drug people actually take.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Because the old model is running out of room

Medicine has to change because the problems are changing: more old people, more chronic disease, resistant bacteria, rising costs and too few staff.

Chapter 09

Older populations and long illnesses

In almost every country the share of people over 65 is rising, and in rich countries the number of people over 80 is growing fastest. Older people often live with several long-term conditions at once, such as diabetes, heart failure, arthritis and dementia, and take many medicines. Health systems built to treat one acute illness at a time struggle with this.

The workforce is also stretched. The WHO has projected a global shortfall of around 10 million health workers by 2030, concentrated in poorer countries. This is one reason why so much effort goes into earlier detection, treatment at home, and tools that let a nurse or a family doctor do what once needed a specialist. The social side is covered in Aging populations. The goal increasingly is not just a longer life but a longer healthy life, the "healthspan" idea from Volume 50.

Chapter 10

The old enemies are still here

Infections are not a solved problem. A major study published in The Lancet in 2024 estimated that bacterial infections resistant to antibiotics directly caused over a million deaths in 2021 and could directly cause about 39 million deaths in total between 2025 and 2050 if trends continue. Few new classes of antibiotics have been discovered in decades, partly because they make little money: a good new antibiotic is kept in reserve and used as little as possible. See Antibiotic resistance.

Pandemics remain a near-certain future event; only the timing and the pathogen are unknown. The lesson of COVID-19 was that fast vaccine platforms work, but that manufacturing, distribution and trust decide how many lives they save. Some of the most important medical progress will be about old problems, not new ones. See Pandemics.

Chapter 11

The cost problem

New drugs are expensive to develop. Estimates of the cost per approved drug vary widely but are often put at more than a billion dollars once failures are counted. In 2012 the analyst Jack Scannell and colleagues noticed that the number of new drugs approved per billion dollars spent on research had halved roughly every nine years since 1950. They called this Eroom's law, Moore's law spelled backwards, because computing kept getting cheaper while drug discovery kept getting more expensive.

Prices follow. Some gene therapies cost over two million dollars per patient. Even widely used drugs like the GLP-1s strain budgets because so many people could benefit. Cheaper generic versions and new technologies may help. But a cure nobody can afford is, for most people, no cure at all. How countries pay for innovation is the subject of Healthcare financing.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous promise, real risks, and a question of who benefits

The new tools could prevent a great deal of suffering. They also bring new kinds of error, new costs and new inequalities.

Chapter 13

Who gets the future first

The hopeful case

Some advances spread quickly to poorer countries, especially cheap ones. Malaria vaccines are being rolled out across Africa. Generic versions of many drugs cost a fraction of the original price. Phones and low-cost tests can bring diagnosis to places without specialists. Deals to supply long-acting HIV prevention at low cost in poorer countries were announced in 2025.

The worrying case

The most advanced treatments need specialist hospitals, cold chains and high budgets. Genetic and AI tools are built mostly on data from wealthy populations. Global health aid was cut sharply in 2025. Without deliberate effort, the gap between what medicine can do and what most people receive may widen.

History offers both patterns. Vaccines and oral rehydration salts reached much of the world within decades. Many cancer drugs still have not. Which pattern the next generation of medicine follows depends less on the technology than on prices, manufacturing and political choices, the subject of Healthcare access in developing countries.

Chapter 15

What probably won't change

It is easy to imagine a future in which technology solves health. The evidence suggests something more modest. The biggest causes of ill health in most countries remain tobacco, high blood pressure, unhealthy diet, inactivity, alcohol, air pollution and poverty. The most effective responses are still largely the ones described in Public health and Exercise physiology: prevention, basic primary care and cheap medicines used well.

Something else will not change either. Medicine is a relationship between people. A diagnosis needs explaining, a choice between treatments needs weighing against a person's values, and a frightened patient needs someone to talk to. The best future of medicine combines powerful new tools with the old basics: honest evidence, fair access and a clinician who listens. That is a fitting place to end this category. The next, Psychology, begins with how the mind stores its own past, in Memory.

The core idea

Medicine is shifting from treating late to detecting early, from averages to individuals, and from drugs found by chance to drugs designed with platforms such as mRNA, gene editing and AI. The pressures driving change are ageing, chronic disease, resistance and cost. Who benefits depends on prices and politics as much as science.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 9 in 10 drugs that enter human trials are never approved.
  • AlphaFold: Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024; mRNA: Nobel in Medicine 2023.
  • Drug-resistant infections could directly cause about 39 million deaths in 2025–2050 (Lancet, 2024).
  • A projected shortfall of about 10 million health workers by 2030 (WHO).
The common mistake

Treating a lab result or an approval as a change in care. Approval is not access, and most breakthroughs in mice never reach people.

The words, in plain words
Platform technology
A method that can be quickly reused against new targets by changing one part, such as the mRNA instruction.
AlphaFold
An AI system that predicts the 3D shape of proteins from their building-block sequence.
Personalized cancer vaccine
A vaccine written to match the mutations of one patient's tumour.
In vivo gene editing
Editing genes inside the body rather than in cells removed from it.
Base editing
A gene-editing method that changes a single DNA letter without cutting both strands.
Clinical trial phases
The stages a new treatment passes through, from small safety tests to large trials.
Overdiagnosis
Diagnosing a condition that would never have caused symptoms or harm.
Eroom's law
The observation that drug discovery became steadily more expensive per approved drug over decades.
Antimicrobial resistance
The ability of microbes to survive drugs designed to kill them.
Xenotransplantation
Transplanting organs from animals, now usually gene-edited pigs, into people.
Healthspan
The years of life lived in good health.
Long-acting medicine
A drug given rarely, such as twice a year, that keeps working between doses.
