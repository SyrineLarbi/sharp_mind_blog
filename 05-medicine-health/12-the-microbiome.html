The microbiome. An organ you did not inherit, cannot see, and feed three times a day.

For most of medical history, bacteria in the body meant infection. The job was to kill them. Then, around the year 2010, cheap DNA sequencing let scientists count what actually lives in and on us, and the picture changed. Trillions of microbes, mostly in the gut, digest food we cannot, shape the immune system and make chemicals that travel round the body. Some of the findings are solid and already saving lives. Others are early results from mice that have been sold as cures. This file sorts one from the other.

Question 1 · What is it?

A crowd of microbes, and the genes they carry

"Microbiome" means the community of microorganisms living in a particular place, together with their genes. In people, the gut holds by far the largest community.

Chapter 01

The ten-to-one myth

For decades, textbooks and popular books repeated a striking claim: bacterial cells in your body outnumber your own cells ten to one. The figure came from a rough calculation in the early 1970s, and nobody checked it carefully for forty years. In 2016 the Israeli researchers Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo did the sums again with modern data. They estimated about 38 trillion bacteria and about 30 trillion human cells in a typical adult man. The ratio is roughly one to one, not ten to one.

That correction does not make the microbiome less important. Those bacteria weigh only about 0.2 kilograms in total, but they carry far more genes than we do, by most estimates a hundred times more. In terms of chemistry, the microbes add a whole extra toolkit that human cells lack. That is why some researchers describe the microbiome as an organ, one that every person assembles again from scratch after birth.

Chapter 02

Who lives where

The microbiome is not one community. Each part of the body is a different habitat, as different as a desert and a rainforest.

SiteConditionsTypical residents and role
Large intestine (colon)Warm, wet, almost no oxygen, plenty of foodThe densest community; hundreds of species per person. Ferments fibre the body cannot digest.
MouthSaliva, food, hard surfacesHundreds of species. Some cause tooth decay and gum disease when the balance shifts.
SkinDry, salty, cool; oily in some areasFewer microbes. Compete with harmful species and help keep skin acidic.
VaginaAcidicUsually dominated by Lactobacillus, which makes lactic acid and keeps out harmful organisms.
Stomach and small intestineAcidic or fast-flowingFar fewer microbes. Helicobacter pylori in the stomach can cause ulcers.

The community is mostly bacteria, but it also includes viruses (especially phages, viruses that infect bacteria), fungi such as yeasts, and an ancient group of single-celled organisms called archaea. Two healthy people can share surprisingly few species. What they tend to share more is the set of jobs being done. Different species can fill the same role.

Chapter 03

How scientists learned to see them

Until recently, studying a microbe meant growing it in a dish. Most gut bacteria do not survive oxygen or ordinary lab conditions, so they were invisible. The breakthrough was reading DNA directly. In the 1970s the American microbiologist Carl Woese showed that one gene, used in building the cell's protein factories (the 16S ribosomal RNA gene), varies between microbes in a way that works like a barcode. Sequencing that barcode in a stool sample shows which groups of bacteria are present without growing any of them.

A second method, called shotgun metagenomics, sequences all the DNA in a sample, so it reveals not only who is there but what genes they carry. In 2007 the US National Institutes of Health launched the Human Microbiome Project to map healthy communities across the body. As sequencing costs fell, the number of studies exploded. This is important for judging the field: most findings describe which microbes are present, not what they are doing, and a correlation in a stool sample is not proof of cause.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Digesting, training, protecting, signalling

Gut microbes do four main jobs for their host. The first three are well established. The fourth is the most exciting and the least settled.

Chapter 04

A second digestive system

Human enzymes break down sugars, starches, fats and proteins, mostly in the small intestine. What they cannot break down, mainly fibre from plants, passes into the colon. There, bacteria ferment it. Fermentation means breaking food down without oxygen, as yeast does when it makes beer.

The main products are small molecules called short-chain fatty acids: acetate, propionate and butyrate. Butyrate is the preferred fuel of the cells lining the colon. These molecules also help keep the gut wall tight, calm inflammation and send signals to the liver and brain about appetite and blood sugar. Bacteria also make some vitamins, including vitamin K, and change bile acids, which affects how fat is absorbed.

The practical lesson is direct. Fibre is food for your microbes, not just roughage. When people eat little fibre, some bacteria turn to the protective mucus layer lining the gut for food instead, which in mouse studies thins the barrier. This is one reason why diets rich in vegetables, legumes and whole grains are linked to better gut health.

Chapter 05

Training the immune system

The gut is where the immune system meets the outside world most intensely. It has to tolerate food and friendly bacteria while attacking dangerous invaders. Microbes help it learn the difference. Animals raised in sterile conditions, called germ-free animals, have badly developed immune tissue in the gut and respond abnormally to infection.

Resident bacteria also protect us directly. They occupy space and eat nutrients that harmful species would need, and some make substances that kill competitors. This is called colonisation resistance. It explains why a course of antibiotics, which kills friendly bacteria along with harmful ones, can open the door to dangerous infections.

Chapter 06

How each person's microbiome is built

Two details stand out. First, breast milk contains complex sugars that babies cannot digest at all. They exist to feed particular bacteria, mainly Bifidobacterium. In other words, human milk evolved partly to feed the baby's microbes. Second, babies born by caesarean section start with different bacteria, though the differences mostly fade over the first year or so. Whether they have lasting health effects is still debated.

Diet is the strongest everyday influence in adults. Geography matters too. Studies of hunter-gatherer groups such as the Hadza of Tanzania find far more diverse microbiomes than in industrialised cities, including species that seem to have been lost in the West.

Chapter 07

The gut-brain axis: real, but early

The gut and brain talk constantly. The main line is the vagus nerve, a long nerve connecting the brainstem to the gut and other organs. Gut hormones and immune signals carry messages too, and microbes produce or influence many chemicals used by nerves. You will often read that about 90 percent of the body's serotonin is made in the gut. That is broadly true, but gut serotonin does not cross into the brain; it works mostly on the gut itself.

The striking results come from animals. Germ-free mice behave differently in stress tests. Mice given gut bacteria from people with depression have, in some experiments, become more anxious. In humans the evidence is much weaker: people with depression or autism often show different gut microbiomes, but it is unclear which way the cause runs, since diet, medicines and stress all change the microbiome too. The gut-brain link is real; claims that changing your bacteria will treat mental illness are far ahead of the evidence.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

One cure, many clues, and a different way to see disease

The microbiome matters because it already explains one serious infection, because modern life seems to be changing it, and because it affects how people respond to food and medicines.

Chapter 08

The treatment that works: faecal transplants

Clostridioides difficile, often called C. diff, is a bacterium that causes severe diarrhoea and inflammation of the colon. It usually strikes after antibiotics have cleared out the normal gut community, typically in older patients in hospital. It can kill, and it often comes back after treatment with more antibiotics.

The fix sounds unpleasant and works remarkably well. Doctors take stool from a healthy, carefully screened donor and deliver it into the patient's gut, through a colonoscope, a tube or capsules. This is faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). In a Dutch trial published in 2013, about 81 percent of patients with recurring C. diff were cured by an FMT, compared with about 31 percent on the standard antibiotic. The trial was stopped early because the difference was so large.

Since then, FMT has become a standard option for recurring C. diff in many countries, and in 2022 and 2023 the US regulator approved the first standardised microbiome-based products for this use. It is the clearest proof that restoring a damaged community can cure a disease. It has also been tested for many other conditions, with mixed and mostly modest results so far.

Chapter 09

Modern life and the "old friends"

Allergies, asthma, inflammatory bowel disease and some autoimmune conditions have become more common in rich countries over the past century. In 1989 the British epidemiologist David Strachan noticed that children from large families had less hay fever, and suggested early infections might protect against allergy. This became the hygiene hypothesis.

The idea has been refined. It is probably not dirt or infections themselves that matter, but exposure to the harmless microbes humans evolved alongside: from soil, animals, other people and a varied diet. Researchers call these the "old friends". Antibiotics, caesarean births, processed diets and city living all reduce that exposure. Children raised on traditional farms with animals, for example, have lower asthma rates in several European studies. The "old friends" idea is a leading explanation, not a proven one, and it does not mean that hand-washing or vaccines cause allergies. They do not.

Chapter 10

Weight, food responses and medicines

In the mid-2000s the American microbiologist Jeffrey Gordon and his colleagues showed that gut bacteria from obese mice, transferred into germ-free mice, made the recipients gain more fat than bacteria from lean mice. Later experiments did the same with bacteria from human twins where one twin was obese and the other lean. In humans, though, the microbiome seems to be one modest influence on weight among many, not a main cause. See Obesity for the larger picture.

The microbiome also helps explain why people respond differently to the same food. A 2015 study at the Weizmann Institute in Israel tracked blood sugar in hundreds of people and found that responses to identical meals varied widely, and that gut bacteria helped predict them. Microbes also process some medicines, activating or inactivating them. In cancer, several studies since 2018 have linked the gut microbiome to how well patients respond to immunotherapy drugs, and small trials have tried FMT to help patients who were not responding. These are exactly the kind of findings that make personalized medicine more complicated, and more interesting.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A real science with a marketing problem

The strengths of the field are real discoveries. Its weaknesses are methods that struggle to show cause, and products that are sold before they are proven.

The table shows a pattern. Claims about mice are often true; claims about people are often premature. Mice can be raised germ-free, given a single bacterium and dissected, which makes them perfect for showing that a microbe can cause something. Humans differ in diet, genes, medicines and environment so much that the same effect may be tiny or absent.

Chapter 12

Probiotics, prebiotics and fermented food, honestly

What has support

Some probiotic strains modestly reduce diarrhoea linked to antibiotics in some groups. Fermented foods such as yoghurt, kefir and kimchi are safe for most people, and a 2021 Stanford study found that ten weeks of eating more of them increased microbiome diversity and lowered several markers of inflammation. Fibre, sometimes sold as "prebiotics", feeds helpful species.

What is weak or worrying

Most probiotic bacteria pass through and do not settle permanently. Effects depend on the exact strain, so one product's trial says little about another's. A 2018 study from the Weizmann Institute found that taking probiotics after antibiotics delayed the return of the person's own bacteria in many participants. Supplements are lightly regulated in many countries.

The broader weakness is in study design. Many microbiome papers compare sick and healthy people at one moment, so they cannot tell cause from effect. Different labs use different methods, which makes results hard to compare. And "diversity" is often treated as good in itself, although what counts as a healthy microbiome probably differs between people and populations. There is not yet an agreed definition of a healthy microbiome, which is one reason the consumer tests have little to measure against.

Chapter 14

What a sensible person does now

While the science matures, the evidence points to simple habits that are good for many other reasons too. Eat a wide variety of plants: vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts and whole grains. Include fermented foods if you like them. Use antibiotics only when they are needed, and take them for as long as your prescriber specifies, as Volume 44 explains. Be wary of expensive tests and supplements promising to "fix" your gut.

The bigger shift is in how medicine thinks. The old model saw microbes only as enemies. The new one sees the body as an ecosystem, where the goal is often to manage a community rather than to kill a single invader. That idea is likely to outlast any particular product, and it fits a wider trend in medicine described in The future of medicine.

The core idea

The microbiome is the community of microbes living in and on us, mostly in the colon. It ferments fibre into useful molecules, trains the immune system, blocks harmful species and sends signals round the body. One treatment, FMT for C. diff, is proven; many other claims are still early.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells: roughly 1 to 1, not 10 to 1.
  • Microbial genes outnumber human genes by around 100 to 1.
  • FMT cured about 81% of recurring C. diff in a 2013 trial, versus about 31% on antibiotics.
The common mistake

Taking a mouse result as a human fact. Mouse studies show what microbes can do; human studies show what they actually do, and the second is usually smaller.

The words, in plain words
Microbiome
The community of microorganisms in a place, such as the gut, together with their genes.
Microbiota
The microorganisms themselves, without counting their genes. Often used loosely as a synonym.
Fermentation
Breaking down food without oxygen. Gut bacteria ferment fibre.
Short-chain fatty acids
Small molecules such as butyrate made by fermenting fibre. They fuel gut cells and calm inflammation.
Germ-free animal
An animal raised in sterile conditions with no microbes at all, used to test what microbes do.
Colonisation resistance
The protection resident bacteria give by occupying space and food that harmful species need.
16S sequencing
Reading one barcode-like bacterial gene to identify which groups are present.
Metagenomics
Sequencing all the DNA in a sample to see every gene present.
Phage
A virus that infects bacteria. Possible tool for killing specific bacteria.
FMT
Faecal microbiota transplantation: moving a healthy donor's gut community into a patient.
Probiotic
Live microbes taken to give a health benefit. Effects depend on the strain.
Prebiotic
Food, usually fibre, that feeds helpful gut microbes.
Gut-brain axis
The two-way communication between gut and brain through nerves, hormones and immune signals.
