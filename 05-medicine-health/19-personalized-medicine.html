Personalized medicine. Treating the person in front of you, not the average patient in a trial.

Most medicine is built on averages. A drug is tested on thousands of people, it helps the group on average, and it is then given to everyone with the same diagnosis. But nobody is the average patient. The same pill can cure one person, do nothing for a second and harm a third. Personalized medicine, often called precision medicine, tries to predict in advance which of those three you will be. It uses your genes, the genes of your tumour, and other measurements of your body. In a few areas it has already transformed care. In many others it is still more promise than practice.

Question 1 · What is it?

Matching the treatment to the patient's biology

Personalized medicine means using information about an individual, especially their genes, to choose how to prevent, diagnose or treat their disease.

Chapter 01

The same dose, a different person

Codeine is a common painkiller, but on its own it does little. The liver converts part of it into morphine, which does the real work. The enzyme that does this is called CYP2D6, and the gene for it varies a great deal between people. Some people have a version that barely works, so codeine gives them almost no pain relief. Others carry extra copies of the gene and are "ultra-rapid metabolisers": they turn codeine into morphine so fast that a normal dose can become an overdose.

That variation had tragic consequences. Several children died or nearly died after being given codeine following routine throat surgery, and some breastfed babies were harmed when their mothers, who were ultra-rapid metabolisers, took it. In 2013 the US regulator added its strongest warning against codeine for children after these operations, and many countries now avoid it in young children altogether.

The story shows the basic logic of personalized medicine. A dose that is right "on average" can be wrong for a particular person, for reasons written in their genes. If you know those reasons in advance, you can choose a different drug or a different dose.

Chapter 02

Personalized, precision, stratified: and not new

The vocabulary is confusing. Personalized medicine suggests a treatment designed for one person. In practice, most of it is stratified medicine: dividing patients into groups that respond differently, and giving each group what works for it. In 2011 a report by the US National Research Council promoted the term precision medicine for this, and in 2015 the United States launched a Precision Medicine Initiative, which led to a research programme called All of Us that aims to collect health and genetic data from a million Americans. In everyday use the three terms overlap, and this file uses them loosely.

The idea itself is old. Blood transfusions only became safe after Karl Landsteiner discovered blood groups in 1901; matching blood type is personalized medicine. Since the 1960s, newborn babies in many countries have had a heel-prick blood test for phenylketonuria (PKU), an inherited condition in which a protein building block builds up and damages the brain. A special diet from birth prevents the damage. What is new is the scale and detail: doctors can now read a person's whole genome, and a tumour's, cheaply and quickly.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four ways biology becomes a treatment decision

Personalized medicine works through drug-response genes, the genetics of cancer, therapies built from the patient's own cells or genes, and risk prediction. They are at very different stages of maturity.

Chapter 04

Pharmacogenomics: genes that change how drugs behave

Pharmacogenomics is the study of how genes affect the response to drugs. Some genes control how fast the liver clears a drug, like CYP2D6 for codeine. Others control the immune system's reaction to it. A few examples are already part of routine care in many countries.

Preventing dangerous reactions

The HIV drug abacavir causes a serious allergic reaction in people with an immune gene variant called HLA-B*57:01. Testing for it before prescribing, introduced after a large trial in 2008, almost eliminated those reactions. Similarly, the epilepsy drug carbamazepine can cause a severe skin reaction in carriers of HLA-B*15:02, a variant more common in parts of East and South-East Asia, where testing is recommended.

Getting the dose right

Some chemotherapy drugs (fluoropyrimidines such as 5-FU) are broken down by an enzyme coded by the DPYD gene. People with low DPYD activity can be poisoned by a normal dose. UK and EU regulators recommended testing before treatment from 2020. The blood thinner warfarin is another case: genes help explain why the right dose varies so much.

Can testing many genes at once help more broadly? A large European trial called PREPARE, published in 2023, tested about 7,000 patients across seven countries with a panel of 12 drug-response genes before prescribing. The group whose prescriptions were guided by the results had around 30 percent fewer clinically relevant side effects. That is strong evidence that pre-emptive testing can work, though health systems are still working out how to pay for it and build it into everyday prescribing.

Chapter 05

Cancer: from the organ to the mutation

For most of the twentieth century, cancer was classified by where it started: breast, lung, colon. Treatment followed the organ. Then researchers began to classify tumours by their mutations, and oncology became the field where personalized medicine works best.

The landmark is chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a blood cancer. In most cases it is driven by a single faulty fusion gene, called BCR-ABL, created when two chromosomes swap pieces. The drug imatinib, approved in 2001, blocks the faulty protein that gene makes. Before it, CML was usually fatal within years. With imatinib and its successors, most patients now live close to a normal lifespan, taking a daily pill. Another early success was trastuzumab, an antibody approved in 1998 for the roughly one in five breast cancers that make too much of a protein called HER2.

Since then, dozens of targeted drugs have been approved, especially for lung cancer, melanoma and some leukaemias. In 2017 the US regulator approved a cancer drug for the first time based on a tumour's genetic feature regardless of where in the body it started. These "tumour-agnostic" approvals mark the shift in thinking. The catch is that cancers evolve. Under the pressure of a targeted drug, a few resistant cells often survive and grow back. That is why combinations and monitoring matter, as with antibiotics.

Chapter 06

Liquid biopsies: reading a tumour from a blood test

Tumours shed small fragments of their DNA into the bloodstream. A liquid biopsy is a blood test that detects and reads this circulating tumour DNA. It has three promising uses. It can identify a tumour's mutations without cutting out tissue. It can show whether treatment is working, or whether resistance mutations are appearing, months before a scan would. And after surgery, it can show whether any cancer remains.

A fourth use is the most ambitious: screening healthy people for many cancers with one blood test. Several companies sell such tests. Large trials are under way, but as of 2026 it is not yet proven that they reduce deaths from cancer, and false alarms and missed cancers are real concerns. Volume 42 explains why screening is not always better even when a test sounds accurate.

Chapter 07

Living drugs and treatments for one

Some treatments are personal in the most literal sense: they are made from the patient. In CAR-T therapy, doctors remove a patient's immune T cells, genetically modify them in a lab to recognise the patient's cancer, multiply them and infuse them back. In 2012 a six-year-old American girl, Emily Whitehead, became the first child treated this way for leukaemia that had resisted everything else. She was still in remission more than a decade later. The first CAR-T therapies were approved in the United States in 2017, mainly for blood cancers.

Gene therapies go further, correcting or compensating for a faulty gene. In late 2023 the UK and then the US approved the first CRISPR-based therapy, for sickle cell disease and a related blood disorder, using the patient's own edited stem cells. And a few treatments have now been made for a single patient. In 2019 doctors in Boston reported a drug designed for one girl, Mila Makovec, with a rare fatal brain disease, built to correct the effect of her specific mutation. In 2025 a team in Philadelphia reported treating a baby, known as KJ, with a gene-editing therapy custom-made for his rare liver condition within months of his birth. These "n-of-1" treatments show what is possible, though making them routine is a different challenge.

Chapter 08

Predicting risk: polygenic scores

Most common diseases, such as heart disease or type 2 diabetes, are not caused by one gene. They are influenced by thousands of variants, each adding a tiny amount of risk. A polygenic risk score adds up all those small effects into a single number.

At the extremes, these scores can identify people with substantially higher risk, for some conditions comparable to carrying a single high-risk gene. But for most people they add only modest information to what age, family history, blood pressure and cholesterol already tell a doctor. They are also much less accurate for people of non-European ancestry, because most of the studies they were built from involved people of European descent. A risk score is a probability, not a forecast, a point Volume 42 makes about all tests in A result is a probability, not a verdict. They are being trialled in screening programmes, but they are not yet standard care.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fewer wasted treatments, faster diagnoses, and a fairness test

The promise is better results with less harm. The risk is that the benefits go mostly to people who are already well served.

Chapter 09

The cost of treating everyone the same

Many widely used medicines help only a minority of the people who take them. Doctors measure this with the number needed to treat: how many people must take a drug for one person to benefit. For many preventive drugs that number is in the dozens or more. Everyone else takes the drug, pays for it, and risks its side effects without gaining. Adverse drug reactions are also a major cause of hospital admissions in older people, many of whom take several medicines at once.

If even part of this could be predicted, the gains would be large: fewer side effects, less wasted money, and less time spent on a treatment that will not work before trying one that might. In depression, for example, finding the right drug often takes several attempts over months, as described in Medication, without the mythology. The real promise of personalized medicine is not miracle cures but fewer wrong guesses.

Chapter 10

Ending the diagnostic odyssey for rare diseases

There are thousands of known rare diseases, most of them genetic. One alone is rare; together they affect a large number of people. Families often spend years going from specialist to specialist before anyone can name the condition. Doctors call this the diagnostic odyssey.

Whole-genome sequencing shortens it. England's 100,000 Genomes Project, completed in 2018, sequenced patients with rare diseases and cancer and their families, and found a diagnosis for roughly a quarter of the rare-disease patients, many of whom had waited years. In very sick newborns in intensive care, rapid sequencing can now return a result in days and change treatment. England's NHS now offers genome sequencing for some conditions as part of routine care, and a research programme is testing sequencing for large numbers of newborns. A name does not always bring a cure, but it ends uncertainty, guides care, informs family planning, and sometimes points to a treatment.

Chapter 11

Who benefits: ancestry, money and access

Genetic research has drawn mostly on people of European ancestry. That matters medically: a variant can look suspicious simply because it was never seen in the reference data, and risk scores work less well for other groups. Projects in Africa, Asia and among minority communities are working to close the gap, and newer reference genomes are built from many populations, as Volume 13 describes.

Price is the other barrier. Gene therapies can cost more than two million US dollars per patient, and CAR-T therapies several hundred thousand. Even where they are paid for, they require specialist centres. At the same time, medicine has been rethinking how it uses race, which is a social category rather than a precise biological one. In 2021, for example, US kidney specialists dropped a race adjustment from the standard formula for kidney function, because it had delayed care for some Black patients. The goal is biology measured in the individual, not assumed from a label, which is in a sense the core idea of personalized medicine.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Transformative in places, oversold in others

The successes are real and in some cases dramatic. The limits are evidence, cost, privacy and fairness.

Chapter 13

Where the promise ran ahead

When the human genome was completed in 2003, many predicted a quick revolution in which genetic tests would guide the treatment of most common diseases. That did not happen. The genetics of common diseases turned out to be spread across thousands of tiny effects, and environment and behaviour matter as much as genes. For most people today, the most personalized and useful information about their health is still blood pressure, weight, smoking, family history and age.

There have also been warnings. In the 2010s the American company Theranos claimed it could run hundreds of tests from a drop of blood. Its technology did not work as claimed, and its founder, Elizabeth Holmes, was convicted of fraud in 2022. The lesson is not that personalized testing is a fraud. It is that claims in this field need the same evidence as any other: trials showing that using the test actually improves outcomes, not just that it measures something.

Chapter 15

What it may mean for an ordinary patient

For most people, personalized medicine will not arrive as a single dramatic moment. It will arrive quietly. A genetic profile may be taken once and stored in the medical record. When a doctor prescribes a drug, the computer may flag that this patient processes it slowly. If cancer is diagnosed, the tumour will be sequenced and the treatment chosen accordingly, then a blood test will check every few months that it has not returned. Data from a watch may flag an irregular heartbeat before it causes a stroke.

The open questions are social as much as scientific. Who owns genetic data, and who may use it? Can health systems afford gene therapies for everyone who would benefit? Will tests designed in rich countries work for everyone else? The technology is moving faster than the answers. How those questions are settled will decide whether personalized medicine becomes precise medicine for everyone, or for the few. The wider picture of where medicine is going is in The future of medicine.

The core idea

Personalized (precision) medicine uses an individual's biology, especially genes and tumour mutations, to choose treatment. It already works well in cancer, drug-gene testing and rare-disease diagnosis. For common diseases, genes add modest information, and cost and fairness remain the big obstacles.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A 12-gene drug-response panel cut side effects by about 30% (PREPARE, 2023).
  • Imatinib, approved 2001, turned a fatal leukaemia into a manageable one.
  • First CRISPR therapy approved in 2023, for sickle cell disease.
  • Gene therapies can cost over $2 million per patient.
The common mistake

Thinking a genetic test tells you your future. Most genetic results are probabilities, and many are uncertain. The biggest successes are in specific drugs and specific diseases.

Test yourself

Can you explain why codeine can be useless for one person and dangerous for another? If so, you understand pharmacogenomics, the foundation of the field.
