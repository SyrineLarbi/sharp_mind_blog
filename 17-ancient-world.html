The ancient world. How people stopped wandering, and everything followed.

For almost the whole of human existence, people lived the same way — small bands, always moving, hunting and gathering, owning almost nothing. Then, in a few thousand years, in a handful of places, that ended. This is the story of what changed, why it changed beside rivers, and how a scatter of quarrelling Greek towns and one stubborn Italian village came to shape the way half the world still thinks.

Part I

The first cities

Chapter 01

The seed that changed everything

Imagine you have never stayed anywhere for more than a few weeks. Your family walks. You follow the herds in summer and the berries in autumn. You own what you can carry, and you carry it on your back. Everyone you have ever met is someone you are related to. This is not poverty — it is simply how human beings lived, for tens of thousands of years, everywhere on Earth.

Then someone noticed something. Push a seed into the ground, walk away, come back months later, and there is food waiting where you left it.

That is the whole of it. That is farming. And it is probably the single most consequential thing our species has ever worked out, because it broke the one rule that had governed every human life until then: you no longer had to follow the food. The food would stay where you put it. And if the food stayed, you could stay. And if you could stay, you could build something that did not have to be carried.

Everything we call civilisation — cities, writing, law, kings, temples, armies, taxes, art — unrolls from that one observation about a seed.

The odd thing about where it happened

The first great civilisations appeared in four places: beside the Tigris and Euphrates in what is now Iraq, beside the Nile in Egypt, beside the Indus in Pakistan, and beside the Yellow River in China. Four corners of the world. Four groups of people who had never heard of each other. Every single one of them beside a great river.

That cannot be a coincidence. And it isn't.

Chapter 02

The answer is mud

Every year those rivers flooded. The water spread out across the flat land on either side, sat there for a while, and then drained away — and when it went, it left behind a layer of fine dark silt.

That silt was the richest soil on Earth, and it arrived free, every year, without anybody doing anything. A farmer on a river floodplain could plant, harvest, and plant again, and the land would renew itself. A farmer on ordinary ground would exhaust his soil in a few seasons and have to move on.

So the rivers gave the first farmers something no one had ever had before: more food than they needed. A surplus.

It is difficult to overstate what that extra food did, because it answered a question nobody had been able to ask before. If ten families can grow enough for fifteen, what do the other five do?

They do something else. And "something else" is the beginning of everything. One becomes a potter, because someone must store the grain. One becomes a soldier, because stored grain is worth stealing. One becomes a priest, because the flood is unpredictable and terrifying and somebody must explain it. One becomes a scribe, because you cannot remember who contributed what to a granary holding the produce of four hundred families. And one becomes a king, because all of this now needs deciding.

The thread to hold on to

Specialisation is what a surplus buys you. Every complicated thing humans have ever built — a cathedral, a legal code, a symphony, a vaccine — exists because at some point enough food was grown by few enough people that somebody else was free to spend their life on something that was not food.

Chapter 03

Writing began as an accountant's problem

We like to imagine writing beginning with poetry or prayer. It did not. It began with receipts.

In the temple granaries of Mesopotamia, around five thousand years ago, someone had the job of remembering that Enlil-bani delivered nine measures of barley and is owed for them, and that the brewer took four, and that two were lost to rats. When it was forty families you could hold it in your head. When it was four hundred you could not.

So they started pressing marks into wet clay with a cut reed — a wedge here for a measure of grain, a wedge there for the man who brought it. The marks were pictures at first. Then the pictures got simpler and faster, because scribes were in a hurry. Then — and this is the leap — somebody realised the mark could stand not for the thing but for the sound of the word for the thing. Once a mark stands for a sound, you can write anything you can say. You can write a law. You can write a letter to your brother. You can write down a story that people had only ever told aloud.

Clay, it turns out, is a wonderful thing to write on and an almost indestructible thing to have written on. Burn the building down and the tablets bake harder. That is why we can still read the complaints of a Babylonian customer about the poor quality of copper he was sold — a genuine surviving document, and one of the oldest pieces of writing we have that is not a receipt. It is a complaint letter. Nothing about people has changed.

Chapter 04

Egypt, and the strange gift of predictability

Egypt had the same river logic as Mesopotamia, but with one difference that shaped everything about its character: the Nile was reliable.

The Tigris and Euphrates flooded violently and unpredictably; they could arrive early and drown your crop or arrive late and starve it. The Nile rose at roughly the same time every year and to roughly the same height. You could plan around it. You could build a whole calendar around it — and the Egyptians did, dividing the year into the season of the flood, the season of growing and the season of harvest.

Predictability produces a different kind of society from anxiety. Mesopotamian religion is full of capricious, dangerous gods who might destroy you on a whim, because that is what their river was like. Egyptian religion is orderly, cyclical and deeply concerned with things continuing exactly as they are — because that is what their river was like. The land taught them what to expect from the universe.

And because the flood was dependable, Egypt could do something remarkable: it could take the months when the fields were underwater and nobody could farm, and put the entire population to work on something else. That is where the pyramids come from. Not slaves under the whip, as the old films have it, but a seasonal workforce of ordinary farmers — fed, housed, organised into gangs with names like "Friends of Khufu" — doing the state's work during the months when the water made farming impossible.

Part II

The Greek experiment

Chapter 05

A small, quarrelsome people

Around two and a half thousand years ago, on a scatter of rocky peninsulas and islands in the eastern Mediterranean, a fairly small number of people produced an explosion of new ideas — about how to be governed, how to think, how to study the world, how to build, how to put a story on a stage — that the West has been living off ever since.

People sometimes call this the Greek miracle. It was not a miracle, and the reason it happened tells you something useful about where new ideas come from.

The first thing to understand is that Greece was not a country. There was no king of Greece, no capital, no government. There were dozens of separate city-states, each an independent little country with its own army, its own coins, its own laws and its own personality. Athens was the city of argument and art. Sparta was a grim military machine that took boys from their mothers at seven and raised them to be soldiers. Corinth was rich and commercial. They all spoke Greek and worshipped the same gods and considered themselves one people — and they fought each other constantly.

And that division was the secret. Because no one ruler controlled them all, no one could decide what everybody was allowed to think. Ideas had to compete. Different cities could try different arrangements and everyone could see which ones worked. A philosopher unwelcome in one city could walk to the next one. The Greeks experimented endlessly, not because they were wiser than everyone else, but because their geography made a single authority impossible.

Chapter 06

The invention of arguing in public

In Athens, they tried something genuinely strange. Instead of a king deciding, the citizens would decide — by turning up, listening to the arguments, and voting.

We should be honest about the limits. "Citizens" meant adult free men whose parents were Athenian: perhaps one adult in five. Women had no vote. The city ran on slave labour. By our standards it was not a democracy at all.

But something happened in that assembly that had not happened before, and it mattered more than the franchise. Decisions had to be argued for in public, in front of people who could vote against you. That single arrangement invented an entire skill set — how to construct a case, how to anticipate an objection, how to spot when someone is manipulating you — and it is why Athens produced the first people to study persuasion, logic and evidence as subjects in their own right.

It also, eventually, killed Socrates. The most famous Athenian thinker spent his life going up to confident people in the marketplace and asking them questions until it became obvious that they did not know what they were talking about. This is an extremely valuable public service and an extremely annoying personal habit, and in the end the assembly voted that he had corrupted the young and he was required to drink poison. He could have escaped; friends arranged it. He refused, on the grounds that a man who had spent his life arguing that citizens must obey the law could not run away from the law the moment it inconvenienced him.

Three men, and why we still have their names

Socrates asked the questions and wrote nothing down. Plato was his student, wrote the conversations down, and argued that the world we see is a rough copy of something more perfect behind it. Aristotle was Plato's student, disagreed, and insisted that if you want to know how the world works you should go and look at it — collect the animals, dissect them, classify them, count. He was wrong about a great many specific things and right about the method, and the method is what survived.

Aristotle then tutored a teenage prince in the north, which is where the next chapter starts.

Chapter 07

Alexander, and ideas that travelled

The prince was Alexander of Macedon, and by the time he was thirty he had conquered from Greece to the edge of India — the largest empire the world had yet seen, assembled in about a decade by a man who never lost a battle.

Then he died at thirty-two, probably of fever, possibly of drink, and his generals immediately tore the empire apart fighting each other over it. As an empire it lasted no time at all.

But the conquest had done something that outlived the conquest. Alexander founded cities everywhere he went — many of them called Alexandria — and settled Greeks in them. Greek became the language you used to do business from Egypt to Afghanistan. Greek ideas, Greek architecture, Greek ways of arguing spread across the whole eastern Mediterranean and stayed there for centuries after the empire was dust.

The greatest of those cities, Alexandria in Egypt, built a library and tried to collect every book in the world. Ships docking in the harbour had their scrolls confiscated and copied. It is the first time anyone had attempted to gather all human knowledge in one building, and the idea has never really gone away — every library and every search engine since is a descendant of it.

Part III

Rome

Chapter 08

A village that would not stop

Rome began as an unremarkable settlement on a river in central Italy, surrounded by neighbours who were richer, older and more sophisticated. There was no reason to expect anything of it.

What Rome had was not brilliance. It was two habits.

The first was that it did not know how to stop. Rome lost battles constantly — catastrophically, repeatedly, against Hannibal in particular, who spent fifteen years marching up and down Italy destroying every army sent against him. Any sensible state would have negotiated. Rome raised another army. And another. It simply refused to accept that it had lost, and eventually the other side ran out of men first.

The second was stranger and more important: Rome absorbed the people it conquered instead of merely ruling them. Defeated enemies were offered a path to becoming Roman — first allies, then partial citizens, eventually full citizens with the vote. A Spaniard or a Gaul or a Syrian could end up a Roman senator; several emperors were born in the provinces. No previous empire had done this. It meant that as Rome grew, its manpower grew with it, while its enemies' manpower shrank. Every war made it stronger.

Chapter 09

The day the Republic died

For roughly five hundred years Rome had no king. It was a republic, governed by elected officials and a senate, with an almost religious horror of one-man rule — they had thrown out their last king and wanted no more.

The thing that killed it was success. An empire that size could not be administered by officials elected for a single year. Generals commanded armies for years in distant provinces, and those armies came to be loyal to the general who paid and fed them rather than to a senate they had never seen. Once that is true, the republic is already over; it just does not know it yet.

Julius Caesar was the man who demonstrated it. Brilliant, charming, spectacularly in debt, and enormously popular with his soldiers and the poor, he conquered Gaul, was ordered by the senate to disband his army and come home, and instead marched on Rome with it. He won the civil war that followed and had himself made dictator for life.

On the fifteenth of March, a group of senators — including Brutus, whom he had pardoned and promoted — surrounded him in a meeting and stabbed him to death. They believed they were saving the republic.

What they had actually done was start another civil war, which was won by Caesar's adopted son Octavian, who learned the lesson of his father's murder precisely: he took absolute power but never called himself king. He called himself princeps, "first citizen", and kept the senate meeting, and let everyone pretend the republic continued. He is known to history as Augustus, and he was the first emperor.

The lesson the assassins missed

They killed the man and assumed the problem was the man. The problem was the arrangement — armies loyal to generals, an empire too large for annual officials, and a population that no longer particularly cared who governed as long as the grain arrived. Remove Caesar and the arrangement produces another Caesar. It produced one within fifteen years.

Chapter 10

The morning the mountain came down

On an August morning in the year 79, the people of Pompeii — a comfortable seaside town of perhaps twelve thousand, full of bakeries and bars and election graffiti — noticed that the mountain behind them was making a noise.

Vesuvius had been quiet for so long that nobody living thought of it as a volcano at all. There were vineyards on its slopes. When the column of ash went up, miles into the sky, people came out into the streets to look at it.

Then the ash began to fall, and kept falling, for hours, until roofs collapsed under the weight of it. Many people sheltered indoors and were buried. Others fled to the shore and waited for boats. Late that night and into the next morning, the column collapsed and came down the mountain instead — a surge of gas and ash moving faster than a car and hot enough to kill instantly. It reached the town before anyone could react.

And then the strangest part. The ash sealed Pompeii and kept it, exactly as it was, for sixteen hundred years. Not a ruin — a town. Loaves still in the oven. A dog still on its chain. Graffiti on the walls advertising a gladiator and insulting a neighbour and recording that someone called Gaius had been here. Election posters for a candidate who would never learn the result.

The bodies decayed inside the hardened ash and left cavities in the shape of the people who had lain there. Centuries later an archaeologist thought to pour plaster into those cavities, and the result is the most unbearable thing in archaeology: not skeletons, but people — a man holding his face, a dog straining at its collar, a family together.

We know more about how ordinary Romans actually lived from this single catastrophe than from all the surviving literature put together. The disaster that destroyed the town is the only reason we have it.

Chapter 11

How an empire ends

People expect the fall of Rome to be a date and a battle. It is neither. It is a slow accumulation of pressures over roughly two hundred years, and most people alive at the time would not have described themselves as living through a fall at all.

The empire got too large to defend — every frontier needed an army, and armies are ruinously expensive. Paying them required taxes that the provinces increasingly resented. Emperors were made and unmade by their own soldiers, sometimes several in a year. The currency was debased until it was worthless. Plague came through more than once. And beyond the frontier, whole peoples were on the move — themselves fleeing other peoples further east — and they came into the empire in numbers that could not be absorbed in the old way.

The city of Rome was sacked in 410 and again in 455, and in 476 a Germanic commander deposed the last emperor in the west and did not bother to replace him. That is the conventional date. It passed without much comment, because by then the office had been meaningless for a generation.

And the eastern half did not fall at all. It carried on, governed from Constantinople, speaking Greek, calling itself Roman, for another thousand years — a fact that surprises almost everyone, and one we will pick up in the next volume, because its ending is one of the great hinges of history.

Reference

What to carry forward

The one idea

A surplus of food buys specialisation, and specialisation is civilisation. Once some people are free from growing food, you get priests, soldiers, scribes, potters and kings — and everything that follows from them.

Why rivers

Annual floods left free fertiliser, so the same land could be farmed indefinitely. Four civilisations began this way independently. Egypt's flood was predictable, which gave it a calendar, a settled religion and a spare workforce every year — which is where the pyramids come from.

Why the Greeks

Not genius — competition. Dozens of small rival states meant no single authority could decide what people were allowed to think, and public decision-making meant arguments had to be made in front of people who could vote you down.

Why Rome

It refused to accept defeat, and it turned the conquered into Romans rather than merely ruling them — so every war made it larger and its enemies smaller. It fell not in a day but over two centuries of frontiers, money and plague. The eastern half carried on for another thousand years.
