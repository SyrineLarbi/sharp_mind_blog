Sharp Mind 06 · substance

Having something to say. Being interesting is a supply problem, not a personality problem.

You have been in the conversation where everyone else has something and you have nothing. The standard explanation is that you are not naturally interesting, which is both false and useless. The real explanation is boring and fixable: the people who always have something to say have stocked the shelf, usually on purpose, and you can see exactly how if you watch them for a week. This handbook is how to stock yours.

The same thesis as file 01, pointed somewhere else

File 01 argued that speed in conversation is retrieval rather than processing — the quick person is not computing faster, they are pulling something off a shelf you have not stocked.

This handbook is that argument applied to content instead of to answers. The person who always has something interesting to say is not more curious than you in the moment. They read something three weeks ago, told someone about it once, and now it is retrievable. That is the entire mechanism, and it is the good news.

Part I · The supply problem

Stock, not talent

Three sections on what you are actually short of, what a full shelf looks like, and which kinds of material are worth putting on it.

Something to Say 01

It is supply, not personality

Two people stand in the same group at the same event. One contributes four times, the other twice. The difference is almost never willingness, and almost never intelligence. It is whether anything is loaded.

And the most important distinction in this handbook is the one people never draw:

INPUT IS NOT STOCK INPUT STOCK ───── ───── an article you read a thing you have SAID a video you watched OUT LOUD at least once a podcast in the car forty headlines scrolled ⇒ retrievable under social pressure ⇒ produces the FEELING of ⇒ has a shape: hook, being informed middle, handoff ⇒ produces almost nothing ⇒ survives being retrievable when someone interrupted is looking at you ⭐ NOTHING BECOMES STOCK UNTIL YOU HAVE SAID IT OUT LOUD. Not written down. Said. The first telling is where a pile of facts turns into a thing with an order, and the second telling is where it gets good. ⇒ WHICH MEANS THE FIX IS NOT "READ MORE". Most people who feel they have nothing to say are consuming plenty. They are converting none of it.

This also explains a common and demoralising experience: reading a great deal and still feeling empty in conversation. You are not failing to learn. You are failing to convert, and conversion is a separate ten-second step that nobody tells you to do.

The conversion step, in full

You read something interesting. Before you close the tab, say it out loud — to a person, to the room, to yourself in the car — in about forty seconds, in this shape: here's what everyone assumes · here's the surprising bit · here's why it matters.

That is it. Ten seconds of effort converts an input into stock. Skip it and the article is gone within a week, which you already know from experience.

Something to Say 02

The repertoire — twenty topics

A working repertoire is about twenty topics you can open, hold for four or five minutes, and hand back. Not twenty subjects you are expert in. Twenty things you can start and pass on.

The shape of a full shelf
5 · FROM YOUR OWN DOMAIN
    Things you know because of what you do, that a
    normal person does not know and finds genuinely
    interesting. ⭐ THE MOST UNDERVALUED CATEGORY BY FAR.
    You are bored of it; nobody else has ever heard it.
    e.g. why a delivery is late in a way that has
    nothing to do with the driver; what actually happens
    to a returned item; why shops run out of one size.

5 · CURRENT
    Things in the news this quarter that you understand
    one level deeper than the headline. Not opinions —
    MECHANISMS. ⇒ this is what files 11–16 are for.

5 · EVERGREEN CURIOSITIES
    How something works; a historical episode; a
    surprising number. These never go stale, which is
    why they are the backbone of the shelf.
    ⇒ this is what ../culture/ is for (§08).

3 · PERSONAL
    Something you are doing, learning, failed at, or
    changed your mind about. Costs nothing to maintain
    and does more warmth work (file 04 §01) than any
    other category.

2 · RELIABLE QUESTIONS
    Topics you open by ASKING rather than telling.
    The safety net for when you have nothing: §06.

  ⇒ TWENTY. And you will use about six of them ninety
    percent of the time.

What one topic actually looks like

A topic card · three lines, no more
TOPIC · Desalination

  HOOK    "Most of the Gulf drinks the sea. Israel gets
           the majority of its household water that way
           too — it's not exotic any more, it's plumbing."

  MIDDLE  "The bit I didn't expect: the hard part isn't
           removing the salt. It's the energy and the
           leftover brine. You push seawater against a
           membrane at enormous pressure, about half
           comes through fresh — and the other half
           leaves twice as salty as the sea, and has to
           go somewhere."

  HANDOFF "Which makes it basically a power-price
           question. Do you know if anywhere is doing
           this at scale without cheap energy?"

⭐ THREE LINES. ABOUT FORTY SECONDS.
  HOOK      one sentence that creates a question
  MIDDLE    a mechanism, a number, or a turn — the part
            that is not obvious
  HANDOFF   a question that puts it back in the room

⚠ THE HANDOFF IS NOT OPTIONAL. A topic without one is a
  monologue, and a monologue is the single most common
  way an interesting person becomes tiring. It is also
  what makes a card REUSABLE — it ends in a place where
  someone else speaks.
Maintenance

Add two a month, retire two a month. The current five go stale fastest — a topic more than a quarter old has usually stopped being current and has not yet become evergreen, which is the worst of both. The domain five and the evergreen five barely need touching.

Keep the cards in the same file as your answer bank (file 01, §11). Same habit, same file, different section.

Something to Say 03

What travels, and what kills

Travels — works at almost any tableKills — costs you the room
How something works. Mechanisms are universally interesting and require no prior knowledge from the listener. The monologue. Anything over ninety seconds without a handoff. The most common failure, and the speaker is always the last to notice.
Origin stories. Why a thing is the way it is. Why keyboards are QWERTY, why shipping containers are that size. Correcting people. Being right about a small detail someone got slightly wrong buys you nothing and costs real warmth.
A surprising exact number. One number, scaled to something human, beats five adjectives. Topics needing an opinion they don't have. If they have no way in, you have made them a spectator.
Things you changed your mind about. Rare, warm, and it makes everything else you say more credible (file 04, §06). Grievances. Your commute, your colleague, your landlord. Fine for two sentences with a friend; corrosive anywhere else.
What you are bad at. Almost impossible to overuse, and it does more for how you are received than any display of competence. One-upping. Following someone's story with a better version of it. Reads as competition even when it was enthusiasm.
Local and specific knowledge. The thing you know because of where you are or what you do. See §02. Anything that tests them. "Have you read—?" "You know about X, right?" Makes people defensive in one sentence.
Other people's expertise. Asking about it is a topic in itself, and the strongest one you have (§06). Health detail, money detail, and politics with the wrong room. Not never — but know the room, and never lead with them.
Two rules that do most of the filtering
① THE WAY-IN TEST — the single best filter
   "Does the other person have a way into this?"

   Can they contribute something — a question, an
   experience, a disagreement — without having read
   what you read?

   ✗ "Have you seen the new export figures?"
     → they either have or they haven't. Dead end either
       way, and slightly embarrassing if they haven't.
   ✓ "Do you know why so much stuff still moves by sea
      even when it's urgent? I always assumed it was
      just cheaper."
     → anyone can engage with that. It offers them a
       guess, which is the friendliest possible opening.

② THE ASYMMETRY RULE
   Talk about what you find INTERESTING, not about what
   you think is IMPRESSIVE.

   Genuine interest is contagious and unfakeable, and it
   licenses the other person to be enthusiastic too.
   Impressiveness invites evaluation, and people who feel
   evaluated go quiet.

   ⇒ THE TELL: if you are monitoring how it is landing
     while you say it, you picked the impressive one.

Something to Say 04

Turning a fact into a story

A fact stated is forgettable. The same fact in a three-beat shape is repeatable — and repeatable is the actual standard, because the compliment you are aiming at is someone telling your thing to someone else next week.

The three beats
① SETUP    — what everyone assumes
   Say the ordinary belief out loud. This is the step
   people skip, and skipping it is why their interesting
   fact lands flat: WITHOUT THE ASSUMPTION STATED, THERE
   IS NOTHING FOR THE TURN TO OVERTURN.

② TURN     — the thing that is not obvious
   The surprise. One beat. Do not bury it in a clause.

③ SO-WHAT  — why it matters, or what it means for us
   Connects it to something the listener already cares
   about. Then hand off.

WORKED — the flat version and the shaped version

  ✗ FLAT
    "Desalination uses about 3 to 4 kilowatt-hours per
     cubic metre."
    → true, inert, and impossible to respond to

  ✓ SHAPED
    ① "You'd think the hard part of making seawater
       drinkable is getting the salt out."
    ② "It isn't, really — that's a membrane and a very
       big pump. The hard parts are the electricity bill
       and the brine: you get fresh water out of about
       half of it, and the other half comes back out
       twice as salty as the sea."
    ③ "Which is why it's really an energy story. Every
       country that does this at scale is one that has
       cheap power or no choice."
    → and then the handoff.

⭐ THE NUMBER THAT LANDS — one, exact, scaled to a human
  ✗ "it uses a lot of energy"                — nothing
  ✗ "3.4 kWh per cubic metre"                — nobody
                                                feels this
  ✓ "about a tenth of what it took in the 1970s"
  ✓ "roughly what your kettle uses in three hours,
     for a tonne of drinking water"
  ⇒ ONE number, and give it something to lean on.

⚠ AND THE DISCIPLINE THAT MATTERS MOST: LENGTH.
  Forty seconds. The single most common way a genuinely
  interesting thing fails is that it ran for four
  minutes. If it needs longer, it is a conversation, not
  a card — so hand off at forty seconds and let them
  pull you further.
Something to Say 05

Entering a topic you only half know

Most of what you will want to talk about, you half know. That is normal and it is fine. The only thing that is not fine is pretending otherwise, and there is a clean way to do this that actually gains you standing.

State your model and invite correction
⭐ THE MOVE
  "My mental model of this is [the model]. Is that
   roughly right, or am I missing something?"

  WHY IT WORKS SO WELL
    · people LOVE correcting a well-formed model and
      find it tedious to correct a vague gesture. You
      are giving them something good to work with.
    · a wrong model stated clearly gets fixed in one
      sentence. A vague one never gets fixed at all.
    · it is the fastest learning mechanism available in
      any room, and it costs you nothing — because you
      already flagged it as a model rather than a fact.

WORKED
  "My model of desalination is: big pump, fine membrane,
   half comes through fresh, and the whole economics is
   really the power price. Is that about right?"
  → an expert will now tell you three things you did not
    know, warmly, because you have made it easy and
    signalled that you respect what they know.

THE HONEST SCALE — say which one you are on
  "I only know the outline of this —"
  "As I understand it, though I'm second-hand on this —"
  "I read one thing about this, so treat it accordingly —"

  ⚠ THIS IS BOUNDING, NOT HEDGING. File 04 §04 tells you
    to cut apologetic hedges; marking the edge of what
    you know is the opposite of that — it is precision,
    and it makes everything inside the boundary more
    credible, not less.

⚠ NEVER BLUFF. The cost is retroactive: the moment
  someone finds one confident thing you invented, every
  other confident thing you have said comes under
  review. Nothing in this handbook is worth that.

⭐ THE ONE-LEVEL RULE
  Know ONE level deeper than the conversation needs.
  Not three. One level down means you can answer the
  first follow-up, which is all that is ever asked.
  Three levels down means you will over-explain, which
  is §04's failure mode.
Something to Say 06

Making other people interesting

The fastest route to being thought interesting is not to be interesting. It is to be interested, specifically and persistently, in a way almost nobody bothers with.

This is the social application of the second-question discipline from file 02, §08: the first answer is the rehearsed one, and the second question is what gets you the real one — and gets you remembered.

The questions that work on anyone
⭐ THE UNIVERSAL ONE
  "What's the part of your job that people always get
   wrong?"

  Works on a surgeon, a plumber, a teacher, an auditor.
  Everyone has an answer, everyone enjoys giving it, and
  the answer is ALWAYS interesting because it is the gap
  between the public story and the real one.
  ⇒ if you remember one sentence from this handbook,
    this is a strong candidate.

THE OTHERS, IN ROUGH ORDER OF RELIABILITY
  "What got you into it?"
     origin story — warm, easy, always available
  "What's changed about it in the last five years?"
     invites expertise without demanding a thesis
  "What's the hardest part that isn't obvious?"
     goes straight past the rehearsed answer
  "What would you be doing if you weren't doing that?"
     good late in a conversation, never as an opener
  "Who's good at this that you learn from?"
     generous, and you often get a real recommendation

THEN THE SECOND QUESTION — where the value actually is
  Q1 "What got you into architecture?"
  A1 "I sort of fell into it after uni."     ← rehearsed
  Q2 "What made you stay?"
  A2 [the real answer, and they are now enjoying this]

⭐ AIRTIME
  In a good conversation you should be talking somewhere
  under half the time, and in a conversation with someone
  who knows something you do not, well under.
  ⚠ people remember how a conversation FELT, and it felt
    good if they got to be interesting. Being the most
    informative person present is a worse outcome than
    being the person they enjoyed talking to.

⭐ THE RECALL MOVE — the highest-return thing in this file
  Write down one thing afterwards. Ask about it next time.
  "Did the extension ever get approved?"
  Almost nobody does this. It is remembered for years.

Where the material comes from

The supply chain. One subject deep, many shallow, a route into the library next door, and a way of following the world that does not consist of scrolling.

Something to Say 07

Depth and breadth

THE SHAPE YOU ARE AIMING FOR ──────────────────────────────────────────────── BREADTH — enough to ask a good question about almost anything. Not enough to hold forth. ──────────────────────────────────────────────── │ │ DEPTH — one subject you │ know a level deeper than │ an interested amateur │ ▼ WHY YOU NEED BOTH, AND WHAT EACH ONE BUYS BREADTH buys ENTRY. It is what lets you join any conversation rather than waiting for your subject to come up. The target is low: enough to ask one good question and follow the answer. DEPTH buys CREDIBILITY, and it is also what you TRADE. Conversations run on exchange. If you only ever receive, you are an audience. One subject you genuinely know makes you a participant everywhere, because every field has an analogue of every other field's problems. ⚠ THE COMMON MISTAKE IS CHASING BREADTH ONLY. It produces someone who has heard of everything and can say nothing about anything, which is a worse conversational position than knowing one thing well.

Your depth subject is usually your work — and if it is, the five domain topics from §02 are already sitting there unused. If it is not your work, it can be anything with real internal structure: a sport, a period of history, cooking, a city. The only requirement is that you know it a level past the interested amateur, because that is exactly where the interesting material lives.

Something to Say 08

Mining the culture library

You already own 247 chapters of general knowledge in culture/, written as narrative with an In a Nutshell summary at the end of each one. It is the single best source you have for the evergreen five, and it is currently sitting there unconverted.

Best for conversationWhy
20 · Great inventions and discoveriesThe richest seam by a distance. Origin stories, accidental discoveries, materials that changed history — all three-beat shaped already.
01 · History — the disasters and turning pointsTitanic, Chernobyl, the Great Fire, Bhopal, Challenger. Everyone half-knows these, which means your one extra detail lands perfectly.
16 · Daily lifeHow things work, kitchen science, statistics in the news. Maximum way-in: everybody has the referent already.
08 · TechnologyHow the internet, phones, chips and AI models actually work. Universally relevant, and almost nobody knows the mechanism.
06 · Astronomy and spaceReliably delightful, zero prerequisites, and nobody has ever objected to a fact about the sun.
05 · Medicine and healthHow medicines work, imaging, vaccines. High interest — just keep it mechanical, not personal.
Which parts are for you rather than for the table

Philosophy, mathematics and psychology are excellent reading and much harder to place in conversation — philosophy because it invites disagreement rather than curiosity, mathematics because the way-in test usually fails, psychology because it slides toward diagnosing the people present. Read them for yourself. Convert selectively.

The conversion routine · one chapter, ten minutes
① PICK a chapter from the list above. Read it properly
   once — they are narrative, not reference.

② TAKE the "In a Nutshell" at the end. That is your
   raw material, already compressed.

③ WRITE ONE CARD — hook, middle, handoff (§02).
   ⚠ ONE. Not four. A chapter with four cards in it
     will produce zero, because you will not remember
     which was which.

④ SAY IT OUT LOUD ONCE. To anyone, or to nobody.
   ⇒ THIS IS THE STEP THAT CREATES THE STOCK (§01).
     Skipping it means you read a chapter, which is
     pleasant and produces nothing.

⭐ ONE CHAPTER A WEEK = 50 CARDS A YEAR.
  You need twenty. So you can be selective, and you can
  retire freely.

⇒ AND FOR THE *CURRENT* FIVE, THE SOURCE IS THIS LIBRARY:
  FILES 11 TO 16 are the technology and process briefings
  — desalination, semiconductors, mRNA, batteries,
  shipping chokepoints, LLMs and the rest — each one
  written with how it works, who controls it, why it is
  in the news, and THE LINE TO SAY, which is a finished
  card you do not have to write yourself.
Something to Say 09

Following the world without drowning in it

Most people confuse being current with being informed. They are different, they age differently, and only one of them gives you anything to say.

TWO WAYS TO FOLLOW THE WORLD HEADLINES MECHANISMS ───────── ────────── what happened today how the thing works actors without a system a system that explains many days of headlines ⇒ stale in a week ⇒ useful for years ⇒ produces OPINIONS you ⇒ produces UNDERSTANDING cannot defend you can extend ⇒ high volume, no stock ⇒ low volume, high stock ⭐ ONE MECHANISM IS WORTH ABOUT A HUNDRED HEADLINES. Understand how LNG shipping works and you can read three years of energy news without being told what to think about any of it. ⚠ AND SCROLLING IS THE WORST CASE OF ALL: it delivers the FEELING of being informed with none of the retrievable structure. You will have absorbed forty fragments and be unable to say one sentence about any of them an hour later.
A weekly rhythm that actually produces stock
ONE LONG READ        one properly reported piece, start
  (30–40 min)        to finish, on anything
ONE MECHANISM        one "how does this actually work"
  (20 min)           explainer — files 11–16, or culture/
ONE CARD             convert ONE of the two into a card
  (10 min)           and say it out loud (§01)

  ⇒ ABOUT AN HOUR A WEEK. That is the whole system, and
    it beats an hour a day of scrolling by a wide margin.

THREE SOURCES, NOT THIRTY
  ① one that reports rather than reacts
  ② one specialist source in your own field
  ③ ⭐ one that disagrees with you, read charitably
     Not for balance as a virtue — for the practical
     reason that you cannot defend a position you have
     only ever heard defended.

THE SIX-MONTH TEST — apply it before you spend attention
  "Will this matter in six months?"
  Most headlines fail. Most mechanisms pass. This one
  question cuts reading volume enormously with almost
  no loss.

THE QUESTIONS FILE — the habit that compounds
  Keep a running list titled "things I don't understand".
  Every time you nod along to a word you could not
  actually define — basis points, feedstock, tokenisation,
  capacity factor, FOB — write it down. Do not look it
  up in the moment.
  Then work through two a week.
  ⇒ within a year this list, not the news, is where most
    of your genuinely interesting material comes from.
  ⇒ and it converts a small daily embarrassment into a
    queue, which is a much better place for it.
The permission you probably need

You are allowed to not follow most things. A person who understands eight systems properly is far better company, and far better informed, than one who has skimmed four hundred stories. Depth in a few places plus honest ignorance elsewhere is a completely respectable position — and "I don't know anything about that, what's the shape of it?" is one of the better sentences available to you (file 04, §05).

Something to Say 10

The drills

1 · The conversion · whenever you read something ⭐

Before closing the tab: say it out loud in forty seconds — assumption, turn, so-what. Ten seconds of work. This single habit is the difference between reading a lot and having something to say, and no other drill here matters if you skip it.

2 · Build the twenty · this month

Write the cards. Five domain, five current, five evergreen, three personal, two questions. Three lines each. It takes about two hours total and you will draw on it for years.

3 · One chapter a week

One culture/ chapter, one card, said out loud once. Fifty cards a year from a library you already own.

4 · The universal question · weekly

Ask someone "what's the part of your job people always get wrong?" and then ask the second question. Watch what happens. It has an unusually high hit rate.

5 · The questions file · daily

Write down every word you nodded at without being able to define. Look up two a week. Within a year this is your best source of material.

6 · The recall · after any real conversation

One line about what they told you. Ask about it next time. Costs thirty seconds, remembered for years, and almost nobody does it.

The minimum viable version

Drill 1 and drill 4. The conversion habit fills your shelf; the universal question means you are never dependent on the shelf being full. Between them you will not be the person standing in the group with nothing to say again.

That completes the six skills: diagnose, ask, structure, deliver, read the room, and have something worth saying. What remains is fuel and practice. Files 07, 08 and 09 are thirty-two books stripped to what is usable in them, so you can stop wondering which ones to read. File 10 is the eight-week programme that turns all of this from things you know into things you do. And files 11 to 16 are the technology and process briefings — how desalination, chips, mRNA, batteries and the rest actually work, who controls them and why they are in the news — which is where your current five come from, already written as cards.

On sourcing. This handbook is craft rather than research — there is no experimental literature on how to be good company, and anyone claiming otherwise is overselling. What is here is drawn from the parts of the conversation and rhetoric literature that survive contact with practice, reorganised around one testable claim: that the binding constraint is usually stock rather than ability. Test that claim on yourself for a month. The adjacent researched material — social anxiety, the mechanics of small talk, active listening — is in master-yourself/phase-11, file 02 and is not restated here.
