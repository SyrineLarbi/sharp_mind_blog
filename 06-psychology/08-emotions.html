Emotions. Not the opposite of reason, but the system that tells reason what matters.

An old idea, still common, says emotions are noise that gets in the way of clear thinking. Psychology has mostly turned that idea around. Emotions are fast judgements about what a situation means for you, and they prepare your body and mind to deal with it. They can be wrong, badly timed or too strong. But a person without them does not become a perfect thinker. They struggle to decide anything at all. This file is about what emotions are, how they arise, what they are for, and how people manage them.

Question 1 · What is it?

A whole-body response to what something means for you

An emotion is not just a feeling. It is a package: a judgement, a change in the body, a facial expression, an urge to act, and the feeling that sits on top of it all.

Chapter 01

Darwin's other book

In 1872, thirteen years after On the Origin of Species, Charles Darwin published The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals. He collected photographs of faces, asked missionaries and travellers around the world how local people showed grief or surprise, and compared human expressions with those of dogs, cats and apes. A snarling dog bares its teeth; an angry human often does something similar with the upper lip.

Darwin's argument was that emotional expressions evolved, just as bodies did. They had once served a practical purpose, and many had become signals that others of the same species could read. This was a radical claim at the time. It treated emotions not as a mysterious spiritual matter, but as part of biology, open to study. Most of the modern science of emotion starts from that move.

Chapter 02

Emotion, feeling and mood are not the same

Everyday language uses these words loosely. Researchers separate them.

Many researchers describe an emotion as having several components: an appraisal (your quick judgement of what the event means), bodily changes (heart rate, breathing, muscle tension), an expression (face, voice, posture), an action tendency (an urge to flee, fight, approach or hide), and the feeling itself. They usually arrive together, but not always. You can feel afraid while your face stays calm, or have a racing heart without knowing why.

Chapter 03

What emotions are for

Each common emotion seems to answer a recurring problem in human life. Fear prepares you to escape a threat. Anger prepares you to confront someone who has blocked you or treated you unfairly. Disgust keeps you away from things that could make you ill, such as rotten food, and by extension from people and acts seen as morally "rotten". Sadness slows you down after a loss and signals to others that you need support. Joy rewards and encourages what is going well.

Emotions also do social work. Embarrassment, shame and guilt keep people in line with the group's expectations and push them to repair damage they caused. Expressions let others predict what you will do. An emotion is information for you and a signal to everyone else. It answers the question "what does this mean for me, and what should I do about it?" faster than conscious reasoning can.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A century-long argument about what comes first

Do you tremble because you are afraid, or feel afraid because you tremble? The answers to that question built the main theories of emotion.

Chapter 04

The body first: James and Lange

Common sense says you see a bear, feel afraid, and then run. In the 1880s the American psychologist William James, and independently the Danish physician Carl Lange, argued for the opposite order. You see the bear, your body reacts (your heart pounds, you start to run), and the feeling of fear is your awareness of those bodily changes. In James's famous phrasing, we feel sorry because we cry, not the other way round.

In 1927 the physiologist Walter Cannon raised strong objections, later developed with Philip Bard. Very different emotions, such as fear and anger, produce similar bodily changes, so the body alone cannot tell them apart. And bodily changes are often too slow to explain how quickly emotions appear. The Cannon–Bard theory held that the brain produces the feeling and the bodily reaction at the same time.

Neither side won outright. Modern research suggests the body does shape the feeling, but it is not the whole story. Signals from the heart, gut and lungs feed into how emotional you feel, a sense called interoception, which Neuroscience · Emotions and the brain explains.

Chapter 05

Arousal plus a label

In 1962 Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer proposed a two-factor theory. An emotion needs two things: general physical arousal, and an explanation of that arousal. A pounding heart could be fear, excitement, anger or love. Which one you feel depends on how you interpret the situation.

A famous 1974 study by Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron tested a version of this on two bridges in Canada. An attractive female researcher approached men either on a high, swaying suspension bridge or on a low, solid one, and gave them her phone number "in case they had questions". More men from the scary bridge called her. One interpretation is that some men misattributed their racing hearts from the bridge to attraction. The study is still widely taught, but it was small, and later research on misattribution has given mixed results, so it is best seen as an illustration rather than proof.

The practical lesson holds up better. Before a big presentation, people who tell themselves "I am excited" instead of trying to calm down have, in some studies, performed better. The same arousal can be read as threat or as readiness, and the reading changes how it feels.

Chapter 06

Appraisal: the meaning decides the emotion

From the 1960s, the psychologists Magda Arnold and Richard Lazarus put the judgement at the centre. An appraisal is your often instant, often unconscious assessment of what an event means for your goals and wellbeing. Is this good or bad for me? Who caused it? Can I cope with it?

Imagine two colleagues who are both passed over for promotion. One thinks, "My boss treated me unfairly." She feels angry. The other thinks, "I wasn't good enough." He feels ashamed. A third thinks, "I didn't really want that job." She feels relief. The event was the same. The appraisal was different, so the emotion was different.

Appraisal theory explains why people react so differently to the same thing, and why the same person reacts differently on different days. It is also the basis of many effective psychological therapies. If emotions follow appraisals, then changing the appraisal can change the emotion, as Chapter 09 explains.

Chapter 07

The great debate: basic or constructed?

The liveliest current argument in emotion science is about whether there is a fixed set of emotions built into all humans.

Basic emotions

From the 1960s the American psychologist Paul Ekman studied facial expressions across cultures, including among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, who had little contact with Western media at the time. He reported that people everywhere recognised a small set of expressions, usually listed as happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust. He argued these are basic emotions: evolved programmes, each with its own face, body pattern and brain circuit.

Constructed emotions

The psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett, in work summarised in her 2017 book How Emotions Are Made, argues that no emotion has a single fixed signature in the face or brain. The brain builds each emotion on the spot from basic feelings of pleasure and arousal, using concepts learned from culture and language. Anger in one person, at one moment, can look quite different from anger in another.

The evidence is genuinely contested. Large reviews have found that people do not reliably make the "standard" faces when they feel an emotion, and that recognition across cultures falls when people are not given a list of words to choose from. Supporters of basic emotions reply that some universality remains and that the debate is partly about definitions. Many researchers now hold a middle position: emotions have biological roots, but culture and context shape them more than the classic picture allowed.

Chapter 08

How emotions steer thinking

Emotions do not just follow thoughts. They shape them. Fear narrows attention onto the threat. Sadness tends to make people more careful and detailed in their judgements. Positive emotions tend to widen attention and make people more open to new ideas; the psychologist Barbara Fredrickson called this the broaden-and-build idea.

Specific emotions also change how people judge risk. In studies by Jennifer Lerner and Dacher Keltner, angry people tended to see risks as smaller and felt more in control, while fearful people saw the same risks as larger. Two people facing the same decision can lean opposite ways because one is angry and one is afraid, even when the emotion came from something unrelated. A feeling from one situation often leaks into judgements about another.

Patients with damage to parts of the brain that link emotion to planning can reason well in tests but make poor choices in life, a finding associated with the neuroscientist Antonio Damasio. That is the strongest evidence that emotion is part of good decision-making, not its enemy. The brain side is in Neuroscience · Decision-making, and the behaviour side in Psychology · Decision-making.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

You cannot choose your first reaction, but you can shape what follows

Most of the practical value of emotion science is in regulation: how people change which emotions they have, when, how strongly and how they show them.

Chapter 09

Five points where an emotion can be changed

The Stanford psychologist James Gross described a process model of emotion regulation in the late 1990s. It shows that you can step in at five points as an emotion unfolds.

                                                              not a verdict"

Two strategies have been compared in many studies. Reappraisal (point 4) means changing how you interpret a situation, as in the appraisal theory of Chapter 06. Expressive suppression (point 5) means hiding the emotion after it has started. On average, reappraisal reduces the feeling itself and has few costs. Suppression hides the face but usually leaves the inner feeling and the bodily stress in place, and in some studies it made people remember a conversation less well and seem less warm to their partners.

This does not make suppression always wrong. A doctor delivering bad news or a negotiator under pressure may need it, and cultures differ in how much emotional display is expected. Reappraisal also has limits: it is hard when an emotion is already intense, and it is the wrong tool for a situation that should be changed rather than reinterpreted. The skill is flexibility, not one favourite strategy.

Chapter 10

Putting feelings into words

A simple act turns out to have measurable effects. In brain-imaging studies led by Matthew Lieberman, people looking at angry or frightened faces showed less activity in the amygdala, a structure involved in threat responses, when they labelled the emotion in words than when they did not. This is called affect labelling. Writing about difficult experiences, an approach studied by James Pennebaker since the 1980s, has shown modest benefits for wellbeing in many studies.

Barrett's work adds the idea of emotional granularity: the ability to tell fine differences between your feelings. Someone who can only say "I feel bad" has fewer options than someone who can say "I'm not angry, I'm disappointed, and a bit embarrassed." Each word points to a different cause and a different response. Precise words for feelings are tools for managing them.

Chapter 11

Emotions are contagious

People tend to pick up the emotions of those around them, often by unconsciously mimicking faces, voices and postures. This is emotional contagion. It explains why a calm leader can steady a team in a crisis, why a single angry person can sour a meeting, and why laughter spreads in a theatre.

In 2014 researchers at Facebook and Cornell University published a study in which the news feeds of around 690,000 users had been adjusted, for one week, to show fewer positive or fewer negative posts. Users then wrote slightly more posts matching the mood they had been shown. The effect was very small. The reaction was not. Many people were angry that their emotions had been experimented on without their knowledge, and the study became a landmark in debates about research ethics online. At the scale of billions of users, even tiny emotional effects add up.

Chapter 12

Emotional intelligence, carefully

The psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced the term emotional intelligence in 1990: the ability to perceive, understand, use and manage emotions in yourself and others. Daniel Goleman's 1995 bestseller made it famous, with broad claims that it could matter more than IQ. Volume 08 summarises the book.

The research picture is more cautious than the popular one. Ability-based tests, which measure emotional skills like a test of reasoning, predict some outcomes at work and in relationships, but modestly. Self-report questionnaires often overlap heavily with personality traits, which raises the question of whether they measure anything new. Most researchers agree that emotional skills are real and can be improved, but that the claim they outweigh general intelligence was overstated.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. If emotions are regularly overwhelming or persistently low, a professional can help. Volume 43 explains what the different therapies do and how to get help.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fast, honest and sometimes badly wrong

Emotions are an ancient design running in a modern world. Their strengths and weaknesses come from the same features.

Chapter 13

FeatureStrengthWeakness
SpeedReacts to danger before slow reasoning finishes.Reacts to emails and comments as if they were predators.

ContagionShared calm, shared joy, team spirit.Panic, outrage and fear spread fast, especially online.

The table points to a clear conclusion. Most emotional problems are not cases of emotions being "irrational". They are cases of an emotion that fits one situation firing in a different one: too strong, too long or aimed at the wrong target. That is why the most effective approaches, in therapy and in everyday life, do not try to remove emotions. They try to make them fit better.

Chapter 15

Feeling well in a world built to make you feel

Many of the products people use every day are designed with emotions in mind. News headlines, advertisements and social media posts that provoke outrage or fear tend to spread further than calm ones. Researchers studying online sharing have repeatedly found that emotionally charged, and especially moral-emotional, language increases how widely posts travel.

That makes emotion regulation a public skill as well as a private one. Noticing that you are being made to feel something, and asking who benefits, is the first step to choosing your response. The ideas in this file give a practical toolkit: name the feeling precisely, check the appraisal behind it, and decide whether the situation needs changing, reinterpreting, or simply time.

The core idea

An emotion is a fast, whole-body response to what an event means for you. It combines an appraisal, bodily changes, an expression, an urge to act and a feeling. Emotions inform you and signal others. They can be regulated, most cheaply by changing situations, attention or interpretation early on.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Darwin's book on emotional expression appeared in 1872.
  • The classic list of basic emotions has six: happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust. Whether such a list is real is contested.
  • The process model of regulation has five points of intervention (Gross).
The common mistake

Thinking good thinking means having no emotions. Emotions give options their value. Without them, people struggle to decide at all. The aim is emotions that fit the situation, not no emotions.

Test yourself

Two people miss the same train. One is furious, one is calm. Explain the difference using appraisal theory, and say which regulation strategy could help the furious one.
