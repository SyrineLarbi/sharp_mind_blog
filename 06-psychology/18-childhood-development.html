Sharp Mind · Psychology · Topic 18 of 20

Childhood development. A brain that builds itself, guided by the people around it.

A newborn cannot hold up its own head. Five years later the same child talks in full sentences, tells lies, understands that other people believe different things, and argues about fairness. No other period of life changes a person so much, so fast. Developmental psychology studies how that happens. Its answers have changed how schools teach, how orphanages are run and how governments spend money. Some of its most popular claims, though, turned out to be weaker than parents were told.

Question 1 · What is it?

Growth in body, mind, language and relationships, all at once

Development is not one process but several, running in parallel and feeding each other. And it rests on a brain that does much of its building after birth.

Chapter 01

Four strands of one story

Researchers usually divide development into four areas, called domains. Physical development covers the body and movement: sitting, walking, holding a pencil. Cognitive development covers thinking: memory, attention, reasoning, understanding cause and effect. Language development covers understanding and speaking. Social and emotional development covers relationships, feelings and self-control.

The domains are separate for study but tangled in life. When a baby learns to crawl, around 8 or 9 months, the change is not only physical. Crawling babies start to fear heights, check their parent's face before moving toward something new, and understand space differently. A new ability in one domain opens doors in all the others.

Doctors track milestones, the typical ages at which most children reach a skill: smiling at around 6 weeks, first words around 12 months, walking somewhere between about 9 and 18 months. The range is wide. A child who walks at 15 months is not behind a child who walked at 10. Milestones are for spotting real delays early, not for ranking children.

Chapter 02

A brain that finishes itself outside the womb

Human babies are born unusually helpless compared with other primates. One reason is that a larger head would not fit through the birth canal. So much of the brain's growth happens afterwards. At birth the brain is around a quarter of its adult size. By about age five or six it is close to 90 per cent.

The growth is not only in size. In the first years the brain forms new connections between neurons, called synapses, at an enormous rate, far more than it will keep. Then, over childhood and adolescence, it removes the ones that are rarely used. This is called synaptic pruning. The principle is "use it or lose it". Experience decides which connections survive, which is why the same genes can produce very different adults in different environments.

For some abilities there are sensitive periods, windows when the brain is especially ready to learn from a certain kind of experience. Vision is the clearest case. If a baby is born with cataracts (clouded lenses) and they are not removed early, the child may never see normally from that eye, even after later surgery. The brain's visual system needed input during the window, and did not get it. Language has a sensitive period too, more gradual, which is why children pick up a native accent in a new language far more easily than adults.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Stages, helpers, bonds and words

Four big ideas explain most of what researchers know. Each began with one influential thinker, and each has been revised since.

Chapter 03

Piaget: children are little scientists

The Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget began his career marking children's answers on intelligence tests. He became more interested in their wrong answers, because children of the same age made the same mistakes. He concluded that children do not just know less than adults; they think differently. They build their understanding by exploring and testing, and it develops through stages.

Piaget's stages are still taught everywhere, but later research changed them in two ways. First, babies know much more than he thought. Using clever methods that measure how long babies look at surprising events, researchers such as Renée Baillargeon found signs that infants of a few months expect hidden objects to keep existing. Second, development is less tidy than fixed stages suggest. A child can reason well about one topic and poorly about another. The lasting idea is Piaget's central one: children actively construct their understanding.

Chapter 04

Vygotsky: nobody learns alone

The Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky, who died young in 1934, stressed what Piaget underplayed: other people. He described the zone of proximal development, the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help. Learning happens best in that zone. A task that is too easy teaches nothing; a task that is too hard, even with help, only frustrates.

Later researchers called the right kind of help scaffolding. A parent helping a child with a puzzle might first point to the corner pieces, then only hint, then just watch. Like scaffolding on a building, the support is removed as the structure can stand by itself. Good teaching at any age follows this pattern. The broader topic is in Learning.

Chapter 05

Attachment: the secure base

In the 1950s the American psychologist Harry Harlow raised infant rhesus monkeys with two artificial "mothers". One was made of wire and held a milk bottle. The other was covered in soft cloth and gave no food. The monkeys fed from the wire mother but spent most of their time clinging to the cloth one, and ran to it when frightened. The experiments were cruel by modern standards. Their lesson was that comfort, not just food, is what binds a baby to its carer.

The British psychiatrist John Bowlby built this into attachment theory. Babies are born with a drive to stay close to a few trusted adults, because in our evolutionary past that kept them alive. A reliable carer becomes a secure base: the child explores, and returns when worried.

Mary Ainsworth, who worked with Bowlby, created a laboratory test called the Strange Situation. A toddler plays in a room, the parent leaves briefly, a stranger enters, and the parent returns. What researchers watch most closely is the reunion. Most children are securely attached: upset when the parent leaves, quickly comforted when they return. Others are insecure, either avoiding the parent on return or staying upset and clinging. A fourth, disorganised pattern was added later. Secure attachment is linked, on average, to better relationships and emotional control later. The links are real but moderate, and attachment can change when circumstances change.

The strongest evidence came from tragedy. In Romania in the 1980s, under the Ceaușescu regime, tens of thousands of children grew up in state orphanages with almost no individual care. After 1989, researchers followed children adopted into families abroad and, in the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, children randomly placed in foster care. Children removed from institutions early recovered far better than those removed later, in IQ, language and emotional development. Many countries have since moved away from large orphanages toward foster families.

Chapter 06

Language: born ready for any language

A baby can hear the difference between speech sounds in every human language. Japanese adults often struggle to hear the difference between English "r" and "l", but Japanese babies of six months can. By around their first birthday, babies have tuned in to the sounds of the language around them and begun to lose the ones they do not hear. Researchers such as Janet Werker and Patricia Kuhl documented this narrowing.

Speech arrives in a well-known order: cooing, then babbling ("ba-ba-ba") around six months, first words around one year, then a vocabulary spurt in the second year, when many children add new words very quickly. Two-word sentences ("more juice") come next, and by age four most children use complex grammar they were never explicitly taught.

The input matters. A 1995 study by Betty Hart and Todd Risley claimed that by age three, children in professional families had heard about 30 million more words than children in families on welfare. The "30-million-word gap" became famous, but it was based on a small sample, and later studies found smaller and more variable differences. What holds up better is the value of conversation, not just words: back-and-forth exchanges in which the adult responds to the child's sounds and gestures. Researchers call this serve and return.

Chapter 07

Reading minds and waiting for marshmallows

Show a three-year-old this story. Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves. Anne moves it to a box. Where will Sally look for her marble when she returns? Most three-year-olds say "in the box", where it really is. Most four- and five-year-olds say "in the basket", because Sally thinks it is there. Passing this false-belief test shows theory of mind: the understanding that other people have beliefs that can differ from reality and from yours. It is the foundation of empathy, cooperation, and also of lying.

Self-control develops more slowly, because the front of the brain that supports it, the prefrontal cortex, matures into the mid-twenties. The famous test is Walter Mischel's "marshmallow test" from Stanford in the late 1960s and 1970s. A child could eat one treat now or wait alone for two. Follow-up studies suggested that children who waited longer did better years later. But a 2018 replication with a larger, more varied sample found the link was much smaller once family background was taken into account. Children's ability to wait also depends on whether they trust the adult to keep the promise. Self-control is real, but it is shaped by circumstances as much as it shapes them.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The early years echo through a lifetime

What happens in childhood affects health, earnings and relationships decades later. That finding has shaped public policy. What parents can control turns out to be more limited, and more important, than either side of the parenting debate says.

Chapter 08

Why governments invest in toddlers

In the 1960s, in Ypsilanti, Michigan, the Perry Preschool Project gave a high-quality preschool programme to a group of children from poor families, and compared them with a similar group who did not receive it. Researchers followed both groups for decades. The preschool group, on average, earned more, committed fewer crimes and were more likely to finish school. The study was small, but similar results came from other programmes.

The economist James Heckman, a Nobel laureate, used such studies to argue that money spent in early childhood brings a higher return than money spent later, because skills build on skills. A child who learns to concentrate and get along with others at four learns more at seven. Economists still debate how large the returns are and whether large public programmes match the results of small, carefully run ones. The policy side is in Education.

The other side of the coin is harm. A large American study published in 1998 by Vincent Felitti, Robert Anda and colleagues asked adults about adverse childhood experiences (ACEs): abuse, neglect, and household problems such as violence or addiction. The more such experiences people reported, the higher their adult rates of heart disease, depression and many other problems. Researchers describe severe, long-lasting stress without a supportive adult as toxic stress, which can affect the developing brain and body. An ACE count is useful for populations, but it should not be used to predict an individual child's future.

Chapter 09

How much do parents really shape a child?

In the 1960s the psychologist Diana Baumrind described parenting styles along two lines: how warm and responsive parents are, and how much they set rules and expectations. Later researchers added a fourth style.

Authoritative

Warm and firm. Clear rules, explained reasons, and listening. Linked, on average, with the best outcomes in many Western studies.

Authoritarian

Firm but cold. "Because I said so." Linked with more anxiety and less independence, though the effects vary between cultures.

Permissive

Warm but few limits. Children may struggle more with self-control.

Neglectful

Neither warm nor firm. Consistently linked with the worst outcomes.

Then came a challenge. In The Nurture Assumption (1998), Judith Rich Harris pointed out that most parenting studies cannot separate parenting from genes. Warm, organised parents may pass on genes for warmth and organisation. Twin and adoption studies tend to find that the shared family environment (what siblings growing up together have in common) explains surprisingly little of adult personality. Harris argued that genes and peers matter more than parents think.

The balanced view today has three parts. Severe neglect, abuse and poverty clearly harm children. Within the normal range of decent parenting, specific techniques matter less than parents fear. And parents still matter enormously in other ways: they choose the neighbourhood, the school and the peers, and their warmth affects how children feel during childhood itself, which counts for its own sake. One specific finding is consistent: physical punishment is linked with worse outcomes, not better behaviour. Sweden banned it in 1979, and many countries have since followed.

Chapter 10

Resilience: the ordinary magic

In 1955 the psychologist Emmy Werner and colleagues began following every child born that year on the Hawaiian island of Kauai, for four decades. About a third of the high-risk children, those who grew up with poverty, family conflict or a parent's illness, grew into capable, caring adults. What helped most was at least one stable, caring adult, a grandparent, teacher or neighbour, plus an easy-going temperament and some skill they were proud of.

The psychologist Ann Masten later called resilience "ordinary magic", because it comes from common human resources rather than rare qualities. Resilience is not a fixed trait that some children have. It is the result of protections around the child, and protections can be added.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A flexible design, and a science that sometimes overpromised

The long, flexible human childhood is our species' great advantage and its great vulnerability. The science of it has also had its share of hype.

Chapter 12

When the science was oversold

In 1993 a short paper reported that college students did slightly better on a spatial task after listening to Mozart. The effect was small and lasted minutes, and it was tested on adults. Within a few years it had become the "Mozart effect", the claim that classical music makes babies smarter. In 1998 the governor of Georgia proposed giving every newborn in the state a classical music CD. Later studies found little or no lasting effect.

Something similar happened with baby videos marketed as educational. Studies found that very young children learn words far better from live people than from screens, and the Baby Einstein company offered refunds in 2009 after complaints about its educational claims. The wider claim that "everything is decided by age three" also overstated the evidence. The early years matter a great deal, but the brain stays changeable for life, and later childhood and adolescence have sensitive periods of their own.

The lesson for parents is reassuring. What young children need most is not special products but ordinary things: responsive conversation, play, sleep, safety and affection.

Chapter 14

Is childhood itself changing?

In many rich countries, children today spend less time playing outdoors without adults than their parents did, and more time on screens. Some researchers, notably Jonathan Haidt in The Anxious Generation (2024), argue that the shift from play-based to phone-based childhood has contributed to rising anxiety and depression among teenagers, especially since the early 2010s. Others, such as Candice Odgers and Amy Orben, argue that the evidence linking phones to mental health is weak and mostly correlational. This is a live, unsettled debate. It is covered from the technology side in Psychology of technology.

What both sides agree on is that children benefit from independent play, sleep, face-to-face friendship and some freedom to take small risks. Childhood also now lasts longer in another sense: young adults finish education, leave home and start families later than earlier generations. Some psychologists call this new stage "emerging adulthood". The wider social picture is in Generational differences.

The core idea

Children develop in physical, cognitive, language and social-emotional domains at once. The brain overbuilds connections and prunes them by experience. Children actively construct understanding (Piaget), learn best with support just beyond their level (Vygotsky), and explore from a secure base (Bowlby and Ainsworth).

The numbers worth knowing
  • At birth the brain is around a quarter of adult size; close to 90% by about age five or six.
  • Most children pass the false-belief test around age four.
  • In the Kauai study, about a third of high-risk children grew into capable adults.
The common mistake

Believing that special products or techniques make children smarter. Responsive conversation, play, sleep and a stable, caring adult do more than any app or video.

Can you explain why children adopted early from Romanian orphanages recovered better than those adopted later? If you can, you have understood sensitive periods, attachment and plasticity together.

The words, in plain words
Milestone
A skill most children reach within a typical age range, such as walking.
Synaptic pruning
The removal of rarely used brain connections during childhood and adolescence.
Sensitive period
A window when the brain learns a particular kind of thing especially easily.
Object permanence
Understanding that things still exist when you cannot see them.
Conservation
Understanding that an amount stays the same when its shape changes.
Zone of proximal development
The range of tasks a child can do with help but not yet alone.
Scaffolding
Support given to a learner and gradually removed as they become able.
Attachment
The deep emotional bond between a child and their main carers.
Secure base
A trusted adult a child explores from and returns to when worried.
Theory of mind
Understanding that other people have their own beliefs, which can be wrong.
Serve and return
Back-and-forth exchanges in which an adult responds to a child's sounds and gestures.

Abuse, neglect and serious household problems in childhood, linked to later health problems.

Doing well despite serious adversity, usually thanks to protective people and conditions.
