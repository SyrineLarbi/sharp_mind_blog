Sharp Mind · Psychology · Topic 02 of 20

Learning. The brain changes most when it is surprised and struggling, not when it is comfortable.

Most people judge how well they are learning by how smooth it feels. A clear lecture, a neat summary, a page reread for the third time: it all feels like progress. The research says the opposite. Learning is a lasting change in what you know or can do, and it happens mostly when the brain's predictions fail and it has to work to fix them. Once you see that, a lot of school, training and self-improvement looks upside down.

Question 1 · What is it?

A lasting change, caused by experience

Psychologists define learning as a relatively lasting change in behaviour or knowledge that comes from experience. Each word in that definition rules something out.

Chapter 01

The physiologist who was studying something else

Ivan Pavlov won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his work on digestion, not on learning. He was measuring how much saliva dogs produced when food reached their mouths. Then his assistants noticed a nuisance. The dogs started to salivate before the food arrived: at the sight of the person who fed them, or at the sound of footsteps. Pavlov realised the nuisance was the more interesting result.

He set up a simple test. A neutral signal, such as a metronome or a buzzer (popular retellings usually say a bell), sounded just before food. After enough pairings, the sound alone made the dogs salivate. The dogs had learned that one event predicts another. This is classical conditioning: a signal that meant nothing comes to trigger a response because it reliably comes before something that matters.

It is why the smell of a hospital can make your stomach tighten, and why advertisers put their product next to happy faces. Your body learns what predicts what, without asking your permission.

Chapter 02

Three ways to learn from experience

Pavlov's kind of learning is only one of three basic forms. Most real learning mixes all three.

FormWhat is learnedClassic studyEveryday example
Classical conditioningThat one event predicts anotherPavlov's dogs, around 1900Feeling hungry when you see a fast-food logo
Operant conditioningThat an action leads to a consequenceThorndike's cats escaping puzzle boxes (1898); Skinner's boxes (1930s on)A child learning that whining gets sweets
Observational learningWhat happens when others actAlbert Bandura's "Bobo doll" studies (1961)Picking up a colleague's way of handling angry clients

Bandura's studies are worth a closer look. Children watched an adult hit, kick and shout at an inflatable clown doll. Left alone with the doll afterwards, many copied the attack, sometimes with the same words. Children who had watched a calm adult were much less aggressive. Humans do not need to be rewarded or punished themselves to learn. Watching is enough. This is the basis of apprenticeship, of culture, and of the worry about what children learn from what they see.

Chapter 03

Learning is not the same as performance

The most useful idea in modern learning science comes from the psychologists Robert and Elizabeth Bjork. They separate two things that look alike. Performance is how well you do right now, during or just after practice. Learning is the lasting change that will still be there next week, in a different room, when nobody is helping.

The two often move in opposite directions. Practising the same tennis serve forty times in a row makes you look good by the fortieth. Mixing serves with other shots makes you look worse during the session. Tested a week later, the mixed group usually does better. How well you do in practice is a poor guide to how much you have learned, and most people, including most teachers and managers, judge by practice.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Consequences, surprise, structure and repetition

Four mechanisms do most of the work. Actions are shaped by what follows them. Learning is driven by the gap between what you expected and what happened. New knowledge is hung on old knowledge. And repetition turns effortful steps into automatic ones.

Chapter 04

Consequences: the law of effect, and the slot machine

In the 1890s Edward Thorndike put hungry cats in wooden boxes with a latch. At first they scratched around at random until they hit the latch by accident. Each time, they escaped faster. Thorndike called the principle the law of effect: actions followed by satisfying results become more likely, and actions followed by unpleasant results become less likely.

B. F. Skinner turned this into a precise science. He used the word reinforcement for any consequence that makes a behaviour more frequent. His most surprising finding concerned timing. If a rat gets food every time it presses a lever, it presses steadily, and it stops quickly when the food stops. If food comes after an unpredictable number of presses, the rat presses furiously and keeps going long after the food has gone.

This variable reward pattern is the engine of the slot machine. It is also the engine of the social media feed you refresh to see whether something new has arrived. Sometimes there is, mostly there is not, and the uncertainty is the hook. Unpredictable rewards build the most stubborn behaviour of all.

Punishment is weaker than it looks

Punishment can stop a behaviour in the moment, but research on both animals and children finds it teaches less than reward. It shows what not to do without showing what to do instead. It also teaches the learner to avoid the punisher, and to hide the behaviour.

Chapter 05

Surprise: you learn from prediction errors

For a long time people assumed that pairing two things often enough was all it took. Then, in the late 1960s, the psychologist Leon Kamin found a puzzle called blocking. First he taught rats that a sound predicted a mild shock. Then he paired the sound and a light together with the shock. The rats learned almost nothing about the light, even though it had been paired with the shock many times.

The explanation, formalised by Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner in 1972, is simple and powerful. The sound already predicted the shock perfectly, so the light added no new information. The brain learns only in proportion to how surprised it is. The gap between what you expected and what actually happened is called a prediction error. Big errors drive big changes. No error, no learning.

This idea now runs through psychology, neuroscience and artificial intelligence. Brain cells that release the chemical messenger dopamine fire in a pattern that looks very much like a prediction error signal. Many machine-learning systems learn by shrinking their own prediction errors. The brain side of the story is in Neuroscience · Decision-making.

The practical lesson is direct. If you already expect the answer, you will not learn from seeing it. That is why guessing first, even guessing wrong, helps you remember the right answer: the guess creates an expectation that the answer can then correct.

Chapter 06

Desirable difficulties

Robert Bjork coined the phrase desirable difficulties in the 1990s for conditions that make learning feel harder and slower but make it last longer. The Memory topic covered two of them, testing yourself and spacing practice over days. Two more are less well known.

Interleaving

Mixing different kinds of problems in one session, instead of doing all of one kind and then all of the next. In a 2008 study by Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork, people learned to recognise painters' styles. Those who saw paintings by different artists mixed together identified new paintings better than those who saw each artist in a block. Most participants still believed the blocked version had worked better. Interleaving forces you to notice what makes each kind different, which is exactly what a real test demands.

Generation

Producing an answer yourself, even an incomplete one, before you are shown it. Filling in a missing word, predicting the next step of a proof, or explaining a concept aloud all count. The effort of producing, and the prediction error when you get it wrong, both strengthen the memory.

The word "desirable" matters. Not every difficulty helps. A badly written textbook or a noisy room makes things harder without making you learn more. A difficulty is desirable when it makes you do the mental work the final skill will require: retrieving, telling things apart, building the answer.

Chapter 07

Structure: new knowledge hangs on old knowledge

People do not learn facts one at a time like coins dropped in a jar. They fit each new fact into a schema, an organised mental framework of what they already know about a subject. A chess player who reads about a new opening attaches it to hundreds of positions they know. A beginner has nothing to attach it to, so it slides away.

The Australian educational psychologist John Sweller built cognitive load theory on this idea from the late 1980s. Working memory, as the Memory topic explained, holds only a few chunks at once. A beginner facing a complex problem uses all of them just to keep track of the pieces, and has nothing left over for noticing the pattern. That is why beginners usually learn more from studying a worked example, a problem solved step by step, than from struggling with the same problem alone.

There is a twist. For people who already know a subject, worked examples soon become a waste of time, and solving problems themselves works better. This is called the expertise reversal effect. The best way to teach depends on what the learner already knows, which is one reason a single method rarely works for a whole class.

Chapter 08

Skill: from clumsy to automatic

In 1967 the psychologists Paul Fitts and Michael Posner described three stages that most skills pass through. Think of learning to drive a car with a manual gearbox.

The three stages of skill learning (Fitts and Posner)
 1. COGNITIVE        "clutch down, first gear, mirror, signal..."
    slow, full of errors, needs all of your attention, you talk yourself through it
           |
           v  practice + feedback
 2. ASSOCIATIVE      steps start to join into smooth sequences
    fewer errors, still needs attention when anything unusual happens
           |
           v  much more practice
 3. AUTONOMOUS       you change gear while holding a conversation
    fast, smooth, hard to describe, and hard to change

The third stage is the goal and also a trap. Once a skill is automatic, you stop paying attention to it, so you stop improving it. Many people drive, type or write for decades at the same level. The psychologist Anders Ericsson argued that experts keep improving because they deliberately pull parts of the skill back into the first stage: they target a weakness, get immediate feedback, and practise at the edge of their ability. Volume 07 summarises his argument in Peak. Experience alone does not make you better. Experience with feedback and effort does.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Schools, workplaces, and the stories we tell about ability

Almost every institution is built on a theory of learning, usually an unexamined one. Getting it wrong costs years of people's time.

Chapter 09

The illusion of fluency

When you reread a chapter, it feels familiar, and the brain mistakes that easy familiarity for knowing. Psychologists call this the illusion of fluency. It is why a polished training video can leave a team unable to do the task a week later.

The same illusion affects teachers and trainers. A session where learners struggle, make mistakes and look confused feels like a failure, so it gets replaced with a smoother one. Student ratings reward smoothness too. The methods that feel worst in the moment are often the ones that work best over time, and people almost never see the delayed result that would correct them.

Chapter 10

Two popular ideas that do not hold up

Learning styles. Many people believe they are "visual" or "auditory" learners and learn best when teaching matches their style. Surveys over the years have found most teachers in several countries believe this. In 2008 a review led by Harold Pashler looked for the kind of experiment that could prove it: people sorted by style, taught in matched and mismatched ways, then tested. It found almost no adequate studies, and the few that existed did not support the idea. What matters more is the material: maps are best learned visually and music by ear, for everyone.

Brain training. Apps that promised to make you smarter by playing memory games became a large industry in the 2000s and 2010s. Players do get better at the games. The evidence that this spreads to everyday thinking is weak. In 2016 the US Federal Trade Commission settled with Lumos Labs, the maker of Lumosity, over claims about its benefits that the regulator said were not backed by evidence. You get better at what you practise, and usually only that. Chapter 13 explains why.

Chapter 11

Kind and unkind teachers: why some experts never improve

Some fields produce real experts quickly: chess, weather forecasting, many sports. Others produce people with decades of experience who are barely better than beginners, a pattern researchers have reported in areas such as long-range political forecasting and some kinds of clinical judgement. The difference is usually the feedback.

The psychologist Robin Hogarth described kind learning environments, where feedback is fast, clear and accurate, and wicked ones, where feedback is late, missing or misleading. A chess player learns within minutes whether a move was bad. A manager who hires someone may never learn how the rejected candidates would have done. Learning from experience needs the experience to give honest answers. Volume 07's summary of Range builds on this distinction.

The practical point is that where feedback is missing, you have to build it. Write down predictions and check them later. Ask for reviews of finished work. Keep a record of decisions and what happened next.

Chapter 12

Beliefs about ability

Carol Dweck's work on mindset argued that people who see ability as something that grows with effort respond better to difficulty than people who see it as fixed. Volume 09 summarises the book. The core idea fits everything above: if struggle is how learning happens, then avoiding struggle to protect your image blocks it.

Honesty requires a caution here. Early studies reported large effects from short mindset lessons. Larger later studies and reviews found the average effect on school grades to be small, concentrated mainly among students who were struggling, and the size of the effect is still argued over. Belief helps people persist, but it is not a substitute for good methods and good feedback. A student who believes in growth and still only rereads will not grow much.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A learning system built for survival, used for algebra

The same mechanisms that make humans the most flexible learners on the planet also make them learn the wrong things, and hold on to them.

Chapter 13

Strengths and their shadows

Feature of human learningStrengthWeakness
Learning from one bad experienceOne food poisoning can create a lifelong aversion to that food. That protects you from repeating a deadly mistake.Fears and phobias can form from a single event and resist all reasoning.
Learning from rewardsBehaviour that works gets repeated without any planning.Designers can hijack it with variable rewards in games, gambling and apps.
Learning by watchingSkills and culture pass between generations without everyone rediscovering them.Bad habits, prejudices and false beliefs spread just as easily.
Automatic skillsFrees attention for new problems. You can drive and plan at once.Automatic errors are hard to notice and hard to unlearn.
Building on schemasExperts learn new material in their field very fast.Information that does not fit an existing schema is distorted or ignored.
Specific learningSkills become precise and efficient for the job they were practised for.They rarely transfer far to different tasks.

The first row has a famous experiment behind it. In the 1960s John Garcia showed that rats made sick hours after tasting a flavour learned to avoid it after a single pairing. They did not learn the same thing easily from a light or a sound. The finding showed that animals are prepared by evolution to learn some links far more easily than others, a blow to the old idea that any two things could be paired equally well.

The last row, about transfer, deserves a word. Transfer means applying learning from one situation to another. Near transfer, from one kind of maths problem to a similar one, is common. Far transfer, from chess to business strategy or from Latin to logical thinking, is much rarer than people hope. What does transfer well is deep understanding of principles, and the habit of asking "what kind of problem is this?" before diving in.

Chapter 15

Learning when the machine already knows

Every new tool has raised the question of what is still worth learning. Calculators did not end the need to understand arithmetic, but they changed which arithmetic people practise. AI tools that can write, summarise and solve problems raise the question more sharply than ever.

The science in this file suggests an answer. Tools are good at retrieving answers. Learning happens through effort, prediction and correction. If a tool removes all the effort, it removes the learning too. A student who asks an AI to write the essay gets the essay and none of the understanding. A student who writes first and then asks the AI to find the weak spots gets a tireless source of feedback, which is exactly what most learners have always lacked.

Knowing things by heart still matters, because you cannot think with what you do not know. Alongside it, judging whether an answer is good is becoming the skill that sets people apart.

The core idea

Learning is a lasting change caused by experience. It comes from associations (what predicts what), consequences (what follows an action) and watching others. It is driven by prediction error, built on what you already know, and made automatic by practice. Feeling fluent is not the same as having learned.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Working memory holds only a few chunks, so beginners learn best from worked examples.
  • Skills pass through three stages: cognitive, associative, autonomous (Fitts and Posner, 1967).
  • A 2008 review found no good evidence that matching teaching to "learning styles" helps.
The common mistake

Judging learning by how smooth practice feels. Performance during practice is not learning, and the conditions that make practice feel easiest often teach the least.

Test yourself

Explain blocking to a friend: why did Kamin's rats learn nothing about the light? If you can, you understand why guessing before you look at an answer helps you learn it.

The words, in plain words
Classical conditioning
Learning that one event signals another, so the signal alone starts to trigger a response.
Operant conditioning
Learning that an action leads to a consequence, so the action becomes more or less frequent.
Observational learning
Learning by watching what others do and what happens to them.
Reinforcement
Any consequence that makes a behaviour happen more often.
Variable reward
A reward that arrives unpredictably. It builds very persistent behaviour.
Prediction error
The gap between what you expected and what happened. Learning grows with its size.
Blocking
Failing to learn about a new signal because an old one already predicts the outcome.
Desirable difficulty
A condition that makes practice harder but makes learning last longer.
Interleaving
Mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session.
Schema
An organised mental framework of what you already know about a subject.
Cognitive load
The amount of working memory a task uses up.
Transfer
Using what you learned in one situation in a different one.
Illusion of fluency
Mistaking easy familiarity with material for real knowledge of it.
