Behavioral economics. Economics for the people who actually exist.

For most of the 20th century, economic theory assumed a person who calculates perfectly, never procrastinates, does not care about fairness and treats every euro the same way. Nobody has ever met this person. Behavioral economics is what happened when economists started checking their assumptions against how people really behave. It has won several Nobel Prizes, changed how pension systems and tax letters are designed, and given companies new ways to sell. It has also been oversold, and the field is now sorting its strongest findings from its weakest.

Question 1 · What is it?

Economics that takes psychology seriously

Behavioral economics keeps the questions of economics (how people spend, save, work and trade) but replaces a convenient assumption with evidence.

Chapter 01

Econs and Humans

Standard economic models describe a creature often called homo economicus, "economic man". He knows what he wants, and his wants do not change with his mood. He weighs every option, calculates costs and benefits correctly, and chooses the best. He saves the right amount for retirement. He never buys a gym membership he will not use.

The economist Richard Thaler, writing with the legal scholar Cass Sunstein in the 2008 book Nudge, called these imaginary people Econs, and the rest of us Humans. Humans have limited attention, limited willpower and limited time. We are affected by how choices are presented, by what other people do, and by what feels fair.

The important point is that these departures from the model are not random. If human errors were random, they would cancel out in large markets and the old models would still work on average. But many errors push in a predictable direction. Most people save too little, not too much. Most people stick with whatever option is already selected. Because the errors are systematic, they can be studied, predicted and designed for.

Chapter 02

Four Nobel Prizes and a field that was once heresy

The roots go back to Herbert Simon, an American scholar who worked across economics, psychology and computer science. In the 1950s he argued that people have bounded rationality: they lack the information and mental capacity to find the best option, so they satisfice, picking the first option that is good enough. He won the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978.

The decisive work came from two Israeli psychologists, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. From the early 1970s they ran simple experiments showing how people judge probabilities and make risky choices. Their 1979 paper on prospect theory became one of the most cited papers in economics. Tversky died in 1996. Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in 2002, shared with Vernon Smith, a pioneer of testing economic theories in laboratory experiments.

Thaler, who had spent years collecting "anomalies" that standard theory could not explain, won in 2017. Robert Shiller, who studied how emotion and storytelling drive asset prices, shared the 2013 prize. For decades many economists dismissed the field as a set of curiosities. Today its ideas are part of mainstream economics, taught in standard courses and used by central banks, regulators and governments.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Five ways real people differ from the model

The field's findings fall into a few families. Each one explains a familiar behaviour that the standard model calls a mistake.

Chapter 03

Prospect theory: value depends on where you stand

Standard theory says people judge outcomes by their total wealth. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, usually what they have now or what they expected. A salary of 50,000 feels wonderful if you expected 45,000, and disappointing if you expected 55,000.

Part of the theoryWhat it saysWhat it explains
Reference dependenceOutcomes are judged against a starting pointWhy the same pay rise can please or anger, depending on expectations

Diminishing sensitivityThe difference between 10 and 20 feels bigger than between 1,010 and 1,020Why people drive across town to save 10 on a cheap item but not on an expensive one
Probability weightingSmall chances are overweighted; near-certainties are underweightedWhy the same person buys lottery tickets and insurance

The last row resolves an old puzzle. A lottery ticket is a bad bet, and so, on average, is insurance, since insurers make a profit. Yet many people buy both. Prospect theory explains it. People treat a tiny chance as much bigger than it is, whether it is the chance of winning millions or the chance of the house burning down.

Chapter 04

Present bias: the future self always loses

Ask people: would you prefer 100 euros today or 110 in a week? Many take 100 today. Now ask: 100 in a year, or 110 in a year and a week? Almost everyone waits the extra week. The trade is the same in both cases, seven days for ten euros. Only the distance from today changed.

Economists call this present bias. People are far more impatient about the immediate present than about the future. A rational Econ discounts the future at a steady rate. A Human discounts very steeply between now and soon, then much more gently after. The technical name for this curve is hyperbolic discounting. It explains why we plan to start saving, dieting or studying "next month", and then, when next month arrives, it has become "now" and the plan loses.

The cure is a commitment device: a decision made in advance that binds your future self. The oldest example is Ulysses in the Odyssey, who had his crew tie him to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without steering toward them. A modern one is Save More Tomorrow, a plan designed by Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi. Employees agree today to save a share of future pay rises. Because the money is not yet theirs, it does not feel like a loss. In the first company to try it, participants' average saving rate rose from about 3.5 per cent to over 13 per cent within a few years.

Chapter 05

Mental accounting and the value of owning

To an Econ, money is fungible: every euro is the same as every other. Humans keep separate mental "accounts", as Thaler documented. Money for holidays, money for bills, a tax refund, a casino win. People spend a windfall more freely than salary, and some keep money in a savings account earning little interest while carrying credit-card debt at a high rate. That is costly, but it can be a crude self-control tool: the savings account is "not for spending".

A related effect is the endowment effect: people value things more once they own them. In a famous 1990 experiment by Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Thaler, students were randomly given a coffee mug. Those who owned a mug demanded roughly twice as much to sell it as other students were willing to pay to buy one. The mugs were identical, and nobody had owned theirs for more than a few minutes. Loss aversion explains it: giving up the mug is a loss, buying one is only a gain.

Chapter 06

Fairness, other people and limited attention

In the ultimatum game, one player receives some money, say 100 euros, and offers a split to a second player. If the second player accepts, both keep their shares. If they refuse, both get nothing. An Econ would accept any offer, even one euro, because one euro is better than nothing. Real people, in many countries, often reject offers below about a fifth or a quarter of the total. They will pay to punish unfairness. Knowing this, most people who make offers propose something close to an even split. Fairness is not a cost people ignore; it is something they will pay for.

People are also strongly influenced by what others do. Telling households that their neighbours use less energy than they do has been shown to reduce energy use modestly. This is called a social norm message.

Finally, attention is scarce. People do not read every term in a contract or compare every mobile phone plan. They notice what is prominent and skip what is buried. The single most powerful consequence is the force of the default, the option that applies if you do nothing. That is where policy enters.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Defaults, nudge units, and the businesses that got there first

The field's biggest impact has been practical. Governments use it to help people; companies use it to sell. The same tools serve both.

Chapter 07

The power of doing nothing

Thaler and Sunstein defined a nudge as any feature of a choice that changes behaviour predictably without forbidding any option or significantly changing financial incentives. Putting fruit at eye level in a canteen is a nudge. Banning chocolate is not. They called the design of how choices are presented choice architecture, and pointed out that there is no neutral design. Something has to go first on the menu.

Defaults are the strongest nudge. In a well-known 2001 study, Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea examined an American company that changed its pension plan. Before, employees had to sign up. After, new employees were enrolled automatically and could leave if they wished. Participation among new employees jumped from under half to well over 80 per cent. Nobody was forced, and the financial incentives were unchanged. Only the default moved.

The United Kingdom applied the same idea nationally. From 2012, employers were required to enrol eligible workers in a workplace pension automatically. The share of eligible private-sector employees saving into a pension rose from under half to above 85 per cent within a few years. Similar schemes exist in other countries, such as New Zealand's KiwiSaver. Auto-enrolment is behavioral economics' clearest large-scale success.

Organ donation shows both the power and the limits. Countries where citizens are donors unless they opt out show far higher registered consent than countries where people must opt in. But actual donation rates differ much less, because families are usually consulted and medical systems matter more than the register. A default changes a form; it does not change everything behind it.

Chapter 08

Nudge units inside governments

In 2010 the British government set up the Behavioural Insights Team, soon nicknamed the "Nudge Unit". One of its early projects concerned unpaid taxes. Letters were rewritten to say that most people in the recipient's area had already paid on time. Payment rates rose. The change cost almost nothing, because the letters were being sent anyway.

Since then, governments across the world have created similar units, and the World Bank, the OECD and the United Nations use the approach. Typical methods are simple: make the desired action easy (pre-filled forms), make it timely (reminders just before a deadline), make it social (what others do) and make it attractive (personal, clear messages). Their great strength is that they are cheap and testable. A unit can send two versions of a letter to random groups and measure which works better, the same method used in medical trials. How such tools fit into the wider process is covered in How governments create policies.

Chapter 09

The same tools, used to sell

Businesses were using these effects long before economists named them. A few common examples:

  • Free trials that convert automatically to paid subscriptions use the default and the endowment effect together.
  • The decoy effect: adding a third option that nobody wants, such as a medium drink priced almost like a large, makes the large look like a bargain. Researchers Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto documented this in 1982.
  • Prices ending in .99 exploit the way people read the first digit.
  • "Only 2 left!" messages use scarcity and fear of missing out.

When these designs work against the customer's interest, Thaler uses the word sludge: friction that makes good choices harder, such as a subscription that can be started in one click but cancelled only by phone. Designers call deliberately manipulative interfaces dark patterns. The customer side is in Customer behavior, and digital design in Psychology of technology.

Chapter 10

Markets made of Humans

The field also changed how economists think about financial markets. Standard theory says prices reflect all available information, because rational traders quickly correct any mistake. Behavioral finance points out that traders are Humans too. They are often overconfident, follow the crowd, and extrapolate recent trends into the future. Shiller warned of an overvalued stock market around 2000 and of an overheated US housing market before 2008, arguing that stories and enthusiasm were driving prices.

The debate is not settled. Markets are hard to beat consistently, which supports the standard view. But prices can stay far from sensible values for years, which supports the behavioral one. Volume 38 explains the pattern in Bubbles, and why they keep happening.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A real advance, and a reckoning

Behavioral economics fixed real gaps in the old models. It also suffered from hype, weak studies and some scandals.

Chapter 12

Smaller effects, failed replications, and the paternalism question

From the 2010s, psychology went through a replication crisis: when researchers repeated well-known experiments with larger samples, many effects shrank or disappeared. Behavioral economics was affected too. Some popular findings, such as the idea that willpower runs out like fuel ("ego depletion"), have largely failed to replicate. A famous study suggesting that signing an honesty pledge at the top of a form reduces cheating was retracted in 2021, after evidence emerged that some of its data had been fabricated.

The size of nudge effects has also been questioned. A 2022 study by Stefano DellaVigna and Elizabeth Linos compared academic papers with the trials actually run by two large US nudge units. The academic papers reported average effects several times larger than the nudge units found in practice. The units' average effect was real but small, roughly a percentage point or so. Small effects can still be worth it when they cost almost nothing and reach millions of people. The honest summary is: defaults are powerful, most other nudges are modest.

A different critique is about principle. Thaler and Sunstein call their approach libertarian paternalism: steering people toward better choices while leaving them free. Some critics ask who decides what is "better", and warn that governments are made of Humans with their own biases. Others, including the behavioral scientists Nick Chater and George Loewenstein in a 2022 paper, argue that the focus on nudging individuals has let governments and companies avoid harder structural policies, such as regulating products or taxing pollution.

Chapter 14

From nudges to boosts and systems

One direction is to teach rather than steer. Researchers such as Ralph Hertwig and Till Grüne-Yanoff propose boosts: building people's own skills so they choose well without anyone designing the choice for them. Teaching simple rules for reading risk, such as converting percentages into "out of 100 people" numbers, is a boost. It respects autonomy and its benefits travel with the person.

Another direction is combining behavioral insight with ordinary economic tools. Auto-enrolment worked because it was combined with a law and employer contributions, not because of clever wording. The field's mature form is likely to be less about tricks and more about designing whole systems that work for Humans. Economics itself is in the Economics folder, starting from Supply and demand.

The core idea

People are not perfect calculators. They judge gains and losses from a reference point, favour the present, keep mental accounts, care about fairness and stick with defaults. Because these departures are systematic, policies and products can be designed around them, for good or ill.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains.
  • UK pension auto-enrolment (from 2012) lifted eligible private-sector participation from under half to above 85%.
  • Nudge units' typical effects are around a percentage point, much smaller than academic papers suggested.
The common mistake

Thinking that every clever nudge has a big effect. Defaults are powerful; most other nudges are modest, and many famous lab effects shrank when retested.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the same person buys both lottery tickets and home insurance, using prospect theory? If you can, you have understood reference points and probability weighting.
