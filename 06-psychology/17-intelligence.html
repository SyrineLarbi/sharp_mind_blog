Intelligence. The most reliable measure in psychology, and the most misused.

An IQ test takes about an hour. The number it produces predicts school results, job performance, income and even how long people live better than almost anything else psychologists measure. That is a remarkable fact, and an uncomfortable one. It has been used to help children who were falling behind, and to justify forced sterilisation. The honest picture sits between the two popular views: IQ is neither meaningless nor destiny. It measures something real and narrow, shaped by genes and environment together.

Question 1 · What is it?

A general ability to learn, reason and solve problems

Intelligence is hard to define and surprisingly easy to measure. The first test was built to help children, and its inventor warned against the way it was later used.

Chapter 01

A test built to help, not to rank

In 1904 the French government asked the psychologist Alfred Binet a practical question. Schools had just become compulsory for all children. How could teachers identify the ones who needed extra help, without relying on a teacher's impression? With his colleague Théodore Simon, Binet built a series of short tasks: naming objects, repeating numbers, defining words, spotting what was wrong in a picture. The tasks got harder step by step. A child's score showed which age group's tasks they could manage.

Binet was careful about what his test meant. He insisted that it did not measure a fixed quantity and that children's abilities could be improved by teaching. The first intelligence test was a tool for finding children to help, not a label for life. Within twenty years, in other countries, it was being used for almost the opposite purpose, as Chapter 10 explains.

A widely used modern definition came from a 1994 statement signed by 52 researchers: intelligence is a very general mental capability that involves the ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, understand complex ideas, learn quickly and learn from experience. Notice what is not on the list: knowledge of particular facts, artistic talent, kindness or good judgement. Those matter, but they are different things.

Chapter 02

What the number 100 means

"IQ" stands for intelligence quotient. Early tests divided a child's "mental age" by their real age and multiplied by 100. That stopped working for adults, so modern tests use a different method. Your score shows where you stand compared with a large sample of people your age.

Tests are built so that the average is 100 and the standard deviation (a measure of how spread out the scores are) is 15. About two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115. About 95 per cent score between 70 and 130. A score of 130 means you did better than about 98 people in 100 of your age. Because the scale is relative, tests must be re-normed every so often against a fresh sample, which led to one of the strangest discoveries in the field (Chapter 06).

Chapter 03

One intelligence, or many?

In 1904 the English psychologist Charles Spearman noticed something about schoolchildren's results. Children who did well in one subject tended to do well in others, even unrelated ones such as music and maths. Every test of mental ability correlated positively with every other. This pattern is called the positive manifold, and it has been found in almost every large set of mental tests since.

Spearman explained it with a single shared factor, which he called g for "general intelligence". Today most researchers use a layered model. At the top sits g. Below it sit broad abilities, the best known being fluid intelligence (solving new problems you have never seen) and crystallised intelligence (knowledge and skills you have built up, such as vocabulary). The idea came from Raymond Cattell and John Horn. Fluid intelligence peaks in early adulthood and slowly declines; crystallised intelligence keeps growing well into middle age.

A popular theory with weak support

In 1983 Howard Gardner proposed multiple intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and later naturalist. Teachers loved it. But most of these "intelligences" correlate with each other, which is what g predicts, and the theory has little support from testing. It is better read as a reminder that people have many different talents, not as a description of how the mind is organised.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Tests, genes, schools and the brain

What exactly is being measured, and where do the differences between people come from? The answers are more interesting than either side of the old nature-versus-nurture argument expects.

Chapter 04

Inside an IQ test

The most widely used adult test is the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, first published by David Wechsler in 1955 and revised several times since. It is given one-to-one by a trained psychologist and combines several kinds of task:

AreaTypical taskWhat it draws on
Verbal comprehensionDefine a word; explain how two things are alikeVocabulary, crystallised knowledge
Perceptual reasoningArrange blocks to copy a pattern; complete a visual puzzleFluid reasoning, spatial skill
Working memoryRepeat a list of numbers backwardsHolding and manipulating information
Processing speedMatch symbols to numbers as fast as possibleMental speed and accuracy

A shorter, word-free test is Raven's Progressive Matrices. You see a grid of patterns with one missing piece and choose the piece that completes the logic. Because it needs no language, it is often used to measure fluid intelligence. Even so, no test is fully "culture-free". Familiarity with tests, schooling and abstract puzzles all help, and they vary between societies.

Chapter 05

Genes: what heritability does and does not mean

Studies of twins and adopted children allow researchers to estimate how much of the variation in IQ within a population is linked to genetic differences. That estimate is called heritability. Identical twins share all their genes; non-identical twins share about half. If identical twins are more alike, genes are contributing.

The results are consistent and surprising. Heritability of IQ is low in infancy, around 40 per cent in childhood, and rises to roughly 60 to 80 per cent in adulthood in wealthy countries. Genes seem to matter more with age, probably because adults choose environments that suit their natural tendencies. A curious child reads more books, which makes them more knowledgeable, which makes them seek out still more.

Heritability is easily misread, so three warnings:

  • It describes a population, not a person. "70 per cent heritable" does not mean 70 per cent of your IQ came from your genes.
  • It depends on the environment. When everyone gets similar schooling and food, the remaining differences look more genetic. Some US studies found heritability was lower among poor families, where environmental differences were larger. Studies in other countries have not always found the same.
  • High heritability does not mean unchangeable. Height is highly heritable, and average height rose by several centimetres in many countries during the 20th century because of better food.

Researchers have now found thousands of small genetic variants linked to educational attainment and test scores. Each has a tiny effect. Combined into a "polygenic score", they predict only a modest share of the differences between individuals, far less than twin studies imply. That gap is an active research question (see Genetics).

Chapter 06

Environment: the scores that kept rising

In the 1980s the New Zealand political scientist James Flynn noticed something in test data from many countries. Each time a test was re-normed, people scored higher on the old version than the previous generation had. Raw scores had risen by around three IQ points per decade through much of the 20th century. This is the Flynn effect. Taken literally, an average person from 1920 would score around 70 against today's norms. That is clearly not because our grandparents were impaired.

Genes cannot change that fast, so the cause must be environmental. The leading explanations are better nutrition, smaller families, less disease, more and longer schooling, and a modern world full of abstract categories and hypothetical thinking. Flynn himself argued that people learned to "wear scientific spectacles", sorting things into classes rather than by practical use. The Flynn effect is the strongest single proof that intelligence test scores respond to environment.

Since about the 1990s the rise has stalled or reversed in some rich countries, including Norway, Denmark and Finland. A large Norwegian study of brothers found the decline happened within families, which again points to environmental causes rather than to who was having children. What those causes are is still debated.

Schooling has direct evidence too. A 2018 analysis combining many studies, by Stuart Ritchie and Elliot Tucker-Drob, estimated that each extra year of education raises IQ by roughly one to five points. Other clear environmental harms include iodine deficiency in pregnancy, lead exposure in childhood and severe early neglect.

Chapter 07

What a more intelligent brain looks like

There is no single "intelligence centre". Brain size correlates with IQ, but only weakly, and many people with smaller brains score higher than people with larger ones. Imaging studies point to a network linking the frontal lobes (planning, reasoning) and the parietal lobes (integrating information), and to how efficiently and quickly brain regions communicate. Researchers Rex Jung and Richard Haier summarised this in 2007 as the parieto-frontal integration theory. It remains a framework rather than a full explanation.

Brains also change with use. Learning a demanding skill reshapes the parts of the brain involved, a capacity called plasticity, covered in Brain plasticity. That does not mean any training raises general intelligence, as Chapter 12 shows.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

What the score predicts, what it misses, and how it was abused

IQ predicts many life outcomes, on average and with many exceptions. It misses some things people care about most. And its history is a warning.

Chapter 08

An hour-long test that predicts decades

On 1 June 1932, almost every 11-year-old in Scotland sat the same mental test. Decades later, researchers led by Ian Deary traced many of them. Children who had scored higher were, on average, more likely to be alive in old age. Part of the link runs through education, income and health behaviour. Part may reflect a generally healthier body and brain. Either way, a test taken at 11 carried information about life at 80.

Across many studies, IQ correlates with school grades at around 0.5, which is strong for psychology (a correlation runs from 0, no link, to 1, a perfect link). It also predicts years of education, income and job performance, especially in complex jobs. Older reviews put the link with job performance near 0.5; a 2022 reanalysis by Paul Sackett and colleagues argued it is lower, nearer 0.3, which is still useful. IQ is a good predictor of averages and a poor predictor of any single person. Plenty of people with modest scores do extremely well, and plenty of high scorers do not.

Chapter 09

Smart is not the same as wise

In the 1920s the American psychologist Lewis Terman began following about 1,500 California children with very high IQ scores. As adults they did well on average: good health, good jobs, many professionals. But none won a Nobel Prize. Two boys who were tested and did not make the cut, William Shockley and Luis Alvarez, later won the Nobel Prize in physics. The story is often told to show that, above a certain level, other things matter more.

Several of those other things are now well studied:

  • Rationality. The psychologist Keith Stanovich showed that people with high IQ are only slightly less prone to many thinking biases. Intelligence gives you processing power; it does not make you check your reasoning. Clever people are often better at defending conclusions they reached for bad reasons.
  • Conscientiousness, the personality trait of being organised and persistent, predicts school and work success independently of IQ.
  • Emotional and social skill matters for leadership and relationships. As a test score, "emotional intelligence" is less well established than IQ, but the skills it names are real.
  • Creativity overlaps with IQ only moderately (see Creativity).
Chapter 10

A dark history, and a still-heated debate

Early in the 20th century, testing was taken up by the eugenics movement, which aimed to "improve" populations by controlling who had children. In the United States, test results were used in arguments for the 1924 Immigration Act, which sharply restricted arrivals from Southern and Eastern Europe. In 1927 the US Supreme Court, in Buck v. Bell, allowed the forced sterilisation of a young woman labelled "feeble-minded". Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilised under such laws, and similar programmes ran in other countries. Nazi Germany took the ideas to their worst end.

The most contested question today is about differences in average scores between groups. Such gaps have been measured in some countries, including between racial groups in the United States. The scientific mainstream holds that there is no good evidence that these gaps are genetic, and that heritability within a group says nothing about the causes of differences between groups. Several gaps have narrowed over time, and the Flynn effect shows how large environmental effects can be. A small number of researchers dispute this, and the subject remains politically charged. What is not disputed is that a group average says nothing about any individual.

Chapter 12

Can you raise your own intelligence?

Around 2008 a study suggested that training working memory with a demanding game raised fluid intelligence. A brain-training industry followed. In 2016 the maker of the Lumosity app paid a settlement to the US Federal Trade Commission over claims it could not support. A large 2016 review led by Daniel Simons concluded that brain-training games make you better at the games, and at very similar tasks, but show little evidence of improving general intelligence or everyday performance. Practice transfers narrowly.

What does work is less exciting. Education raises IQ scores, as Chapter 06 showed. Good sleep, exercise and treating hearing loss support thinking in the short and long term. Learning real skills and knowledge builds crystallised intelligence for life. And, as Dweck's work suggests, believing that ability can grow makes people keep trying when things get hard, which is how they learn. The effect of mindset training in schools has turned out smaller than first claimed, but the basic logic holds.

Chapter 14

What being smart means when machines are smart too

AI has made one old point clearer. Scoring well on a test is not the same as understanding the world. A system can solve puzzles that stump most adults and still make mistakes no child would make. Human tests worked because, in humans, the abilities they sample tend to come together. In machines they do not. A test score is a sample of a mind, not a measurement of the whole thing.

For people, the balance of useful abilities may shift. When answers and calculations are cheap, value moves toward knowing which questions to ask, checking whether an answer is right, and judging what matters. Those draw on knowledge, rationality and character as much as on raw reasoning speed. The volume on AI covers the tools themselves (How to actually work with one).

The core idea

Intelligence is a general ability to reason, learn and solve problems. IQ tests measure it reliably as a position against people of your age. Scores reflect genes and environment together, predict averages well and individuals poorly, and leave out rationality, character and wisdom.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Average IQ is set at 100, with about 95% of people between 70 and 130.
  • The Flynn effect: scores rose about 3 points per decade in the 20th century.
  • Each extra year of school adds roughly 1–5 IQ points (2018 analysis).
The common mistake

Reading "highly heritable" as "fixed" or "decided at birth". Heritability describes variation in a population in a given environment, and scores still respond strongly to schooling, nutrition and time.

Test yourself

Can you explain how IQ can be highly heritable and yet have risen sharply in a few generations? If you can, you have understood heritability, the Flynn effect and the role of environment.

The words, in plain words
IQ
Intelligence quotient: a score showing how a person did on a test compared with others of the same age.
Standard deviation
A measure of spread. For IQ it is 15 points.
Positive manifold
The finding that people who do well on one mental test tend to do well on all the others.
g
General intelligence: the shared factor behind the positive manifold.
Fluid intelligence
The ability to solve new problems without relying on learned knowledge.
Crystallised intelligence
Knowledge and skills built up over time, such as vocabulary.
Heritability
The share of variation in a trait, within a population, that is linked to genetic differences.
Polygenic score
A combined estimate from thousands of small genetic variants, used to predict a trait.
Flynn effect
The rise in average test scores across generations during the 20th century.
Correlation
A number from 0 to 1 (or −1) showing how closely two measures move together.
Eugenics
A discredited movement to "improve" populations by controlling who could have children.
Transfer
Whether practice on one task improves performance on different tasks. For brain training, it is small.
