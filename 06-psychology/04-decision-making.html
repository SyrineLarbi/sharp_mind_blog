Sharp Mind · Psychology · Topic 04 of 20

Decision-making. People are not bad calculators. They are good guessers with predictable blind spots.

Economics long assumed that people choose by weighing every option and picking the best one. Psychology found that almost nobody does this, and that it is often impossible. Real decisions are made with too little time, too little information and a small working memory. So the mind uses shortcuts. Most of the time they work remarkably well. Sometimes they fail in ways so regular that you can predict them. This file is about both halves: the shortcuts, and when to distrust them.

Question 1 · What is it?

Choosing when you cannot be sure

A decision is a choice between options whose results you cannot fully know. The interesting part of decision-making is always the uncertainty.

Chapter 01

The economist's puzzle

In 1953 the French economist Maurice Allais put two choices to leading decision theorists. First: would you rather have one million francs for certain, or a gamble with a 10% chance of five million, an 89% chance of one million and a 1% chance of nothing? Most people, experts included, took the certain million.

Second: would you rather have an 11% chance of one million, or a 10% chance of five million? Here most people took the 10% chance of five million. The trouble is that, under the standard theory of rational choice, these two answers contradict each other. Remove the shared 89% chance of one million from both options in the first choice and you get exactly the second choice. A consistent chooser should pick the same side both times.

This is the Allais paradox. It showed early that people do not treat probabilities the way the mathematics says they should. In particular, the jump from "almost certain" to "certain" is worth far more to us than the formula allows. That gap between the theory and real people is what the psychology of decision-making studies.

Chapter 02

Risk is not the same as uncertainty

In 1921 the American economist Frank Knight drew a distinction that is still essential. Under risk you know the odds, as with dice or roulette. Under uncertainty you do not know the odds, and sometimes you do not even know all the possible outcomes, as with launching a product or choosing a career.

People treat these differently. In 1961 Daniel Ellsberg described a now-famous thought experiment. One urn has 50 red and 50 black balls. Another has 100 balls in an unknown mix of red and black. Asked to bet on red, most people prefer the first urn. Asked to bet on black, they still prefer the first urn. That cannot be consistent with any single belief about the second urn. People simply dislike not knowing the odds. This is called ambiguity aversion.

Most real decisions are made under uncertainty, not risk. That is why methods that work well for casinos and insurance can mislead in business and life, where the probabilities are guesses dressed up as numbers.

Chapter 03

Three questions about any decision

Researchers ask three different kinds of question, and it helps to keep them apart.

ApproachThe question it asksWho works on it
NormativeHow should an ideal reasoner decide?Mathematicians, economists, philosophers
DescriptiveHow do real people decide?Psychologists, behavioural economists
PrescriptiveHow can real people decide better, given their limits?Decision analysts, designers of policies and procedures

Much confusion comes from mixing them up. Showing that people break a normative rule does not by itself prove they are foolish. Sometimes the rule ignores real costs, such as time and effort. The useful question is the prescriptive one: given how minds actually work, what helps?

Question 2 · How does it work?

From the ideal calculator to the real mind

The story of decision science is a series of corrections to one beautiful idea: that people compute the value of each option and pick the highest.

Chapter 04

Expected value, and why it is not enough

The simplest rule is expected value: multiply each possible outcome by its probability, add them up, and choose the option with the highest total. A bet that pays 100 with a 50% chance has an expected value of 50. If it costs less than 50 to play, the rule says play.

In the eighteenth century a puzzle broke this rule. Imagine a game where a coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the prize doubles with every toss. The expected value of this game is infinite, yet almost nobody would pay more than a few coins to play. In 1738 the mathematician Daniel Bernoulli offered an answer. What matters is not money but how much the money is worth to you, which he called utility. A thousand euros means a great deal to someone with nothing and very little to a millionaire. This is diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit adds less value than the one before.

The resulting theory, expected utility, was given a formal basis in the 1940s by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern. It is still the standard model of rational choice in economics. The Allais and Ellsberg puzzles from Q1 are both cases where real people break it.

Chapter 05

Good enough: bounded rationality

In the 1950s Herbert Simon, who later won the Nobel Prize in economics, made a more basic objection. Real people cannot compute expected utility for most choices. They do not know all the options, cannot estimate all the probabilities and have no time to do the arithmetic. He called this bounded rationality: being as rational as possible within limits of information, time and mental capacity.

Simon argued that people satisfice, a word he built from "satisfy" and "suffice". They set a standard of "good enough", look at options one at a time, and take the first that meets the standard. You do not visit every flat in the city before renting one. You set your must-haves and sign for the first that fits. Satisficing is not lazy optimising. It is often the most sensible strategy when searching has a cost.

Chapter 06

Heuristics: rules of thumb, and their biases

In 1974 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a paper in the journal Science that changed the field. They argued that people judge probability using a small set of mental shortcuts, called heuristics, and that each shortcut produces its own typical errors, called biases. Three were central: judging by how much something resembles a typical case, judging by how easily examples come to mind, and starting from a number and adjusting too little. Volume 35 covers the last two in availability and anchoring.

The first, representativeness, produced one of the most famous examples in psychology. People read about "Linda", a 31-year-old who studied philosophy and cared deeply about social justice. Asked which was more likely, that Linda is a bank teller or that she is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement, most people chose the second. That is impossible: a group cannot be bigger than a group that contains it. But the second description sounds more like Linda. Plausibility feels like probability, and it is not.

Representativeness also explains base-rate neglect: ignoring how common something is in the first place. If a quiet, tidy man loves books, people guess "librarian" over "salesman", forgetting that salesmen vastly outnumber librarians. Volume 39 explains why base rates matter so much in How common is it in the first place?

Chapter 07

Prospect theory: how people really weigh risks

In 1979 Kahneman and Tversky published prospect theory, a description of how people actually choose between risky options. It has three main parts.

Reference points

People judge outcomes as gains or losses from where they stand now, not as final amounts of wealth. A salary of 60,000 feels good after 50,000 and bad after 70,000.

Loss aversion

Losses weigh more than equal gains, often estimated at about twice as much. Volume 35 covers this in Losses hurt more than gains please.

Distorted probabilities

People give too much weight to small chances and too little to large but uncertain ones. The step from 99% to 100% feels enormous. So does the step from 0% to 1%.

Together these produce a pattern that explains a lot of everyday behaviour. People buy lottery tickets, because a tiny chance of a huge gain feels bigger than it is. The same people buy insurance, because a tiny chance of a huge loss also feels bigger than it is. And people facing a sure loss often take wild gambles to avoid it, which is why traders who are losing sometimes double their bets. Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002, largely for this work. Tversky had died in 1996, and the prize is not awarded after death.

Chapter 08

The other camp: shortcuts that are smart

Not everyone accepted the "heuristics and biases" story. The German psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer argued that heuristics are often not errors at all, but tools fitted to their environment. He called this ecological rationality. In some of his studies, simple rules that use one or two clues predicted as well as complex statistical models, and sometimes better, because they did not get fooled by random noise in past data.

The American psychologist Gary Klein studied firefighters, nurses and military commanders making fast decisions under pressure. He found they rarely compared options. They recognised the situation as a type they knew, imagined the first option that came to mind playing out, and acted if it seemed to work. He called this the recognition-primed decision model. Volume 01 describes it in How experts actually decide.

The two camps came closer than their arguments suggested. In 2009 Kahneman and Klein wrote a joint paper on when intuition can be trusted. Their answer: intuition is reliable when the environment is regular enough to learn from and the person has had long practice with quick feedback. Chapter 13 returns to this.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Shops, courts and boardrooms

Once you know how people actually decide, you can design the setting of the decision. Companies and governments already do.

Chapter 09

More choice, worse choices?

In a well-known study published in 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a jam-tasting stand in a California supermarket. Sometimes it offered 24 flavours, sometimes 6. The big display attracted more visitors. But far more of the people who saw the small display bought a jar, roughly 30% against 3%. The study launched the idea of choice overload: beyond some point, more options make people less likely to choose and less happy with what they choose.

The story has since become more complicated. A 2010 review that combined many studies found the average effect close to zero, with large differences between studies. Later work suggests choice overload is real under particular conditions: when options are hard to compare, when people do not know what they want, and when the decision is complex. Choice overload is not a law, it is a risk, and good designers reduce it by sorting, filtering and offering sensible starting points.

The psychologist Barry Schwartz added a related idea. Some people are maximisers, who must find the best option. Others are satisficers, in Simon's sense. In his studies, maximisers often made objectively better choices but felt less satisfied with them.

Chapter 10

The power of the default

When people are unsure or busy, they tend to stay with whatever happens if they do nothing. This pre-set option is the default. A 2003 study by Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared European countries. Where people had to opt in to be organ donors, registration rates were low. Where they had to opt out, rates were very high. People's values were not so different between neighbouring countries. The form was.

Defaults work for several reasons at once: doing nothing is easy, the default looks like a recommendation, and changing it can feel like a loss. Many pension systems now enrol workers automatically, and participation rises sharply. Whoever sets the default makes the decision for most people. How governments use this, and the debate about whether they should, is in Behavioral economics.

Chapter 11

Noise, and judging a decision by its result

Biases push judgements in one direction. Noise is something different: random scatter. Give the same case to different judges, doctors or insurance underwriters, and they often reach very different conclusions. Give the same case to the same person on different days, and they may disagree with themselves. Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein argued in their 2021 book Noise that this variation is large in many professions and rarely measured.

A popular example claimed that judges granted parole far less often just before lunch than just after. Other researchers pointed out that the order of cases was not random, which could explain the pattern. The finding is still contested, and it is a good reminder to treat striking single studies carefully.

A second common error is judging a decision by its outcome. A good decision can turn out badly through bad luck, and a reckless one can turn out well. The poker player Annie Duke calls this mistake resulting. Volume 07 summarises her book in Thinking in Bets. Judge a decision by what was known and reasonable at the time, not by what happened next.

Decision hygiene

Simple habits reduce both bias and noise. Write down your reasoning and your prediction before you know the result. Ask "how common is this?" before judging a single case. Get independent opinions before group discussion, so the first speaker does not anchor everyone. Before a big commitment, run a premortem, an exercise Gary Klein proposed: imagine it is a year later and the plan has failed, then list the reasons why.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Fast and frugal, or quick and wrong

The same shortcut can be wise in one setting and disastrous in another. The skill is knowing which setting you are in.

Chapter 13

When to trust intuition

Kahneman and Klein's 2009 answer gives a practical test. Ask two questions. Is the environment regular, so that the same cues usually lead to the same outcomes? And has this person had years of practice with quick, honest feedback? If both answers are yes, as for an experienced nurse spotting a patient in trouble or a chess master, trust the gut. If either is no, as for stock picking or long-range political forecasting, treat the gut feeling as one opinion among several.

Confidence is not a test. People feel just as sure in irregular environments as in regular ones. The feeling is produced by how coherent the story seems, not by how often such stories turn out true.

Chapter 15

Who designs the choice?

More and more decisions are made inside screens: what to watch, what to buy, which route to drive, which news to read. Each screen has a designer who chooses the order of options, the default and what is shown first. Recommendation systems learn what keeps people clicking, which is not always what people would choose if they thought about it.

The likely future is a tug of war between two uses of decision science. One uses it to help people choose what they would want on reflection. The other uses it to steer them toward what benefits the designer. Both use the same knowledge. Knowing how your own decisions are shaped is the best defence against having them shaped for you.

The core idea

People decide with bounded rationality: limited time, information and attention. They use heuristics that are usually good and sometimes systematically wrong. They weigh outcomes from a reference point, fear losses more than they value gains, and distort small probabilities. The setting of a decision, including the default, shapes it heavily.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains.
  • In the jam study, about 30% versus 3% bought from the small and large displays, though later reviews found the effect inconsistent.
  • Prospect theory dates from 1979. Kahneman's Nobel came in 2002.
The common mistake

Judging a decision by its outcome. Good decisions can end badly and bad ones can end well. Judge the process and the information available at the time.

Test yourself

Why can the same person rationally buy both a lottery ticket and insurance, according to prospect theory? If you can explain it, you understand probability weighting.

The words, in plain words
Expected value
Each outcome times its probability, added up. The average result if you could repeat the choice many times.
Utility
How much an outcome is actually worth to the person, as opposed to its money value.
Risk
A situation where the odds are known.
Uncertainty
A situation where the odds, or even the possible outcomes, are unknown.
Ambiguity aversion
Preferring known odds to unknown ones, even when the unknown ones could be better.
Bounded rationality
Being as rational as you can within real limits of time, knowledge and mind.
Satisficing
Choosing the first option that is good enough rather than searching for the best.
Heuristic
A mental rule of thumb that gives a fast answer.
Base-rate neglect
Ignoring how common something is in general when judging a single case.
Prospect theory
Kahneman and Tversky's description of how people really choose between risky options.
Choice overload
When having too many options makes choosing harder or less satisfying.
Default
What happens if you do nothing.
Noise
Random variation in judgements that should be the same.
