Personality. Not a type you are sorted into, but a set of dials that are set differently in everyone.

People love to sort each other into types: introverts and extroverts, leaders and followers, four letters from a workplace test. The science of personality says that almost nobody is a type. Most people sit somewhere in the middle of a handful of broad dimensions, and their positions on those dimensions are real, fairly stable and surprisingly useful for predicting how lives turn out. They are also less fixed than people assume. Personality is your typical way of thinking, feeling and acting, and "typical" is the key word.

Question 1 · What is it?

Your typical pattern, across time and places

Personality is the set of relatively lasting patterns in how a person thinks, feels and behaves, and the ways those patterns differ from other people's.

Chapter 01

Eighteen thousand words for people

In 1936 the American psychologists Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert went through a large English dictionary and pulled out every word that could describe how a person behaves or what they are like. They found close to eighteen thousand. Words like "kind", "nervous", "bold", "lazy", "talkative", "stubborn".

Their reasoning became known as the lexical hypothesis. If a difference between people matters in daily life, languages will develop words for it. So the dictionary is a map of the ways people have found it useful to describe each other. The hard part came next. Many of those words overlap. "Talkative", "outgoing" and "sociable" go together; people described by one are usually described by the others. Over the following decades, researchers used statistics to find which words cluster, hoping to reduce thousands of words to a few basic dimensions. The modern science of personality was built by asking what the dictionary already knew.

Chapter 02

Traits, states and types

TypeA category you either belong to or do not"She's an introvert", as if it were yes or no

Research strongly favours traits over types. If you measure a trait such as sociability in thousands of people, you do not find two groups with a gap between them. You find a bell curve: most people in the middle, fewer at the extremes. Calling someone an "introvert" when they score just below the middle is like calling a person of average height "short". Most people are ambiverts on most traits, somewhere in between, and this matters when tests sort them into boxes, as Chapter 10 explains.

Chapter 03

Person or situation?

In 1968 the psychologist Walter Mischel, later famous for the marshmallow test, published a book that shook the field. He reviewed studies showing that a person's behaviour in one situation predicts their behaviour in another quite weakly. A child honest in a classroom test might cheat in a game. If traits were strong, Mischel asked, why was behaviour so inconsistent? The argument, called the person-situation debate, went on for two decades.

It ended in a sensible compromise. Single actions are hard to predict, because situations push everyone around. But averages across many situations are highly predictable. You cannot say whether a talkative person will speak at one meeting, but you can say with confidence that they will speak more than most people over a year of meetings. Mischel himself later argued that personality shows up as stable if-then patterns: "if criticised, she gets defensive; if praised, she gets quiet". Both the person and the situation matter, and they interact.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Five dimensions, partly inherited, slowly changing

The main model of personality has five broad traits. Twin studies show where they come from, and long-term studies show how they change.

Chapter 04

The Big Five

From the 1960s to the 1990s, researchers working with word lists and questionnaires in different countries kept finding the same five broad clusters. Important names include Ernest Tupes and Raymond Christal, who found them in data from the US Air Force, Lewis Goldberg, and Paul Costa and Robert McCrae, who built a widely used questionnaire. The result is the Big Five, often remembered with the letters OCEAN.

Two points avoid common confusion. First, neither end of a trait is simply good. Low agreeableness can make a good negotiator; high openness can make someone restless. Second, each broad trait contains narrower parts. Extraversion includes both sociability and assertiveness, and a person can be high on one and low on the other.

Chapter 05

A sixth dimension: honesty

When the psychologists Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee repeated the dictionary method across many languages in the early 2000s, they often found a sixth factor. They called it honesty-humility: sincerity, fairness, lack of greed and modesty. Their six-factor model is called HEXACO.

Low honesty-humility is closely related to the three "dark" traits described in Volume 37 (Three traits that travel together): manipulativeness, grandiosity and coldness. The Big Five can describe these people only awkwardly, as low in agreeableness. HEXACO gives them a dimension of their own. Researchers still debate whether five or six factors is the better summary, but both models agree on the big picture: a small number of broad, measurable dimensions.

Chapter 06

Nature, nurture, and the surprise in the middle

The best tool for separating genes from environment is comparing twins. Identical twins share all their genes; fraternal twins share about half, like ordinary brothers and sisters. If identical twins are more alike in a trait than fraternal twins, genes probably play a part. The strongest version of this study follows twins who were separated early and raised in different families, as in the Minnesota study of twins reared apart led by Thomas Bouchard from 1979.

Across many such studies, roughly 40 to 50 per cent of the differences between people in the Big Five traits are linked to genetic differences. This figure is called heritability. It describes differences within a group, not how much of one person's personality comes from their genes.

The surprise is about the rest. You might expect that growing up in the same family, with the same parents, house and rules, would make siblings alike. For adult personality, that shared environment turns out to matter very little. Adopted children raised together end up barely more alike in personality than strangers. Most of the non-genetic part comes from experiences siblings do not share: different friends, different teachers, different events, and plain chance. This finding is well replicated, though it applies to personality traits, not to everything families pass on, such as language, religion or values.

Chapter 07

Before personality: temperament

Babies differ from birth. Some are calm and easy to soothe, some cry at every change, some reach eagerly for new toys and others pull back. Psychologists call these early, biologically based differences temperament. In the 1950s Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess began a long-term study in New York that described "easy", "difficult" and "slow-to-warm-up" babies.

The Harvard psychologist Jerome Kagan studied infants who reacted strongly to unfamiliar sights and sounds. He found that many became shy, cautious children, but by no means all. Parenting and experience changed the path for many of them. Temperament is a starting point, not a destiny. It is the raw material that later becomes adult personality, shaped by thousands of experiences along the way. Childhood development has its own topic in Childhood development.

Chapter 08

Does personality change?

The answer is yes, slowly, and in a fairly predictable direction. Large studies that follow people for years, summarised in reviews such as a 2006 analysis led by Brent Roberts, find that on average people become more conscientious, more agreeable and less neurotic between their late teens and middle age. Researchers call this the maturity principle. Taking on adult roles, such as a job, a partner or children, seems to push people in that direction.

At the same time, people's position relative to others becomes more stable with age. A twenty-year-old's ranking can still move a lot; a fifty-year-old's moves less. Studies of therapy also suggest that treatment for anxiety or depression is often followed by lasting drops in neuroticism. Personality is stable enough to predict, and flexible enough to change. Some studies find that people who want to change a trait and practise the behaviour can shift it a little, though this research is newer and effects are modest.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Work, health, love, and a lot of bad tests

Personality traits predict real outcomes. That makes them valuable, and it makes a large industry of unreliable tests worth knowing about.

Chapter 09

What traits predict

Conscientiousness is the trait with the widest reach. Large reviews since the 1990s, starting with work by Murray Barrick and Michael Mount in 1991, found that it predicts job performance across almost every kind of job. The effect is modest for any one person, but consistent. It also predicts school results beyond what intelligence explains, and better health habits.

The most striking evidence is about life and death. The Terman study began following clever Californian children in the 1920s. Decades later, researchers including Howard Friedman found that children rated as conscientious tended to live longer. A 2007 review led by Brent Roberts concluded that personality traits predict death, divorce and job success about as strongly as social class and intelligence do.

Other traits matter too. High neuroticism is one of the strongest risk factors for anxiety and depression. Agreeableness and low neuroticism tend to go with more stable relationships. Extraversion goes with more frequent positive emotions. None of these effects is large enough to decide a single person's fate. Across millions of people, they add up.

Chapter 10

The four-letter problem

The most popular personality test in the world is probably the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI). It was developed by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers from the 1940s, based on the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung's 1921 theory of psychological types. It sorts people into 16 types, each described by four letters, such as INTJ or ESFP. It is widely used in companies and coaching.

Most personality researchers are critical of it, for three main reasons. First, it sorts people into types when the traits are actually continuous, so someone just over a line is labelled the opposite of someone just under it. Second, many people get a different type when they take the test again weeks later, because they were near a line. Third, it leaves out emotional stability, one of the traits that predicts most. Its four scales overlap partly with four of the Big Five, which is why it can feel accurate.

There is another reason any test can feel accurate. In 1949 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave his students what he said was a personal analysis. It was the same text for everyone, partly taken from horoscopes. On average students rated it as a very good description of themselves. This is the Forer effect, also called the Barnum effect: vague, flattering descriptions feel personal even when they fit almost anyone.

Chapter 11

Personality in hiring and politics

Many employers use personality questionnaires in hiring. Good ones, based on the Big Five or similar models, add some real information, especially about conscientiousness. They have clear weaknesses: applicants can present themselves as they think the employer wants, and measuring traits that are not related to the job can be unfair. Rules on what can be tested, and how, differ between countries.

Personality also became a political story. In 2013 researchers including Michal Kosinski, David Stillwell and Thore Graepel showed that Facebook "likes" could predict people's Big Five scores reasonably well. In 2018 it emerged that the consulting firm Cambridge Analytica had obtained data from millions of Facebook users and claimed to target political advertising by personality. How much that targeting actually changed anyone's vote is disputed, and many researchers think the firm's claims were exaggerated. The scandal still led to large fines and wider debate about data protection.

Chapter 12

Is the Big Five universal?

Most personality research has been done with university students in a few rich Western countries. In 2010 the anthropologist Joseph Henrich and colleagues called such samples WEIRD: Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic. They argued that these people are unusual compared with most of humanity, so findings based only on them may not travel.

The Big Five has been found in questionnaire studies in dozens of countries, which is a real strength. But a 2013 study led by Michael Gurven among the Tsimane, a group of farmers and foragers in Bolivia, found that the usual five factors did not separate cleanly. The researchers suggested that in small societies with fewer social roles, traits may cluster differently. The five dimensions are a good description of personality in many societies, but perhaps not a law of human nature.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A useful summary, a dangerous label

Trait models are among psychology's most successful tools. They are also easy to misuse, by others and by ourselves.

There is also a trade-off inside each trait. Extraversion brings more social rewards and positive mood, and also more risk-taking. Neuroticism brings distress, and also alertness to danger. Agreeableness makes cooperation easy, and can make it hard to say no. From an evolutionary point of view this may be why the variation exists: no single setting of the dials works best in every environment, so a population with a range of settings copes with more kinds of challenge.

Chapter 15

Machines with personalities

A newer question is what happens when software has something like a personality. AI chatbots are designed with a consistent tone: friendly, patient, cautious or playful. Researchers have given personality questionnaires to language models and found that their answers can look stable, and can be shifted by how the system is set up. Whether this counts as "personality" in any deep sense is doubtful, since the answers are produced by patterns in text rather than by a lasting inner nature.

For humans, the practical points from this file are more useful. Treat traits as tendencies, not verdicts, in yourself and others. Prefer measures built on continuous scales over type tests. And remember that the situation often explains more about a single action than character does. Personality tells you what someone usually does. It never tells you what they must do.

The core idea

Personality is a person's typical pattern of thinking, feeling and acting. It is best described by continuous traits, mainly the Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism), not by types. Traits are partly inherited, shaped mostly by experiences siblings do not share, fairly stable but changeable, and they predict real life outcomes on average.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 18,000 person-describing words in Allport and Odbert's 1936 count.
  • Roughly 40–50% of differences in Big Five traits are linked to genetic differences.
  • Five broad traits in the main model; six in HEXACO, which adds honesty-humility.
The common mistake

Treating a four-letter type as who you are. Most people are in the middle on most traits, and type tests often give a different answer the second time.

Test yourself

Explain how a single behaviour can be hard to predict while averages over a year are easy to predict. If you can, you understand how the person-situation debate was settled.
