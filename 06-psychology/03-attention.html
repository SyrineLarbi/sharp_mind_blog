Sharp Mind · Psychology · Topic 03 of 20

Attention. You do not see the world. You see the small part of it you selected.

At every moment your senses take in far more than your mind can handle. Attention is the process that decides what gets through. It works so smoothly that you never notice what it throws away, so you assume you see everything in front of you. Experiments show that people can miss a gorilla walking through a scene they are watching closely. Understanding attention explains why multitasking fails, why phones are so hard to put down, and why focus has become one of the most valuable things a person can own.

Question 1 · What is it?

A selector, not a camera

Attention is the mind's way of choosing a small amount of information to process deeply while ignoring almost everything else. The choosing is the point.

Chapter 01

The gorilla nobody saw

In 1999 the psychologists Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris made a short video. Two teams, one in white shirts and one in black, pass basketballs to each other. Viewers were asked to count the passes made by the white team. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walks into the middle of the scene, faces the camera, beats their chest and walks off. It is on screen for several seconds.

About half of the viewers did not see it. When shown the video again, many refused to believe it was the same tape. This is inattentional blindness: failing to notice something in plain view because your attention is busy with something else. The viewers' eyes were working perfectly. The gorilla reached their eyes, and it never reached their awareness.

The lesson is not that people are careless. It is that seeing needs attention as well as eyes. What you are not attending to is, for most purposes, not there for you.

Chapter 02

Three jobs that share one word

The American psychologist William James wrote in 1890 that "everyone knows what attention is". More than a century of research has shown that it is several abilities at once. The psychologist Michael Posner proposed one of the most influential maps, with three networks in the brain.

NetworkWhat it doesEveryday example
AlertingGets you ready to respond and keeps you awake to what comes nextSitting up straight when the teacher says "this will be on the exam"
OrientingPoints your attention at a place or a thingTurning toward a sudden noise, or scanning a crowd for a friend
Executive controlChooses between competing demands and blocks distractionsReading a report while colleagues chat next to you

Psychologists also describe attention by what it is asked to do. Selective attention picks one source over others. Sustained attention stays on one thing over time. Divided attention tries to cover several things at once, and, as Chapter 07 shows, it mostly cannot.

Chapter 03

The cocktail party

You are at a noisy party, listening to one person. Dozens of other voices reach your ears, and you understand none of them. Then someone across the room says your name, and you hear it at once. In the 1950s the British researcher Colin Cherry studied this "cocktail party problem". He played two different messages, one into each ear, and asked people to repeat one of them aloud. They could do it, and they could say almost nothing about the other message. They often did not notice if it switched language.

Yet the ignored message was not fully ignored. Later experiments by Neville Moray found that people's own names in the unattended ear often broke through. The mind monitors far more than it reports, and it brings things into awareness only when they seem to matter.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A bottleneck, a spotlight, and a limited budget

Attention exists because the brain cannot process everything. How it chooses, and what happens to the rest, explains most of its strengths and failures.

Chapter 04

The bottleneck: where the filtering happens

In 1958 the British psychologist Donald Broadbent proposed that the mind has a filter. Information arrives through many channels, but only one passes the filter to be understood. The rest is dropped early, based on simple physical features such as which ear it came from or what the voice sounded like.

The name-breaking-through finding showed this was too strict. Anne Treisman suggested that the filter turns unattended information down, like a volume knob, instead of switching it off. Weak signals still get through if they are important enough, such as your name or the word "fire". Other researchers argued that much is processed for meaning before the selection happens. The debate about exactly where the bottleneck sits ran for decades. The modern answer is that it depends on how busy you are. When a task is very demanding, little else gets through. When it is easy, spare capacity spills over and distractions leak in.

Chapter 05

Pushed and pulled: two ways attention moves

Your attention moves in two ways. Top-down attention is driven by your goals: you are looking for your red suitcase, so red things stand out. Bottom-up attention is driven by the world: a flash, a sudden movement or a loud bang grabs you whether you want it to or not. The two compete all the time.

Anne Treisman showed something else in 1980. If you look for a red letter among green ones, it "pops out" instantly however many letters there are. If you look for a red X among red Os and green Xs, you have to check items one by one, and it takes longer the more items there are. Her feature integration theory said simple features are picked up across the whole scene at once, but combining them into one object needs focused attention, one place at a time. Researchers often describe this focused attention as a spotlight that moves across the scene and lights up what it lands on.

Designers use this every day. A single red notification badge on a grey screen is built to pop out. Bottom-up capture is fast, automatic and very hard to resist, which makes it useful for warnings and powerful for anyone who wants your attention for their own reasons.

Chapter 06

What you miss when nothing seems to change

Inattentional blindness has a cousin called change blindness. In experiments, people look at a photo that flickers briefly and comes back slightly changed: a building vanishes, a person's coat changes colour. Many people stare for a long time without spotting the change. In one well-known study by Simons and Daniel Levin, an experimenter asking a stranger for directions was swapped for a different person during a brief interruption. Many strangers carried on talking without noticing.

These findings show that the brain does not store a detailed picture of the scene. It keeps a rough sketch and assumes the rest is still there. The feeling that you see everything in detail is an illusion created by the fact that wherever you look, detail appears.

Chapter 07

Multitasking is really switching

For tasks that need thought, people do not do two things at once. They switch quickly between them. Every switch has a cost. The mind has to drop the rules of one task and load the rules of the other, and for a moment it does neither well. Psychologists call this the switch cost. A single switch costs only a fraction of a second, but switching dozens of times an hour adds up, and error rates rise.

There are exceptions. If one task is fully automatic, such as walking, you can do it while talking. But the exceptions are narrower than people think. Studies by the psychologist David Strayer and colleagues found that drivers talking on a phone, even a hands-free one, reacted more slowly and missed more of what was around them. The problem is not the hands. It is the attention.

After a switch, part of your mind also stays on the old task. Volume 09 calls this attention residue. It is why a two-minute check of email can damage the next twenty minutes of thinking.

Chapter 08

Sustained attention, and the wandering mind

During the Second World War, radar operators watching screens for enemy submarines missed more signals as their shifts went on. In 1948 the British psychologist Norman Mackworth tested this with a clock whose hand occasionally jumped twice the normal distance. Watchers missed more and more of these jumps, with detection falling noticeably within the first half hour. The finding, called the vigilance decrement, holds for airport baggage screeners, lifeguards and anyone watching for rare events.

Even when nothing is demanding, attention drifts. In a 2010 study, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used a phone app to ask thousands of people at random moments what they were doing and what they were thinking about. Their minds were wandering from the task in almost half of the samples. People were also, on average, less happy when their minds wandered. A wandering mind is the brain's default, not a failure of character. Mind wandering is not all bad, either: it is when people plan, remember and sometimes solve problems they had put aside.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Safety, money, health, and the quality of a life

Whatever you attend to becomes your experience. That makes attention a matter of road safety, a business model, a medical question and a personal one.

Chapter 09

Looked, but did not see

Police reports of road crashes often contain the phrase "looked but did not see". A driver checks a junction, a motorcyclist is right there, and the driver pulls out. The driver is usually not lying. Their eyes pointed at the motorcycle. Their attention was searching for cars, so a smaller, less expected shape did not register.

Many countries have banned holding a phone while driving. The research in Chapter 07 suggests that hands-free calls are not much safer. Pilots, surgeons and nuclear plant operators all train with checklists partly for this reason: a checklist moves important checks out of fragile attention and onto paper. Where a missed signal could kill, good systems do not rely on people noticing.

Chapter 10

The attention economy

"A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

Herbert Simon · Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World · 1971

Simon saw early that when information becomes cheap, attention becomes the scarce resource. Today many of the world's largest companies earn most of their money from advertising, which means selling people's attention. Their products are designed to win it and keep it.

The tools are the ones in this file and in the Learning topic. Red badges and sounds use bottom-up capture. Feeds that refresh with something new, sometimes, use variable rewards. Autoplay removes the natural moment when you might stop. None of this is secret or illegal. But it means that when you try to focus near a phone, you are competing against teams of professionals whose job is to break that focus. Simply keeping a phone out of sight and out of reach is one of the easiest defences, though the evidence on how much a nearby phone alone distracts is mixed.

Chapter 11

ADHD: when regulating attention is hard

Health note

This section is general education, not medical advice. Diagnosis needs a qualified professional.

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a condition, usually present from childhood, in which people have persistent trouble regulating attention, activity or impulses, to a degree that causes real problems at school, at work or at home. Widely cited estimates put it at around 5 per cent of children worldwide, with lower rates in adults, and diagnosis rates vary a lot between countries.

The name misleads. People with ADHD do not simply have "less" attention. Many can focus intensely on things that interest them, sometimes for hours. The difficulty is in directing attention where it is needed and keeping it there when the task is dull. That is why it is best understood as a problem of executive control, the third network in Chapter 02. Treatments, including stimulant medication and behavioural approaches, are well studied. The rise in adult diagnoses since the 2010s is debated: some see better recognition, others see overdiagnosis, and both may be true in different places.

Chapter 12

Are attention spans shrinking?

A claim spread widely in the 2010s that the human attention span had fallen to eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish's. Researchers who tried to trace the number could not find a study behind it. "Attention span" is not even a single measurable thing: the same child who cannot sit through a lesson may play a game for three hours.

The honest picture is more modest. What has clearly changed is the environment. People switch between screens and apps very often, and studies of office workers find frequent switching and interruptions. Whether this changes the underlying ability to concentrate, or just how we choose to spend it, is not settled. There is better evidence that our habits of attention have changed than that our capacity for it has. Habits, unlike capacities, can be changed back.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Narrow by design

Every weakness of attention is the other side of a strength. A mind that took everything in would understand nothing.

Chapter 13

What selective attention buys, and what it costs

FeatureAdvantageDisadvantage
Narrow focusDeep processing of what matters. You can read, solve and build.Inattentional blindness: unexpected things in plain view are missed.
Automatic captureDanger, movement and your name get through even when you are busy.Anyone who designs flashing, noisy or novel signals can pull you away.
A rough sketch of the sceneSaves effort. The brain does not waste resources storing what it can look at again.Change blindness, and overconfidence in what you think you saw.
Fast switchingYou can keep several tasks going over a day.Each switch costs time and accuracy, and leaves residue behind.
Mind wanderingPlanning, remembering and incubating ideas happen in the background.Lost information, errors in dull tasks, and lower mood on average.

The pattern across the table is clear. Attention is a trade between depth and breadth, and the brain chooses depth. Most of the time this is the right choice. It fails in a predictable set of situations: rare events, unexpected objects, long watches and environments designed to distract. Good systems design around those situations instead of asking people to try harder.

One practical consequence: you cannot notice your own inattentional blindness. By definition you do not know what you missed. That is why a second pair of eyes, a checklist, or a colleague who reads your work fresh catches things you could stare at for an hour.

Chapter 15

Attention as the skill that remains

As machines take over more routine thinking, the parts left to humans tend to be the ones that need sustained, deliberate attention: judging, reviewing, deciding what matters. Ironically, one of the hardest jobs is watching an automated system that is almost always right, which is the vigilance problem from Chapter 08. Supervisors of self-driving test cars and of automated factory lines face the same challenge as Mackworth's radar watchers.

The likely result is that the ability to choose what to attend to, and to stay with it, becomes rarer and more valuable. It is not a fixed gift. It depends heavily on environment and habits: fewer interruptions, single-tasking on demanding work, and deliberate breaks. A person who controls their attention controls, in a very real sense, what their life is made of.

The core idea

Attention selects a small part of incoming information for deep processing and lets the rest go. It is moved by goals (top-down) and by striking events (bottom-up). It cannot really be divided between demanding tasks, it fades over long watches, and it drifts by default.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About half of viewers missed the gorilla (Simons and Chabris, 1999).
  • Minds wandered in almost half of sampled waking moments (Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010).
  • Vigilance drops noticeably within about 30 minutes of watching for rare signals.
The common mistake

Believing you can multitask demanding work. You are switching, not multitasking, and each switch costs time, accuracy and focus on what comes next.
