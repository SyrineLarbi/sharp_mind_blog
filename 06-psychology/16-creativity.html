Sharp Mind · Psychology · Topic 16 of 20

Creativity. Not a lightning bolt, but old pieces joined in a new way that works.

Most people believe creativity is a gift. A few people have it, the rest of us watch them. The research tells a less romantic and more useful story. New ideas come from knowledge you already hold, recombined under the right conditions, and then filtered hard. The famous moments of sudden insight are real, but they arrive at the end of long preparation, not instead of it. And the people we call geniuses are mostly the people who produced the most, flops included.

Question 1 · What is it?

New, and also good for something

Creativity has a surprisingly precise definition. It has two parts, and most confusion comes from forgetting the second one.

Chapter 01

Two tests every creative idea must pass

Imagine two people asked to design a new chair. The first draws a chair with seven legs and a seat made of glass spikes. It is certainly new. Nobody would sit on it. The second draws an ordinary chair, well made, exactly like ten thousand others. It is useful, and nobody would call it creative. Neither person has done what we mean by the word.

Psychologists settled on a two-part definition decades ago, and it is still the standard one. A creative idea is both original and effective. Original means new, unusual, not the obvious answer. Effective means it works for some purpose: it solves the problem, moves an audience, or fits the situation.

The second test is the one people forget. It explains why creativity needs knowledge. To know whether an idea is new, you need to know what already exists. To know whether it works, you need to understand the problem.

Chapter 02

From a child's drawing to the theory of relativity

The word "creativity" covers everything from a toddler inventing a game to Einstein rethinking time. That is too wide for one category, so the psychologists James Kaufman and Ronald Beghetto proposed four levels, now called the Four C model.

LevelWhat it meansExample
mini-cAn insight that is new to you, even if the world already knows itA student who suddenly sees why fractions work
little-cEveryday creativity that other people noticeA clever fix for a leaking tap, a new family recipe, a funny speech
Pro-cProfessional-level creativity in a field, after years of trainingAn architect's distinctive building, a working scientist's new method
Big-CWork that changes a field and is remembered for generationsDarwin's theory of evolution, Beethoven's symphonies

The model matters because it removes a false choice. You do not have to be a genius to be creative, and you do not have to pretend that a good recipe is the same as a new branch of physics. The same basic process runs at every level. What changes is how much knowledge feeds it and how many people judge the result.

Chapter 03

Opening out and closing down

In 1950 the psychologist J. P. Guilford used his presidential address to the American Psychological Association to complain that his field was ignoring creativity. He then gave it a working vocabulary. He separated two kinds of thinking.

Divergent thinking means producing many possible answers from one starting point. The classic test is the Alternative Uses Task: list all the uses you can think of for a brick. People score points for how many ideas they produce (fluency), how many different categories they cover (flexibility), and how rare their answers are (originality). "Build a wall" is common. "Grind it into red pigment" is rare.

Convergent thinking means narrowing down to the single best answer. Here the classic test is the Remote Associates Test, devised by Sarnoff Mednick in the 1960s. You see three words, such as "cottage", "Swiss" and "cake", and you look for one word linked to all three. (The answer is "cheese".)

The important point is that real creativity needs both, one after the other. Divergent thinking alone gives you a pile of strange ideas. Convergent thinking alone gives you the obvious answer. The skill is to open wide, then close firmly, and to know which phase you are in.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Preparation, a pause, a spark, and a lot of checking

The process behind new ideas was described almost a century ago, and modern research has mostly confirmed it. The surprise is how much of it happens when you are not trying.

Chapter 04

The mathematician on the bus

The French mathematician Henri Poincaré spent weeks struggling with a problem about a class of mathematical functions. He made no progress. Then he left on a geological trip and forgot about work. As he put his foot on the step of a bus, the solution came to him, complete, with a feeling of certainty. He checked it later, and it was correct.

In 1926 the English social psychologist Graham Wallas used stories like Poincaré's to describe four stages of creative thought. The labels are still in use.

Wallas's four stages, with what each one needs
 1. PREPARATION    work hard on the problem, gather knowledge, fail
        |          needs: effort, expertise, a clear question
        v
 2. INCUBATION     stop working on it consciously; do something else
        |          needs: time, a break, a less demanding task
        v
 3. ILLUMINATION   the idea arrives, often suddenly ("aha!")
        |          needs: nothing you can force
        v
 4. VERIFICATION   test it, refine it, prove it, or throw it away
                   needs: critical judgement, more effort

Notice where the famous moment sits. Illumination is stage three, not stage one. Poincaré's idea on the bus came after weeks of hard work, and it was followed by more hard work to check it. People who hope for the spark without the preparation are hoping to skip the part that makes the spark possible.

Chapter 05

Why stepping away helps

Incubation sounds like an excuse for a walk, but it has been tested. In experiments, people work on a creative problem, take a break, and then return. Compared with people who work straight through, those who take a break often do better. A 2009 review that combined many such studies, by Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod, found a real incubation effect. It was strongest for divergent tasks and when the break was filled with an easy activity rather than a demanding one.

Why would a break help? There are two main explanations, and both probably play a part. The first is fixation. When you work on a problem, you often get stuck on a wrong approach, and every new attempt follows the same groove. A break lets that groove fade, so you come back fresh. The second is spreading activation: while you do something else, related ideas in memory stay slightly active and can link up without your direction.

Fixation has a famous form called functional fixedness, the tendency to see an object only in its usual role. In the candle problem, first described by Karl Duncker in the 1930s and 1940s, people get a candle, a box of drawing pins and matches. They must fix the candle to a wall so it burns without dripping on the table. The solution is to empty the box, pin it to the wall and use it as a shelf. Many people miss it, because the box is "a container for pins", not "a shelf". When the pins are placed beside the empty box, people solve it far more often.

Chapter 06

What the brain is doing, and the right-brain myth

You have probably heard that creative people are "right-brained" and logical people are "left-brained". This is a myth. Some functions are lateralised, meaning one side of the brain does more of the work. Language, for most people, relies more on the left side. But creative thinking uses networks spread across both sides, and brain-imaging studies have never found "right-brained people".

The more useful picture involves two large networks. The default mode network is active when your mind wanders, daydreams, remembers or imagines. It is good at generating loose associations. The executive control network is active when you focus, plan and judge. It is good at evaluating. In studies led by Roger Beaty and colleagues, people who produced more original ideas showed stronger cooperation between these two networks, which normally tend to work against each other. That matches the psychology neatly: generation and evaluation, open and close, in quick alternation.

Insight itself has a signature too. Researchers including John Kounios and Mark Beeman recorded brain activity while people solved word puzzles. Solutions that arrived as a sudden "aha" were preceded by distinctive activity, different from solutions reached step by step. The aha feeling is a real event in the brain, not just a story people tell afterwards. But it is still built from material the person already had.

Chapter 07

Quantity, knowledge and constraints

Three findings explain more about creative output than any talk of talent.

First, quality follows quantity. The psychologist Dean Keith Simonton studied the careers of hundreds of famous scientists and composers. He found that the share of a creator's work that becomes a hit stays roughly constant across a career. His "equal-odds rule" says that the people with the most masterpieces are also the people with the most forgettable works. They simply produced more. Bach wrote over a thousand pieces. Edison held more than a thousand US patents, most of them minor. Greatness looks like a high hit rate, but it is mostly a high attempt rate.

Second, knowledge comes first. Studies of composers and painters by John Hayes and others found that almost none produced widely recognised masterworks before about ten years of serious work in their field. The new idea has to be built from something. Epstein's argument in Range adds a twist: knowledge from other fields supplies the distant analogies that produce the most surprising ideas.

Third, constraints help. Total freedom is paralysing, because there are too many directions and no reason to choose any of them. A limit forces you off the obvious path. The best-known story is Dr. Seuss, who wrote Green Eggs and Ham after his publisher bet him he could not write a book using only fifty different words. It became one of the best-selling children's books in English.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The engine of change, and a field full of myths

Creativity drives science, business and art. Because it matters so much, people have built strong beliefs about it, and several of the most popular ones are wrong.

Chapter 08

Every improvement started as an idea someone had

Economists who study long-term growth agree on one broad point. Over centuries, the main source of rising living standards has not been more workers or more machines. It has been better ideas: new technologies, new ways of organising work, new medicines. Every one of them started as an original and useful thought in somebody's head. The field is covered in Economic growth.

At the personal level, creativity is mostly problem-solving when the usual answer fails. Surveys of employers regularly rank creative problem-solving among the skills they most want, and that ranking has risen as routine tasks are automated. The more machines take over the predictable work, the more of human work is the unpredictable part.

Chapter 09

Three myths that do real damage

"Brainstorm as a group"

The advertising executive Alex Osborn popularised brainstorming in the 1950s, claiming groups produce more ideas together. Decades of experiments show the opposite. The same number of people working alone, with their ideas pooled afterwards, usually produce more and better ideas. In a group, only one person speaks at a time, people forget ideas while waiting, and they hold back to avoid looking silly.

"Genius is close to madness"

There is some evidence. Large studies of Swedish population registers found bipolar disorder somewhat more common among people in creative professions, and their close relatives. But most creative people have no serious mental illness, and severe illness usually stops creative work rather than fuelling it. The romantic idea of suffering as a source of art is not a reason to avoid treatment.

"You either have it or you don't"

Some people are more creative than others, and personality plays a part (see below). But creative output depends heavily on knowledge, effort, method and environment, all of which can change. Believing creativity is fixed is one of the most reliable ways to stop trying, which is a theme Volume 09 explores in Mindset.

The fix for brainstorming is not to abandon groups, which are good at judging ideas, but to change the order. Use brainwriting: everyone writes ideas alone and silently first, then the group shares, combines and chooses. That keeps the strength of the individual for divergence and the strength of the group for convergence.

Chapter 10

What actually helps people create

Of the five big personality traits, the one most strongly tied to creativity is openness to experience: curiosity, love of new ideas, interest in art and imagination. The personality file covers the traits in full (Personality). But the situation matters at least as much as the person.

The Harvard researcher Teresa Amabile spent decades studying creativity at work. Her central finding is about motivation. People are most creative when they are driven by intrinsic motivation, interest in the task itself, rather than by rewards or the fear of judgement. In some of her experiments, people who were told their work would be evaluated, or who were working for a reward, produced work that judges rated as less creative. Rewards do not always hurt, and the details are debated. But pressure, surveillance and tight control tend to narrow thinking toward safe answers.

A few smaller conditions have decent evidence behind them:

  • Walking. A 2014 Stanford study by Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz found that people generated more ideas on the Alternative Uses Task while walking than while sitting. The effect lingered briefly after they sat down.
  • Psychological safety. Teams in which people are not afraid of looking foolish share more half-formed ideas, and half-formed ideas are where good ones begin.
  • Separating the phases. Generate first, judge later. Writers call it "write drunk, edit sober" (a line often wrongly credited to Hemingway). The useful version is "write freely, edit hard".
Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A valuable trait with real costs

Creativity is praised everywhere and rewarded less often than people claim. Both sides of that deserve an honest look.

Chapter 12

Everyone says they want it. Fewer choose it.

Organisations say they want creative people. Research suggests that, under uncertainty, people often feel a hidden discomfort with new ideas. A 2012 study by Jennifer Mueller and colleagues found that people who were made to feel uncertain rated a creative idea less favourably, even while saying they valued creativity. New ideas feel risky precisely because they are new, and the person judging them carries the risk.

Many important ideas were rejected at first, by publishers, investors and committees. Early rejection is therefore weak evidence. And for anyone judging ideas: your first reaction to something unfamiliar is partly a reaction to its unfamiliarity, not to its quality.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Creative machines, and what stays human

The arrival of AI that writes, draws and composes is the biggest change to creative work in generations. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 13

Three directions, three levels of confidence

DirectionWhere it standsHonest outlook
AI as an idea generatorSince about 2023, studies have found that large language models score as well as many people on tasks such as the Alternative Uses Task, and that writers using AI suggestions produce stories rated as more creative on average. Several studies also found that the AI-assisted stories were more similar to one another.Fairly sure: AI becomes a standard tool for the divergent phase, producing many options quickly. Humans keep the job of choosing.
A flattening of styleIf millions of people start from the same suggestions, work may drift toward the average. Early evidence points this way, but it is limited.Plausible: more competent, similar work, and a premium on a distinctive human voice. How large the effect will be is not known.
Machines making Big-C contributionsAI systems have produced useful scientific results, such as protein structure predictions, but under human direction and inside human questions.Speculative: an AI that independently changes a field the way Darwin did. See Artificial General Intelligence.

The ownership question is also open. Who owns a picture made with an AI tool, and can AI be trained on artists' work without permission? Courts and governments in several countries are still deciding, and the answers differ by country. The legal side is in Intellectual property.

Chapter 14

What stays human: taste and the problem itself

Look again at the two-part definition. AI is already very good at the first part, producing many original-looking options. The second part, judging what is effective, still depends on knowing what the work is for: who the audience is, what problem actually needs solving, what would feel fresh rather than merely odd. That judgement is called taste, and it is built from the same deep knowledge that creativity has always needed.

Choosing the problem is the other human advantage. Many great creative acts were new questions, not clever answers. As generating options becomes cheap, framing the right question and choosing the right answer become the scarce skills.

The core idea

Creativity means ideas that are original and effective. They come from knowledge recombined, through preparation, incubation, illumination and verification. Good creative work alternates divergent thinking (many options) and convergent thinking (choosing the best), and it improves with volume, constraints and the right conditions.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About ten years of serious work came before most recognised masterworks in studies of composers and painters.
  • Simonton's equal-odds rule: the hit rate stays roughly constant, so more attempts mean more hits.
  • Wallas named four stages of creative thought in 1926.
The common mistake

Judging ideas while generating them. Criticism in the opening phase kills the flow of ideas. Generate alone and freely first, then evaluate together and hard.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a group brainstorm usually produces fewer ideas than the same people working alone, and what you would do instead? If so, you understand divergence, convergence and the social side of creativity together.

The words, in plain words
Creativity
Producing ideas or work that are both new and useful for some purpose.
Divergent thinking
Generating many different possible answers from one starting point.
Convergent thinking
Narrowing many possibilities down to the single best answer.
Four C model
Four levels of creativity: mini-c (new to you), little-c (everyday), Pro-c (professional) and Big-C (field-changing).
Incubation
A period away from a problem, after which a solution often comes more easily.
Illumination
The moment a solution arrives, often suddenly. Also called insight.
Fixation
Getting stuck on one approach, so that every new attempt repeats the same mistake.
Functional fixedness
Seeing an object only in its usual role, such as a box as a container and never as a shelf.
Default mode network
Brain regions active during mind-wandering and imagination; a source of loose associations.
Executive control network
Brain regions active during focused attention and judgement.
Intrinsic motivation
Doing something because the task itself interests you, not for a reward.
Brainwriting
A group method where everyone writes ideas silently and alone before the group discusses them.
Openness to experience
The personality trait of curiosity and interest in new ideas, the one most linked to creativity.
