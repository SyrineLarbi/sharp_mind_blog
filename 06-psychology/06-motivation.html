Motivation. What makes people start, keep going, and sometimes stop for reasons they cannot name.

Most people think of motivation as a fuel tank: some people have a lot, others have little, and you can top it up with a speech or a bonus. The research describes something stranger. Motivation is several systems, and they can pull against each other. Wanting something is not the same as enjoying it. Paying people can make them try harder, or make them lose interest. And the feeling of "not being motivated" is often not a lack of energy at all, but a way of avoiding a bad mood.

Question 1 · What is it?

Whatever starts behaviour, aims it, and keeps it going

Psychologists use "motivation" for the processes that explain why a person acts, which way they go, how hard they try and how long they persist.

Chapter 01

Three questions about any action

Watch someone train for a marathon. Motivation research asks three things about them. Direction: why running, and not swimming or staying in bed? Intensity: how hard do they push in each session? Persistence: do they keep going in week twelve, in the rain, when the excitement has worn off?

The three can come apart. Plenty of people are intensely motivated on the first of January and gone by February. Others never feel very excited but turn up every day for years. Feeling motivated and acting motivated are different things, and much of the useful research is about the second.

Chapter 02

Pushed from inside, pulled from outside

Early theories focused on drives: internal states of need, such as hunger or thirst, that push an animal to act until the need is met. The body tries to keep things like temperature and blood sugar in a steady range, a balance called homeostasis. When they drift, a drive pushes you to fix them.

Drives cannot explain much of human life. People climb mountains, collect stamps and eat dessert when full. So psychologists added incentives: things in the world that pull you toward them because of what you expect to get. The smell of fresh bread pulls you into a bakery whether you are hungry or not. Most real motivation is a mix of a push from inside and a pull from outside.

Chapter 03

Doing it for its own sake, or for something else

The most important distinction in modern motivation research is between two reasons for doing something.

Intrinsic motivation

Doing something because the activity itself is interesting or satisfying. A child building with blocks, an adult doing a crossword, a scientist who cannot stop thinking about a problem. Nobody needs to pay them.

Extrinsic motivation

Doing something to get a separate result: money, praise, a grade, avoiding punishment. Most paid work is at least partly extrinsic. This is not bad. It is how most of the world gets its work done.

Real activities usually mix both. A nurse may love caring for patients and also need the salary. The interesting question, which the next part answers, is how the two interact. Sometimes adding an outside reward strengthens interest. Sometimes it quietly destroys it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Wanting, expecting, needing and aiming

Five ideas explain most of what is known: wanting is separate from liking, effort follows expectations, three basic needs feed lasting interest, rewards can backfire, and clear goals focus effort.

Chapter 04

Wanting is not liking

For years, the brain chemical dopamine was described as the "pleasure chemical". Work by the neuroscientists Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson from the 1980s onward changed that picture. In rats whose dopamine was largely depleted, animals stopped working for food but still showed the facial signs of enjoying sweet tastes when food was put in their mouths. When dopamine activity was boosted, animals worked harder for rewards without seeming to enjoy them more.

Their conclusion was that the brain has separate systems for wanting (the urge to get something) and liking (the pleasure of having it). Dopamine mainly drives the wanting. Usually the two travel together. When they separate, strange things happen. People can crave something they no longer enjoy, which is a central feature of addiction. They can check a phone compulsively and feel nothing good when they do. The strength of an urge is not evidence that the thing will make you happy. The chemistry is in Neuroscience · Neurotransmitters.

Chapter 05

Effort follows expectation

Imagine a salesperson offered a large bonus for reaching a target they think is impossible. The bonus is valuable, but they do not try. Now imagine an easy target with a tiny reward. They do not try much either. Effort is highest when the result is both valued and seen as reachable.

This is the core of expectancy-value theory, developed by psychologists such as John Atkinson and, for the workplace, Victor Vroom in 1964. Put roughly, motivation is the product of two things: how likely you think success is, and how much you care about it. Because it is a product and not a sum, if either one falls to zero, motivation falls to zero, however big the other is. This is why people who have failed repeatedly can seem to stop caring. Often they have simply stopped believing that effort changes anything.

Learned helplessness

In experiments in the 1960s, Martin Seligman and colleagues found that dogs exposed to shocks they could not escape later failed to escape shocks they easily could. Seligman called this learned helplessness. Later work, including by Seligman himself, revised the explanation, but the human lesson has held up: repeated experience of having no control can teach people to stop trying even when control returns.

Chapter 06

Three basic needs: self-determination theory

The most influential modern theory of motivation comes from the American psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, developed from the 1970s and 1980s. It is called self-determination theory. It says that people have three basic psychological needs, and that lasting motivation grows when an environment feeds them.

NeedWhat it meansWhat feeds itWhat starves it
AutonomyFeeling that your actions are your own choiceReal options, explanations of whyClose monitoring, orders without reasons
CompetenceFeeling effective and able to improveChallenges at the right level, useful feedbackTasks far too easy or far too hard, only criticism
RelatednessFeeling connected to and cared for by othersRespect, belonging, a team that notices youIsolation, being treated as replaceable

An important part of the theory is that extrinsic motivation is not all the same. Doing your taxes because you fear a fine feels very different from exercising because you value your health, even though both are for an outside reason. The theory calls the second kind internalised: you have made the reason your own. The more a reason is your own, the longer the effort lasts, and the less it depends on someone watching.

Chapter 07

When rewards backfire

In a 1973 study by Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett, nursery-school children who already liked drawing were split into groups. One group was told they would get a "good player" certificate for drawing, and got it. Another group drew with no reward mentioned. Days later, when markers were simply left out in the classroom, the children who had expected the reward spent less time drawing than the others. Being paid for something they already enjoyed seemed to turn play into work.

This is called the overjustification effect: an outside reward gives you a new explanation for your behaviour ("I do this for the certificate"), which can push out the old one ("I do this because I like it"). How strong and general the effect is has been argued over. A large 1999 review by Deci, Richard Koestner and Ryan found that expected, tangible rewards for interesting tasks reliably reduced intrinsic motivation. Other researchers, notably Judy Cameron and David Pierce, argued the harm was smaller and narrower.

The reasonable middle ground is this. Rewards for dull tasks are fine and often necessary. Rewards that feel controlling, attached to tasks people already enjoy, are risky. Rewards that come as unexpected recognition, or that carry information ("this was excellent work"), tend to do little harm and may help.

Chapter 08

Goals and plans

From the 1960s, the psychologists Edwin Locke and Gary Latham ran and reviewed hundreds of studies on goals. Their main finding is one of the most consistent in organisational psychology: specific, challenging goals usually lead to better performance than vague goals such as "do your best", as long as people accept the goal and have the skill to reach it. A specific goal tells you where to aim and lets you see how far you are from it.

There are limits. For complex tasks that people do not yet know how to do, a tough performance goal can make things worse, and a learning goal ("find three ways to approach this") works better. Goals that are too narrow can also invite cheating or neglect of everything not measured.

The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer added a simple tool, the implementation intention: an if-then plan that links a situation to an action. "I will exercise more" is a goal. "If it is 7 a.m. on a weekday, I put on my running shoes" is an implementation intention. Many studies find that such plans raise the chance that people follow through, because they hand the start of the action to a cue instead of to a fresh decision.

Chapter 09

The famous pyramid, and what it gets wrong

The best-known picture of motivation is Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs, from a 1943 paper. It lists physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and "self-actualisation", the urge to become fully what you could be. It is usually drawn as a pyramid, although the pyramid drawing appears to have come from later writers rather than from Maslow himself.

The broad idea, that some needs feel more urgent when they are unmet, is reasonable. The strict order is not well supported. People in hunger and danger still seek love and meaning; artists live in poverty for their work. Frankl's account of the concentration camps, summarised in Volume 09, is a powerful example of meaning mattering in the worst conditions. Needs overlap and compete; they do not wait in a queue.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Work, self-control, and the art of starting

Every manager, teacher, parent and person with a plan is trying to apply a theory of motivation. Some of the most popular theories turn out to be the shakiest.

Chapter 10

Motivation at work

In 1959 the American psychologist Frederick Herzberg proposed that the things that make people unhappy at work are not simply the opposite of the things that make them engaged. Poor pay, bad conditions and unfair rules cause dissatisfaction. Fixing them removes the complaint but does not, on its own, create enthusiasm. Enthusiasm comes from the work itself: achievement, responsibility, growth. The details of his theory have been criticised, but the core insight is widely used.

Money does motivate. Piece rates raise output for simple, measurable work. But pay works best as a fair base that people stop thinking about, not as a lever pulled for every action. When pay feels unfair compared with others, it becomes a powerful demotivator. People compare, and a fair salary felt as unfair can do more harm than a low one felt as fair. Beyond that, self-determination theory predicts what many workplaces find: people put in lasting effort when they have some say in how they work, can see themselves improving, and feel part of a team.

Chapter 11

Willpower: two famous findings, revisited

In the 1960s and 1970s, Walter Mischel offered young children one marshmallow now, or two if they could wait alone for several minutes. Follow-up studies of some of the same children years later linked longer waiting to better school results. The marshmallow test became a symbol of self-control as destiny. A larger 2018 study with a more varied group of children found a much weaker link, and most of it disappeared once family background and early abilities were taken into account. Waiting may partly reflect whether a child has learned that adults keep promises.

In 1998 Roy Baumeister and colleagues proposed ego depletion: that self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use, like a muscle that tires. It was hugely popular. In 2016 a large effort across many laboratories, using an agreed method, found an effect close to zero. Researchers still disagree about whether some version of it is real.

The lesson that survives is practical. People who seem to have great self-control mostly arrange their lives so they need less of it. They avoid temptation rather than resist it, and they rely on routines and habits. Volume 35's chapter on habits explains why changing the environment beats trying harder.

Chapter 12

Procrastination is a mood problem

Almost everyone procrastinates sometimes. It looks like a time-management problem. Researchers such as Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois argue it is mainly an emotion regulation problem. A task makes you feel bored, anxious, confused or afraid of failing. Putting it off makes that feeling go away, immediately. The relief is a reward, so the delay is learned. The cost arrives later, when you are a different, future person.

Economists call the underlying pattern present bias: valuing rewards and costs now far more than the same ones later. Today's discomfort feels huge; next week's crisis feels abstract. The practical fixes follow from the diagnosis. Make the first step tiny so it carries little bad feeling. Name the feeling you are avoiding. Treat yourself kindly after slipping, since studies suggest harsh self-criticism predicts more procrastination, not less.

Health note

A lasting loss of interest in almost everything, together with low mood, can be a sign of depression rather than ordinary low motivation. This file is general education, not medical advice. Volume 43 explains depression and how to get help.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every lever has a cost

There is no motivational technique without side effects. The question is which side effects you can live with.

Chapter 13

LeverAdvantagesDisadvantages
Money and prizesFast, clear and effective for simple, measurable tasks.Can crowd out interest, invite gaming of the measure, and stop working when they stop.
Punishment and pressureCan stop a dangerous behaviour quickly.Produces fear, hiding and minimum effort. Effort stops when the watcher leaves.
Specific, hard goalsFocus effort and make progress visible.Can harm complex learning, narrow attention and encourage cutting corners.

The last row is easy to miss. Jobs that people find deeply meaningful, such as care work, teaching or the arts, are often paid less than their difficulty would suggest. Some researchers see meaning as a real benefit that people choose. Others see employers relying on it. Both can be true at once. Motivation is a resource, and resources can be used well or taken advantage of.

Chapter 15

When effort is optional

For most of history, effort was forced on people by need. Increasingly, machines and services can remove effort from many activities: cooking, navigating, writing, even socialising. This is mostly a gain. But self-determination theory suggests a risk. If competence comes from doing hard things well, and many hard things are no longer needed, people may have to choose difficulty deliberately, as they already do with sport, music and learning languages.

That points to the oldest finding in this file with a new meaning. Lasting motivation comes from autonomy, growth and connection, not from rewards alone. In a world full of cheap, fast rewards that please the wanting system, the people who do best may be those who choose activities that feed the three basic needs, and build their days around them.

The core idea

Motivation sets the direction, intensity and persistence of behaviour. It mixes inner drives and outside incentives, and intrinsic and extrinsic reasons. Wanting is separate from liking. Effort needs both value and a belief that success is possible. Lasting motivation grows from autonomy, competence and relatedness.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Three basic needs in self-determination theory.
  • Specific, hard goals beat "do your best" across hundreds of studies (Locke and Latham).
  • A 2016 multi-lab replication found ego depletion close to zero.
The common mistake

Treating procrastination and "low motivation" as laziness. Often it is avoidance of a bad feeling, and it responds to smaller first steps and kinder self-talk, not to harsher pressure.

Test yourself

Why might paying children to read make some of them read less later? If you can explain it, you understand the overjustification effect and why autonomy matters.
