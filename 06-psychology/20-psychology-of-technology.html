Psychology of technology. We shape our tools, and then the tools are designed to shape us.

The average smartphone owner picks up the device many dozens of times a day, often without deciding to. That is not an accident or a weakness of character. Many apps are built by teams who study human psychology and measure, minute by minute, what keeps people engaged. This file explains the psychology those teams use, what technology is doing to attention, mood and relationships, and why the evidence on harm is more mixed than either the alarmists or the optimists claim.

Question 1 · What is it?

How technology changes minds, and how minds shape technology

The field runs in two directions. It studies what devices do to us, and it studies how designers use knowledge of us to build devices.

Chapter 01

A field at the meeting point

The psychology of technology draws on several research areas. Human-computer interaction studies how people use machines and how to make them easier to use. Media psychology studies the effects of television, games and social media. Cyberpsychology studies behaviour online: identity, relationships, conflict. They share one question: what happens to human thinking and behaviour when so much of life passes through a screen?

The scale makes the question urgent. In 2024 the International Telecommunication Union estimated that about 5.5 billion people, roughly two-thirds of humanity, were using the internet. For many of them, the phone is the first thing they see in the morning and the last at night. Even small effects on attention or mood, multiplied by billions of people and several hours a day, add up to something large.

Chapter 02

Every new medium has been accused of ruining minds

Worry about technology is very old. In Plato's dialogue Phaedrus, written around 2,400 years ago, Socrates tells a story in which an Egyptian king warns that the invention of writing will make people forgetful. They will rely on marks outside themselves, he says, and appear wise without being wise. Later generations worried that novels would corrupt young women, that radio and comic books would harm children, and that television would rot brains.

Some of those fears now look silly, and this pattern is a useful warning. Each new medium sets off a moral panic, a wave of public alarm that runs ahead of the evidence. But the pattern cuts both ways. Writing really did change memory, as Socrates feared. The question is never whether a technology changes us. It is how much, in which ways, and whether the trade is worth it. What is genuinely new today is the deliberate, data-driven design of products to capture attention, and the speed at which that design can be tested and improved.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rewards, friction, attention and the crowd

The most engaging products use a small number of well-understood psychological mechanisms. Once you can name them, you see them everywhere.

Chapter 03

The slot machine in your pocket

In the mid-20th century the American psychologist B. F. Skinner trained pigeons and rats to press levers for food. He found that the way rewards are delivered matters more than their size. If a lever gives food every time, the animal presses when hungry and stops when full. If it gives food unpredictably, sometimes after two presses and sometimes after twenty, the animal presses steadily and keeps going long after rewards stop. This is a variable reward schedule. It is exactly how slot machines work.

It is also how many apps work. When you pull down to refresh a feed or open your messages, you do not know what you will get: something boring, something funny, a message from someone you like. Former Google design ethicist Tristan Harris popularised the comparison between a phone and a slot machine. Uncertainty, not reward itself, is what keeps people checking.

The brain chemistry is often misdescribed. Dopamine, a chemical messenger in the brain, is often called the "pleasure chemical". It is better described as a learning and wanting signal. It responds strongly to rewards that are better than expected, and to cues that predict them. That is why the sound of a notification can pull your attention before you know what it says. The chemistry is in Neurotransmitters.

Chapter 04

Designing behaviour: make it easy, then prompt it

In the late 1990s the Stanford researcher B. J. Fogg began studying what he called persuasive technology, computers designed to change what people believe or do. His behaviour model is simple: a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt come together at the same moment. Several of his students went on to work at, or found, well-known technology companies.

Designers mostly work on the second and third parts, because motivation is hard to change. They make the desired action effortless and then trigger it. Some of the best-known techniques:

The same psychology, applied to the user's goals rather than the company's, is simply good design. The difference lies in whose goals the design serves. The wider science of nudges is in Behavioral economics.

Chapter 05

What constant switching does to attention

The human mind cannot do two demanding tasks at once. What looks like multitasking is fast task switching, and every switch has a cost: a moment to reload the goal and rules of the new task, plus some attention left behind on the old one. A message read during a piece of focused work costs more than the seconds spent reading it. The Attention file covers the basics.

The psychologist Gloria Mark has measured how long office workers stay on one screen before switching. In her studies, the average fell from about two and a half minutes in the early 2000s to under a minute in more recent measurements. A 2009 Stanford study by Eyal Ophir, Clifford Nass and Anthony Wagner found that people who reported heavy media multitasking were worse at ignoring irrelevant information, not better. Later studies found weaker and mixed results, so the direction of cause is unclear. Perhaps multitasking harms focus, or perhaps easily distracted people multitask more.

Some popular findings have not held up well. A 2017 study suggested that the mere presence of a phone on the desk, even switched off, reduced performance on mental tests. Several later attempts to repeat it found little or no effect. The reliable finding is about interruption, not proximity: notifications and the habit of checking break concentration.

Chapter 06

Feeds, bubbles and outrage

Most feeds today are ranked by algorithms, sets of rules that decide what each person sees. They are usually optimised for engagement: clicks, watch time, shares, comments. In 2011 the activist Eli Pariser warned of filter bubbles, in which algorithms show people only what they already agree with.

The evidence turned out to be more complicated. Most people's media diets are more varied than the bubble image suggests, and the strongest bubbles are often chosen by people themselves. In 2023 a group of academic researchers working with Meta published several large experiments on Facebook and Instagram users during the 2020 US election. Changing the feed, for example from ranked to chronological, changed what people saw, but did not measurably change their political attitudes over the few months studied. Critics noted that three months is short and the election was already polarised.

What does seem robust is that emotional, especially moral-outrage content spreads further. Studies of social media posts by William Brady and colleagues found that messages containing moral and emotional words were shared more. Platforms that rank by engagement therefore tend to reward outrage, and users learn that outrage gets attention.

Chapter 07

Why people behave worse online

In 2004 the psychologist John Suler described the online disinhibition effect. People say and do things online that they would not do face to face. Several features combine. You may be anonymous. You cannot see the other person's face, so you miss the signals of hurt that normally make people soften. Replies are delayed, so the conversation feels less real. And it can feel as though online life is a game, separate from real life.

Disinhibition is not always bad. It lets people talk openly about illness, sexuality or grief, and find support they could not find locally. But it also fuels harassment and pile-ons. The same missing face that makes confession easier makes cruelty easier too. Volume 37 discusses how dehumanising language works in Dehumanisation, and the language that precedes violence.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Young minds, compulsive use, and memory outsourced

Three debates matter most for ordinary life: what phones are doing to teenagers, when heavy use becomes a disorder, and what we lose when machines think for us.

Chapter 08

The teenage mental health debate

Rates of reported anxiety and depression among teenagers, especially girls, rose in the United States, the United Kingdom and several other countries from the early 2010s. That is roughly when smartphones and social media became nearly universal among teenagers. The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt, in The Anxious Generation (2024), argues that this is not a coincidence. He points to lost sleep, lost face-to-face time, constant social comparison and the replacement of free play with screens.

Other researchers disagree. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski analysed large datasets in 2019 and found that the average link between screen use and teenage wellbeing was very small, comparable in size, they noted, to the link with eating potatoes. Candice Odgers and others argue that the rise in distress has many possible causes and that correlation is not causation. Both sides agree that some young people are clearly harmed, and that heavy use at night damages sleep.

This is one of the most contested questions in current psychology. Governments have not waited for it to be settled. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health. Many countries have restricted phones in schools. Australia passed a law in 2024 setting a minimum age of 16 for many social media accounts. The social side is in Social media, and the childhood side in Childhood development.

Chapter 09

When does heavy use become a disorder?

General education

This chapter explains research and definitions. It is not medical advice. Anyone worried about their own or a family member's use should speak to a doctor or qualified professional.

People often call phones "addictive". Psychologists are more careful. The World Health Organization's international classification of diseases, in the version adopted in 2019, includes gaming disorder. The definition requires impaired control over gaming, gaming taking priority over other interests and daily activities, and continuing despite harm, usually for at least a year. It is meant for a small minority of players, not for anyone who plays a lot.

"Social media addiction" and "smartphone addiction" are not official diagnoses. Many researchers prefer the phrase problematic use, because heavy use is often a symptom of something else, such as loneliness, anxiety or boredom, rather than a cause. That matches Volume 37's point that pain and circumstance predict addiction better than the substance or activity itself. A useful test is not hours spent but harm done: is it pushing out sleep, work, relationships or things you used to enjoy?

Chapter 10

Outsourced memory, navigation and conversation

The Memory file describes how people already store facts in phones and search engines (The change that is already happening). Navigation offers a sharper example. A well-known study of London taxi drivers, led by Eleanor Maguire around 2000, found that the back part of their hippocampus, a brain structure involved in spatial memory, was larger than in comparison groups, and grew with years of driving. Later research suggests that heavy reliance on satellite navigation is linked with weaker spatial memory. Skills you hand over to a device tend to weaken, and that is a fair trade only if you will never need them.

Conversation is the newest frontier. In 1966 the computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA, a simple program that imitated a therapist by turning users' statements into questions. He was disturbed to find that people, including his own secretary, confided in it as if it understood them. This tendency to read understanding into a machine is called the ELIZA effect. Modern chatbots are vastly more capable, and millions of people now use them for advice, company and emotional support. Volume 49 explains what these systems actually do in What a language model actually does.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous benefits, real costs, and the dose question

Technology is neither good nor bad for the mind in general. The effects depend on what it is used for, how much, when, and by whom.

Chapter 12

Dose, displacement and taking back control

In 2017 Andrew Przybylski and Netta Weinstein proposed the "Goldilocks" hypothesis from a large survey of English teenagers. Moderate use was not linked with lower wellbeing, and in some cases was linked with slightly higher wellbeing. Only very heavy use was linked with lower wellbeing. Like Goldilocks's porridge, the right amount is neither too little nor too much.

A second idea explains much of the harm that does exist: displacement. Screens do damage mostly through what they replace. An hour on a phone at midnight replaces sleep. An evening scrolling replaces seeing friends, exercising or reading. So the most useful question is not "how many hours?" but "instead of what?"

The practical defences follow from the mechanisms in Question 2. Turn off non-essential notifications, which removes the prompts. Keep the phone out of the bedroom, which protects sleep. Remove the most compulsive apps from the home screen, which adds a little friction. Use grey-scale mode or app timers if they help. None of these needs willpower in the moment, which is the point. Change the environment, not your resolve.

Chapter 14

Designing for people, not for engagement

A growing movement inside technology argues for humane design: products judged by whether people's lives improve, not by how much time they spend. Some changes are already common. Phones now report screen time, offer focus modes and let users silence notifications by schedule. Some apps hide like counts or show a message when you have seen everything new. Whether such features will spread depends partly on regulation and partly on business models. Products paid for by users have less reason to maximise attention than products paid for by advertisers.

The deeper lesson of this field is the oldest one in psychology. Human attention, approval and habit can be shaped, by others or by yourself. Knowing the mechanisms does not make you immune, as the research on biases shows. But it lets you design your own environment before someone else designs it for you.

The core idea

Engaging technology works through variable rewards, easy actions plus prompts, social approval and engagement-ranked feeds. Its effects on the mind depend on dose and displacement: what it replaces matters more than hours alone. On teenagers' mental health, the evidence is genuinely contested.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 5.5 billion people were online in 2024, roughly two-thirds of humanity.
  • Gaming disorder entered the WHO's disease classification in the version adopted in 2019.
  • Australia set a minimum age of 16 for many social media accounts in a 2024 law.
The common mistake

Asking "how many hours?" and stopping there. The better question is "instead of what?" Screens harm mostly by displacing sleep, movement and face-to-face time.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a feed you refresh is more compelling than one that updates on a fixed schedule? If you can, you have understood variable rewards, prediction and prompts together.

A set of rules a computer follows; here, the rules that choose what you see.

Harm caused by what a screen activity replaces, such as sleep or exercise.
