Infrastructure. The systems you only notice on the day they stop working.

Turn on a tap and clean water comes out. Flush and the waste disappears. Flip a switch, drive to work, send a message to another continent. Each of those acts depends on structures built by people who are mostly long dead, paid for by taxes and bills, and kept alive by maintenance crews nobody thanks. Infrastructure is the shared physical skeleton of a society. It lasts for generations, which is its great strength. It is also why the decisions made about it today, good or bad, will still shape daily life at the end of this century.

Question 1 · What is it?

The shared systems a society runs on

Infrastructure means the long-lived physical networks and facilities that many people use together and few could build alone: transport, water, energy, communications, and the buildings of public services.

Chapter 01

The summer London could not breathe

In the hot summer of 1858, the River Thames in London was effectively an open sewer. Human waste from millions of people flowed into it, and the heat made the smell so overwhelming that the period became known as the Great Stink. Members of Parliament, whose building stands on the river, hung curtains soaked in chemicals over the windows. London had already suffered repeated cholera epidemics, and the physician John Snow had shown a few years earlier that cholera spread through contaminated water, though many still blamed bad air.

Parliament, finally motivated by its own discomfort, approved a huge sewer system designed by the engineer Joseph Bazalgette. Built over the following years, it intercepted the waste before it reached the central river and carried it far downstream. Cholera largely disappeared from London. Bazalgette made his main sewers much larger than the population of the day required, reasoning that the city would grow. Much of his system is still in use more than a century and a half later, and London has only recently added a major new "super sewer" tunnel to relieve it.

The story contains most of what this file is about. Infrastructure is often built only after a crisis, it outlives everyone who built it, and the people who design it generously are thanked by generations they never meet.

Economists sometimes add "soft" infrastructure: legal systems, education and institutions. This file sticks to the physical kind. Recently, governments have also begun to speak of critical infrastructure, the subset whose failure would seriously damage health, safety or the economy. That list now usually includes digital systems such as payment networks and cloud services.

Chapter 03

Why it is usually public, or heavily regulated

Most infrastructure has two awkward economic features. First, it is a network: a single water main or railway line is useful only because it connects to everything else. Second, it is often a natural monopoly: it would be wasteful to build two sets of water pipes down the same street to create competition. Whoever owns the only pipe can charge what they like, unless someone stops them.

That is why infrastructure is usually owned by governments or, where private, closely regulated, with a regulator setting prices or returns. Countries mix these differently. In France and the UK, private companies run many water services, under very different contracts and regulatory systems. In most of the United States, water is supplied by public utilities. The right mix is a long political argument, and the evidence on whether public or private ownership performs better depends heavily on how well the regulation is designed.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Plan, pay, build, and then the long part

Building is the visible moment. Most of the cost, risk and engineering effort comes before it, in planning and financing, and for decades after it, in operation and maintenance.

Chapter 04

Engineers call the discipline of managing this whole span asset management. Its central idea is whole-life cost: a design that is cheap to build but expensive to maintain is often a worse deal than one that costs more at the start. A concrete road may cost more than asphalt to lay and much less to look after. A bridge built with easy access for inspectors saves money every year for a century.

The difficulty is that the people who decide on construction are rarely the people who will pay for the maintenance. A government that opens a new motorway gets the credit. The governments of the next fifty years inherit its repair bills.

Chapter 05

How infrastructure ages

Everything built deteriorates. Steel rusts, especially when road salt or seawater reaches it. Concrete cracks, lets in water and corrodes the steel bars inside it. Metal parts that are loaded and unloaded millions of times, such as bridge joints under traffic, develop fatigue cracks that grow slowly and invisibly. Water pipes corrode and leak; in many cities a large share of treated water is lost through leaks before it reaches a tap.

Two bridge collapses show how this goes wrong. On 1 August 2007, the I-35W bridge over the Mississippi River in Minneapolis collapsed during evening rush hour, killing 13 people. Investigators found that some steel connecting plates had been too thin since the original design in the 1960s, and that extra weight added over the years, plus construction material on the deck that day, pushed them past their limit. On 14 August 2018, part of the Morandi bridge in Genoa, Italy, collapsed in a storm, killing 43 people. Investigations focused on corrosion of the steel cables inside its concrete stays and on how the bridge had been inspected and maintained.

The common thread is that failures rarely come from one cause. An old weakness, an added load and missed warning signs combine. Good inspection regimes exist to catch the weakness before the other two arrive.

Chapter 06

Who pays

Infrastructure is paid for in three basic ways, usually in combination.

Taxpayers

Governments fund roads, flood defences and schools from taxes or borrowing. Cheap government borrowing suits assets that last decades. The weakness is that maintenance competes every year with more visible spending.

Users

Tolls, fares, water bills and electricity tariffs. Users pay in proportion to use, which is fair and encourages efficiency, but it can price out poorer users and is politically painful to raise.

Private investors

In a public-private partnership (PPP), a company builds and often runs an asset in return for fees over many years. It can bring discipline and speed, but private borrowing usually costs more, and badly written contracts have left governments with expensive long-term bills.

Development banks such as the World Bank, and in recent years China's lending through its Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, finance much of the infrastructure in lower-income countries. The terms of that lending, and the debts it creates, are a subject of real international debate.

Chapter 07

Why big projects almost always overrun

The Danish economic geographer Bent Flyvbjerg has spent decades collecting data on large projects: railways, bridges, dams, Olympic Games, IT systems. His findings are consistent and sobering. The great majority of megaprojects, usually defined as projects costing more than a billion dollars, go over budget, over time, or both, and only a small fraction deliver the promised benefits on time and on budget.

He points to two causes. Optimism bias: planners genuinely underestimate costs and overestimate benefits, because they imagine the project going well. Strategic misrepresentation: sometimes people knowingly present low estimates, because an honest estimate would not get approved, and once construction starts it is hard to stop. His suggested cure is reference-class forecasting: instead of estimating from the details of your own project, look at what similar projects actually cost and took, and start from there.

Examples are easy to find. Boston's "Big Dig" highway tunnel, the Berlin Brandenburg airport, which opened in 2020 about nine years late, and the UK's HS2 high-speed railway have all become symbols of overruns. Flyvbjerg also highlights projects that went well, and finds that they tend to be modular: built from many repeated, standard pieces, like solar farms, rather than one unique giant structure.

Chapter 08

Everything depends on everything else

On 14 August 2003, overloaded power lines in Ohio sagged into trees. A software fault in the control room's alarm system meant operators did not realise what was happening. Within hours, a cascade of failures had cut power to around 50 million people in the north-eastern United States and Ontario, Canada.

The power cut did not stay a power cut. Water pumps stopped, so some cities lost water pressure. Subways and traffic lights stopped. Mobile networks struggled as backup batteries ran down. Infrastructure systems are interdependent: electricity runs water pumps and telecoms; telecoms control the grid; fuel supply needs electricity at the pumps. In 2021 a ransomware attack on the computer systems of Colonial Pipeline, which carries fuel along the US East Coast, led the company to shut the pipeline for several days, causing panic buying and shortages (on ransomware itself, see Volume 14). Engineers now study these connections explicitly, because a failure in one system can cascade into others that nobody thought were linked.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Health, wealth and power

Infrastructure decides how long people live, how productive economies are, and increasingly how countries compete for influence.

Chapter 09

The quiet driver of health and prosperity

Clean water and sewers did more for life expectancy in the 19th and early 20th centuries than almost any medicine. Today, the gap between countries is still largely an infrastructure gap. The International Energy Agency estimates that several hundred million people, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa, still live without electricity. Hundreds of millions lack safely managed drinking water, and far more lack safe sanitation (see Clean water).

Economically, infrastructure lowers the cost of everything else. A paved road turns a day's journey to market into an hour. Reliable power lets a workshop run machines. Container ports made global trade cheap (see Volume 12). Economists debate how large the return on new infrastructure is in rich countries that already have most of it, but there is broad agreement that for poorer countries, basic infrastructure is one of the strongest foundations for growth.

Chapter 10

When cost-cutting poisons a city

In 2014, to save money, the city of Flint, Michigan, switched its drinking water source to the Flint River. The new water was more corrosive, and the treatment did not include the chemicals normally added to stop pipes corroding. Lead from old pipes dissolved into the water supply. For more than a year, residents' complaints about discoloured, foul-smelling water were dismissed by officials. Children were exposed to lead, which harms brain development. Several officials were later charged, and the case became a national scandal.

Flint shows two things. Technically, old infrastructure carries hidden hazards that only stay safe if it is operated with care: lead service pipes are harmless only while their inner surface stays protected. Socially, the people most exposed to failing infrastructure tend to be those with the least political power, as Katrina also showed. Infrastructure is engineering, but the decisions about who gets it and who maintains it are political.

Chapter 11

Infrastructure as geopolitics

Governments increasingly treat infrastructure as a tool of influence. China's Belt and Road Initiative has financed ports, railways and power plants across Asia, Africa and beyond. Supporters point to projects that would not otherwise have been built. Critics point to debt burdens and strategic leverage. The European Union and the G7 have launched competing programmes. Within countries, governments have returned to large spending plans, such as the US infrastructure law of 2021, with about 1.2 trillion dollars in total spending over several years.

Undersea cables, ports, 5G networks and electricity grids are also security questions now. Who builds and controls a country's critical infrastructure has become a question of national security, not just of price.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Long life is both the gift and the trap

The same durability that lets a sewer serve five generations also locks those generations into choices made long before them.

Chapter 13

The maintenance trap

Every engineering society that reports on its country's infrastructure tells a similar story. The American Society of Civil Engineers publishes a regular report card on US infrastructure, and in recent editions the overall grade has hovered in the C range, roughly "mediocre, requires attention", with large backlogs of repairs. Much of the US Interstate highway network, authorised in 1956, and many water systems were built in the mid-20th century and are reaching the age where major renewal is needed at the same time.

The economics of delay are brutal. A small repair postponed becomes a large repair, and a large repair postponed becomes a replacement. But nobody holds a ribbon-cutting ceremony for a resurfaced road, and a bridge that did not collapse makes no headlines. Some governments have adopted "fix it first" rules that require money for maintaining existing assets before new ones are approved. They are hard to keep when budgets tighten.

Chapter 15

Designing for a climate that will not sit still

Infrastructure has always been designed around history. Engineers size a drain for, say, the heaviest rainfall expected once in a hundred years, calculated from past records. Climate change breaks that method, because the past is no longer a reliable guide. Heavier downpours overwhelm drains, heatwaves buckle rails and sag power lines, and rising seas threaten coastal roads, ports and treatment plants.

The response has two parts. One is resilience: designing systems that bend rather than break, recover quickly, and have backup routes. The other is adaptation of what already exists, covered in Climate adaptation. Both must happen within the long life cycle of Chapter 04. A water main laid today may still be in the ground in 2100. Every piece of infrastructure built now is a bet on what the world will be like for the rest of the century, which is why getting the design right matters so much. That is the subject of the next topic.

The core idea

Infrastructure is the long-lived, shared network of transport, water, energy and communications a society depends on. Most of its cost and risk lies in planning, financing and decades of maintenance, not construction, and its failures usually combine an old weakness, a new load and missed warnings.

The numbers worth knowing
  • London's Great Stink: 1858; Bazalgette's sewers are still in use.
  • The 2003 North American blackout cut power to about 50 million people.
  • Most megaprojects run over budget, over time, or both (Flyvbjerg).
The common mistake

Judging a project by its construction cost. Whole-life cost, including maintenance, matters more, and is usually paid by someone else.

The words, in plain words
Infrastructure
The shared, long-lived physical systems a society runs on: transport, water, energy, communications.
Critical infrastructure
The parts whose failure would seriously harm health, safety or the economy.
Natural monopoly
A service where one network is cheaper than several competing ones, like water pipes.
Asset management
Managing a structure across its whole life, from planning to replacement.
Whole-life cost
The total cost of building, running, maintaining and finally replacing an asset.
Fatigue
Slow cracking of material under repeated loading, even below its breaking strength.
Public-private partnership
A contract in which a private firm builds and often runs public infrastructure for long-term payments.
Megaproject
A very large project, usually over a billion dollars.
Optimism bias
The honest tendency to underestimate costs and overestimate benefits.
Reference-class forecasting
Estimating a project from the real outcomes of similar past projects.
Interdependence
The way infrastructure systems rely on each other, so failures can cascade.
Resilience
The ability of a system to absorb a shock and recover quickly.
