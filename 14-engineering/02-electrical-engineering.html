Electrical engineering. Moving energy and information at the speed of a switch.

Electricity has two jobs in the modern world. It carries energy, from a power station to a kettle hundreds of kilometres away. And it carries information, as billions of tiny on-off pulses inside every phone and computer. Electrical engineering is the discipline that does both. It is younger than mechanical engineering, barely 150 years old as a profession, and it changed daily life more completely than almost anything else people have built.

Question 1 · What is it?

Two businesses that share one science

Power engineering moves large amounts of energy. Electronics moves tiny amounts of energy that carry meaning. Both rest on the same few laws.

Chapter 01

The war of the currents

In 1882 Thomas Edison opened one of the first central power stations, on Pearl Street in New York. It supplied direct current (DC), which flows steadily in one direction, as from a battery. It had a serious weakness. At the low voltages Edison used, much of the energy was lost as heat in the cables, so customers had to live within roughly a mile or two of the station. A city would need a power station every few blocks.

A rival system used alternating current (AC), which reverses direction many times a second. The inventor Nikola Tesla designed practical AC motors, and the industrialist George Westinghouse bought his patents and built AC networks. Through the late 1880s the two camps fought in public, with Edison's side stressing the dangers of high-voltage AC. AC won. In the mid-1890s a large AC hydroelectric plant at Niagara Falls began sending power to the city of Buffalo, more than 30 kilometres away, and the argument was effectively over.

The reason AC won is not that it is better in every way. It is that AC voltage could be changed easily with a transformer, and changing voltage turned out to be the key to moving power over long distances. Chapter 04 explains why. Interestingly, the story has partly reversed: the longest power links built today use DC again.

Chapter 03

Voltage, current and resistance, without the fog

The classic analogy is water in pipes, and it works well enough to start with. Voltage is like water pressure: the push that makes charge want to move. It is measured in volts. Current is like the flow rate: how much charge actually passes a point each second. It is measured in amperes, or amps. Resistance is like a narrow section of pipe that makes flow harder, measured in ohms.

The three are linked by Ohm's law, named after the German physicist Georg Ohm: voltage equals current times resistance. Double the push across the same resistance and you double the flow. Then there is power, the rate at which energy is delivered, measured in watts. Power equals voltage times current. A 2,000-watt kettle on a 230-volt European supply draws nearly 9 amps. The same kettle in the United States, on 120 volts, would need almost 17 amps, which is one reason American kettles are usually less powerful.

Every circuit also obeys two bookkeeping rules from the physicist Gustav Kirchhoff. Current flowing into a junction equals current flowing out. And the voltage rises and drops around any closed loop add up to zero. Almost every circuit calculation is these few rules applied patiently.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From spinning magnets to switching billions of times a second

The power side and the electronics side work at wildly different scales, but each depends on one clever trick.

Chapter 04

Why power lines run at enormous voltages

When current flows through a wire, some energy turns into heat. The loss grows with the square of the current: double the current and you lose four times as much. Power, though, depends on voltage times current. So you can send the same power with a high voltage and a small current, and the losses fall dramatically. That is the whole reason transmission lines run at hundreds of thousands of volts.

A transformer makes this possible. It is two coils of wire wound around an iron core. Alternating current in the first coil creates a changing magnetic field, which induces a current in the second coil. If the second coil has more turns, the voltage goes up; fewer turns, and it goes down. Transformers have no moving parts and large ones waste only a small percentage of the power they handle. They work only with AC, because only a changing current makes a changing magnetic field.

Chapter 05

The grid is a balancing act, every second

An electricity grid has almost no storage of its own. The power being used at any instant must be produced at that same instant. When millions of people switch on kettles at the end of a popular television programme, power stations must respond within seconds.

Grid operators watch one number above all: frequency, the rate at which the current alternates. It is 50 cycles per second in Europe, most of Africa and much of Asia, and 60 in North America and some other countries. Traditional generators are huge spinning machines locked in step with the grid. If demand rises faster than supply, they slow slightly and the frequency dips. If supply exceeds demand, it rises. The heavy spinning mass of all those machines, called inertia, acts like a shock absorber that buys time for controls to react.

If the frequency drifts too far, protective equipment disconnects generators and customers to prevent damage. That can cascade. A grid is less like a set of pipes and more like a giant machine that must stay perfectly in step, spread across a continent.

Chapter 06

Motors and generators: the same machine, run in two directions

In 1831 the English scientist Michael Faraday showed that moving a magnet near a coil of wire produces a current. This is electromagnetic induction, and nearly all the world's electricity comes from it. A generator spins coils and magnets past each other, driven by steam, gas, water or wind. A motor does the reverse: current in the coils creates magnetic forces that spin the shaft.

Electric motors are among the most widely used machines on Earth. They run fans, pumps, compressors, conveyor belts, lifts and, increasingly, cars. By often-quoted estimates from the International Energy Agency, electric motors and the systems they drive account for something like half of all electricity use worldwide. So small gains in motor efficiency add up to whole power stations' worth of savings.

Chapter 07

The transistor: a switch with no moving parts

In 1947, at Bell Labs in the United States, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, working in William Shockley's group, built the first working transistor. The three later shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. A transistor is a tiny switch made of a semiconductor, a material such as silicon whose ability to conduct can be turned up or down by a small voltage on a control terminal. It can amplify a weak signal, or act as an on-off switch.

Around 1958 to 1959, Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed the integrated circuit: many transistors and their connections made together on one piece of material. From then on, electronics was a printing process. Modern chips carry billions of transistors, each switching billions of times a second. How those chips are made is covered in Volume 12 (Computer chips).

Switches become logic once you combine them. Two switches in a row make an "AND": current flows only if both are on. Two side by side make an "OR". From those simple gates, stacked up in layers, come adding machines, memories and finally whole processors.

Chapter 08

Signals, noise and turning the world into numbers

Most real-world quantities, such as sound, light and temperature, vary smoothly. That smooth form is called analog. Computers work with numbers. An analog-to-digital converter measures the signal many times a second, a step called sampling, and records each measurement as a number. A rule known as the sampling theorem, associated with Harry Nyquist and Claude Shannon, says you must sample at more than twice the highest frequency you want to keep. Human hearing tops out around 20,000 cycles per second, which is why CD audio uses 44,100 samples a second.

The great enemy is noise: random disturbance from heat, interference and imperfect parts. Digital signals resist noise well. A receiver only has to decide "1 or 0?", and error-correcting codes can detect and repair occasional mistakes. Shannon's 1948 work on information theory showed that every channel has a maximum rate at which it can carry information reliably. Mobile networks, Wi-Fi and fibre links are all, in a sense, engineering ever closer to Shannon's limit.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The achievement everything else plugs into

Remove electricity and almost every other modern system stops within hours: water pumps, hospitals, payments, communications, refrigeration.

Chapter 09

Electrification changed what a day is

In 2000 the US National Academy of Engineering asked engineering societies to rank the greatest engineering achievements of the twentieth century. Electrification came first, ahead of cars, aircraft and computers, because the others depend on it. Cheap electric light lengthened the useful day. Electric motors let factories be laid out for efficiency rather than around a single steam shaft. Washing machines and fridges took hours of household work, much of it done by women, out of each week.

Electrification is still unfinished. As of the mid-2020s, international estimates put the number of people without access to electricity at roughly 700 million to 750 million, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa. For many of them the first supply is now likely to come from solar panels and batteries, not from a long grid line. That route was too expensive only twenty years ago.

Chapter 10

When the machine falls out of step

On 14 August 2003, power lines in Ohio sagged in the summer heat and touched trees that had not been trimmed. A software fault meant an alarm system at the local utility had failed, so operators did not realise what was happening. Line after line tripped as power shifted onto the remaining ones. Within minutes, roughly 50 million people across the northeastern United States and Ontario lost power, some for days. The joint US-Canadian investigation blamed poor tree trimming, a lack of situational awareness and weak coordination, not a single dramatic failure.

Large blackouts follow this pattern again and again. A small local fault, combined with operators who cannot see clearly, turns into a cascade. In April 2025, almost all of mainland Spain and Portugal lost power within seconds. Investigations through 2025 focused on voltage control problems in the Iberian grid; the full sequence was still being analysed and debated. Grid engineering is largely the work of making cascades impossible, through protection relays, reserve capacity and operators with good information.

Chapter 11

Demand is rising again

In many rich countries, electricity demand was roughly flat for years, as efficient lighting and appliances offset growth. That is changing. Electric cars, heat pumps and industrial electrification are all adding load. So are data centres, especially those built for AI, as Volume 14 describes (Data centres, and why AI became an electricity story).

At the same time, the supply side is changing shape. Solar and wind farms do not have heavy spinning generators locked to the grid. They connect through inverters, power electronics that convert their output into grid-ready AC. That creates new problems of inertia and voltage control, and new opportunities, because inverters can respond in milliseconds. These system questions are the subject of Renewable-energy engineering. The twenty-first-century grid has to carry more power, from more variable sources, with less natural inertia.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A perfect carrier of energy, except that it will not wait

Electricity is clean, fast and flexible where it is used. Its weaknesses all come from the fact that it is hard to store.

Chapter 13

The danger you cannot see

Electricity hurts people through current, not voltage alone. Current passing across the chest can disturb the heart's own electrical rhythm. A few tens of milliamps, a tiny fraction of what a kettle draws, can be enough to cause a dangerous rhythm. Voltage matters because it decides how much current can be pushed through the skin's resistance, which is far lower when skin is wet.

Safety, not advice

This is general education. Mains electricity can kill. Work on household wiring should follow local rules, which in many countries require a qualified electrician.

Engineering answers this with layers. Insulation keeps people from live parts. Earthing gives a fault current a safe path. Fuses and circuit breakers cut power when current is too high. A residual-current device, common in bathrooms and new homes, compares the current going out and coming back and cuts the supply in a fraction of a second if some is leaking away, possibly through a person. Safety comes from several independent protections, not from one perfect one.

Chapter 15

The convergence of power and information

For a century the two halves of electrical engineering were separate trades. Power engineers thought in megawatts and decades. Electronics engineers thought in milliwatts and product cycles. They are merging. The grid is filling with chips: inverters, smart meters, battery controllers and software that forecasts the weather to predict solar output. Meanwhile, chip designers now worry mostly about power, because a modern processor is limited less by how fast its transistors can switch than by how much heat it can shed.

The future grid will be part machine and part computer network, and that brings a new weakness: it can be hacked. Securing it is becoming as important as keeping its frequency steady.

The core idea

Electrical engineering moves energy (power systems) and information (electronics). Power travels at high voltage to cut losses, and the grid must balance supply and demand every second. Electronics rests on the transistor, a switch with no moving parts.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Grids run at 50 or 60 cycles per second, depending on the country.
  • Cable losses grow with the square of the current, hence very high voltages.
  • Electric motors use roughly half of the world's electricity, by common estimates.
The common mistake

Imagining the grid as a store of electricity, like a water tank. It stores almost nothing; every unit used is being generated at that moment.

Test yourself

Can you explain why AC beat DC in the 1890s, and why the longest new links use DC again? If you can, you understand transformers, losses and power electronics together.

The words, in plain words
Voltage
The electrical "push" that drives charge through a circuit, measured in volts.
Current
The flow of charge, measured in amps.
Resistance
How strongly a material opposes current, measured in ohms.
Power
The rate of delivering energy: voltage times current, measured in watts.
Direct current (DC)
Current that flows steadily in one direction.
Alternating current (AC)
Current that reverses direction many times a second.
Transformer
A device of two coils that raises or lowers AC voltage.
Frequency
How many times per second AC reverses; the grid's heartbeat.
Inertia
The stored spin of big generators that steadies the grid against sudden changes.
Inverter
Power electronics that turn DC, for example from solar panels, into grid AC.
Transistor
A tiny semiconductor switch or amplifier; the building block of all chips.
Integrated circuit
Many transistors and their wiring made together on one chip.
Sampling
Measuring a smooth signal at regular moments to turn it into numbers.
