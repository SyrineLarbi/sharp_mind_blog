Industrial engineering. The engineering of how work flows, not of what gets made.

Most engineers design a thing: an engine, a bridge, a circuit. Industrial engineers design the system around the thing. How many checkouts should a supermarket open at five o'clock? Where should the fast-selling items sit in a warehouse? In what order should a hospital schedule its operations? How can a factory find the one machine that is quietly making defective parts? These questions have no single physical object as an answer. They have better or worse arrangements of people, machines, time and information. Finding the better one is the job.

Question 1 · What is it?

The engineering of systems that include people

Industrial engineering began on the factory floor. Its methods now run airlines, hospitals, delivery fleets and call centres.

Chapter 01

The couple who filmed bricklayers

Frank Gilbreth started work as a bricklayer's apprentice in Boston in the 1880s. He noticed that every bricklayer he watched used a slightly different set of movements, and that the experienced ones taught apprentices several different ways at once. He began to study the motions themselves. By redesigning the scaffold so bricks sat at waist height, and arranging them so the worker did not have to bend and turn for each one, he cut the number of movements per brick sharply and greatly raised the number of bricks laid per hour.

He later worked with his wife, Lillian Gilbreth, who earned a doctorate in psychology. They filmed workers with a clock in the frame and broke tasks into basic motions, such as reach, grasp and move, to find and remove the wasted ones. Lillian went on to a long career of her own, applying the same ideas to kitchens, to people with disabilities and to the psychology of work. She is often called one of the founders of industrial psychology and ergonomics.

The Gilbreths' central insight still defines the field. Most waste is not in the effort people make. It is in the way the work is arranged around them. Change the arrangement and the same people produce more, with less strain.

Many universities now call the department "industrial and systems engineering", which better describes what it does. Its graduates often work far from industry, in banks, hospitals, airlines, consulting firms and technology companies.

Chapter 03

A system, not a machine

A system, in this sense, is a set of parts that work together and affect each other: people, machines, materials, information and rules. Its behaviour often surprises you, because changing one part shifts the load onto another. Speed up one step in a factory and the next step may simply collect a bigger pile of waiting work. Hire more doctors in an emergency department and patients may still wait, because the real hold-up is the lack of free beds upstairs.

The industrial engineer's first question is not "how do we make this step faster?" but "what is actually limiting the whole system?" The business file on Operations calls this the bottleneck. Engineers then reach for measurement, models and experiments to find it and fix it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Measure, fit, optimise, simulate, control

Five families of methods make up most of the toolkit. Each grew out of a specific problem, and each is still in daily use.

Chapter 04

Measuring work, and its limits

You cannot improve what you have not described. So industrial engineers start by mapping the work: every step, how long it takes, how often it goes wrong, and how long things wait between steps. A common finding surprises managers. In many processes, an item spends far more time waiting than being worked on. A loan application might need an hour of actual work and still take three weeks, because it sits in queues between departments.

Time studies produce a standard time for a task: how long a trained worker should take at a normal pace, plus allowances for rest and delays. Standard times are used to plan staffing, set prices and balance work along a line. They can also be misused, as a stick to push people harder. The best practice now is to measure the process to improve it, not to judge individuals. That shift is one of the main lessons of the history told in the operations file.

Chapter 05

Ergonomics: fit the job to the person

During the Second World War, the US Army Air Forces lost many aircraft in landing accidents that were blamed on "pilot error". A young psychologist, Alphonse Chapanis, looked at the cockpits. In some aircraft, the lever that raised the landing gear and the lever that moved the wing flaps were placed side by side and looked almost identical. Tired pilots, after a mission, pulled the wrong one. Chapanis proposed giving the controls distinct shapes, so that a pilot could tell them apart by touch: a small wheel on the landing-gear lever and a flap shape on the flap lever. The idea, called shape coding, spread through aircraft design.

That is the founding story of human factors, also called ergonomics: the study of how people perceive, decide and move, used to design tools and tasks that suit them. When many people make the same mistake, the design is usually the cause. The same approach shapes modern work in less dramatic ways: the height of a workbench, the weight of a box a warehouse worker lifts, the number of alarms a nurse has to respond to, the layout of a software screen.

Chapter 06

Operations research: finding the best answer among millions

In the late 1930s and during the war, British scientists were asked to help the military use new equipment such as radar more effectively. Their approach, studying operations scientifically, was called operational research in Britain and operations research in the United States. One celebrated analysis concerned Atlantic convoys. It found that larger convoys lost a smaller share of their ships, because the number of escort ships needed grows more slowly than the number of merchant ships they protect.

After the war the field gained powerful mathematics. In 1947 the American mathematician George Dantzig devised the simplex method for linear programming: a way to find the best choice among a huge number of options when the goal and the limits can be written as simple equations. "Which mix of products earns the most, given these machines, this labour and this demand?" is a classic example. Today, related methods plan airline crews, schedule trains, route delivery vans, balance electricity grids and decide where to place warehouses.

Some of these problems are very hard. Finding the shortest route through many delivery stops, the famous travelling salesman problem, has a number of possible routes that explodes as stops are added. Practical tools do not always find the perfect answer. They find a very good one quickly, which is usually what a business needs.

Chapter 07

Simulation, and why averages mislead

Many systems are too messy for neat equations. Customers arrive at random. Machines break without warning. Some jobs take twice as long as others. For these, industrial engineers build simulations: computer models that play out thousands of imaginary days, with randomness built in, to see what happens on average and in bad cases.

Simulation exposes a trap. Plans built on average values often fail, because variability itself causes delays. Imagine a clinic where patients arrive on average every ten minutes and each visit takes on average nine minutes. On paper, the doctor is busy 90 percent of the time and nobody waits long. In reality, arrivals bunch up and some visits run long, and the queue grows badly at times. The operations file explains why a system that is "nearly full" becomes very slow. Simulation lets planners test changes, such as an extra nurse at peak hours or booking appointments differently, before spending money on them.

Chapter 08

Quality engineering: listening to variation

Every process varies a little. Two bolts from the same machine are never exactly the same length. In the 1920s Walter Shewhart, at Bell Telephone Laboratories, made a key distinction. Common-cause variation is the normal, random scatter of a stable process. Special-cause variation is a signal that something has changed: a worn tool, a new batch of material, a different operator. His tool for telling them apart was the control chart, a graph of measurements over time with limits drawn at the edges of normal scatter.

The lesson is subtle and important. Adjusting a stable process every time a measurement moves a little actually makes it worse, because you add your own variation on top. React to signals, not to noise. In the 1980s, Motorola built on these ideas in a programme it called Six Sigma, which set a target of no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities and trained staff in structured problem-solving. It spread widely, notably at General Electric in the 1990s.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The hidden efficiency of modern life

Next-day delivery, cheap flights and short hospital waits all depend on scheduling and optimisation that nobody sees.

Chapter 09

Delivery vans that rarely turn left

The parcel company UPS is well known for designing its routes in the United States to minimise left turns. In countries that drive on the right, a left turn usually means waiting for a gap in oncoming traffic, which wastes time and fuel and raises the risk of accidents. A route with more right turns can be slightly longer and still faster and cheaper. The company later developed route optimisation software for its drivers, and has said it saves many millions of miles a year.

The left-turn rule is a small example of a big idea. Tiny savings, repeated across thousands of vehicles and millions of stops, add up to large sums. The same logic decides where an online retailer stores each product in its warehouse, how many check-in desks an airport opens at each hour, and how an airline assigns aircraft and crews so that one late flight does not ripple across the whole day.

Chapter 10

Hospitals are systems too

Healthcare has become one of the most important fields for industrial engineering. A hospital is a set of linked queues: emergency department, scans, operating theatres, wards and discharge. A patient waiting on a trolley in the emergency department is often waiting not for a doctor but for a bed upstairs, which is waiting for another patient to be discharged, who is waiting for a prescription or a care-home place.

Engineers have helped hospitals smooth the scheduling of planned surgery, which otherwise creates peaks and troughs in demand for beds; separate quick cases from complex ones; and redesign discharge so that beds free up earlier in the day. Some hospitals have adopted Toyota-style methods directly. Results vary, and medicine has its own constraints, but the principle holds. Long waits are usually a flow problem across the whole system, not a shortage at the one place where people are visibly waiting.

Chapter 11

Efficiency for whom?

The field has a troubled side, and an honest account includes it. Time study, from its early years, was resented by many workers, who saw it as a tool to make them work harder for the same pay. In the United States, protests against stopwatch methods in government arsenals led Congress to restrict time study there for decades in the twentieth century.

The debate is alive today in a new form. Software now measures warehouse pickers, delivery drivers and call-centre staff second by second and sets their targets automatically, which is often called algorithmic management. Supporters say it is fair, consistent and productive. Critics, including unions and some health and safety researchers, link high monitored work rates to stress and injury. The evidence varies by workplace, and the debate is contested. The tools of industrial engineering are neutral; how they are used is a choice about people. Good practice, going back to Lillian Gilbreth, treats workers' wellbeing as part of the system's performance, not a cost against it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Big gains from small changes, and the danger of over-optimising

The approach delivers large improvements cheaply. It can also make systems brittle, or treat people as parts.

Chapter 13

Efficient or resilient?

What went right

Decades of industrial engineering made goods, travel and services far cheaper and more reliable. Waiting times fell, defect rates fell, and many dangerous jobs were redesigned to be safer. Much of the rise in productivity in factories and logistics came from better systems, not harder work.

What went wrong

Systems tuned to run with almost no spare capacity struggled when demand or supply changed suddenly, as many supply chains did in 2020 and 2021. The lesson, discussed in the operations file, is that some slack is not waste. It is insurance, and the right amount is a design choice.

Chapter 15

The durable skill: seeing the whole

As software takes over more calculation, the industrial engineer's value shifts toward what software does badly. That means defining the right problem, spotting the assumption that does not match reality, and understanding how people will actually respond to a change. A scheduling algorithm that ignores the fact that nurses swap shifts informally will be ignored by the nurses.

The Gilbreths' film cameras have become sensors and models, but the question is the same. Where does the work really wait, and what arrangement would let it flow? The more automated the world becomes, the more valuable people who can see a whole system are likely to be. The wider picture is in Engineering in the AI era.

The core idea

Industrial engineering designs systems of people, machines and information. Its tools are work measurement, ergonomics, operations research, simulation and statistical quality control, all aimed at the constraint that limits the whole.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Linear programming's simplex method dates from 1947 (Dantzig).
  • Shewhart's control chart dates from the 1920s at Bell Labs.
  • Six Sigma's target: 3.4 defects per million opportunities.
The common mistake

Blaming people for errors the design invites. If many people make the same mistake, fix the system, as the cockpit levers showed.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a clinic that is busy "only" 90 percent of the time can still have long waits? If you can, you understand variability, queues and why simulation exists.

The words, in plain words
System
A set of connected parts, including people and rules, whose behaviour depends on how they interact.
Motion study
Breaking a task into basic movements to remove the wasted ones.
Standard time
The planned time for a trained worker to do a task at a normal pace, with allowances.
Ergonomics (human factors)
Designing tools and tasks to fit human bodies and minds.
Shape coding
Giving controls distinct shapes so they can be told apart by touch.
Operations research
Using mathematics and models to make better decisions under constraints.
Linear programming
A method for finding the best option when goals and limits are simple equations.
Travelling salesman problem
Finding the shortest route through many stops; very hard as stops multiply.
Simulation
A computer model that plays out a system many times, with randomness, to test changes.
Control chart
A graph of a process over time, with limits that separate normal scatter from real signals.
Special-cause variation
Variation caused by a specific change that should be found and fixed.
Six Sigma
A quality programme aiming at very few defects, using structured problem-solving.
Algorithmic management
Software that measures workers and sets their tasks and targets automatically.
