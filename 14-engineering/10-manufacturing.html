Sharp Mind · Engineering · Topic 10 of 20

Manufacturing. Making one of something is craft. Making a million identical ones is engineering.

Almost everything you are touching right now was made in a factory: the screen, the chair, the clothes, the cup. Each was shaped from raw material by a sequence of processes, repeated thousands or millions of times with tiny variation. Manufacturing is the discipline of doing that cheaply, reliably and at scale. Its history explains why some countries grew rich, why supply chains break, and why a shortage of one small chip once stopped car factories around the world.

Question 1 · What is it?

Turning material into products, over and over

Manufacturing is the conversion of raw materials into finished goods using machines, tools, processes and people, usually in large numbers.

Chapter 01

Eighteen steps to make a pin

In 1776 the Scottish economist Adam Smith opened The Wealth of Nations with a visit to a small pin factory. One worker alone, he wrote, could perhaps make one pin a day, and certainly not twenty. But in the factory the job was split into about eighteen separate operations. One man drew out the wire, another straightened it, a third cut it, a fourth sharpened the point, and so on. Ten people working this way made upwards of forty-eight thousand pins a day.

Smith used the pin factory to explain the division of labour. Splitting a job into small repeated tasks makes each worker faster, saves time switching between tasks, and makes it easier to invent a machine for each step. That last point turned out to be the most important. Once a task is simple and repetitive, a machine can do it. The whole history of manufacturing since then is a long conversation between splitting work up and handing the pieces to machines.

Chapter 02

Four ways to give matter a shape

There are thousands of manufacturing processes, but nearly all of them do one of four things.

FamilyThe ideaExamples
SubtractiveStart with a block and cut away what you do not wantDrilling, milling, turning on a lathe, grinding
FormativeForce material into a shape, by melting it into a mould or pressing it while solidCasting, forging, stamping car panels, injection moulding plastic
AdditiveBuild the shape up layer by layer3D printing in plastic, metal or concrete
JoiningConnect separate parts into oneWelding, soldering, gluing, bolting, riveting

Most products use several. A car engine block is cast, then machined to precise dimensions, then assembled with hundreds of other parts. A smartphone contains stamped metal, moulded plastic, glass shaped while soft, chips made by an extraordinary sequence of chemical and optical steps, and a final assembly done largely by hand.

Chapter 03

Four scales of production

How a factory is laid out depends on how many identical things it makes.

Job shop

One-off or very small runs, each different. Machines are general-purpose and workers are highly skilled. A workshop making a custom part for a racing car.

Batch

Hundreds or thousands at a time, then switch to another product. A bakery, a clothing factory, a pharmaceutical plant.

Mass production

Huge numbers of nearly identical items on dedicated lines. Cars, phones, drinks cans.

Continuous process

A product that flows rather than being counted: steel, paper, petrol, cement. The plant may run day and night for years.

Moving down this list, the cost per item falls and flexibility falls with it. The central economic fact of manufacturing is that a high fixed cost spread over many units makes each unit cheap. This is called an economy of scale. It is why a custom part can cost hundreds of times more than a mass-produced one of similar complexity.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Interchangeable parts, moving lines, and controlled variation

Modern manufacturing rests on a few ideas, each invented to solve a specific problem, and each still in use.

Chapter 04

Interchangeable parts, and the idea of tolerance

Before the nineteenth century, every musket was made by hand, and each part was filed to fit its neighbours in that particular gun. If a lock broke on a battlefield, a spare from another musket would not fit. In 1785 the French gunsmith Honoré Blanc showed visitors, including Thomas Jefferson, musket locks whose parts could be mixed and still fit. The idea spread slowly, through government armouries in France and then the United States, where it became known as the "American system".

Interchangeability needs a new concept: tolerance. No two parts are ever exactly the same size. A tolerance is the allowed range, for example a shaft 20 millimetres wide, plus or minus a hundredth of a millimetre. If every shaft and every hole stays inside its tolerance, any shaft fits any hole. Workers check parts with fixed gauges, simple tools that a part either passes or fails.

Tolerance is the hidden language of manufacturing. Tighter tolerances make better-fitting, quieter, longer-lasting products, but they cost more, because they need better machines, slower work and more parts rejected. A good engineer specifies tight tolerances only where they matter.

Chapter 05

The moving assembly line

In 1913, at its Highland Park plant near Detroit, the Ford Motor Company began moving car chassis past workers on a powered line, instead of having workers walk around a stationary car. Each worker did one small task as the car passed. The time to assemble a chassis fell from about twelve hours to around an hour and a half. The price of the Model T fell over the following years from more than $800 to under $300, putting cars within reach of ordinary families.

The work was monotonous, and staff turnover was very high. In 1914 Ford responded by roughly doubling the daily wage to five dollars, partly to keep workers. The assembly line brought cheap goods and, eventually, higher wages, at the cost of work that was dull, fast and controlled by the speed of the line. That trade still defines much factory work, and it is a large part of the argument about automation in Automation.

Ford's system had one big weakness. It was built for one product. Famously, the Model T came in any colour as long as it was black, partly because black paint dried fastest. When customers wanted variety, competitors such as General Motors, offering several models and yearly changes, overtook Ford in the late 1920s.

Chapter 06

The Toyota way: making less, better

After the Second World War, Toyota could not afford Ford's giant stocks of parts and dedicated lines. Engineers led by Taiichi Ohno built a different system over several decades, now called the Toyota Production System and, in the West, lean manufacturing.

Two ideas are at its core. Just-in-time means producing and delivering each part only when the next step needs it, so very little stock piles up. Jidoka, roughly "automation with a human touch", means a machine or worker stops the line the moment something goes wrong. Workers can pull a cord, called an andon, to halt production. That sounds wasteful. The point is that a problem gets fixed at its source immediately, instead of producing a thousand faulty cars to repair later.

Lean spread worldwide from the 1980s, and Industrial engineering covers it in depth. For manufacturing, the lesson is that inventory hides problems. A big pile of spare parts lets a factory keep running while ignoring a faulty machine. Remove the pile, and the faults have to be fixed.

Chapter 07

The economics of the mould

Pick up any plastic object, a bottle cap, a toothbrush handle, a computer mouse. It was probably made by injection moulding. Plastic pellets are melted and forced at high pressure into a steel mould, cooled for a few seconds, and pushed out. A single mould can produce a part every few seconds for years.

The catch is the mould. A complex steel mould can cost tens of thousands of dollars or more and take weeks to make. That cost is fixed whether you make ten parts or ten million. So moulding is absurdly expensive for a handful of parts and absurdly cheap for millions. This shape of curve appears across manufacturing: stamping dies, casting patterns, chip masks. The decision about how to make something often comes down to how many you will make.

Chapter 08

Quality is controlling variation

In the 1920s Walter Shewhart, a physicist at the Bell telephone company's manufacturing arm, made a crucial distinction. Some variation is ordinary noise: tiny random differences from part to part. Some has a specific cause: a worn tool, a bad batch of material, a new operator. He invented the control chart, a simple graph of measurements over time with limits drawn on it. Points scattered inside the limits are normal noise, so leave the process alone. A point outside, or a steady drift, signals a real cause worth finding.

His colleague W. Edwards Deming took these ideas to Japan, lecturing to industrialists from 1950. Japanese manufacturers adopted them thoroughly. By the 1970s and 1980s, Japanese cars and electronics were known for reliability that Western makers struggled to match. Deming's message was that quality is built in by controlling the process, not inspected in at the end. Inspection only finds defects after the cost of making them has been paid.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Wealth, power and fragility

Where things are made shapes how countries grow, how they fight, and what happens when something breaks on the other side of the world.

Chapter 09

The ladder to prosperity

Almost every country that has moved from poverty to wealth in the last two centuries did it partly through manufacturing: Britain, then Germany and the United States, then Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and most recently China. Factories allow people to move from low-productivity farming into work where machines multiply their output. They also teach skills, build suppliers and earn foreign currency through exports.

China's rise is the largest example in history. By the early 2020s it produced roughly three-tenths of the world's manufactured output by value, roughly as much as the United States, Japan and Germany combined. Whether today's poorer countries can follow the same ladder is a live debate among economists. Automation means modern factories need fewer workers, and the cheapest-labour advantage matters less than it did.

Chapter 10

When the chain breaks

Modern products are made by long chains of specialised suppliers spread across many countries. A car contains tens of thousands of parts from hundreds of suppliers. Combined with just-in-time delivery, this is extremely efficient. It is also fragile.

The COVID-19 pandemic showed how fragile. In 2020 carmakers cut chip orders, expecting falling sales. Chipmakers switched capacity to laptops and phones, whose sales boomed. When car demand recovered, there were not enough chips, and chip factories take years to build. In 2021 car plants across the world stood idle for weeks, waiting for components that cost a few dollars each. Volume 12 explains why chip production is so concentrated (Computer chips).

The lesson changed corporate thinking. Efficiency and resilience pull in opposite directions. Many firms now hold more stock of critical parts, use more than one supplier, or move some production closer to home. Each choice costs money in normal times and pays off only in a crisis.

Chapter 11

Manufacturing as security

Governments have rediscovered that making things is a matter of national security. In both world wars, the deciding factor was often industrial capacity: the ability to turn out ships, aircraft and shells faster than the enemy. Volume 21 traces how that played out in the Second World War (Six years).

Since around 2020, many governments have returned to industrial policy: public money and rules designed to bring particular industries home. The United States passed the CHIPS and Science Act in 2022, with tens of billions of dollars for domestic chip production. The European Union, Japan, South Korea and India have their own programmes for chips and batteries. Supporters see this as overdue insurance. Critics warn of waste, subsidy races between allies and higher prices. The debate is genuinely open, and its outcome will depend on choices made over the coming decade.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Cheap abundance, and what it costs

Mass manufacturing gave ordinary people goods once reserved for the rich. It also created dull work, waste and fragile dependencies.

The last row is politically explosive. Economists broadly agree that trade raised total income in rich countries. They also now broadly agree that the losses were concentrated in particular towns and industries, and that many affected workers did not recover. Both statements are true at once, which is why the subject divides people so sharply.

Chapter 14

More output, fewer jobs

One trend has been clear for decades. In the United States, manufacturing employment peaked in 1979 at about 19.5 million jobs. Output has grown a great deal since then, but the number of factory jobs has fallen sharply. Similar patterns appear in Germany, Japan and, more recently, China. The causes include trade, but rising productivity, much of it from automation, is a large part.

So the factory of the future will probably make more with fewer people, and those people will need different skills: programming and maintaining machines, analysing data, solving problems. The world will keep making things. What changes is who does the making, where it happens, and how many people it employs. The next two topics follow two forces behind that change: 3D printing and Automation.

The core idea

Manufacturing shapes material by removing, forming, adding or joining it. Mass production rests on interchangeable parts made within tolerances, high fixed costs spread over many units, and controlled variation. Its great trade-off is efficiency against flexibility and resilience.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Smith's ten pin-makers: about 48,000 pins a day (1776).
  • Ford's moving line, 1913: chassis assembly from about 12 hours to 1.5.
  • China: roughly 30% of world manufacturing output in the early 2020s.
  • US manufacturing jobs peaked in 1979 at about 19.5 million.
The common mistake

Thinking falling factory employment means falling manufacturing. In many rich countries output kept rising while jobs fell, because each worker produces far more.

Splitting a job into small tasks, each done by a different person or machine.

Making a shape by casting, moulding or pressing material.
Additive manufacturing
Making a shape by building it up layer by layer; 3D printing.
Interchangeable parts
Parts made so alike that any one fits any matching product.
Tolerance
The allowed range of variation in a part's size.
Economy of scale
Falling cost per unit as more units share the fixed costs.
Tooling
The moulds, dies and fixtures made for one product, usually expensive and fixed.

The Toyota-derived approach of cutting waste and fixing problems at their source.
Just-in-time
Making and delivering parts only when the next step needs them.

A graph of measurements over time that separates normal noise from real problems.

The network of suppliers and transport that brings parts and materials together.
