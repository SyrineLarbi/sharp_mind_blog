3D printing. Complexity for free, speed at a price.

Around 2012, 3D printing was going to change everything. Every home would have a printer, and you would download a spare part or a toy instead of buying it. That did not happen. Something more interesting did. 3D printing quietly became essential in a few places where nothing else works as well: jet engine parts, hip implants, dental aligners, hearing aids, rocket engines and, above all, the prototypes that engineers make before anything goes into a factory. This file explains how it works, what it is truly good at, and why it complements mass production rather than replacing it.

Question 1 · What is it?

Building up instead of cutting away

3D printing, called additive manufacturing in industry, makes a solid object from a digital file by adding material one thin layer at a time.

Chapter 01

Twenty parts become one

Inside a jet engine, fuel nozzles spray fuel into the combustion chamber. For the LEAP engine, which entered service around 2016 on popular airliners, General Electric's engineers wanted a nozzle with intricate internal channels to mix fuel and air better and keep the tip cool. Made the traditional way, the nozzle was an assembly of about twenty separate pieces, brazed and welded together. Every joint was a possible point of failure.

So they printed it. A laser melted fine metal powder, layer by layer, into a single piece with all the internal channels already inside. According to GE, the printed nozzle was about a quarter lighter and around five times more durable than the assembled version. The company built a factory to print tens of thousands of them.

That is the core of what 3D printing offers: shapes that cannot be cut or moulded, and assemblies collapsed into one part. Notice what it is not. The nozzle is not cheap, and it is not quick to make. It is printed because no other method can make it at all.

Chapter 02

Where it came from

The American engineer Chuck Hull filed a patent in 1984 for stereolithography. He shone ultraviolet light onto a vat of liquid resin that hardens where the light falls. By tracing one thin cross-section at a time and lowering the platform after each, he built a solid object out of liquid. He founded the company 3D Systems to sell the machines. In the late 1980s Scott Crump developed a different method, pushing melted plastic through a heated nozzle, and founded Stratasys.

For two decades these were expensive industrial tools, used mainly to make models of new products. Then two things happened. In 2005 the British engineer Adrian Bowyer started the RepRap project, an open-source printer designed to print many of its own parts. And around 2009 key patents on Crump's method expired. Cheap desktop printers flooded the market, and the hype of the early 2010s followed.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From a file to a finished part

The printing itself is only the middle of the job. Before it comes software, and after it comes a surprising amount of finishing work.

Chapter 04

CAD stands for computer-aided design, the software engineers use to draw parts in three dimensions. The slicer is the program that turns the model into instructions for the machine. It decides how thick each layer is, which route the nozzle or laser takes, and how solid the inside should be. Many plastic parts are printed with a honeycomb-like infill inside rather than solid, to save material and time.

Overhangs are a challenge. A layer cannot be printed on thin air. So the slicer adds temporary supports, thin scaffolding that holds up overhanging parts and is removed afterwards. Good designers shape parts to need as few supports as possible.

Chapter 05

The desktop printer, explained

The common home or office printer uses material extrusion. A spool of plastic filament, often a plant-based plastic called PLA, feeds into a heated nozzle at around 200°C. The nozzle moves across the build plate, laying down a thin melted line that cools and sticks to the line beside it and the layer below. When a layer is done, the plate drops or the nozzle rises by a fraction of a millimetre, and the next layer begins.

This explains the method's main weakness. The part is strong along each layer but weaker between layers, because each layer is bonded to the last only by melting onto it. Pull a printed bracket in the direction the layers were stacked and it may split along a layer line. This property, called anisotropy, meaning different strength in different directions, affects most printing methods to some degree.

Chapter 06

Printing metal: welding a million times

Metal powder bed fusion is the method behind most serious industrial parts. A blade spreads a layer of metal powder a few hundredths of a millimetre thick, thinner than a human hair. A powerful laser traces the part's cross-section, melting the powder into solid metal. The bed drops, a new layer is spread, and the process repeats thousands of times inside a chamber filled with inert gas to stop the hot metal burning.

In effect, the machine performs millions of tiny welds. That creates engineering problems familiar from Materials engineering. Rapid heating and cooling leave residual stress locked inside the part, which can warp it. Tiny gas bubbles or unmelted spots, called porosity, can start fatigue cracks. So critical parts usually go through heat treatment, sometimes hot pressing in a gas chamber to close the pores, and machining of key surfaces. Then they are inspected, often with X-ray scanning.

For safety-critical parts, proving that every printed part is as good as the tested ones is often harder than printing them. That is why certification of printed aircraft parts took years.

Chapter 07

What printing makes possible

In conventional manufacturing, complexity costs money. Every hole, curve and internal channel needs another machining step or a more expensive mould. In 3D printing, a complex part takes about as long as a simple part of the same size. Engineers say complexity is free. That unlocks three things.

Internal channels

Cooling passages that follow a part's curves, impossible to drill. Moulds with them cool faster and make plastic parts quicker.

Lattices

Light internal structures like bone or honeycomb. Used in implants so bone can grow in, and in lightweight brackets.

Part consolidation

Many parts printed as one, as with the fuel nozzle. Fewer joints, less assembly, fewer leaks.

Software now helps designers use this freedom. In topology optimisation, the engineer tells the computer where a part is fixed and what loads it carries. The program removes material wherever it is not needed. The results often look organic, like bones or tree roots, because nature solves the same problem of carrying load with minimum material.

Chapter 08

Why it has not replaced the factory

The answer is speed and cost per part. An injection moulding machine can make a plastic part in seconds. A printer might take an hour for the same part. Metal printers build at a rate measured in small volumes per hour, and the machines and powders are expensive. Then come support removal, finishing and inspection.

The cost curve explains where printing wins. Its cost per part is roughly the same whether you make one or a thousand, because there is no mould to pay for. Moulding and stamping are the reverse: a large upfront cost, then very cheap parts. So printing wins for small numbers, custom shapes and parts that cannot be made any other way. Mass production wins for large numbers of simple parts. The crossover point depends on the part, and it moves slowly in printing's favour as machines get faster.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where it has already changed things

The revolution was real. It just happened in places most people never see.

Chapter 09

Medicine: every patient a batch of one

Medicine is full of products that must fit one person exactly. That is where printing shines. Clear dental aligners, the invisible braces used by millions, are typically formed over printed models of each patient's teeth at each stage of treatment, millions of unique models a year. Custom hearing-aid shells are now widely printed from scans of the ear canal. Surgeons use printed models of a patient's heart or skull to plan operations, and printed guides that clip onto bone to direct a drill or saw.

Implants are printed too. Titanium hip cups and spinal implants with lattice surfaces encourage bone to grow into them. Custom implants have been printed for patients who lost part of the jaw or skull. In 2015 the US approved the first 3D-printed drug, a tablet for epilepsy whose porous printed structure dissolves very quickly. Biomedical engineering covers the wider field (Biomedical engineering).

Chapter 10

Aerospace and rockets

In aircraft and spacecraft, every kilogram saved is worth a lot of money over the life of the vehicle, and production numbers are small. Both favour printing. Beyond fuel nozzles, printed brackets, ducts and engine parts fly on commercial aircraft.

Rocket companies have gone furthest. Rocket engines need complex internal cooling channels and are made in small numbers, perfect for printing. SpaceX prints the combustion chambers of its SuperDraco thrusters, and printed parts are now common across the launch industry. Printing lets rocket engineers change a design and test a new version in weeks rather than months.

Chapter 11

The real revolution: faster design

The biggest effect of 3D printing is easy to miss. It is rapid prototyping, the use of printers to make physical models of a new design within hours. Before printing, a prototype part could take weeks from a machine shop. Now an engineer can print a version in the evening, hold it, test it, and print a better one the next morning.

That changes how products are designed. Teams try more ideas and find problems earlier. Factories also print their own jigs and fixtures, the custom holders and guides used on assembly lines, cheaply and overnight. Many products made by injection moulding today were shaped by dozens of printed prototypes first. The printer did not replace the factory. It made the factory's designs better and faster to reach.

The difficult side

Printers can also make things societies want controlled. Printed guns, and kits for building untraceable firearms, have pushed governments to respond. In the United States a federal rule on so-called ghost gun kits was upheld by the Supreme Court in 2025. Rules differ widely between countries, and enforcing them against a design shared online is hard.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A specialist tool, not a universal one

Almost every advantage of printing is the mirror image of a disadvantage of moulding, and the reverse.

There are wider trade-offs too. Printing makes local production possible, which could shorten supply chains for spare parts. It also makes copying a design easy, raising questions about intellectual property. And the home printing revolution stalled for a simple reason. Most people do not want to design, print and finish their own objects, and a shop-bought one is usually cheaper and better.

Chapter 14

A complement, not a replacement

Printers have also flown to space. Since 2014 the International Space Station has had printers that make tools and small parts on board, testing the idea that future missions could print what they need rather than carry every spare. The wider vision is covered in Space manufacturing.

The most likely future on Earth is a hybrid factory. Printing will make the moulds, the jigs, the prototypes and the complex, low-volume or custom parts. Conventional methods will make the millions of simple ones. Some machines already combine printing and machining in one cell, building a rough shape and then cutting critical surfaces to precision.

The early hype got the direction right and the scale wrong. 3D printing did not put a factory in every home. It put a new tool in every factory and every design studio. That is less dramatic, and arguably more important.

The core idea

3D printing builds an object layer by layer from a digital file. It needs no tooling, so complexity and customisation are nearly free, but each part is slow and relatively expensive. It wins for prototypes, custom parts and shapes nothing else can make, and loses for mass-produced simple parts.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Stereolithography patent filed in 1984 by Chuck Hull.
  • GE's printed fuel nozzle: about 20 parts into 1, roughly 25% lighter.
  • Typical layers: about 0.05–0.3 mm thick.
  • First 3D-printed drug approved in the US: 2015.
The common mistake

Asking when 3D printing will replace factories. It mostly will not. It changes what factories can design and make, and takes over where volumes are small or shapes are impossible.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a printed plastic part may snap along its layers, and why a jet-engine maker still prints parts despite the cost? If so, you understand both the limits and the strengths.

The words, in plain words
Additive manufacturing
The industry term for 3D printing: making parts by adding material layer by layer.
CAD
Computer-aided design: software for drawing parts in three dimensions.
STL file
A common file format that describes a shape's surface as many small triangles.
Slicer
Software that cuts a 3D model into layers and plans the printer's path.
Infill
The internal pattern, often a honeycomb, used instead of solid material.
Supports
Temporary scaffolding printed under overhangs and removed afterwards.
Stereolithography
Printing by hardening liquid resin with light, layer by layer.
Powder bed fusion
Printing by melting thin layers of powder, often metal, with a laser or beam.
Anisotropy
Having different strength in different directions, such as weaker between layers.
Porosity
Tiny holes or gaps inside a part that weaken it.
Topology optimisation
Software that removes material wherever a part does not need it.
Rapid prototyping
Making quick physical models of a design to test and improve it.
Bioprinting
Printing with living cells to build tissue-like structures.
