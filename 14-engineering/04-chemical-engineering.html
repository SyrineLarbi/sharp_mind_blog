Chemical engineering. From a flask on the bench to a million tonnes a year.

A chemist discovers that two substances react to make something useful. That is the beginning, not the end. Someone then has to make the reaction happen continuously, in steel vessels the size of houses, safely, cheaply and with almost nothing wasted, for thirty years. That someone is a chemical engineer. The petrol in a car, the fertiliser that grows most of the world's food, the plastic in a phone and the pills in a medicine cabinet all came out of plants that chemical engineers designed and run.

Question 1 · What is it?

The engineering of processes

Chemical engineers do not design a product so much as a process: the chain of steps that turns raw materials into something people want, continuously and at scale.

Chapter 01

The reactors that kept bursting

In 1909 the German chemist Fritz Haber showed that nitrogen from the air and hydrogen could be combined into ammonia, using a catalyst at high temperature and very high pressure. His apparatus fitted on a laboratory bench and made roughly a cup of ammonia an hour. The chemical company BASF gave the job of making it work industrially to one of its chemists, Carl Bosch.

Bosch's problem was not chemistry. It was engineering. No one had ever run large steel vessels at such pressures and temperatures for weeks at a time. His early reactors failed after a short time. Investigation showed that hot hydrogen at high pressure was seeping into the steel and reacting with the carbon in it, making the metal brittle until it cracked. Bosch's team solved it with a clever design: a thin inner lining of low-carbon iron to face the hydrogen, inside a thick steel shell to take the pressure, with small holes to let any hydrogen that leaked through escape harmlessly. The first full plant opened at Oppau in 1913.

The gap between "this reaction works" and "this plant works" can take years of engineering, and it is often where the real invention happens. Bosch later received a Nobel Prize in Chemistry for high-pressure methods, one of the few awarded largely for engineering.

Chapter 02

Unit operations: the alphabet of every plant

Early chemical plants were each designed from scratch. Around 1915 the American consultant Arthur D. Little championed an idea that became the foundation of the discipline: unit operations. Every process, whether it makes soap, sugar or petrol, is built from the same small set of basic steps. Learn those steps once and you can design any plant.

A refinery, a brewery and a vaccine factory look nothing alike from outside. On a diagram they are all boxes from this table, joined by pipes. The diagram is called a process flow diagram, and it is the chemical engineer's basic drawing.

Chapter 03

Chemist and chemical engineer: different questions

A chemist asks: which reaction makes this molecule, and why? A chemical engineer asks: how do we make 500,000 tonnes of it a year, at a cost customers will pay, without poisoning anyone? The second question involves things the first ignores. How fast must heat be removed? How do we separate the product from leftover raw materials? What happens to the waste? What if the power fails at 3 a.m.?

That is why the degree mixes chemistry with physics, mathematics, economics and safety. A chemical engineer may never discover a new molecule. Their skill is to make a known chemistry happen reliably, cheaply and safely at enormous scale.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Balances, reactors, separations and scale

Four ideas carry most of the discipline: account for every gram and every joule, control the reaction, separate the product, and respect what changes when things get bigger.

Chapter 04

The balance sheet of matter and energy

Matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical plant. Whatever goes in must come out somewhere, as product, as waste, as emissions, or by building up inside the equipment. A mass balance writes this down for every stream: so many kilograms per hour in, so many out. An energy balance does the same for heat and work.

This sounds like simple bookkeeping, and it is. It is also the most powerful tool in the field. If the balance does not close, if a hundred kilograms go in and only ninety-seven can be found coming out, then something is leaking, reacting unexpectedly or being measured wrongly. Most process problems are found by noticing that the books do not balance.

Chapter 05

Reactors: speed, selectivity and heat

Reactors come in two broad types. A batch reactor is like a cooking pot: you fill it, run the reaction, empty it and start again. It suits small, high-value products such as medicines. A continuous reactor has feed flowing in and product flowing out all the time, sometimes for years without stopping. It suits huge volumes such as fuels and fertiliser, because it never wastes time filling and emptying.

Engineers watch three numbers. Conversion is how much of the raw material reacts. Selectivity is how much of what reacts becomes the product you want, rather than unwanted by-products. And yield combines the two. A catalyst, a substance that speeds a reaction without being used up, often matters most for selectivity, as the file on Catalysts explains.

Then there is heat. Many industrial reactions release heat, and heat speeds them up, which releases more heat. If cooling cannot keep pace, temperature and pressure climb faster and faster. That is a runaway reaction, and it is behind many of the industry's worst accidents. Controlling temperature is often the hardest part of reactor design.

Chapter 06

Separation: the expensive half

Reactions rarely produce a pure product. They leave a mixture of product, leftover feed, by-products and solvent. Getting the product out cleanly usually costs more than making it. In many plants, the separation equipment is far larger than the reactor.

The workhorse is distillation. Heat a liquid mixture and the parts with lower boiling points evaporate first. In a tall distillation column, vapour rises through a series of trays while liquid runs down, and at each tray the vapour gets a little richer in the lighter component. An oil refinery's tall towers are distillation columns, splitting crude oil into gas, petrol, kerosene, diesel and heavy oils according to boiling range.

Distillation is reliable but energy hungry, because it boils liquids again and again. A widely cited 2016 estimate by the chemical engineers David Sholl and Ryan Lively put the energy used for industrial separations at 10 to 15 percent of the world's total energy. Cheaper alternatives, such as membranes that filter molecules by size, are one of the field's big research goals.

Chapter 07

Moving heat around, and wasting less of it

A chemical plant is full of streams that need heating and others that need cooling. The hot product leaving a reactor must be cooled; the cold feed entering it must be warmed. A heat exchanger lets one stream warm the other through a metal wall, without mixing them. Arranging a whole plant's exchangers so that heat is reused as much as possible is called heat integration. In the 1970s and 1980s, after the oil price shocks, a method known as pinch analysis, developed largely at the University of Manchester by Bodo Linnhoff and colleagues, gave engineers a systematic way to do it. It cut energy use in many plants substantially.

This is a recurring theme. The cheapest unit of energy in a chemical plant is the one you already have and have not yet thrown away.

Chapter 08

Scale-up: why bigger is not just bigger

A reaction that behaves perfectly in a one-litre flask can misbehave in a 50,000-litre tank. The main reason is geometry. When you make a vessel bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area. Double the width of a tank and its volume rises about eight times, but its walls only about four times. Heat produced inside depends on volume; heat removed through the walls depends on surface. So large reactors are much harder to cool than small ones.

Mixing changes too. In a flask, a stirrer reaches everything in a second. In a huge tank, some regions may be poorly stirred, hotter or richer in one ingredient than others. So engineers scale up in steps: from the lab to a pilot plant, a small working version that might produce kilograms or tonnes, and only then to full size. Each step reveals problems that the previous one hid. Skipping steps to save time is one of the classic ways projects fail.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Food, medicine, materials, and a heavy carbon bill

Chemical engineering sits underneath most physical goods. It also carries a large share of industry's risks and emissions.

Chapter 09

Penicillin in tanks

Alexander Fleming noticed penicillin in 1928, but for more than a decade it could be made only in tiny amounts, by growing mould on the surface of liquid in flasks and bottles. During the Second World War, a joint effort by US and British scientists, government laboratories and drug companies changed that. The key step was deep-tank fermentation: growing the mould throughout large stirred, aerated tanks, using a higher-yielding strain found on a mouldy melon in Illinois. Pfizer, among others, turned this into large-scale production. By the 1944 Normandy landings, Allied forces had penicillin in substantial quantities.

The science of penicillin was biology. Getting enough of it to matter was chemical engineering: keeping huge tanks sterile, supplying oxygen evenly, and separating a delicate drug from a broth. Today's vaccines, insulin and antibody drugs depend on the same discipline, now called bioprocess engineering. The same skills underpin precision fermentation, described in Volume 15 (Precision fermentation).

Chapter 10

Process safety: Flixborough and what followed

On 1 June 1974, a chemical plant at Flixborough in England was destroyed by an explosion. Weeks earlier, a cracked reactor had been removed and replaced by a temporary pipe to keep production going. The pipe was not properly designed for the pressure and movement it faced. It failed, releasing a large cloud of hot cyclohexane, a flammable liquid, which ignited. Twenty-eight people died. Fortunately it was a Saturday, and the offices nearby were mostly empty.

Flixborough and Bhopal, ten years later (Volume 23), shaped modern process safety. Two ideas stand out. First, management of change: any modification to a plant, even a "temporary" pipe, must be reviewed by qualified engineers as carefully as the original design. Second, inherently safer design, championed by the British engineer Trevor Kletz: instead of adding more alarms and barriers around a hazard, reduce the hazard itself. Store less of the dangerous chemical, use a less dangerous one, or run at milder conditions. The chemistry side of this idea appears in Green chemistry.

HAZOP

A standard safety review is the hazard and operability study. A team walks through the plant drawing line by line and asks structured questions: what if there is more flow than intended? No flow? Reverse flow? Higher temperature? It is slow and tedious, and it finds problems that no individual would have spotted.

Chapter 11

A big share of industry's emissions

The chemical industry is one of the largest industrial energy users in the world. It burns fossil fuels for heat and also uses them as raw materials, because oil and gas supply the carbon and hydrogen that plastics, solvents and fertilisers are made of. Ammonia production alone is often estimated at around 1 to 2 percent of global CO₂ emissions, mostly from making hydrogen out of natural gas.

That makes chemical engineering central to cutting emissions, and also hard. Much of the heat is needed at very high temperatures. Plants last for decades, so today's investment decisions fix emissions until the 2050s. And the products are traded globally on thin profit margins, so a plant that pays more for clean energy can lose business to one that does not. Volume 11 covers two of the proposed answers, Green hydrogen and Carbon capture.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous efficiency, concentrated risk

Large continuous plants make things astonishingly cheaply. The same scale makes their failures large and their changes slow.

Chapter 13

The paradox of the cheap product

What went right

Fertiliser, plastics, clean water chemicals, detergents and medicines became cheap enough for billions of people. Process engineering is a major reason that a family can buy, for very little money, things that would have been luxuries a century ago.

What went wrong

Because products are so cheap, their hidden costs were long ignored: emissions, plastic waste and contamination around older sites. The industry's efficiency made overuse easy. Most of the current debates about chemicals are about pricing in those costs.

Chapter 15

Process intensification: doing more in less

For a century, the industry's rule was "bigger is cheaper". A newer school of thought, process intensification, asks whether plants could be far smaller instead. Ideas include combining a reactor and a separator in one unit, running reactions in tiny channels that remove heat almost instantly, and building plants from standard modules made in factories rather than on site.

Smaller plants could be placed next to where the raw materials or customers are, such as a small fertiliser unit beside a wind farm. They could be safer, because each holds less dangerous material. Whether they can beat the giant plants on cost is still uncertain. The future of chemical engineering may be decided by whether it can stay cheap while getting smaller and cleaner.

The core idea

Chemical engineering designs processes, built from a standard set of unit operations. It rests on mass and energy balances, careful reactor control, efficient separation, and stepwise scale-up, with process safety above everything.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Industrial separations use roughly 10–15 percent of world energy (a widely cited 2016 estimate).
  • Ammonia production causes around 1–2 percent of global CO₂ emissions.
  • Double a tank's width and its volume rises about 8×, its walls only about 4×.
The common mistake

Assuming that if a reaction works in the lab, making it at scale is a detail. Scale-up is where most of the engineering, cost and risk lie.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a reaction that is easy to control in a flask can run away in a large tank? If you can, you understand heat release, surface area and scale-up together.

The words, in plain words
Process
The whole chain of steps that turns raw materials into a product.
Unit operation
One standard step, such as distillation or filtration, used in many processes.
Process flow diagram
A drawing of a plant's units and the streams that join them.
Mass balance
An account showing that everything entering a process also leaves or builds up.
Batch reactor
A vessel filled, reacted and emptied in cycles.
Continuous reactor
A vessel with materials flowing in and out all the time.
Selectivity
The share of reacted material that becomes the wanted product.
Runaway reaction
A reaction whose heat speeds it up faster than cooling can remove the heat.
Distillation
Separating liquids by repeatedly boiling and condensing them.
Heat exchanger
Equipment that passes heat between two streams through a wall.
Pilot plant
A small working version of a process, built before full scale.
Inherently safer design
Reducing a hazard itself rather than adding protection around it.
HAZOP
A structured team review that asks "what if?" about every line of a plant.
