Engineering design. Deciding, under constraints, what a thing will be before anyone builds it.

Every bridge, phone, kettle and aircraft began as a problem and a blank page. Between the two lies engineering design: the disciplined process of turning a need into a precise description that a factory or a construction crew can follow, and that will still work, safely, years later in conditions nobody fully predicted. It is not mainly about drawing. It is about making hundreds of decisions, each one a trade-off, and checking them so carefully that the mistakes are found on paper rather than in the real world. When it fails, the consequences can be counted in lives. When it succeeds, nobody notices at all.

Question 1 · What is it?

Making decisions about things that do not exist yet

Engineering design is the work of choosing a solution that meets a need within limits of cost, safety, materials, time and law, and specifying it precisely enough to be built.

Chapter 01

The small change that brought down two walkways

On a July evening in 1981, about 1,600 people gathered for a tea dance in the atrium of the new Hyatt Regency hotel in Kansas City. Above them hung walkways on the second and fourth floors, suspended from the ceiling by steel rods. As people stood on the walkways to watch, the fourth-floor walkway tore loose and fell onto the second-floor one, and both crashed onto the crowd. 114 people died. It was, for a long time, the deadliest structural collapse in US history.

The investigation found that the original design used one long rod passing through both walkways, with each walkway held on that rod by a nut. That would have been hard to build. During construction the detail was changed to two shorter rods: one from the ceiling to the upper walkway, and a second from the upper walkway down to the lower one. It looked like a minor simplification. In fact it meant the upper walkway's connection now carried the weight of both walkways instead of one. The original design was already weaker than the building code required. The change roughly doubled the load on a connection that could not take it.

The engineers responsible lost their licences. The case is taught in engineering schools worldwide, because it shows that in design, a detail is never just a detail. Every change has to be checked against how forces actually flow through the structure.

Chapter 02

What makes design different from science

Science tries to find out how the world works. Design uses that knowledge to decide what to make. The difference matters. A scientific question usually has one right answer. A design problem has many acceptable answers and no perfect one. Ask a hundred engineers to design a chair and you get a hundred chairs, most of which work.

Design problems are also open-ended and under-specified. The customer says "a cheaper car" but does not say how cheap, how safe, how fast or how long it must last. Part of the designer's job is to turn vague wishes into measurable targets. Another part is to make decisions before all the information exists, because waiting for perfect knowledge means never building anything. Engineering design is the art of deciding well under uncertainty, and then checking the decision hard enough that the uncertainty does not kill anyone.

Chapter 03

Requirements and constraints

Every design starts with two lists. Requirements say what the thing must do: carry four people, lift 500 kilograms, run for ten hours on a charge. Constraints are the limits it must respect: a budget, a weight, the size of a doorway, the law, the materials and factories available. A good requirement is measurable and testable. "Light" is a wish. "Under 1.2 kilograms" is a requirement.

Kind of requirementQuestion it answersExample for a bicycle helmet
FunctionalWhat must it do?Absorb a defined impact without passing too much force to the head
PerformanceHow well?Pass the impact tests of the relevant safety standard
Safety and legalWhat rules apply?Meet the required national or regional certification
ManufacturingCan we make it?Moulded in one piece on existing machines
UserWill people use it?Comfortable, ventilated, adjustable, not ugly
CostCan we afford it?Under a target factory cost per unit

The user row is easy to underrate. A helmet that passes every lab test but is too hot to wear protects nobody. A design that people will not use as intended has failed, however good the engineering.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A loop of ideas, choices and tests

Textbooks draw the design process as a line of steps. In practice it is a loop: each test teaches something that sends the team back to an earlier step.

Chapter 04

In the concept stage, the team deliberately produces many different ideas before judging any of them, because the first idea is rarely the best. In detailed design, they settle every dimension, material, tolerance and fastener, usually in computer-aided design (CAD) software, which produces exact 3D models and drawings. Testing checks the design against the requirements. Large, complex projects such as aircraft use a formal approach called systems engineering, which breaks the whole into subsystems, gives each its own requirements, and then tests the pieces and the assembled whole against them.

Chapter 05

Trade-offs: you cannot have everything

Almost every requirement fights another. Stronger usually means heavier. Lighter usually means more expensive. Safer usually means slower to build. A famous rule of thumb in project work says: good, fast, cheap, pick two. Design is largely the art of choosing which way to lean.

Engineers make these choices visible with simple tools. A decision matrix lists candidate concepts in rows and criteria in columns, gives each criterion a weight, and scores each concept. One well-known version, the Pugh matrix, named after the British design professor Stuart Pugh, compares every concept against a reference design as better, the same, or worse. The numbers do not make the decision. Their value is that they force a team to say out loud what it cares about, so arguments are about priorities rather than personalities.

Optimised versus robust

A design tuned perfectly for one set of conditions is often fragile when conditions change. A racing car is superb on a smooth track and useless on a farm road. Engineers often prefer a robust design, one that performs acceptably across a wide range of conditions, over an optimised one that performs brilliantly in a narrow band.

Chapter 06

Safety factors: the margin for what you do not know

No engineer knows the exact strength of every batch of steel, the exact weight a floor will carry, or the exact wind a tower will face. So they design for more than they expect. The ratio between what a part can take and what it is expected to take is the factor of safety. A factor of 2 means the part should survive twice the expected load.

The size of the factor reflects how much is unknown and how bad failure would be. Aircraft structures are typically designed to withstand 1.5 times the largest load they are ever expected to meet, a low factor, because extra weight is very costly in flight and because aircraft materials, loads and inspections are tightly controlled. Lifts, cranes and climbing ropes use much larger factors, because loads are less predictable and a failure is catastrophic. A safety factor is an honest admission of ignorance, written as a number.

Margins can also be eaten away quietly. Each small change, a heavier component, a cheaper supplier, a new use the designers never imagined, uses up a little of the margin. This slow erosion is behind many accidents, including the gradual acceptance of damaged seals described in the Challenger chapter.

Chapter 07

Designing for failure

Good designers assume things will break and ask what happens next. A systematic way of doing this is failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), developed by the US military in the 1940s and later adopted in aerospace and car making. For each part, the team lists every way it could fail, what the effect would be, how likely it is, and how easily it would be noticed. The worst combinations get redesigned first.

Redundancy

Have more than one. Airliners have several independent hydraulic systems, so losing one does not mean losing control. Redundancy only works if the copies cannot all fail for the same reason.

Fail-safe

When it fails, fail into a safe state. Railway signals default to "stop" if they lose power. A lift's emergency brakes clamp on if the cable goes slack.

Defence in depth

Several independent barriers, each imperfect. The psychologist James Reason pictured them as slices of Swiss cheese: an accident happens only when the holes in every slice line up.

Chapter 08

Prototypes, simulation and testing

Before they flew in 1903, Wilbur and Orville Wright built a small wind tunnel in their bicycle workshop and tested a large number of wing shapes, because they had found that published data on lift was unreliable. Their success came less from a single insight than from careful, cheap, repeated testing. The inventor James Dyson has said he built more than five thousand prototypes before his first bagless vacuum cleaner worked as he wanted. The principle is the same: make mistakes when they are cheap, on paper, in a computer or in a cardboard model, not after a product is in a million homes.

Today much of that testing happens in software. Finite element analysis divides a part into thousands or millions of tiny pieces and calculates the stress on each under load, showing where it would bend or crack. Fluid simulations predict airflow over a car or through an engine. Simulation is fast and cheap, but it is only as good as its assumptions, so physical tests remain essential, especially for anything safety-critical. Cars are still crashed into walls. Aircraft wings are still bent in test rigs until they break.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Design decides cost, safety and how people behave

The choices made at the drawing stage fix most of what a product will cost, how safe it is, and how easily people will use it correctly.

Chapter 09

Most of the cost is decided early

A widely repeated observation in manufacturing is that the large majority of a product's lifetime cost is committed during design, long before much money has actually been spent. Once the shape, materials and number of parts are fixed, the factory can only make them more or less efficiently. Changing a design is cheap early and very expensive late: moving a line on a screen costs minutes, while changing a part after tooling has been made, or recalling a product already sold, can cost millions.

This is why approaches such as design for manufacture and assembly exist. They ask designers to reduce the number of parts, use standard components, and make parts that snap together in only one way. Fewer parts mean fewer suppliers, fewer assembly steps and fewer things to go wrong. The same logic applies to maintenance: a machine designed to be easy to inspect and repair is cheaper for its whole life, as the previous topic on infrastructure explained.

Chapter 10

Designing for real people

In 1988 the cognitive scientist Don Norman published a book, later titled The Design of Everyday Things, that changed how engineers think about users. His famous example is the door you push when you should pull. If people keep getting a door wrong, he argued, the fault is the door's, not theirs: a flat plate says "push", a handle says "pull", and a door that sends the wrong signal is badly designed. Such doors are now often called "Norman doors".

The stakes can be far higher than a door. During the 1979 accident at the Three Mile Island nuclear plant in Pennsylvania, a light in the control room showed that a signal had been sent to close a relief valve, not that the valve had actually closed. It had stuck open. Operators, believing it was shut, made decisions that worsened the accident. The lesson for designers is that an interface must show the real state of the machine, not what the machine was told to do. This field is called human factors or ergonomics, and in aviation, medicine and power plants it is now a required part of design.

Chapter 11

How the profession learns from failure

In his 1985 book To Engineer Is Human, the engineer and historian Henry Petroski argued that failure is central to engineering progress. Success tells you a design worked this time. Failure tells you where the limits are. Three cases show the pattern.

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State, opened in 1940, was elegant and slender. Four months later, in a steady wind, it began to twist in growing waves and tore itself apart. Nobody was killed. Engineers had designed it for the static force of wind but not for the way wind and a flexible deck could feed energy into each other. Since then, long bridges are tested as models in wind tunnels.

London's Millennium Bridge, a footbridge across the Thames, opened in June 2000 and began to sway sideways as crowds crossed. Walkers instinctively adjusted their steps to the movement, which pushed it further, a feedback effect that designers had not anticipated at that scale. It closed within days, was fitted with dampers to absorb the motion, and reopened in 2002.

And the Hyatt Regency collapse of Chapter 01 changed how responsibility for design changes is assigned and checked. Each famous failure becomes a rule, a test or a line in a design code, so that the next generation of engineers does not have to learn it the same way.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Discipline that saves lives, and can also slow things down

Formal design methods are the reason modern engineering is so reliable. They also carry costs that engineers argue about.

Chapter 12

Weighing the methods

Formal process and reviewsCatches errors early; creates a record of why decisions were made.Can become box-ticking, slow and bureaucratic.
Codes and standardsCarry lessons from past failures; make designs comparable and certifiable.Lag behind new materials and methods; can discourage innovation.

The tension in the last row is real and ongoing. Some companies, notably in rocketry, have deliberately accepted test failures in order to learn faster, while traditional aerospace has favoured exhaustive analysis before any flight. The right pace depends on who bears the risk: a test rocket with no one aboard can fail; a passenger aircraft cannot.

Chapter 13

The engineer's responsibility

In many countries engineers who sign off on public safety work must be licensed or chartered, and professional codes of ethics state that the safety of the public comes first. In Canada, many engineering graduates take part in a ceremony and receive an iron ring, worn on the little finger of the working hand, as a reminder of their obligations. A popular story links the rings to the Quebec Bridge collapses of the early 20th century; historians say that link is more legend than fact.

The hardest situations are like Challenger: an engineer who believes something is unsafe, under pressure from schedule and management. Design processes can help by making the evidence visible and giving engineers formal ways to stop work. But the final safeguard is still an individual willing to say no, and organisations that listen when they do.

Chapter 15

What stays the same

Tools change quickly. In a working lifetime, engineers went from drawing boards to CAD, from hand calculations to simulation, and now to AI assistance. The core of design changes slowly. Someone still has to understand what people actually need, turn it into measurable requirements, weigh trade-offs honestly, and ask what will happen when something goes wrong.

If anything, more powerful tools raise the stakes of that judgement. A computer can produce a thousand candidate designs in an afternoon, but it cannot know that the requirement it was given was wrong. The most dangerous error in design is solving the wrong problem perfectly, and catching it remains a human job.

The core idea

Engineering design turns a need into a buildable specification through a loop of requirements, concepts, choices and tests. It is about trade-offs under uncertainty, protected by safety factors, failure analysis and testing, and improved by learning from every failure.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hyatt Regency walkway collapse, 1981: 114 deaths from one changed detail.
  • Aircraft structures: typically designed for 1.5 times the largest expected load.
  • Most of a product's lifetime cost is committed in design, before it is spent.
The common mistake

Treating small design changes as harmless. Every change must be checked against the whole design, because it can quietly double a load or eat a margin.

The words, in plain words
Requirement
A measurable statement of what a design must do.
Constraint
A limit the design must respect, such as cost, size, weight or law.
Concept
An early, rough idea for how to meet the requirements.
CAD
Computer-aided design: software for making precise 3D models and drawings.
Systems engineering
A method for designing complex projects by breaking them into subsystems with their own requirements and tests.
Trade-off
Accepting less of one good quality to get more of another.
Decision matrix
A table that scores options against weighted criteria.
Factor of safety
How many times stronger a part is than the load it is expected to carry.
FMEA
Failure mode and effects analysis: listing every way a part can fail and what would follow.
Redundancy
Duplicate systems, so one failure does not cause total loss.
Fail-safe
Designed to fall into a safe state when something fails.
Finite element analysis
Computer simulation that splits a part into tiny pieces to calculate stresses.
Human factors
Designing machines around how people actually perceive, think and act.
