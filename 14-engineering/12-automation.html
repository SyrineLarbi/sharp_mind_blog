Automation. Every machine that runs by itself hands people a different job, not no job.

Automation is older than electricity and newer than this morning's software update. It is the loom that reads its pattern from cards, the thermostat that switches the boiler, the controller that runs a refinery through the night, and the script that files invoices while the office sleeps. Each one takes over a task that a person once did. This file explains how automated systems actually work, why they make some jobs disappear and others grow, and why the most automated systems often fail in the most surprising ways.

Question 1 · What is it?

Handing a task to a machine, start to finish

Automation means using machines, controllers or software to carry out a process with little or no human action while it runs.

Chapter 01

The loom that read cards

Weaving a patterned silk was once slow, skilled work. For each pass of the thread, a helper had to lift exactly the right set of threads by hand, following the pattern. Around 1804 the French weaver Joseph-Marie Jacquard perfected a loom attachment that did this automatically. The pattern was stored as a chain of stiff cards with holes punched in them. Where there was a hole, a rod passed through and its thread was lifted. Where there was none, the thread stayed down. Change the cards and the same loom wove a different pattern.

Two ideas lived in those cards. The first was that a machine could follow a sequence of instructions without a person deciding each step. The second was that the instructions could be separated from the machine and changed. The English mathematician Charles Babbage planned to use punched cards to program his Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer designed in the 1830s. His collaborator Ada Lovelace drew the comparison directly.

"We may say most aptly that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves."

Ada Lovelace · notes on the Analytical Engine, 1843

The Jacquard loom also met fierce resistance from weavers in Lyon, who feared for their livelihoods. Automation and the argument about jobs were born together.

Chapter 02

Kinds of automation

Engineers distinguish automation by how easily it can be changed and by what it handles.

KindWhat it isExample
FixedMachines built for one sequence, very fast, hard to changeA bottling line, a dedicated engine-block machining line
ProgrammableMachines that can be reprogrammed between batchesComputer-controlled machine tools, robot arms
FlexibleSwitches between products with almost no pauseA car line building different models one after another
ProcessContinuous control of flows, temperatures and pressuresRefineries, power stations, water treatment plants
SoftwarePrograms that carry out office and information tasksAutomatic invoice matching, scheduled reports, chatbots

Automation is also a matter of degree. A car with cruise control is partly automated. A car with lane-keeping and adaptive cruise is more so. The industry body SAE International defines six levels of driving automation, from 0 (none) to 5 (no driver needed anywhere). Most cars on sale in the mid-2020s sit at level 2, where the driver must stay in charge at all times. Most real automation is partial: the machine does some of the job and a person does the rest. That boundary, as Question 3 shows, is where many problems live.

Chapter 03

Sense, decide, act

Every automated system, from a toaster to a chemical plant, does three things that a person used to do.

Sense

Sensors measure the world: temperature, position, pressure, a camera image, a barcode, a new email.

Decide

A controller, a chip or a computer program, compares what it sensed with what it should be and chooses an action.

Act

Actuators change the world: motors, valves, heaters, a robot gripper, or a database record updated.

The Jacquard loom had no sensing at all. It followed its cards whatever happened. That is called open-loop control. A thermostat measures the room and switches the heating on or off in response. That is closed-loop, or feedback, control. The difference matters enormously. An open-loop system cannot notice when things go wrong. A closed-loop system corrects itself, within limits.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From governors to controllers to code

Automation runs in layers, from sensors on the factory floor up to software that plans the day's production. Each layer has its own technology.

Chapter 04

Feedback, the oldest trick

Steam engines in the late eighteenth century had a problem. When the load changed, their speed changed. James Watt adapted a device called the centrifugal governor: two heavy balls on hinged arms, spun by the engine. When the engine sped up, the balls flew outwards and, through a linkage, partly closed the steam valve. When it slowed, the balls dropped and the valve opened. The engine held its own speed without anyone touching it.

This is feedback in its purest form. The output, speed, is measured and used to adjust the input, steam. The same principle keeps your body temperature steady, your car's cruise control on target and an aircraft level. Getting feedback right is subtle: correct too weakly and the system drifts, correct too strongly and it overshoots and oscillates. That is the subject of Control systems.

Chapter 05

The factory's brain: the PLC

Until the late 1960s, factory machines were controlled by large cabinets of electrical relays, switches that click open and shut. Changing the sequence meant rewiring hundreds of them, which could take weeks. In 1968 General Motors asked for something better. An engineer called Dick Morley and his colleagues developed the programmable logic controller, or PLC, a rugged industrial computer whose logic could be changed by reprogramming instead of rewiring. Their company called it the Modicon.

PLCs are now everywhere: in factories, water plants, lifts, airport baggage systems and traffic lights. They are built to survive heat, dust and electrical noise, and to run the same loop reliably for years. Many are still programmed in ladder logic, a graphical language that looks like the old relay wiring diagrams, so electricians could understand it.

The layers of a typical automated plant
  BUSINESS SYSTEMS    orders, stock, planning              (hours to days)
         |
  PLANT SOFTWARE      schedules, tracks every batch        (minutes)
         |
  SUPERVISORY (SCADA) operator screens, alarms, trends     (seconds)

  CONTROLLERS (PLCs)  run each machine's logic             (milliseconds)

  SENSORS / ACTUATORS switches, gauges, motors, valves     (the physical world)

SCADA, short for supervisory control and data acquisition, is the layer where human operators watch the whole plant on screens, see alarms and change settings. Notice the time scales. The lower the layer, the faster it must react, and the less a human is involved.

Chapter 06

Plants that run themselves at night

In a modern oil refinery, chemical works or power station, thousands of control loops run at once, each holding a temperature, pressure, level or flow at its target. A small team in a control room supervises the whole site. The system that coordinates the loops is called a distributed control system, because control is spread across many controllers instead of one central computer that could fail.

Here the operator's job is not to run the plant. The automation does that. The operator's job is to handle what the automation cannot: start-ups, shutdowns, unusual conditions and emergencies. Those are exactly the moments when the plant is most dangerous and the operator has had the least practice. Chapter 10 returns to this.

Because these systems control physical processes, their security matters. In 2010 a piece of malicious software called Stuxnet, widely attributed to the United States and Israel, was discovered to have targeted the controllers of uranium centrifuges in Iran, making them spin destructively while showing operators normal readings. It showed that attacks on automation can cause physical damage.

Chapter 07

Robot arms and the structured world

The first industrial robot, the Unimate, went to work at a General Motors plant in New Jersey in 1961. It lifted hot metal parts from a die-casting machine, a dangerous and unpleasant job. It was designed by George Devol and Joseph Engelberger. Today millions of industrial robots work in factories, mostly welding, painting, assembling and moving parts in car and electronics plants. South Korea has the highest density, roughly one robot for every ten manufacturing workers by the early 2020s.

These robots are fast, precise and tireless, but traditionally they are not clever. They repeat programmed movements in a carefully arranged space, often behind safety fences. Factory automation works by making the world predictable for the machine: parts arrive in the same place, the same way round, every time. When that is not possible, as with soft, tangled or varied objects, people are still often better. Volume 50 explains why (Robots, vehicles and why the easy things are hard), and Robotics covers the machines in depth.

Chapter 08

Automating the office

Automation is not only physical. Much of it runs in offices. A payroll program, an online bank transfer, an airline's automatic rebooking, a spreadsheet macro: each takes over work that clerks once did by hand. Since the 2010s, companies have used robotic process automation, software that copies the clicks and keystrokes a person makes across different programs, for repetitive tasks such as copying data from emails into accounting systems.

Traditional software automation follows exact rules, so it breaks when an input looks unexpected. Machine learning and language models are widening the range, handling messy inputs such as scanned letters, free-text emails and customer questions. For the first time, automation reaches tasks that need judgement about language and images, not just following rules. Whether that changes the old pattern of automation and jobs is the open question in Question 5.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Prosperity, jobs, and the strange new ways to fail

Automation made modern living standards possible. It also reshapes who works, and it changes the kind of mistakes that happen.

Chapter 09

Tasks, not jobs

Between 1811 and 1816, English textile workers known as Luddites smashed machines that threatened their skilled trades. They were not simply against technology. They were protesting falling wages and lost status, and many of them did lose out. Yet over the following century, mechanised textiles made clothing far cheaper, and total employment grew. Both things were true: society gained, and specific people lost.

Economists today usually analyse automation in terms of tasks rather than whole jobs. A job is a bundle of tasks. Automation takes over some of them, which changes the job rather than deleting it. The cash machine is the classic example. When ATMs spread in the United States, many expected bank tellers to disappear. For decades they did not, because cheaper branches meant banks opened more of them, and tellers shifted towards customer service and sales. Teller numbers did decline later, as online banking grew.

Research led by the economist David Autor and others found another pattern in rich countries from the 1980s onwards. Automation hit routine middle-skill jobs hardest: clerks, machine operators, bookkeepers. Jobs grew at the top, in professional work, and at the bottom, in personal services that are hard to automate. This job polarisation is one of the explanations offered for rising wage inequality. How much of that inequality is due to automation, as opposed to trade, policy or other causes, remains debated.

Chapter 10

The ironies of automation

In 1983 the British psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper called "Ironies of Automation". Its argument has proved remarkably durable. When you automate a system, you leave the human the tasks the designers could not automate, which are the hardest ones. At the same time, because the machine does the everyday work, the human gets little practice. So the more reliable the automation, the less prepared the person is for the moment it fails.

Air France Flight 447 is the most cited example. In June 2009, flying from Rio de Janeiro to Paris over the Atlantic at night, the aircraft's speed sensors iced up briefly. The autopilot, receiving inconsistent readings, disconnected and handed control to the pilots. In the confusion that followed, the crew made inputs that put the aircraft into a stall, and it fell into the ocean. All 228 people on board died. The French investigators' report pointed among other things to the pilots' lack of training in manual flight at high altitude and in recognising a stall.

The aviation industry responded with more training in hand-flying and in handling surprises. The broader lesson applies to control rooms, hospitals and cars with driver assistance: a human "supervisor" who has nothing to do for hours is not a reliable safety system. This problem is called automation complacency.

Chapter 11

When automation overrules the people

The opposite failure is automation that acts against the humans. In October 2018 and March 2019, two Boeing 737 MAX airliners crashed shortly after take-off, in Indonesia and Ethiopia, killing 346 people in total. Both were brought down by a flight-control feature called MCAS. It was designed to push the aircraft's nose down automatically in certain conditions. It relied on a single sensor measuring the angle of the wing to the airflow. When that sensor failed, MCAS repeatedly pushed the nose down. Many pilots had not been told the system existed.

The aircraft was grounded worldwide for about twenty months. Investigations by US congressional committees and others criticised the design, the reliance on one sensor, and how the system was assessed and certified. Automation needs to be designed together with the people who will work alongside it: they must know what it does, see when it is acting, and be able to stop it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Cheaper, safer, faster, and more brittle at the edges

Automation's gains are large and spread widely. Its costs are concentrated and its failures can be unusual.

Chapter 12

The balance sheet

AspectAdvantageDisadvantage
ProductivityMore output per worker. The long-run source of higher living standards.Gains may flow mainly to owners of capital unless wages or policy share them.
SafetyMachines take over dangerous, dirty and heavy work: hot castings, toxic paint, deep mines.New failure modes: automation surprises, complacency, cyberattack.
Quality and consistencyEvery part done the same way. Fewer human slips.Errors in the program are repeated perfectly, thousands of times.
WorkDrudgery removed. New jobs in design, programming and maintenance.Job losses concentrated in particular places and skills. Remaining work can be deskilled or tightly monitored.
FlexibilityRuns day and night without fatigue.Expensive to change. Copes badly with surprises outside its design.
Human skillPeople freed for judgement and oversight.Skills fade when unused, just when they are needed most.

The table hides a timing problem. The benefits of automation are usually broad and slow, while the costs arrive fast and land on specific people. A cheaper product helps millions a little. A closed plant hurts one town a lot. That asymmetry explains why automation is so politically charged, even when economists judge its total effect positive.

Chapter 14

Who shares the gains

The technical question of what can be automated is increasingly joined by a political one: how the gains are shared. Proposals range widely. Some, including Bill Gates in 2017, have suggested taxing robots that replace workers. Many economists object that this would be hard to define and would slow productivity. Others favour retraining programmes, wage insurance for displaced workers, shorter working weeks, or a universal basic income. None has been tested at the scale the most dramatic forecasts would require.

There is also an engineering answer. Designers can choose to build automation that supports people rather than replaces them: systems that keep humans skilled and informed, take over the tedious parts, and hand back control clearly. That approach, developed in aviation after the hard lessons of Chapters 10 and 11, is now spreading into medicine, driving and office software. The future of automation is not decided only by what machines can do. It is also decided by how we choose to design the work around them.

The core idea

Automation hands a process's sensing, deciding and acting to machines. Feedback lets them correct themselves. Most automation is partial, so the critical design problem is the boundary with the human. Its economic effect is usually to change tasks within jobs, with broad gains and concentrated losses.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Jacquard's card-driven loom: about 1804. First industrial robot: 1961.
  • The PLC was developed for General Motors from 1968.
  • SAE defines six levels of driving automation, 0 to 5.
  • Two 737 MAX crashes, 2018–19: 346 dead, linked to one automated system.
The common mistake

Assuming that automating a task removes the human from it. It moves the human to the hardest part: monitoring, and taking over in a crisis, often with less practice than before.

The words, in plain words
Automation
Using machines or software to carry out a process with little human action while it runs.
Sensor
A device that measures something in the world, such as temperature or position.
Actuator
A device that makes something happen, such as a motor or a valve.
Controller
The part that decides what the actuators should do, based on what the sensors report.
Open-loop control
Following a fixed sequence without checking the result.
Closed-loop control
Measuring the result and adjusting to correct it; also called feedback.
PLC
Programmable logic controller: a rugged industrial computer that runs machines.
SCADA
The supervisory layer where operators watch and adjust a whole plant.
Distributed control system
Control spread across many linked controllers, used in large process plants.
Robotic process automation
Software that copies a person's clicks and keystrokes to do repetitive office tasks.
Job polarisation
Growth of high- and low-paid jobs while routine middle jobs shrink.
Automation complacency
People relaxing their attention because the machine usually gets it right.
