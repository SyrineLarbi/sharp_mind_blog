Smart buildings. A building that senses, decides and adjusts, ideally without anyone noticing.

Most buildings are dumb in a precise sense. Their heating runs on a timetable, their lights stay on in empty rooms, and their ventilation blows the same air whether a meeting room holds two people or twenty. A smart building adds three things: sensors that notice what is happening, a network that carries the information, and control software that acts on it. Done well, the result uses much less energy, feels more comfortable and can even help balance the power grid. Done badly, it is an expensive collection of gadgets that nobody understands and that a hacker can walk through.

Question 1 · What is it?

A building with a nervous system

A smart building is one whose systems, heating, cooling, ventilation, lighting, lifts, security and power, are measured and controlled together by software, rather than each running on its own fixed settings.

Chapter 01

Monday morning in a building that is paying attention

It is 6:30 on a winter Monday. The building has been cool all weekend to save energy. Its software knows from the weather forecast that the morning will be cold and from past Mondays how long the third floor takes to warm up, so it starts the heat pumps early, at a time chosen when electricity is cheapest. By 8:00 the offices are at 21 °C.

At 9:00 a meeting room fills with twelve people. A carbon dioxide sensor on the wall sees the level climbing, because people breathe out CO2, and the ventilation system brings in more fresh air. Along the windows, light sensors notice bright sunshine and dim the lamps nearest the glass. On the fifth floor, half the desks are empty because many staff work from home on Mondays. Occupancy sensors notice, and that zone's heating and lights stay low.

None of this is science fiction. Each part has existed for years. What makes a building "smart" is not any single device but the fact that the devices share information and act on it together.

The heart of it in a commercial building is the building management system (BMS), sometimes called a building automation system. It is the software where engineers set schedules and setpoints, see alarms and track energy. In a home, the equivalent is usually a smart thermostat and a phone app, which is a much simpler version of the same idea.

Chapter 03

From air pressure to software

Large buildings have been automatically controlled for most of a century. Early systems were pneumatic: thermostats sent signals as small changes in air pressure through thin tubes to valves and dampers. From the 1980s, direct digital control replaced the tubes with microprocessors. In 1995 the American heating and ventilation engineers' society, ASHRAE, published a standard communication protocol called BACnet, so equipment from different makers could talk to each other. In Europe a parallel standard called KNX became common, especially for lighting and homes.

The latest step is cheap wireless sensors and cloud software, the building version of the Internet of Things. A sensor that used to need a cable costing more than the sensor can now be stuck on a wall with a battery that lasts years. Measurement became cheap, and that changed what was worth controlling.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Control loops, layered on control loops

A smart building is a stack of the feedback loops described in Control systems, coordinated by software that sets their targets.

Chapter 04

Heating, cooling and ventilation: where most of the energy goes

In most commercial buildings, the largest energy users are heating, cooling and ventilation, grouped by engineers as HVAC. A typical system has a central plant that makes hot or chilled water, pipes that carry it, and air-handling units that blow conditioned air through ducts into rooms. Each part has its own loop: a valve opens until the water reaches its target temperature; a fan speeds up until the duct pressure is right; a damper in each room opens until the room reaches its setpoint.

Three techniques produce most of the savings. Scheduling stops heating and cooling empty buildings. Variable-speed drives let fans and pumps run slower when less is needed; because the power a fan uses rises steeply with its speed, running at a lower speed saves a disproportionate amount of energy. Demand-controlled ventilation uses CO2 sensors to bring in outside air in proportion to how many people are present, instead of a fixed maximum. Fresh outside air has to be heated or cooled, so bringing in only what is needed saves energy without making the air stale.

Why CO2 is a clever measurement

Carbon dioxide at indoor levels is not the main danger. It is useful as a proxy, a stand-in measurement, for how much breathed-out air is in the room, and therefore for how well the room is ventilated. That made CO2 monitors popular during the COVID-19 pandemic, when stale air became a recognised infection risk.

Chapter 05

Light, presence and the people inside

Lighting was once a large share of office electricity. LED lamps cut that sharply on their own, and controls cut it further. Occupancy sensors switch lights off in empty spaces. Daylight harvesting dims lamps near windows when the sun is bright. Because LEDs can be dimmed precisely and each fitting can carry a small sensor, lighting networks have become a convenient way to spread sensors across a whole ceiling.

Knowing where people are is useful well beyond lights. Desk sensors, badge readers and Wi-Fi connection counts tell managers how much of the space is actually used. After the pandemic, many offices found occupancy far below what they were built for, and data from these systems has driven decisions to close floors or redesign them. The most energy-efficient square metre is the one you no longer need to heat. The same data, of course, raises questions about monitoring employees, which Chapter 13 returns to.

Chapter 06

The integration problem: devices that do not speak the same language

A real building is rarely designed as one system. The heating comes from one supplier, the lighting from another, the lifts from a third, the access control from a fourth, often installed years apart. Each speaks its own protocol or a different dialect of a standard one. Getting them to share data is often the hardest, most expensive part of a smart building project.

Standards help. BACnet and KNX in commercial buildings, and in homes a standard called Matter, launched in 2022 by a group of large technology companies, aim to let devices from different brands work together. Another effort tries to standardise the meaning of data, so that software can know that "AHU-3 SAT" is the supply-air temperature of air-handling unit three. Without that, every building's data is a private language understood only by the people who installed it.

Chapter 07

Finding the faults nobody noticed

Buildings fail quietly. A valve sticks half open, so heating and cooling run at the same time and fight each other. A sensor drifts, so a room is always slightly too cold and someone brings in an electric heater. A schedule set for a special event is never reset, so a floor stays lit every night. Nothing breaks and no alarm sounds, but energy leaks away for years.

Modern analytics software looks for exactly these patterns, a practice called automated fault detection and diagnostics. It compares how equipment behaves with how it should behave and flags the difference. Some systems go further, using a detailed computer model of the building, a digital twin, to test changes before making them. Studies of commissioning, the process of checking that a building's systems actually work as designed, have long found that it pays for itself quickly in energy savings. Much of a building's "smartness" is simply noticing that it is doing something stupid.

Chapter 08

Buildings that help the grid

As grids rely more on wind and solar (see the previous topic), the time at which electricity is used starts to matter as much as the amount. Buildings are well placed to help, because they store heat. A building can be cooled a little extra at noon when solar power is plentiful and then let its temperature drift up slowly through the expensive evening peak, with occupants barely noticing. Its concrete and water tanks act as a thermal battery.

Add electric-vehicle chargers, on-site batteries and solar panels, and the building becomes a small power system of its own. The US Department of Energy uses the term grid-interactive efficient buildings for ones that can shift, reduce or modulate their demand in response to grid signals or prices. Pooled together, thousands of such buildings can act like a power station that produces flexibility instead of electricity.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where we spend our lives and much of our energy

Buildings are one of the largest energy users on the planet, and also the place where most people spend most of their time. Both facts make their control unusually important.

Chapter 09

The energy stakes

The International Energy Agency estimates that operating buildings accounts for roughly 30 percent of the world's final energy use, and a little over a quarter of energy-related CO2 emissions when the electricity they use is counted. Most of that is heating, cooling, hot water and lighting. Cooling is the fastest-growing part, as incomes rise in hot countries and air conditioners spread.

Controls are among the cheapest ways to cut this. Replacing a building's walls or windows is expensive and disruptive. Fixing its schedules, setpoints and faults is mostly software and attention. Savings in the range of ten to thirty percent from better controls and commissioning are widely reported, though results vary greatly from building to building. For an existing building, better control is often the fastest, cheapest energy saving available.

Chapter 10

Health, comfort and productivity

People in rich countries are often said to spend about 90 percent of their time indoors. Indoor air quality therefore matters a great deal for health. Poor ventilation concentrates airborne viruses, moisture and pollutants from cleaning products, furniture and cooking. Research, including controlled studies at Harvard on office workers, has found that better-ventilated offices are linked with better performance on thinking tasks, although the size of the effect varies between studies.

Comfort is also a control problem. Thermal comfort depends on air temperature, humidity, air movement, the temperature of nearby surfaces, clothing and activity. Classic office temperature standards were based on older assumptions about typical occupants and clothing, and researchers have argued that they can leave many people feeling cold. A smart building can give occupants some local control, a little warmer at this desk, cooler in that room, which tends to raise satisfaction even when the averages barely change.

Chapter 11

The performance gap

One of the most consistent findings in building research is the performance gap: buildings often use substantially more energy in operation than their designers predicted, sometimes much more. Causes include construction that differs from the drawings, equipment that was never set up properly, occupants who use the building differently than assumed, and controls that were simplified to stop complaints.

A widely cited showcase is The Edge, an office building in Amsterdam that opened in the mid-2010s and received a record-breaking sustainability rating at the time. It uses tens of thousands of sensors, an app that lets staff set the light and temperature at their desk, and solar panels and underground thermal storage. Its lesson was less about any gadget than about operation: a building is only as smart as the team that runs it, and the data only helps if someone acts on it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Real savings, real new risks

The benefits are measurable in energy bills and comfort. The costs appear in complexity, security and the question of who is being watched.

Chapter 13

Security and privacy: the door you forgot

In 2013 attackers stole the payment card details of tens of millions of customers of Target, the US retailer. Investigations found that they had first entered the company's network using login details stolen from a heating and refrigeration contractor that had remote access for monitoring. The building system was not the target. It was the door. Building controls were designed for closed networks, and connecting them to the internet exposed decades-old equipment with weak security.

Good practice now separates building networks from office networks, removes default passwords, limits remote access and keeps devices updated. Many older controllers cannot be updated at all, which is one reason security experts treat building systems as a growing concern.

Privacy is the second issue. Sensors that count people can, with enough detail, track individuals: when they arrive, where they sit, how long they spend away from their desk. In the European Union, data that can identify a person falls under the GDPR data-protection law, and rules on workplace monitoring differ between countries. Many designers now choose sensors that only count or detect presence, and aggregate data before anyone sees it.

Chapter 15

The real challenge is the buildings we already have

New buildings are only a small fraction of the stock in any year. In Europe, a large majority of the buildings that will exist in 2050 have already been built. Most of them have old boilers, simple timers and no sensors at all. The biggest impact will therefore come not from showcase towers but from cheap retrofits: wireless sensors, smart thermostats, heat pumps and software added to ordinary offices, schools and apartment blocks.

That shifts the hard problems from technology to people and money: who pays when the owner buys the equipment but the tenant gets the lower bill, who maintains the system, and who has the skills. A smart building is ultimately a well-run building, and the next decade will test whether software can make good operation cheap enough to be normal.

The core idea

A smart building links sensors, actuators, controllers, a network and supervisory software so heating, cooling, ventilation and lighting respond to real conditions. Most of the value comes from not wasting energy on empty or misbehaving spaces, and increasingly from shifting demand to help the grid.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Operating buildings: roughly 30% of world final energy use (IEA).
  • Better controls and commissioning often save around 10–30%, varying widely.
  • BACnet, the main building protocol, was standardised in 1995.
The common mistake

Thinking smartness is about gadgets. Most savings come from fixing schedules, setpoints and faults, and they vanish if nobody runs the system.

The words, in plain words
Building management system (BMS)
The software that monitors and controls a building's technical systems.
HVAC
Heating, ventilation and air conditioning: usually a building's largest energy user.
Air-handling unit
A large box of fans, filters and coils that conditions air and pushes it through ducts.
Damper
A movable plate in a duct that controls how much air flows.
Variable-speed drive
Electronics that let a motor run at any speed instead of only full speed or off.
Demand-controlled ventilation
Supplying fresh air according to how many people are present, often measured by CO2.
Daylight harvesting
Dimming electric lights automatically when daylight is enough.
BACnet / KNX / Matter
Communication standards that let devices from different makers talk to each other.
Commissioning
Checking and adjusting a building's systems so they actually work as designed.
Fault detection and diagnostics
Software that spots equipment behaving wrongly and suggests why.
Performance gap
The difference between the energy a building was designed to use and what it really uses.
Grid-interactive building
One that shifts or reduces its electricity use in response to grid signals or prices.
