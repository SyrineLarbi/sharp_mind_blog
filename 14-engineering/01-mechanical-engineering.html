Mechanical engineering. The art of making things move without breaking.

Look around the room. The hinge on the door, the fan in the laptop, the pump that brings water to the tap, the compressor in the fridge, the car outside. Each one was designed by someone who asked the same three questions. What forces will act on this? What will it be made of? And how will it fail? Mechanical engineering is the oldest and broadest of the engineering disciplines. It is less about machines than about a way of thinking: predict the loads, choose the material, and leave a margin for everything you did not predict.

Question 1 · What is it?

The discipline of force, motion and heat

Mechanical engineering designs anything with moving parts, anything that carries load, and anything that turns heat into work or moves heat from one place to another.

Chapter 01

A wasted half of the steam

In 1712 an English ironmonger named Thomas Newcomen installed an engine to pump water out of a coal mine. Steam filled a large cylinder. Then cold water was sprayed inside, the steam condensed, and the vacuum it left let the air push a piston down. It worked, and it was terribly wasteful. Every stroke cooled the whole cylinder, and the next charge of steam spent most of its heat warming the metal up again.

About fifty years later, James Watt, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, was repairing a model of this engine. He saw the problem clearly: the same cylinder was being heated and cooled over and over. His fix, patented in 1769, was a separate condenser. The steam was led away into a second vessel to be cooled, so the main cylinder could stay hot all the time. Watt's engines used a fraction of the coal of the older design. By common estimates they needed roughly a quarter as much.

That story holds the whole discipline in miniature. Watt did not discover a new law of nature. He looked at a working machine, found where the energy was being lost, and redesigned it. Mechanical engineering is the craft of understanding exactly where a machine wastes energy, carries stress, or wears out, and changing the design so it does less of that.

Chapter 02

The map of the field

Because almost every physical product has a mechanical side, the field is huge. Most mechanical engineers work in one of these areas.

Many specialities grew out of this trunk and became disciplines of their own. Aerospace, automotive, naval and biomedical engineering all began as mechanical engineering applied to a particular kind of machine. The related file on aerospace engineering shows one of those branches in detail.

Chapter 03

Engineering is not applied science

A common belief is that scientists discover and engineers merely apply. It is half true. Science asks how the world works. Engineering asks how to make something that works, reliably, at a price, by a deadline, with incomplete knowledge. Those last four conditions change everything.

A physicist can study a perfect steel bar. An engineer has to design with real steel, which contains tiny flaws, varies from batch to batch, and will be used by people who overload it, forget to oil it and leave it out in the rain. So engineering has its own methods: rules of thumb tested over generations, deliberately cautious margins, standards written by professional bodies, and tests that break things on purpose. Often the machine came first and the science later. The steam engine was working for a century before thermodynamics explained why.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Loads, materials, heat and motion

Four ideas carry most of the discipline. Learn them and you can follow almost any conversation between mechanical engineers.

Chapter 04

Stress, strain and the margin of safety

Hang a heavy bag from a thin wire and from a thick rope. The weight is the same, but the wire is more likely to snap. What matters is not the force alone but the force divided by the area that carries it. Engineers call that stress. A material stretches a little under stress; the amount it stretches, as a fraction of its length, is strain.

For most metals, at small loads, stress and strain rise together in a straight line, and the part springs back when the load is removed. This is the elastic region, described by Hooke's law, named after the seventeenth-century English scientist Robert Hooke. Push past a point called the yield strength and the metal bends permanently. Push further and it reaches its ultimate strength and breaks.

No engineer designs a part to sit just below its yield strength. Instead they apply a factor of safety: they make the part strong enough to carry a multiple of the largest load they expect. The number reflects how much is unknown. Aircraft structures are classically designed to survive 1.5 times the largest load they should ever see, because every extra kilogram costs fuel and the loads are well studied. Lifting equipment and pressure vessels often use much larger factors, because loads are less predictable and failure kills. A safety factor is a measured statement of ignorance. The less you know, the bigger it must be.

Chapter 05

Fatigue: how small loads break strong parts

Take a paper clip and bend it once. Nothing happens. Bend it back and forth twenty times and it snaps, although no single bend was strong enough to break it. That is fatigue, and it causes a large share of mechanical failures in service.

Engineers learned this the hard way on the railways. In 1842 a train axle broke near Versailles, outside Paris, and the derailment and fire that followed killed dozens of passengers. Axles were failing even though calculations said they were strong enough. Over the following decades the German railway engineer August Wöhler tested axles by spinning them under load millions of times. He showed that each material has a stress below which it can survive a very large number of cycles, and above which every turn adds damage. Engineers still draw his kind of chart, a stress against number-of-cycles curve, today.

Fatigue cracks usually start at a stress concentration: a sharp corner, a hole, a scratch, a weld. Stress flows through a part rather like water through a channel, and it bunches up wherever the shape changes suddenly. That is why well-designed parts have rounded inside corners (called fillets), and why a small change in shape can matter more than a stronger alloy.

Chapter 06

Turning heat into work, and the limit nobody can beat

Every engine that burns fuel, from a lawnmower to a power station, does the same thing. It takes heat from something hot, turns part of it into motion, and dumps the rest into something colder, usually the air or a river. In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot proved that no heat engine can turn all its heat into work. The best possible share depends only on how hot the hot side is and how cold the cold side is. The bigger the gap, the better the ceiling.

This is why engineers fight so hard for higher temperatures inside turbines and engines, and why materials that survive heat are so valuable. Real machines fall well short of the ceiling because of friction and other losses. As rough, typical figures: an ordinary petrol car engine turns perhaps a quarter to a third of its fuel energy into motion at best, and less in city driving. The best modern gas power plants, which use the hot exhaust of a gas turbine to raise steam for a second turbine (a combined cycle), convert around 60 percent. An electric motor, which does not use heat at all, typically converts more than 90 percent of the electricity it receives.

The reverse machine matters just as much. A refrigerator or a heat pump uses work to push heat from a cold place to a warm one. Volume 11 explains why that makes heat pumps so efficient (Heat pumps).

Chapter 07

Mechanisms: trading force for speed

A bicycle shows the next idea. In a low gear you pedal fast and the bike moves slowly, but climbing is easy. In a high gear each pedal stroke is hard, but the bike goes fast. Gears, levers, pulleys and screws all make the same trade. They cannot create energy. They let you exchange force for distance, or in rotating machines, torque for speed.

Torque is turning force: how hard something twists. Power is how fast work gets done, which for a rotating shaft is torque multiplied by rotational speed. A truck engine and a racing motorbike engine may have similar power. The truck delivers it as huge torque at low speed; the motorbike as small torque at very high speed. A gearbox is the device that matches what the engine produces to what the wheels need.

Underneath every mechanism are humbler parts that decide whether it lasts: bearings to let shafts spin with little friction, seals to keep oil in and dirt out, springs, fasteners and lubricants. Most machines do not die from their clever parts. They die from a worn bearing or a leaking seal.

Chapter 08

From sketch to part

A modern design moves through a predictable path. An engineer first sketches the idea and does rough hand calculations to check it is in the right range. Then the part is modelled in CAD (computer-aided design) software as a precise three-dimensional shape. The model is tested virtually using finite element analysis, which chops the shape into thousands or millions of tiny pieces and calculates the stress in each one. Fluids are simulated in a similar way, called computational fluid dynamics.

Simulation does not replace testing. Prototypes are still built, loaded, heated, shaken and broken, because a model is only as good as its assumptions. Finally the engineer produces a drawing for the factory, with tolerances: the allowed variation in each dimension. A hole might be specified as 10 millimetres, plus or minus two hundredths of a millimetre. Tight tolerances make parts fit better and cost more. Choosing them well is one of the quiet skills that separates a good engineer from a merely clever one. The full process is the subject of Engineering design.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Precision, mass production and the codes written in accidents

The modern world depends on parts that are cheap, identical and trustworthy. Each of those three words was an engineering achievement.

Chapter 09

The machine that makes the machines

Before about 1800, every screw was made by hand, and a nut from one bolt rarely fitted another. Around that time the English toolmaker Henry Maudslay built a practical screw-cutting lathe, a machine that cut threads accurately and repeatably. Machine tools like it were the hidden turning point of the industrial age. A machine tool is a machine that makes the parts of other machines, so every gain in its precision spread through the whole economy.

In 1841 Maudslay's former apprentice Joseph Whitworth proposed a standard screw thread, with a fixed angle and a fixed number of threads per inch for each size. British industry adopted it widely. Standard parts sound dull, but they made interchangeable parts possible: a broken component could be replaced from stock rather than hand-fitted by a craftsman. That idea, pursued in arms factories in France and the United States, led on to the moving assembly line and mass production, explored in Manufacturing.

Chapter 10

The invisible machinery of daily life

Most of what mechanical engineers design is never noticed while it works. A building's heating, ventilation and air conditioning (usually shortened to HVAC) keeps the air breathable and the temperature steady. Pumps move water up tower blocks and sewage away from them. Lifts, escalators, conveyor belts and refrigerated trucks keep cities supplied. Food reaches supermarkets through a chain of compressors and insulated boxes, described in Volume 15 (The cold chain).

Energy is the largest arena. Almost all electricity that does not come from solar panels is produced by a spinning machine: a steam, gas, water or wind turbine driving a generator. Those turbines are mechanical engineering at its most demanding. Their blades spin thousands of times a minute in some cases, in gas hot enough to melt the metal they are made of, kept alive by internal cooling channels and ceramic coatings. The shape of the energy transition is partly a mechanical engineering problem: wind-turbine gearboxes, heat pumps, and the shift from engines to electric motors.

Chapter 11

Codes written in accidents

In the nineteenth century, steam boilers exploded with grim regularity in factories, mines, ships and locomotives. One of the worst came in 1905, when a boiler blew up at the Grover shoe factory in Brockton, Massachusetts. The building collapsed and burned, and dozens of workers died. Disasters like it pushed the American Society of Mechanical Engineers to write a boiler code, first published in 1915. It set rules for materials, wall thickness, welding, inspection and safety valves. Boiler explosions, once common, became rare.

This pattern repeats across engineering. A design code is accumulated experience, mostly bought with accidents, written down so that the next engineer does not have to repeat them. It is one reason a young engineer can safely design a pressure vessel without personally having seen one fail. It is also why engineers take the codes seriously even when a calculation suggests they could cut a corner.

Failure mode thinking

Good engineers ask "how could this fail?" before "how will this work?". A formal version, called failure mode and effects analysis, lists every way each part could fail, how bad the result would be, and how likely it is. The high-scoring items get redesigned first.

Chapter 13

The fight between cost, weight and life

What goes right

A mature mechanical product, such as a modern washing machine, lift or car engine, can run for many years with little attention. That is the result of generations of small improvements in materials, lubrication and tolerances. Very few other human achievements are as dependable as a well-made machine.

What goes wrong

Every design trades cost against weight against durability, and the market often rewards cheapness. Products can be designed to be hard to repair, with glued cases and special screws. The engineering is sound; the incentives are not. Debates over a "right to repair" in the EU and some US states are partly about this trade-off.

Chapter 15

What stays the same

Each wave of new tools has led people to predict that engineering judgement would become less important. The opposite usually happens. Software can calculate stress in a million elements, but somebody still has to decide which loads to apply, which assumptions are honest, and whether a strange result is a discovery or a mistake. Faster tools make wrong assumptions travel faster too.

So the core of the discipline looks durable. Understand where the forces go. Respect fatigue and heat. Leave a margin for what you do not know. Test before you trust. Watt would recognise every one of those habits, even if he would not recognise the screen.

The core idea

Mechanical engineering designs things that carry loads, move, and handle heat. Its central habits are calculating stress, respecting fatigue, working within the thermodynamic limit, and adding a factor of safety for what is unknown.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Aircraft structures are classically designed for 1.5 times the largest expected load.
  • A typical petrol car engine converts roughly a quarter to a third of its fuel energy into motion at best.
  • The best combined-cycle gas plants reach about 60 percent; electric motors usually exceed 90 percent.
The common mistake

Thinking that a part breaks only when a load is too big. Most service failures are fatigue: many small loads, repeated, starting a crack at a sharp corner.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a gearbox cannot increase an engine's power, but can still make a truck climb a hill? If you can, you understand torque, speed and power together.

The words, in plain words
Stress
Force divided by the area carrying it. It tells you how hard a material is being worked.
Strain
How much a material stretches or squashes, as a fraction of its size.
Yield strength
The stress above which a material bends permanently instead of springing back.
Factor of safety
How many times stronger a part is than the largest load it should meet.
Fatigue
Failure caused by many repeated loads, each too small to break the part alone.
Stress concentration
A spot, such as a sharp corner or hole, where stress bunches up.
Thermodynamics
The science of heat, work and energy, and the limits on converting one to another.
Combined cycle
A power plant that uses a gas turbine's hot exhaust to drive a second, steam turbine.
Torque
Turning force: how hard a shaft is being twisted.
Tolerance
The allowed variation in a dimension on a drawing.
CAD
Computer-aided design: software for drawing parts precisely in three dimensions.
Finite element analysis
A simulation that splits a part into tiny pieces to calculate stress in each.
Digital twin
A computer model of a specific real machine, kept up to date with its sensor data.
