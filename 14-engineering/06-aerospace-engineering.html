Aerospace engineering. Where every gram costs money and every failure is studied.

A modern airliner weighs as much as a hundred family cars, carries hundreds of people at close to the speed of sound, ten kilometres above the ground, in air too thin to breathe and far below freezing. It does this thousands of times over its life with a safety record that few human activities can match. Aerospace engineering is the discipline behind aircraft, rockets and spacecraft. Its two obsessions explain almost everything about it: weight and reliability.

Question 1 · What is it?

Engineering for air and space

The field has two halves. Aeronautics deals with vehicles that fly inside the atmosphere. Astronautics deals with those that leave it.

Chapter 01

The first jet airliner, and the cracks nobody expected

In 1952 the British de Havilland Comet became the world's first jet airliner in passenger service. It was fast, quiet and smooth compared with propeller aircraft, and it flew high above most weather. Then, in 1954, two Comets broke up in the air over the Mediterranean within a few months. All aboard died. The fleet was grounded.

Investigators did something new. They put a complete Comet fuselage in a giant water tank at Farnborough and pumped water in and out to imitate the pressure changes of flight after flight. After a few thousand simulated flights, the cabin split open. A fatigue crack had grown from the corner of an opening in the fuselage, where stress concentrated. Each flight, the cabin is pressurised, pumped up with air so passengers can breathe at high altitude, and then released on landing. Each cycle had added a little damage. The popular version says "square windows" caused it; the real story is about stress concentrations at the corners of openings and how the metal around them was designed and tested.

The Comet changed aircraft design for good. Windows became rounded, fuselages were tested for fatigue through many lifetimes of flights, and engineers adopted the idea of damage tolerance: assume cracks will form, and design so that they grow slowly, are found during inspection, and cannot bring the aircraft down before they are. Aerospace safety is built on the assumption that something will go wrong.

Aerospace grew out of mechanical engineering, and it borrows heavily from electrical engineering and computing. What makes it a discipline of its own is the extreme combination of low weight, high loads and very high reliability, all at once.

Chapter 03

The tyranny of weight

On the ground, a slightly heavier car uses slightly more fuel. In the air, extra weight compounds. A heavier aircraft needs more lift, so bigger wings, which add weight and drag. It needs more thrust, so bigger engines, which add weight. It needs more fuel, which adds weight and needs still more fuel to carry. On a long-haul flight, a large share of the take-off weight is the fuel itself.

For rockets the effect is far more severe. As the file on Space exploration explains, the rocket equation means that most of a rocket's launch mass must be propellant. In aerospace, a kilogram saved in the structure is a kilogram of passengers, cargo or fuel gained, every flight, for decades. This is why the industry pays far more per kilogram for lightweight materials than any car maker would.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Lift, thrust, structure and redundancy

An aircraft in steady flight balances four forces: lift against weight, and thrust against drag. Engineering it means mastering each, and then making sure no single failure can upset the balance.

Chapter 04

How a wing really makes lift

A wing is shaped and tilted so that it turns the air flowing past it downward. By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up. At the same time, the air speeds up over the curved top surface and its pressure falls, while pressure under the wing is higher. That pressure difference across the wing is the lift. The two descriptions, "turning the air down" and "lower pressure on top", are two views of the same physics, not rival theories.

A popular explanation says the air over the top must travel further and therefore "has to" meet the air underneath at the back edge at the same moment. That is wrong. The air over the top actually arrives at the trailing edge earlier, and nothing requires the two streams to meet again. It is one of the most widespread myths in school science.

Lift grows with air density, wing area, the square of airspeed, and the angle of attack: the angle between the wing and the oncoming air. Raise the angle and lift rises, up to a point. Beyond it, the air can no longer follow the upper surface smoothly. It breaks away, lift collapses, and the wing stalls. A stall has nothing to do with the engine stopping; it is purely about airflow over the wing. Flaps and slats, the panels that slide out of a wing before landing, let it make enough lift at low speed without stalling.

Chapter 05

Jet engines: the big fan does most of the work

A jet engine pulls air in at the front, squeezes it in a compressor, burns fuel in it, and lets the hot gas rush out through a turbine, which spins the compressor, and then out of the back. The reaction pushes the aircraft forward. That is the basic turbojet, used on early jets like the Comet. It is powerful but thirsty and loud.

Almost every airliner today uses a turbofan. A large fan at the front, driven by the engine core, pushes a huge amount of air around the outside of the core rather than through it. The ratio of air going around to air going through is the bypass ratio, and on modern airliner engines it is roughly ten to one or more. The reason is a basic principle of propulsion: it is more efficient to push a lot of air gently than a little air violently. A big, slow jet of air wastes less energy than a small, fast one, and it is much quieter. That is why airliner engines have grown so wide.

The hottest part of the engine, just after the combustor, runs at temperatures above the melting point of the metal turbine blades. They survive because they are made of special nickel alloys, often grown as a single crystal, coated in ceramic and cooled by air flowing through tiny internal channels. Jet engines are among the most demanding machines humans make.

Chapter 06

Structures: thin skins, carefully stiffened

An aircraft fuselage is not a solid tube. It is a thin skin, a few millimetres thick in many places, held in shape by rings called frames and lengthwise strips called stringers. The skin itself carries much of the load, an approach called semi-monocoque construction. It is extremely light for its strength, and it must still survive pressurisation, gusts, turbulence and heavy landings.

For decades the main material was aluminium alloy. Newer airliners make wide use of composites: carbon fibres set in plastic resin, which are strong, light and do not corrode or fatigue the way metals do. On the Boeing 787 and the Airbus A350, composites make up roughly half the structure by weight. They bring their own challenges. Damage can be hidden inside the material, and repairs need special methods.

Every new design is tested to destruction. Manufacturers bend a full-size wing upward until it breaks, to prove it can carry 1.5 times the largest load it should ever meet in service, the same safety factor explained in Mechanical engineering.

Chapter 07

Rocket engines: carrying your own air

A jet engine takes its oxygen from the air. A rocket cannot, because there is no air in space, so it carries its own oxidiser as well as fuel. Common pairings are kerosene and liquid oxygen, liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, and, increasingly, liquid methane and liquid oxygen. Each has trade-offs. Hydrogen gives the most push per kilogram but is very bulky and must be kept extremely cold. Kerosene is dense and easy to store but leaves soot inside engines, which complicates reuse. Methane sits in between and burns cleanly.

Engineers compare rocket engines by specific impulse: roughly, how much push you get from each kilogram of propellant. Higher is better, and it is set mostly by how hot the exhaust is and how light its molecules are. The turbopumps that feed a large rocket engine are remarkable machines, pushing huge flows of propellant into the combustion chamber at very high pressure. How rockets reach orbit, and why landing boosters changed costs, is told in Space exploration.

Chapter 08

One in a billion: designing for failure

Aviation regulators in the United States and Europe set targets for how often a failure may happen, depending on how bad it would be. For a failure that could destroy the aircraft, the classic target is that it must be "extremely improbable", usually interpreted as less than about one in a billion per flight hour. No single part can be made that reliable. So engineers use redundancy: several independent copies of each critical system, so that one can fail without danger.

A large airliner typically has several separate hydraulic systems, several electrical generators plus batteries and a small emergency wind turbine, and multiple flight computers. Redundancy only works if the copies fail independently. If two "independent" systems share one sensor, one wire route or one software flaw, a single problem can take out both. Modern aircraft are controlled by fly-by-wire, with computers between the pilot and the control surfaces, described in Control systems. Their software is written and checked to special standards, with the most critical code tested far more thoroughly than ordinary programs.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A safety culture, a connected world, and a climate problem

Aerospace shrank the world, built a model of safety other industries copy, and created one of the hardest emissions problems to solve.

Chapter 09

Why flying became so safe

Commercial air travel is among the safest ways to travel per kilometre, and fatal accidents became far rarer per flight from the 1970s onward, even as traffic grew enormously. Better engines, better structures and better navigation all helped. But much of the gain came from a culture of learning from every failure.

Several habits define it. Every serious accident is investigated by an independent body, such as the NTSB in the United States or the BEA in France. Under international rules, the aim of the investigation is to prevent the next accident, not to assign blame. Aircraft carry flight data and cockpit voice recorders, the "black boxes", which are actually painted bright orange. Pilots and engineers can report errors and near misses confidentially, so problems surface before they cause a crash. Crews are trained to challenge each other, a method called crew resource management, developed after accidents where a junior pilot noticed a problem but did not speak up firmly enough.

Hospitals, nuclear plants and railways have all borrowed these ideas. Aviation's real invention may be less the jet engine than the habit of treating every mistake as information.

Chapter 10

The 737 MAX: when the system around the design fails

In October 2018 a Lion Air Boeing 737 MAX crashed into the sea off Indonesia. In March 2019 an Ethiopian Airlines 737 MAX crashed shortly after take-off from Addis Ababa. Together, 346 people died. In both cases a flight-control feature called MCAS repeatedly pushed the aircraft's nose down, triggered by a single faulty sensor, and pilots who had not been fully told about it could not regain control in time. The aircraft type was grounded worldwide for around twenty months.

The control-system details are in Control systems. The wider lessons are about systems engineering and oversight. Investigations by the US Congress and others pointed to several factors: a design that relied on one sensor where redundancy was expected, assumptions about how quickly pilots would react, commercial pressure to avoid costly extra pilot training, and a certification process in which the regulator had delegated much of the checking to the manufacturer itself. Good engineering can be undone by the organisation around it, a lesson Volume 23 also draws from Challenger (Challenger).

Chapter 11

Aviation's climate problem

Aviation produces roughly 2 to 3 percent of global CO₂ emissions from fossil fuels, according to commonly cited estimates. That share sounds small, but there are three complications. First, demand for flying grows fast as countries get richer. Second, other sectors have easier paths to cutting emissions, so aviation's share is expected to rise. Third, aircraft also warm the climate in ways other than CO₂, especially through the thin clouds called contrails that can form behind engines in cold, humid air. Scientists agree these effects are significant, but their exact size is still uncertain and debated.

Aviation is also one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. Batteries are far too heavy for long flights: jet fuel stores much more energy per kilogram than the best batteries. Hydrogen is light but bulky, needing large, heavy, very cold tanks. So the main near-term options are more efficient aircraft and different fuels, discussed in Question 5.

Chapter 12

Space as everyday infrastructure

The space side of aerospace matters to daily life more than most people realise. Satellites provide navigation, weather forecasts, television, internet links to remote places, and timing signals that banks and power grids rely on. Volume 12 explains two of these: GPS and Satellite constellations.

Reusable rockets have cut the cost of reaching orbit sharply in the past decade, and launches have multiplied. That has made space more useful and more crowded at once. The engineering challenges have moved from simply getting there to operating thousands of satellites safely, and avoiding collisions and debris.

Extraordinary performance at extraordinary cost

The aerospace approach delivers safety and performance no other transport matches. The price is slowness, expense and concentration in a few firms.

Chapter 15

The slowest industry with the fastest machines

An airliner design can stay in production for decades, and individual aircraft fly for twenty or thirty years. So even a breakthrough adopted today would take a generation to spread through the world's fleet. That is the central fact about the future of aviation. Change in aerospace is real but slow, because every new idea must prove itself to the highest safety standard in transport.

Space is moving faster, driven by reusable rockets and mass-produced satellites. The coming decades may therefore see two very different speeds inside one discipline: rapid experiment in orbit, and patient, cautious evolution in the sky. The parts of the industry that make things in space, rather than just launch them, are covered in Space manufacturing.

The core idea

Aerospace engineering builds vehicles where weight compounds and failure can be fatal. Wings make lift by turning air downward; turbofans push a lot of air gently; structures are tested for fatigue; and redundancy plus a culture of learning from failure keep flying safe.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Catastrophic failure target: below about one in a billion per flight hour.
  • Modern turbofan bypass ratios are about 10 to 1 or more.
  • Aviation causes roughly 2–3 percent of global fossil CO₂, plus uncertain non-CO₂ warming.
The common mistake

Thinking a stall means the engine has stopped. A stall is the wing losing its smooth airflow, usually because its angle to the air is too steep.
