Sharp Mind · Engineering · Topic 13 of 20

Control systems. Measure, compare, correct, and do it again a thousand times a second.

Almost every machine you trust is quietly arguing with the world. A fridge measures its temperature and switches the compressor on when it drifts too warm. A car's engine computer adjusts the fuel many times per second. A modern airliner would be hard to fly without software that corrects the controls continuously. The idea behind all of them is the same and it is very old: look at what is actually happening, compare it with what you wanted, and push in the direction that closes the gap. That simple loop is called feedback control. It is one of the most powerful ideas engineering ever had, and when it is badly designed it can shake a machine to pieces.

Question 1 · What is it?

The science of making things behave

A control system is anything that keeps some quantity where you want it, despite a world that keeps pushing it somewhere else. The quantity can be a temperature, a speed, a height, a voltage or the level of water in a tank.

Chapter 01

Two spinning balls and a steam engine

In the 1780s James Watt and his business partner Matthew Boulton had a problem. Their steam engines were driving mills, and the load on a mill changes all the time. When the load dropped the engine raced. When it rose the engine slowed. Millers wanted a steady speed. The answer Watt adapted from windmills was the centrifugal governor, often called the flyball governor.

Picture two heavy metal balls on hinged arms, spun by the engine itself. When the engine speeds up, the balls fly outward. The arms are linked to the steam valve, so as they swing out they partly close it. Less steam means less speed. When the engine slows, the balls drop and the valve opens again. The machine measures its own speed and corrects itself.

Governors spread across the industrial world, and so did a puzzle. Some of them "hunted": the engine speed swung up and down instead of settling. In 1868 the physicist James Clerk Maxwell, better known for his work on electricity and magnetism, published a paper called "On Governors". He wrote down the mathematics of the machine and showed when it would settle and when it would swing. That paper is often treated as the start of control theory as a science, not only a craft.

Chapter 02

Open loop and closed loop

There are two basic ways to control something. A cheap toaster uses open-loop control. You set a timer and it heats for that long. It never checks whether the bread is actually brown. If the bread was frozen, you get pale toast. If it was thin, you get charcoal. The toaster carries out its instructions and has no idea whether they worked.

A thermostat uses closed-loop control, also called feedback control. It measures the room temperature, compares it with the temperature you asked for, and turns the heating on or off depending on the difference. Open a window and it notices and works harder. The information about the result "feeds back" into the decision, which closes a loop.

Open loop is cheap, simple and cannot become unstable, and it works when the world is predictable. But whenever the world is uncertain, closed loop wins, because it does not need to predict the disturbance. It only needs to notice its effect.

Chapter 03

Question 2 · How does it work?

How hard to push, and when to stop

Knowing which way to push is easy. The whole difficulty is knowing how hard, because every real system responds late, and a late response combined with a strong push makes things swing.

Chapter 04

The hotel shower: delay is the enemy

You step into an unfamiliar hotel shower. The water is cold, so you turn the tap toward hot. Nothing happens, because the hot water takes a few seconds to travel up the pipe. You turn it further. Then the hot water arrives all at once and scalds you. You turn it sharply back toward cold, and a few seconds later you are freezing again. You have just built an unstable control system with your own hands.

Your aim was fine and your direction was right. The problem was the combination of two things: a delay between action and result, and a strong reaction to the error. Each correction arrived after the situation had already changed, so it overshot. This is called oscillation, and if the corrections get bigger each time it becomes instability. The experienced shower user does the right thing without thinking: small turns, then waiting.

The loop described so far uses negative feedback. "Negative" does not mean bad. It means the correction opposes the error: too hot leads to cooler. Positive feedback does the opposite, so a change causes more of the same change. A microphone held next to its own speaker produces a howl that grows until something saturates. In a control loop it is usually a disaster.

Chapter 05

PID: the three questions every controller asks

The single most common controller in industry is called PID, for proportional, integral, derivative. Surveys of process plants have long found that the large majority of their control loops are PID or a simpler version of it. The name sounds mathematical, but each part answers a plain question. Think of a car's cruise control trying to hold 100 km/h.

P · How far off am I now?

Push in proportion to the current error. At 90 km/h, press the accelerator a lot. At 99, press it a little. Simple and fast, but on a hill P alone settles slightly below the target, because it only pushes while there is still an error.

I · How long have I been off?

Add up the error over time. If the car has been one km/h slow for a whole minute, this term keeps growing until the throttle is opened enough to remove the gap entirely. It fixes the steady error that P leaves behind.

D · How fast is it changing?

Look at the rate of change. If the speed is rushing up toward 100, ease off early so you do not overshoot. It acts like a brake on enthusiasm, and makes the loop calmer.

The controller's output is the three answers added together, each multiplied by a number called a gain. Choosing those three gains is called tuning. Too little gain and the system is sluggish. Too much and it oscillates like the hotel shower. A well-tuned loop reaches its target quickly, overshoots a little or not at all, and shrugs off disturbances.

A detail that bites

If the actuator hits its limit, for example the throttle is already fully open on a steep hill, the integral term can keep growing even though nothing more can be done. When the hill ends, all that stored-up push is released and the car overshoots badly. Engineers call this integral windup and add logic to stop the integral growing while the actuator is maxed out.

Chapter 06

Stability: the question that matters most

A control engineer's first question about any design is not "is it fast?" but "is it stable?". A stable system, when disturbed, comes back to its setpoint. An unstable one swings further and further away, or runs off to a limit and stays there.

In the 1920s to 1940s, engineers working on telephone amplifiers and military equipment, among them Harold Black, Harry Nyquist and Hendrik Bode at Bell Labs, developed tools that let them judge stability from how a system responds to vibrations of different speeds. The key idea is simple enough to state in words. Every loop has a frequency at which its delay turns a correction into a push in the wrong direction. If the loop still amplifies at that frequency, it will oscillate. Designers therefore leave a safety margin, often called gain margin and phase margin, so that the system stays stable even when the real machine differs from the model on paper.

That margin matters because the model is always wrong somewhere. Loads change and parts wear. A design that is perfect on paper but unstable for a slightly heavier machine is a bad design. The field calls this property robustness.

Chapter 07

Beyond PID: prediction, estimation and computers

PID only reacts to errors that have already happened. Several other ideas go further.

Feedforward acts on a disturbance before it shows up in the output. If a sensor sees a load of cold material arriving on a conveyor, the furnace can add heat in advance instead of waiting for the temperature to fall.

Model predictive control (MPC) uses a mathematical model of the plant to simulate the next few minutes for many possible plans, picks the best plan that respects limits such as maximum pressure, applies only the first step, then repeats the whole calculation. It became standard in oil refineries and chemical plants from the 1980s and is now used in cars, buildings and power electronics.

State estimation deals with the fact that sensors are noisy and some quantities cannot be measured at all. The best-known tool is the Kalman filter, published by Rudolf Kálmán in 1960. It blends a prediction from a model with noisy measurements, weighting each by how much it can be trusted. A version of it helped the Apollo spacecraft work out where they were on the way to the Moon, and descendants of it run inside phone navigation and drones today.

Finally, almost all control is now digital: a chip samples the sensor and updates the actuator in a fixed rhythm, often thousands of times a second. In factories this runs on rugged computers called PLCs (programmable logic controllers), first built for the car industry in the late 1960s.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The invisible layer that holds modern life steady

Control loops are rarely noticed because, when they work, nothing happens. They are the reason aircraft fly smoothly, power stays at the right frequency, and chemical plants do not explode.

Chapter 08

Where loops hide in everyday life

Your own body is the oldest example. It holds core temperature near 37 °C, keeps blood sugar in a narrow band using insulin and glucagon, and adjusts breathing to the carbon dioxide in your blood. Biologists call this homeostasis. Type 1 diabetes is, in this language, a broken loop, and an automated insulin pump with a glucose sensor is an attempt to close it again.

A modern car contains dozens of loops. The engine computer adjusts fuel and ignition timing using an oxygen sensor in the exhaust. Anti-lock brakes sense when a wheel is about to lock and release and reapply the brake many times a second, so the driver can still steer. Electronic stability control compares where the driver is steering with where the car is actually going and brakes individual wheels to correct a skid. Studies in several countries have credited stability control with a large reduction in fatal single-vehicle crashes.

In aviation, the Airbus A320, which entered service in 1988, was the first airliner with fully digital fly-by-wire controls. The pilot's side-stick does not move the control surfaces directly. It tells a computer what the pilot wants, and the computer moves the surfaces, keeping the aircraft inside safe limits. Many military jets go further: they are built unstable on purpose for agility and can only be flown with a computer correcting them constantly. The most spectacular public example is a rocket booster landing upright on a pad, which SpaceX first achieved with a Falcon 9 in December 2015. That is a control problem solved in real time, with fuel running out.

Chapter 09

The biggest machine on Earth: the power grid

An electricity grid is a gigantic control system. Supply and demand must match every moment, and the signal that tells everyone whether they match is the frequency: 50 cycles per second in Europe and most of the world, 60 in North America and parts of other regions. When demand rises faster than supply, the big spinning generators slow down very slightly and the frequency drops. Power stations sense this and automatically open their throttles. Grid operators then add slower layers of control on top, scheduling extra plants over minutes and hours.

Wind, solar and batteries connect through electronics instead of spinning masses, which strains this design. Keeping the grid stable with them is the subject of the next topic.

Chapter 10

When the loop itself is the danger

Because control systems act on their own, a mistake in their design acts on its own too. Three cases show three different ways it happens.

Bad sensor data. The Boeing 737 MAX had a system called MCAS that pushed the nose down automatically if it judged the nose was pitched too high. In the two crashes, Lion Air in October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines in March 2019, the system acted on a faulty reading from a single angle-of-attack sensor and repeatedly pushed the nose down. In total 346 people died. A controller is only as good as the sensor it believes. The fix included comparing two sensors and limiting how much authority the system had.

The wrong sign. At Chernobyl in 1986, the RBMK reactor's design meant that, in the conditions of that night, rising steam in the cooling water made the reaction stronger, which made more steam. That is positive feedback in the physics of the plant itself, and the control rods could not overcome it in time (Volume 23 tells the full story).

The handover. On Air France flight 447 in 2009, ice blocked the airspeed sensors over the Atlantic. The autopilot, now without reliable data, disconnected and handed a confused situation to the pilots, who did not recover the aircraft. All 228 people on board died. It is studied as a case of automation failing suddenly onto people who were not actively flying.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Tireless precision, with new ways to fail

Feedback control lets cheap, imperfect parts perform like precise ones. The price is a set of failure modes that only exist because the loop is there.

The last row is real. The Stuxnet malware, discovered in 2010, targeted the controllers of uranium centrifuges in Iran and is widely reported to have damaged them. An attack on a control system can break physical machines.

Chapter 12

The irony of automation

In 1983 the psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper called "Ironies of Automation" that is still widely cited. Her point was that automating a task does not remove the human. It changes the human's job into monitoring, which people are poor at, and leaves them with only the hardest cases, the ones the automation could not handle. And their manual skill fades exactly when it is most needed.

Good loop design

Makes clear what the automation is doing and why, fails gradually rather than suddenly, warns early, and keeps people practised. Pilots train for manual flight in simulators for this reason.

Poor loop design

Hides its own state, switches modes silently, and hands over control at the worst moment. Many accident reports describe crews asking, in effect, "what is it doing now?"

The lesson: control engineering is also human-factors engineering. The people supervising the loop are part of it.

Chapter 14

The real bottleneck: proving it is safe

The hardest open problem is not making controllers clever. It is showing, before deployment, that they will behave. Classical control comes with mathematics that guarantees stability within known margins. Learned controllers mostly come with test results. One promising pattern lets a learned controller propose actions while a simple, verifiable "safety filter" vetoes any action that would leave a proven-safe region.

The second shift is scale. As homes gain heat pumps, batteries and electric cars, millions of small controllers will respond to grid signals. Coordinating them is tied to smart buildings and autonomous systems. The future of control is less about one brilliant loop and more about billions of ordinary ones working together without fighting each other.

The core idea

A control system measures a result, compares it with a setpoint, and acts to shrink the error, over and over. Feedback makes systems robust to the unknown, but delay plus strong correction causes oscillation, so every design must first be stable.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Maxwell's "On Governors", the founding paper of control theory: 1868.
  • Most industrial loops are PID, a design many decades old.
  • Grids run at 50 Hz (Europe, most of the world) or 60 Hz (North America).
  • The two 737 MAX crashes in 2018 and 2019 killed 346 people.
The common mistake

Thinking more aggressive correction always means better control. Pushing harder against a delayed system makes it swing, like the hotel shower.

The words, in plain words
Feedback control
Using a measurement of the result to decide the next action. Also called closed-loop control.
Open-loop control
Acting on a fixed plan without checking the result, like a timer.
Setpoint
The value the system is trying to hold.
Error
The gap between the setpoint and what is actually measured.
Plant
Whatever is being controlled: an engine, a room, a reactor, an aircraft.
Actuator
The part that physically changes the plant: a valve, a motor, a heater.
Disturbance
Any outside influence that pushes the plant away from its setpoint.
Negative feedback
A correction that opposes the error. The normal, stabilising kind.
Positive feedback
A response that reinforces a change, so it grows. Usually dangerous in control.
PID controller
A controller combining the current error, its accumulated history and its rate of change.
Stability
The property of returning to the setpoint after a disturbance instead of swinging away.
Model predictive control
A controller that simulates the near future, picks the best plan within limits, and replans constantly.
Kalman filter
A method that combines a model's prediction with noisy measurements to estimate the true state.
PLC
Programmable logic controller: a rugged industrial computer that runs factory control loops.
