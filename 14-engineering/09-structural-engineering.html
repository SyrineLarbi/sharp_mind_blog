Structural engineering. The art of getting every kilogram safely to the ground.

Every building is quietly fighting gravity, wind and, in many places, the ground itself. Structural engineers are the people who make sure it wins, for fifty or a hundred years, through storms and crowds and earthquakes nobody can predict. When they succeed, nobody notices. When they fail, it is front-page news. This file explains the forces, the shapes that tame them, the safety margins built in, and the failures that wrote the rules.

Question 1 · What is it?

Designing the skeleton of everything built

Structural engineering is the branch of civil engineering that makes sure buildings, bridges, towers and dams can carry every load they will meet, without breaking, sagging too far or shaking too much.

Chapter 01

The walkways in Kansas City

On a Friday evening in July 1981, about 1,600 people were at a dance in the new Hyatt Regency hotel in Kansas City. Above the lobby hung two walkways, one above the other, suspended from the roof by steel rods. Some people stood on the walkways to watch. At about 7 pm the connections gave way. Both walkways fell into the crowd. 114 people died. It was the deadliest structural collapse in United States history until 2001.

The cause was a small change made during construction. The original design used single long rods that passed through the upper walkway and carried on to the lower one. That was hard to build. So the plan was changed to two shorter rods: one from the roof to the upper walkway, and a second from the upper walkway down to the lower one. It looked like the same thing. It was not. Now the connection under the upper walkway had to carry the weight of both walkways. The load on it roughly doubled, and even the original design had been weaker than the building code required.

The engineers responsible lost their licences. The case is taught to nearly every engineering student. Its lesson is that a structure is only as strong as the path the load takes to the ground, and a small change to that path can be fatal.

Chapter 02

Loads and the load path

A structural engineer's first job is to list every force the structure must carry. These are called loads.

LoadWhat it isWhy it is tricky
Dead loadThe weight of the structure itself: floors, walls, beamsAlways there, but often the largest part of the total
Live loadPeople, furniture, vehicles, stored goodsMoves and changes. A crowd can jump in rhythm.
WindPressure and suction on surfacesGusts, swirling eddies and vibration, worst on tall buildings
Snow and rainWeight collecting on roofsDrifts pile up unevenly. Ponding water can sag a flat roof further.
EarthquakeThe ground shaking sideways under the buildingHuge, rare and unpredictable
TemperatureMaterials expand when warm and shrink when coldA long bridge can change length by many centimetres

Then the engineer traces the load path: the route every force takes from where it is applied down to the soil. Weight on a floor goes into the slab, then into beams, then into columns, then into foundations, then into the ground. Every link must be strong enough. A failure at any one link, as in Kansas City, breaks the chain.

Chapter 03

Four ways to push and pull

Every piece of a structure is being squeezed, stretched, bent or sheared. These four actions are the vocabulary of the whole field.

Compression

Squeezing. A column holding up a floor. Stone and concrete are excellent in compression. Long thin pieces can fail by suddenly bowing sideways, called buckling.

Tension

Pulling. A rope or cable holding up a weight. Steel is excellent in tension. Stone and plain concrete are poor and crack easily.

Bending

A beam carrying weight across a gap. The top is squeezed and the bottom is stretched at the same time. Bending is how most floors and bridges carry load.

Shear

Sliding forces, like the blades of scissors. Strongest near supports, where a beam's load is handed over to a column.

Reinforced concrete exists because of this list. Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension. So engineers bury steel bars near the bottom of a concrete beam, where bending stretches it. Each material does the job it is good at. This combination, developed in the second half of the nineteenth century, became the most widely used structural material on Earth.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Shapes, margins and motion

A structure works through the shape of its parts, a deliberate margin of safety, and control over how it moves and vibrates.

Chapter 04

The basic shapes, and why each exists

Engineers have a small toolkit of structural forms. Most buildings and bridges combine several.

FormHow it carries loadFamous example
BeamBending: top squeezed, bottom stretchedMost floors, short road bridges
ArchPure compression, pushing outwards at its feetRoman aqueducts, the Sydney Harbour Bridge
TrussTriangles of straight members, each only in tension or compressionThe Eiffel Tower, railway bridges, roof frames
CablePure tension, hanging in a curveSuspension bridges such as the Golden Gate
FrameBeams and columns rigidly joined, resisting sideways pushMost office buildings
ShellA thin curved surface spreading load in all directionsDomes, the Sydney Opera House roofs

The arch and the cable are mirror images. Hang a chain between two points and it forms a curve in pure tension. Turn that exact curve upside down and you have an arch in pure compression. The seventeenth-century English scientist Robert Hooke described this idea, and the Catalan architect Antoni Gaudí designed buildings by hanging weighted strings and photographing them upside down.

The truss works because a triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side. A square of four bars joined at the corners folds flat at a push. Add one diagonal and it becomes rigid. That is why cranes, pylons and roof frames are full of triangles.

Chapter 05

Why a steel beam is shaped like the letter I

When a beam bends, the material at the very top and very bottom does most of the work. The middle, along the centre line, is barely stressed at all.

So engineers move material away from the middle and into two wide plates at the top and bottom, called flanges, joined by a thin web. The result, the I-beam, is far stiffer and stronger than a solid rectangle of the same weight. Good structural design is mostly about putting material only where the forces are. Bones, bird skeletons and plant stems are often hollow or ribbed for the same reason.

Depth matters even more than weight. Doubling the depth of a beam makes it roughly eight times stiffer against bending. That is why long spans need deep beams or trusses, and why a ruler is easy to bend flat and almost impossible to bend on its edge.

Chapter 06

Safety factors and building codes

Engineers never design a structure to carry exactly the load they expect. They build in a margin, because loads are uncertain, materials vary, construction is imperfect and calculations simplify reality.

Modern codes do this in two steps. The expected loads are multiplied up by load factors, for example making the design assume more crowd weight than will probably ever occur. The material strengths are reduced by other factors. The structure must still work with the loads made bigger and the materials made weaker. This approach is called limit state design. It checks two kinds of limit: the structure must not collapse, and in ordinary use it must not sag, crack or vibrate enough to bother anyone.

These rules live in building codes: the Eurocodes across Europe, model codes adopted by states and cities in the United States, and national codes elsewhere. They vary by country but share the same logic. Many of their clauses can be traced back to a specific collapse. In 1968 a gas explosion in a flat on the 18th floor of Ronan Point, a tower block in London, blew out a wall. The floors above fell and took the whole corner of the building down with them. Four people died. Codes afterwards required robustness: losing one element must not bring down far more than its share.

Chapter 07

When structures move: wind and vibration

Strength is not enough. Structures also have to behave well when they move. In November 1940 the newly opened Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State twisted itself apart in a moderate wind. Film of the deck rolling and twisting is among the most famous footage in engineering. The deck was very slender and shallow. The wind flowing past it produced twisting forces that grew with each twist, a self-feeding effect called aeroelastic flutter. It is often described as simple resonance, but engineers today explain it as flutter. No person died. A dog left in a car did.

Modern bridge decks are tested in wind tunnels and shaped to let air pass smoothly. But motion can still surprise. When the Millennium Bridge opened in London in June 2000, crowds made it sway sideways. As it moved slightly, people adjusted their steps to keep their balance, which pushed it further in time with the sway. It was closed after two days, fitted with dampers, and reopened in 2002. The structure was strong enough. The interaction between people and the structure was the problem nobody had modelled.

Chapter 08

Tall buildings fight the wind, not their weight

For a skyscraper, carrying its own weight downwards is the easy part. The hard part is the wind pushing sideways. Wind also creates swirling eddies that peel off alternately from each side of a tall building and can make it sway. Long before a tower is in danger of falling, the sway can make occupants seasick.

Engineers use several tricks. They build a very stiff central core around the lifts and stairs. They tie the core to the outer columns with deep beams. They shape the building to confuse the wind: the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, the world's tallest building at about 828 metres, has a Y-shaped plan that steps back as it rises, so eddies cannot organise along its height. And some towers carry a tuned mass damper, a huge weight near the top that swings opposite to the building's motion. Taipei 101's damper is a steel ball of about 660 tonnes, visible to visitors.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Most of the time, structures are what stand between people and disaster

Earthquakes kill mostly through falling buildings. Structural engineering decides whether a strong earthquake is a news story or a catastrophe.

Chapter 09

Earthquakes: designing to bend, not to resist

An earthquake shakes the ground sideways, back and forth. A building's heavy floors want to stay still, so the shaking produces large sideways forces. Designing a building to resist the biggest possible earthquake without any damage would be extremely expensive. So modern codes take a different approach. A building is allowed to be damaged in a rare, severe earthquake, but it must not collapse. People must be able to walk out.

The key word is ductility, the ability to bend a long way without breaking. Engineers choose certain places in the frame, usually the ends of beams, to yield and bend like a paper clip, absorbing energy. Columns are made stronger so they keep standing. Steel bars in concrete are tied with closely spaced hoops so the concrete stays confined even when it cracks.

Another approach is base isolation. The building sits on bearings of layered rubber and steel, or on sliding plates. When the ground shakes, the bearings move and the building above moves much less. Many hospitals and emergency centres in Japan, New Zealand and California use it, because they need to keep working after the earthquake, not just stay standing.

The deadliest problem is often not knowledge but enforcement. In February 2023, earthquakes in southern Turkey and northern Syria killed more than 50,000 people. Turkey had modern seismic codes on paper. Investigations and reporting found many collapsed buildings that had not been built to them, and amnesties that had legalised unsafe construction. A building code protects no one unless it is actually followed and checked.

Chapter 10

The engineer who fixed his own tower in secret

The Citicorp Center in New York, completed in 1977, stands on four huge columns placed at the middle of each side, not at the corners, because a church owned the corner of the site. Its structural engineer was William LeMessurier. In 1978, after a question from an engineering student, he rechecked the design for winds hitting the building diagonally, on its corners. The forces in some diagonal braces were much higher than the standard calculation had assumed. Worse, the braces had been joined with bolts instead of the stronger welds he had specified.

By his own estimate, a storm strong enough to bring the tower down could be expected roughly once every sixteen years. LeMessurier could have stayed quiet. Instead he told the owners. Over the following weeks, welders worked at night reinforcing more than two hundred joints with thick steel plates, while emergency plans were drawn up for the surrounding blocks. A hurricane approached during the work and turned away. The story became public only in 1995.

It is taught as a model of professional ethics. An engineer's first duty is to public safety, even when that means admitting their own error.

Chapter 11

The silent work of maintenance

Structures age. Steel corrodes. Concrete cracks, lets in water and salt, and the steel bars inside rust and expand, splitting the concrete from within. Bridges carry far heavier lorries than they were designed for decades ago. Much of the profession's work today is not designing new structures but assessing and repairing old ones.

When that work is neglected, the results can be terrible. In August 2018 part of the Morandi Bridge in Genoa, Italy, collapsed during a storm, killing 43 people. The bridge used an unusual design in which a few concrete-encased steel cables carried the deck, making corrosion hard to inspect. In 2021 part of the Champlain Towers South apartment building in Surfside, Florida, collapsed at night, killing 98 people. Earlier inspections had reported serious damage to concrete beneath the pool deck. Investigations into the precise causes took years.

A structure is not finished when it is built. It is a commitment to inspect and maintain for its whole life. The wider question of who pays for that is covered in Infrastructure.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Choosing a material and choosing a margin

Every structural decision trades cost, speed, carbon and safety against each other.

Chapter 12

The main structural materials weighed

MaterialStrengthsWeaknesses
Reinforced concreteCheap, fire-resistant, can be cast into any shape, very durable when done wellHeavy, slow to cure, cement carries a large carbon footprint, hidden corrosion of the steel inside
Structural steelStrong for its weight, fast to erect, ductile in earthquakes, recyclableLoses strength in fire unless protected, corrodes, carbon-intensive to make from ore
Mass timberLight, quick to assemble from factory panels, stores carbon from the treeFire and moisture need careful design; height limits and insurance rules still evolving
Masonry (brick, stone)Durable, cheap locally, good in compressionWeak in tension, dangerous in earthquakes unless reinforced

The profession's caution is also a trade-off. Large safety margins and slow-changing codes are why buildings rarely fall down. They also mean structures use more material, and therefore more carbon, than a perfectly known load would need. Critics argue that some codes are so conservative they waste concrete and steel. Defenders reply that the uncertainty is real and the cost of collapse is lives. Both have a point, and better measurement may narrow the gap.

Chapter 14

The carbon hidden in the frame

For decades, the green building debate focused on energy used by buildings in operation: heating, cooling and lighting. As buildings become more efficient, attention is shifting to embodied carbon, the emissions released making the materials and constructing the structure. For a new, efficient building, the structure itself can be a large share of its lifetime carbon, and that carbon is all released before anyone moves in.

Structural engineers can cut it in several ways: using less material through smarter shapes, choosing lower-carbon concrete and steel, designing for timber, and above all reusing existing structures instead of demolishing them. The greenest structure is often the one already standing. That is a real change in the profession's sense of itself. Its old job was to make structures safe. Its new job is to make them safe while using as little of the planet as possible.

The core idea

Every structure must carry its loads along a continuous load path to the ground. Its parts work in compression, tension, bending and shear. Shapes such as arches, trusses and I-beams put material only where the forces are, and safety factors cover what we cannot know.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hyatt Regency, 1981: 114 dead from one changed connection.
  • Double a beam's depth and it becomes about eight times stiffer.
  • Taipei 101's damper weighs about 660 tonnes.
  • Turkey–Syria earthquakes, 2023: more than 50,000 dead, many in buildings that ignored the code.
The common mistake

Thinking strength is everything. Many failures come from connections, vibration, corrosion or a changed load path, not from a main beam being too weak.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a steel beam is an I rather than a solid block, and why a ruler bends easily flat but not on edge? If so, you understand bending.
