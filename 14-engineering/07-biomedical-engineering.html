Biomedical engineering. Building machines for a customer who is alive, unique, and fights back.

Most engineers design for a world they can measure. A bridge engineer knows the strength of steel and the weight of a lorry. A biomedical engineer designs for the human body, which varies from person to person, changes over a lifetime, heals, inflames, and treats almost anything foreign as an enemy. The field gave us the pacemaker, the artificial hip, dialysis, the pulse oximeter and the insulin pump. It also gave us some of the most instructive failures in engineering history. This file explains what the field is, how its devices actually work, and why "it works on the bench" is only the start.

Question 1 · What is it?

Engineering where the material is a person

Biomedical engineering applies the methods of mechanical, electrical, chemical and software engineering to diagnosing, treating and supporting the human body.

Chapter 01

A heart that ran on a battery

In 1958 a Swedish engineer called Arne Larsson was dying. His heart's own electrical signal kept failing, and he was fainting many times a day. His wife pressed the doctors at the Karolinska Hospital in Stockholm to try something that had never been done. The surgeon Åke Senning and the engineer Rune Elmqvist built a small device, sealed in resin, that sent a regular electrical pulse to the heart. They implanted it under the skin of his abdomen. The first unit failed within hours, and they put in a second one.

Larsson lived until 2001. Over those 43 years he received more than twenty replacement pacemakers as batteries ran down and designs improved. He outlived both the surgeon and the engineer. His story contains the whole field in miniature: an engineering idea, a medical need, a first version that barely worked, and decades of patient iteration.

A modern pacemaker is smaller than a matchbox. It fires only when the heart misses a beat, speeds up when you climb stairs, and reports its status wirelessly to a clinic.

What unites them is a way of working. An engineer starts from a requirement, a precise statement of what the thing must do, then designs and tests until it is proved.

Chapter 03

Why the body is the hardest customer

Three things make this engineering unusually difficult.

First, variation. Steel behaves the same every time. People differ in size, age, skin colour, bone density and disease. A device tested on healthy young adults may behave differently in an eighty-year-old with kidney failure.

Second, the body responds. Metal in a bridge just sits there. In a person, proteins coat it within seconds, the immune system attacks it, and scar tissue may wall it off. The device changes the body, and the body changes the device.

Third, you cannot test freely. An aircraft engineer can break a wing on purpose to find its limit. A biomedical engineer cannot do that with patients. Testing moves from lab benches and computer models to animals and finally controlled human trials. This is why a medical device often takes many years and a large budget to reach the market, even when the engineering idea is simple.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sense, support, replace, and prove it

Almost every biomedical device does one of three jobs. It measures the body, it supports a part that is failing, or it replaces a part entirely. Behind all three sits a fourth job that consumes most of the effort: proving that it is safe.

Chapter 04

Measuring the body: turning life into a signal

Your body is full of physical signals. Every heartbeat starts with an electrical wave that spreads through the heart muscle. Muscles produce electrical activity when they contract. Blood changes colour depending on how much oxygen it carries. A sensor is a device that turns one of these physical changes into an electrical signal a machine can read.

The electrocardiogram, or ECG, is the classic example. Electrodes on the skin pick up tiny voltages, about a thousandth of a volt, as the heart's electrical wave passes. The hard part is separating that from noise: mains hum, breathing, a twitching chest muscle. Much of bioinstrumentation is signal processing, the mathematics of pulling a weak pattern out of a loud background.

The pulse oximeter, the clip you put on a finger, is a lovely piece of physics. It was developed by the Japanese engineer Takuo Aoyagi in the early 1970s. It shines red and infrared light through the fingertip. Blood rich in oxygen absorbs these two colours differently from blood poor in oxygen. By comparing how much of each colour gets through, and by looking only at the part of the signal that pulses with each heartbeat (so it measures arterial blood, not skin and bone), the device estimates the oxygen level. No needle, no blood sample, a reading in seconds.

How a pulse oximeter reads oxygen
   red LED  ---->  [ fingertip: skin, bone, blood ]  ---->  light detector
   infrared ---->                                    ---->
                         |
     oxygen-rich blood:  absorbs more infrared, lets more red through
     oxygen-poor blood:  absorbs more red, lets more infrared through
                         |
   keep only the part that pulses with each heartbeat (arterial blood)
                         |
   ratio of red to infrared  -->  calibration table  -->  "SpO2 = 97%"

Notice the last step. The ratio is turned into a number using a calibration table, a set of measurements made on real volunteers. If those volunteers were not representative, the device will be less accurate for the people who were missing. Chapter 11 returns to this.

Chapter 05

Materials the body will accept

The central idea in implants is biocompatibility. It does not mean "harmless". It means the material produces an acceptable response in the specific place and job it is used for. A material that is fine for a few days in a catheter may be a disaster in a hip for twenty years.

One of the field's great discoveries was an accident. In the early 1950s the Swedish researcher Per-Ingvar Brånemark put small titanium chambers into rabbit bone to watch blood flow. When he tried to remove them, he could not. The bone had grown tightly onto the titanium. He called this osseointegration, the direct bonding of living bone to an implant surface. In 1965 he placed titanium dental implants in a human patient, and the modern dental implant industry grew from that work.

Hip replacement followed a similar path of careful trial and error. In the 1960s the English surgeon John Charnley developed the "low-friction" hip: a small metal ball moving in a plastic socket, fixed to bone with an acrylic cement. The engineering insight was about friction and wear. A smaller ball rubs over less area, so less material wears away. Wear matters because tiny worn particles trigger inflammation that slowly loosens the implant from the bone. In implants, the enemy is often not sudden breakage but slow wear and the body's reaction to the debris.

Chapter 06

Machines that stand in for organs

Some devices take over an organ's job, either for a few hours or for life.

Dialysis replaces the kidney's filtering. During the Second World War, in the occupied Netherlands, the doctor Willem Kolff built an artificial kidney from sausage casing wrapped around a rotating drum in a bath of fluid. The casing was a semi-permeable membrane: small waste molecules could pass through into the bath, while blood cells and large proteins stayed in. Most early patients died, but in 1945 one survived. Modern machines use the same principle with thousands of hollow fibres and keep millions of people alive.

The heart-lung machine lets surgeons stop the heart and operate on it. It pumps blood and adds oxygen outside the body. The American surgeon John Gibbon spent about two decades on it and first used it successfully in 1953. The challenge was to move blood through pumps without crushing its cells or letting it clot.

The cochlear implant replaces part of the inner ear. A microphone picks up sound, a processor splits it into frequency bands, and a thin array of electrodes threaded into the cochlea stimulates the hearing nerve directly. The Australian surgeon Graeme Clark implanted an early multi-channel version in 1978. By the early 2020s roughly a million devices had been implanted worldwide. It is the most successful connection ever made between electronics and the nervous system.

Chapter 07

Proving it: design controls and regulators

A consumer gadget can ship with bugs and get an update. A medical device that fails can kill. So every serious market regulates devices, and the rules shape engineering from the first sketch.

Regulators sort devices by risk. In the United States the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) uses three classes. Class I covers low-risk items such as bandages and tongue depressors. Class II covers moderate risk, such as infusion pumps and many scanners. Most Class II devices reach the market through a route called the 510(k), which requires showing the new device is "substantially equivalent" to one already on sale. Class III covers devices that sustain life or are implanted, such as pacemakers and heart valves. These usually need full premarket approval with clinical evidence.

The European Union uses a similar risk-based system. Its Medical Device Regulation, adopted in 2017 and applied from 2021, tightened the rules after scandals including faulty breast implants. Details vary by country.

Inside the company, regulation means design controls. Every requirement is written down. Every design decision is traced back to a requirement. Every requirement has a test that proves it is met. A risk analysis lists what could go wrong, how bad it would be, and what reduces it. This paperwork is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It is how an organisation remembers why each decision was made, so that a change years later does not quietly break something.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Longer lives, and lessons written in harm

The field has turned fatal conditions into manageable ones. Its failures have taught every other branch of engineering how software, materials and people fail together.

Chapter 08

What was fatal is now routine

Before dialysis, kidney failure was a death sentence. Before the heart-lung machine, most heart defects could not be repaired. Before joint replacement, severe hip arthritis often meant a wheelchair.

A stent, a small mesh tube pushed into a blocked artery on a thin wire, restores blood flow without opening the chest. An artificial lens replaces the clouded one in cataract surgery. A large share of the gains in quality of life in old age come from devices, not pills.

Diabetes shows the direction of travel. People with type 1 diabetes once pricked a finger several times a day and injected by hand. Today many use a continuous glucose monitor, a small sensor under the skin that reads sugar levels every few minutes, linked to an insulin pump. In 2016 the FDA approved the first "hybrid closed-loop" system, in which software adjusts insulin delivery automatically based on the sensor. It is sometimes called an artificial pancreas, though the user still tells it about meals.

Chapter 09

Therac-25: when the software was the weak point

Between 1985 and 1987 a radiation therapy machine called the Therac-25 massively overdosed at least six patients in the United States and Canada. Several died. The machine could deliver a low-power electron beam or a much stronger beam meant to be spread out by a metal target. Earlier models had physical safety interlocks, mechanical parts that made a dangerous combination impossible. The Therac-25 relied on software instead.

The software had a flaw that appeared only when a skilled operator typed quickly. If she corrected an entry within a few seconds, the machine could end up in an inconsistent state: high power, with the spreading target out of the way. The screen showed a cryptic error. Operators had learned that such errors were usually harmless and pressed a key to continue. Patients received doses many times the intended amount.

Investigators found no single villain, but a recurring pattern: hardware safeguards removed because software "would handle it", software reused without re-testing, error messages nobody understood, and reports of harm dismissed as impossible. The case is now taught in software courses worldwide.

Chapter 10

Implants that wore out too fast

Materials can fail as badly as software. In the 2000s, several hip designs used a metal ball moving in a metal socket. The idea was that metal would wear less than plastic, which suited younger, active patients. In practice some designs shed metal particles and released metal ions into the body. These caused tissue damage around the joint, and many patients needed a second, harder operation. Major products were recalled from 2010 onward, and lawsuits followed.

A design can look excellent on a laboratory wear machine and still behave differently in thousands of real bodies. That is why many countries now keep joint registries, national databases that follow every implant over years. Registries spotted the problems with some metal-on-metal hips earlier than individual surgeons could have. Long-term tracking of real patients is the only way to see failures that take years to appear.

Chapter 11

Who the device was designed for

Return to the pulse oximeter and its calibration table. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a 2020 study in the New England Journal of Medicine compared finger readings with direct blood tests in hospital patients. It found that dangerously low oxygen levels went undetected by the oximeter roughly three times as often in Black patients as in white patients. Darker skin pigment absorbs some of the light, and many devices had been calibrated mostly on lighter-skinned volunteers.

Regulators, including the FDA, began reviewing how oximeters are tested. The general rule is simple. A device embodies assumptions about its users, and the people missing from testing are the people most likely to be failed. The same applies to algorithms trained on data from a few hospitals, and to equipment designed for hospitals with stable electricity.

That last point matters. Donated equipment in poorer countries often sits unused for lack of spare parts, training or power. A branch of the field, sometimes called global health engineering, designs for those conditions from the start: robust, cheap and easy to repair.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Enormous benefit, slow and costly to prove

The same caution that protects patients also slows innovation and raises prices. There is no setting of the dial that removes both problems.

The deepest tension is between speed and certainty. Approve quickly and some harmful devices get through, as the metal-on-metal hips did. Approve slowly and patients go without useful ones. Countries strike the balance differently, and the argument is ongoing.

Chapter 14

The software turn, and its risks

The biggest change already under way is that more and more of a device's behaviour lives in software. Regulators now approve software as a medical device, programs that diagnose or guide treatment with no special hardware at all. Hundreds of AI-based tools, mostly for reading images, had FDA clearance by the mid-2020s.

Software brings a problem that the Therac-25 only hinted at: cybersecurity. Researchers have shown that some older pacemakers and insulin pumps could, in principle, be reprogrammed wirelessly by an attacker. Manufacturers issued fixes, and since 2023 the United States has required new connected devices to include a cybersecurity plan before approval. An update for a device inside someone's chest must be tested as carefully as the original.

The engineering questions will increasingly be about trust: who checks the algorithm, who owns the data, and who supports the device for the twenty years it stays in a body.

The core idea

Biomedical engineering builds devices that measure, support or replace parts of the body. Its special difficulty is that the body varies, reacts to anything foreign, and cannot be tested freely. So most of the work is proving safety, and the worst failures come from assumptions nobody checked.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First implanted pacemaker: 1958, Stockholm. The patient lived 43 more years.
  • The US sorts devices into three risk classes. Implants that sustain life are Class III.
  • Roughly a million cochlear implants worldwide by the early 2020s.
  • Undetected low oxygen about three times as common in Black patients in a 2020 oximeter study.
The common mistake

Thinking a device that works in the lab works in patients. Bodies vary and respond, and many failures appear only after years and thousands of users, which is why registries and long-term follow-up matter.

Can you explain why a pulse oximeter could be less accurate for some people, using the words "calibration" and "light absorption"? If so, you understand both how sensors work and why who is tested matters.

The words, in plain words
Biomedical engineering
Engineering applied to diagnosing, treating and supporting the human body.
Medical device
Any instrument, implant, machine or software used for a medical purpose that does not work mainly through chemistry, as a drug does.
Sensor
A part that turns a physical change, such as voltage, light or pressure, into a signal a machine can read.
ECG
Electrocardiogram: a recording of the heart's electrical activity from electrodes on the skin.
Pulse oximeter
A finger clip that estimates blood oxygen by comparing how red and infrared light pass through tissue.
Calibration
Tuning a device's output against known reference measurements.
Biocompatibility
A material's ability to cause only an acceptable response from the body in a specific use.
Osseointegration
Living bone bonding directly to an implant surface, usually titanium.
Closed loop
A system that measures, decides and acts automatically, such as a sensor-driven insulin pump.
Design controls
The documented process linking every requirement to a design decision and a test.
Joint registry
A national database that tracks implanted joints over years to spot failures early.
