Engineering in the AI era. Faster answers, and a harder question: how do you know they are right?

Engineers have used computers to calculate for as long as there have been computers. What is new is software that learns patterns from data instead of following equations someone wrote down. It can predict the airflow over a wing in a fraction of a second, spot a crack in a photo of a turbine blade, or suggest a bracket shape no human would draw. It can also be confidently wrong, in ways that are hard to detect. Engineering is the profession whose mistakes fall on people's heads. So the arrival of AI in engineering is less a story about speed than a story about trust: what to hand over, what to check, and who signs.

Question 1 · What is it?

A new kind of tool in an old, cautious profession

"AI in engineering" covers several quite different technologies. What they share is that they learn from data, and that their reasoning cannot be checked line by line the way an engineer's hand calculation can.

Chapter 01

The antenna nobody drew

In 2006 NASA launched three small satellites for a mission called Space Technology 5. Each carried a radio antenna that looked like a bent paperclip, a twisted little wire with no obvious logic to its shape. No engineer had designed it in the usual sense. A team at NASA's Ames Research Center had given a computer the requirements and let it "evolve" the shape: generate thousands of random designs, simulate how well each one worked, keep the best, mix and mutate them, and repeat. The winner met the mission's requirements, and it flew.

That method, called an evolutionary algorithm, is an older branch of AI. The story still captures the central promise and the central worry of AI in engineering. The promise: machines can search through far more possibilities than a person ever could, and find designs we would not think of. The worry: when the result looks strange, the engineer has to be able to show that it works for the right reasons, not just in the simulation it was trained on.

Most of these are not "general" AI. They are narrow tools trained on one kind of data for one job. The language assistants are the exception, and also the least proven for safety-critical engineering decisions.

Chapter 03

Why engineering is not like software

In software, a bad suggestion from an AI often produces an error message, and the fix is a new version. In physical engineering, a bad suggestion can become a cracked beam, a leaking pipe or a falling aircraft, and you cannot ship an update to a poured concrete foundation. Three features make the field especially demanding.

First, physics is non-negotiable. A design must obey conservation of energy and the strength of materials whether or not the training data covered that case. Second, data is scarce where it matters most. There are millions of photos of cats online, but very few recordings of a specific bridge failing, because failures are rare and every structure is different. Third, engineering is regulated. Aircraft, medical devices, buildings and power plants must be certified, and certification rules assume that someone can explain why the design is safe. Those three facts shape everything else in this file.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Learning from simulations, sensors and documents

AI enters the engineering workflow at three points: when exploring designs, when watching machines in operation, and when handling the enormous amount of text engineering produces.

Chapter 04

Surrogates: fast imitations of slow physics

A detailed simulation of airflow around a car, or of stress in a turbine disc, can take hours or days on a powerful computer. That limits how many designs can be tried. A surrogate model is a machine-learning model trained on the results of many such simulations. Once trained, it predicts the answer for a new design almost instantly. Engineers can then explore thousands of variations, and run the full simulation only on the most promising few.

The most striking public example comes from weather forecasting, which is fluid dynamics on a planetary scale. In 2023 Google DeepMind published GraphCast, a machine-learning model trained on decades of past weather data. On many standard measures it matched or beat the leading traditional forecast system for forecasts up to about ten days, while running far faster. Major forecasting centres now run AI models alongside their physics-based ones.

The catch is the edge of the data. A surrogate is good at interpolating, filling in between cases it has seen, and unreliable at extrapolating to situations outside its training. A new material, an extreme load or an unusual shape can produce a confident, wrong answer. One response is physics-informed machine learning, which builds known equations into the model so that its predictions cannot break basic physical laws.

Chapter 05

Generative design: describing the problem, not the answer

In traditional design, an engineer draws a part and then checks it. In generative design, the engineer describes the problem: where the part attaches, what loads it carries, where it must not go, what material and manufacturing method will be used. Software then searches for shapes that meet those conditions with the least material. The results often look like bone or tree roots, because nature solved similar problems of carrying load with little material.

The underlying technique, topology optimisation, predates the current AI wave. Machine learning speeds it up and helps search wider. Many of the resulting shapes can only be made by 3D printing, which is why the two technologies have grown together. The engineer's role shifts from drawing the part to stating the problem precisely, which, as the previous topic argued, was always the hardest part of design.

Chapter 06

Watching machines: inspection and predictive maintenance

Inspecting infrastructure has traditionally meant people on ropes, ladders and scaffolding, looking closely at surfaces. Drones and robots can now photograph a wind turbine blade, a bridge deck or a tunnel lining in minutes, and computer-vision models flag images that show cracks, corrosion or damage. The human inspector reviews the flagged cases instead of every image. This does not remove the inspector. It lets them cover far more ground.

Predictive maintenance applies the same idea to sensor data. A bearing about to fail usually vibrates differently weeks before it breaks. A model that has learned the normal pattern for each machine can warn when the pattern changes, so the part is replaced at a planned stop instead of during a breakdown. Aircraft engine makers have monitored engines in flight for many years, and machine learning has extended this approach to pumps, trains, wind turbines and factory equipment. Combined with a detailed model of the asset, this becomes a digital twin.

Why false alarms matter

A warning system that cries wolf too often is soon ignored. For predictive maintenance to work, the model must be right often enough that crews trust it, and its alerts must arrive early enough to act on. Getting that balance is usually harder than building the model.

Chapter 07

Language models at the engineer's desk

Engineering produces mountains of text: standards, specifications, test reports, maintenance manuals, regulations and emails. Large language models, the technology behind chat assistants (explained in Volume 49), are well suited to searching, summarising and drafting it. Engineers use them to find the relevant clause in a long standard, draft a first version of a report, translate documents, or write scripts that automate simulations and data analysis.

The danger is the one Volume 49 describes: they can produce fluent, plausible text that is wrong, including invented references and numbers (why it invents things). In most writing that is an annoyance. In engineering, a wrong load value or a misquoted standard can be dangerous. The safe pattern is to use them to find and draft, and to verify every number and requirement against the original source. Many firms now also connect them only to their own approved documents, so answers can be traced back to a page.

Chapter 08

A contested case: chips designing chips

Designing a computer chip involves placing billions of tiny components and connecting them efficiently, a problem with an astronomical number of possible layouts. Chip-design software has used automated optimisation for decades. In 2021 a Google team published a paper in Nature reporting that reinforcement learning could produce chip floorplans, the large-scale arrangement of blocks on a chip, comparable to or better than human experts, in hours rather than weeks.

The result was later disputed. Other researchers questioned whether the method really beat strong existing tools when compared fairly, and the debate became public and heated. Whatever the final verdict, AI-assisted features are now standard in commercial chip-design software. The episode is a useful reminder that dramatic claims about AI in engineering deserve the same scrutiny as any other engineering claim: what was it compared against, on what tests, and can others reproduce it?

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Speed, a new kind of demand, and a changing job

AI changes how fast engineers can work, creates enormous new engineering projects of its own, and shifts what engineers need to know.

Chapter 09

Shorter loops between idea and test

The design loop described in the previous topic, idea, model, test, learn, used to be limited by how long each turn took. When a simulation drops from a day to a second, an engineer can try ideas during a meeting instead of waiting for overnight results. When a drone survey replaces weeks of manual inspection, maintenance can be planned on fresh data.

Faster loops do not just save time. They change behaviour. When trying an idea is cheap, people try more ideas, including odd ones, and the range of designs explored widens. The risk is the opposite of the old one: instead of too few options considered carefully, too many options considered shallowly, with the final choice resting on a model nobody fully checked.

Chapter 10

AI itself is an engineering megaproject

The other side of this topic is engineering for AI. Training and running large models requires data centres packed with chips that consume large amounts of electricity and produce heat that must be removed (see Volume 14). Since the early 2020s, the largest technology companies have been building data centres at a pace that strains local grids, water supplies and supply chains for transformers and switchgear.

This has turned AI into a story about very traditional engineering. Electrical engineers must connect huge new loads. Mechanical engineers are moving from air cooling to liquid cooling that pipes coolant directly to the chips. Power companies are signing deals for new generation, including nuclear. Chip factories, among the most complex structures ever built, are being constructed on several continents (see Volume 12). The most immediate effect of AI on engineering may be the sheer amount of physical engineering it requires, linking it to grids and infrastructure.

Chapter 11

What happens to the engineer's job

The honest answer is that nobody knows the full effect yet, and studies of AI and jobs are early and disputed (see How work may change because of AI). Some patterns are already visible in engineering. Routine tasks, such as first drafts of documents, standard calculations and sorting inspection images, are being automated. Tasks that involve defining problems, judging trade-offs, dealing with clients and regulators, and taking responsibility are not.

There is a subtler risk, familiar from the "irony of automation" in Control systems. If junior engineers never do hand calculations because a tool does them, they may never build the intuition that lets a senior engineer glance at a result and say "that cannot be right". The skill AI makes most valuable is the ability to tell when AI is wrong, and that skill is built by doing the work it replaces. Engineering schools and firms are debating how to train for it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Speed and reach, against opacity and new failure modes

The benefits are concrete and already measurable in some areas. The risks are concentrated where engineering is most unforgiving.

Chapter 13

Who signs? Certification and accountability

In regulated engineering, a named, qualified person or organisation takes legal responsibility for a design. That system assumes the reasoning can be checked. Learned models do not fit it easily. Regulators are adapting. The European Union Aviation Safety Agency has published a roadmap and guidance for using machine learning in aviation, starting with lower-risk assistance functions. The EU's AI Act, adopted in 2024, treats AI used as a safety component in regulated products as "high-risk", with requirements for testing, documentation and human oversight.

The common principle emerging is that responsibility stays with people. An AI tool can propose, flag and draft, but an engineer must be able to justify the final design by means they understand: a check calculation, a physical test, an independent simulation. Many firms put it simply. The tool does not sign the drawing.

Chapter 15

What stays human

Engineering has absorbed new tools before. The slide rule gave way to the calculator, the drawing board to CAD, hand calculation to simulation. Each time, the routine work shrank and the judgement work grew. Each time, there were failures caused by trusting the new tool too much before the profession learned its limits.

AI is likely to follow the same path, with one difference: its errors are harder to see. A wrong calculator answer can be checked by redoing the sum. A learned model's wrong answer can look exactly like a right one. The engineers who matter most in the AI era will be the ones who know physics well enough to doubt a confident machine, who can state problems precisely, and who accept that, however good the tools become, the responsibility for what gets built remains theirs.

The core idea

AI helps engineers through surrogate simulations, generative design, inspection, predictive maintenance, learned control and language assistants. It is strongest within its training data and weakest at the edges, so verification and human accountability remain central.

The numbers worth knowing
  • NASA's evolved antenna flew on Space Technology 5 in 2006.
  • GraphCast (2023) matched or beat a leading physics forecast on many measures up to about 10 days.
  • The EU AI Act, adopted 2024, treats AI safety components as high-risk.
The common mistake

Trusting a fast, confident answer because it usually works. Models fail most where data is thinnest: new materials, extreme loads, rare events.
