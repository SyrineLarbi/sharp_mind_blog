Business models. Two companies can sell the same thing and live completely different lives.

A business model is the logic of how a company creates something people want and turns part of that value into money. It answers three plain questions: who pays, for what, and how often. The questions sound simple, but the answers decide almost everything else, from how fast a company can grow to how it treats its customers. The same song can be sold on a disc, for 99 cents as a download, or as one of millions included in a monthly subscription. Each choice created a different industry.

Question 1 · What is it?

The recipe, not the dish

A product is what you sell. A business model is the whole arrangement around it: who it is for, how it reaches them, who pays, and why the sums work.

Chapter 01

Same film, three businesses

Think about how people have paid to watch a film at home. In the 1990s you walked to a rental shop and paid a few dollars per film, plus a fine if you returned it late. Netflix, founded in 1997, started by mailing DVDs for a monthly fee with no late fees. From 2007 it began streaming over the internet, and over the next few years streaming replaced the discs.

The films were often identical. What changed was the business model. The rental shop earned per transaction, and a meaningful part of its income came from penalties. A subscription earns every month whether you watch or not, so the company's job becomes keeping you subscribed rather than charging you each time. When the way of earning changes, the company's incentives change, and so does the experience of the customer.

Chapter 02

A model on one page

In 2010 Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur published Business Model Generation, which popularised a one-page template called the Business Model Canvas. It splits any model into nine boxes. You do not need to memorise them, but they show what the word covers.

AreaThe boxesThe plain question
The customerCustomer segments, relationships, channelsWho is it for, and how do we reach and keep them?
The offerValue propositionWhat problem do we solve, and why us?
The engineKey activities, key resources, key partnersWhat must we do and own, and who helps?
The moneyRevenue streams, cost structureWho pays, how, and what does it cost us?

The useful insight is that the boxes depend on each other. A cheaper channel changes the cost structure. A new customer segment may need a different relationship. That is why a model can be copied in outline and still fail: the pieces only work in the right combination.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Seven patterns that cover most of the economy

Most companies use one of a small number of recurring patterns, or a blend of two or three. Learn the patterns and you can see the machinery behind almost any brand.

Chapter 03

Selling things, one at a time

The oldest model is the plainest. You make or buy a product and sell it for more than it cost. A bakery, a carmaker and a furniture shop all work this way. The strength is clarity: the customer pays, the customer gets the thing, the deal is done.

The weakness is that every year starts from zero. Last year's customers owe you nothing. Carmakers must persuade people to buy again, which may be a decade later. That is why product companies work so hard on brand loyalty and on services added later, such as maintenance, spare parts and financing, which often earn better margins than the product itself.

Chapter 04

Subscriptions: renting the relationship

A subscription charges a regular fee for continuing access. Newspapers and gyms have done this for generations, but software made it dominant. In 2013 Adobe stopped selling boxed versions of its Creative Suite, which included Photoshop, and moved to Creative Cloud, a monthly subscription. Many customers were angry at first. Over time Adobe's revenue became far more predictable and grew strongly.

The key measures are recurring revenue (what existing subscribers will pay in the coming period) and churn (the share who cancel). A business losing 5 percent of subscribers a month must replace roughly half its customers every year just to stand still. In a subscription business, keeping a customer is worth as much as winning one.

Chapter 05

Razors and blades

Sell the base cheaply, then earn on what it needs to keep working. Home printers are the familiar case: the printer can be inexpensive, while the ink cartridges carry high margins. Coffee-capsule machines and many game consoles follow the same logic. The pattern is named after razors, whose makers are said to have given away handles and profited on replacement blades.

The real history of the razor is messier than the textbook story, and economists have argued about how closely early razor firms followed it. But the pattern is real. It works only if customers cannot easily use cheaper supplies from someone else. So companies protect it with patents, chips in cartridges, and designs that only accept their own refills. The model depends on a lock, and customers and regulators tend to push against locks.

Chapter 06

Advertising: the user is not the customer

Google Search and Facebook are free to use. Their owners, Alphabet and Meta, are among the most profitable companies in the world. The trick is a two-sided arrangement. Users get a service for free and give their attention, and often their data. Advertisers pay to reach those users. For both companies, advertising has provided the large majority of revenue.

The model changes incentives in a specific way. The company earns more when users spend more time and when ads are better targeted. That pushes it to make the product absorbing and to collect detailed information about behaviour. Whoever pays is the real customer, and the product is designed around them. This tension is behind most of the privacy and attention debates of the past decade (see Privacy, what is actually collected).

Chapter 07

Marketplaces and platforms

A marketplace does not sell its own goods. It connects buyers and sellers and takes a cut of each deal, called the take rate. eBay connects people selling objects. Airbnb connects hosts and travellers. Uber connects drivers and passengers. The platform owns very little of what changes hands.

Marketplaces are powered by network effects: each new user makes the service more valuable for everyone else. More sellers attract more buyers, which attract more sellers. Once a marketplace leads, it is very hard to dislodge. The hard part is the start, often called the chicken-and-egg problem: no buyer comes to an empty market, and no seller lists where there are no buyers. Founders solve it by subsidising one side, starting in one small city or niche, or supplying the first inventory themselves.

Chapter 08

Freemium: free for most, paid for a few

Freemium (free plus premium) gives a basic version away and charges for more: more storage, no ads, extra features. Spotify, Dropbox and LinkedIn all use versions of it. The free tier is the marketing. It costs little to serve each extra free user, so the company can let millions try the product.

Usually only a small share of users ever pay, often a few percent for software tools, though music streaming has converted far more. The economics only work if free users are cheap to serve and the paid tier is valuable enough. Get the line between free and paid wrong in one direction and nobody upgrades. Get it wrong in the other and nobody bothers to try.

Chapter 09

Licensing and franchising: earning from others' work

Some companies earn by letting others use what they own. Arm, a British chip-design company, does not make chips. It designs processor architectures and licenses them to other companies, earning a royalty on each chip sold; its designs are used in almost every smartphone. Character and brand licensing works the same way for toys and clothing.

Franchising applies the idea to a whole business system. A franchisee, usually a local business owner, pays to operate a restaurant or shop under a brand, following its methods, and pays fees and royalties on sales. Most McDonald's restaurants worldwide are run by franchisees, and the company earns much of its income from their rent and royalties rather than from selling burgers itself. The franchisor grows using other people's money and effort, and gives up some control in exchange.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The model often matters more than the product

Great products die inside bad models, and ordinary products thrive inside clever ones. Model changes have reshaped entire industries.

Chapter 10

How music changed its model three times

For most of the late twentieth century, the recorded-music business sold physical albums. A fan who wanted one song often had to buy twelve. Then file-sharing services, starting with Napster in 1999, let people copy music for free, and industry revenue fell for years. In 2003 Apple opened the iTunes Store, selling single songs for 99 cents in the United States. It was legal and simple, but it also unbundled the album, and fans bought the hits rather than the whole record.

Streaming services such as Spotify, launched in 2008, changed the model again: a monthly fee, or free with ads, for access to almost everything. After many lean years, global recorded-music revenue returned to growth in the mid-2010s, driven mainly by streaming subscriptions. The music was not the problem. The model that sold it had stopped fitting how people behaved. The argument continues over whether streaming pays artists fairly, because payments are split per play across a very large catalogue.

Chapter 11

Winning with a new model in an old industry

Low-cost airlines are a good example of model innovation. Southwest Airlines in the United States and later Ryanair and easyJet in Europe flew mostly one aircraft type to simplify maintenance and training, used cheaper secondary airports, turned planes around quickly, sold tickets directly, and charged separately for extras such as bags and seat choice. None of these ideas was a new technology. Together they made a different machine, with costs low enough that older airlines found it hard to match prices.

This is why investors and strategists ask about the model first. A product can be copied in months. A well-fitted model, where every piece supports the others, is much harder to copy, because a rival would have to change everything at once. The idea is developed further in Business strategy and Competitive advantage.

Chapter 12

Why it matters to you as a customer

Knowing a company's model tells you how it will behave. A subscription service wants to make cancelling slightly harder than signing up. An advertising-funded app wants your time. A razor-and-blades seller wants you locked to its refills. A marketplace wants both sides dependent on it, then may raise its cut. None of this is sinister in itself. It is simply what the model rewards.

Ask "how does this company make money from me?" and most of its design choices start to make sense. It is the single most useful question for judging a free offer, a loyalty scheme or a "deal" that seems too good.

Chapter 14

The hidden risk: depending on someone else's model

Many businesses are built on top of another company's platform: app developers depend on Apple's and Google's app stores, sellers depend on Amazon, news sites depend on search and social traffic. When the platform changes its rules, fees or algorithm, the dependent business can lose a large share of its income overnight. Disputes over app-store commissions, which for many years were typically 15 to 30 percent, have led to lawsuits and to new rules in the European Union.

A business model is only as safe as its weakest dependency. Founders often discover that their real landlord is a platform they never signed a lease with.

Whether that improves things depends on who controls the measurement. These shifts connect to Pricing and to Business disruption, where a new model is often the weapon.

The core idea

A business model answers who pays, for what, and how often. Most companies use one or a blend of a few patterns: products, subscriptions, razors and blades, advertising, marketplaces, freemium, licensing and franchising. Each shapes the company's incentives, and so its behaviour toward customers.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Losing 5 percent of subscribers a month means replacing about half every year.
  • App-store commissions were typically 15–30 percent for many years.
  • Freemium software often converts only a few percent of users to paid.
The common mistake

Judging a company only by its product. A great product inside a model that does not fit the market still fails, and a modest product inside a well-fitted model can dominate.

Test yourself

Pick an app you use daily. Who pays for it, for what, and what does that make the company want you to do? If you can answer all three, you can read a business model.

The words, in plain words
Business model
The logic of how a company creates value for customers and captures part of it as money.
Value proposition

Business Model Canvas
A one-page template that splits a model into nine linked boxes.
Subscription
A regular fee for continuing access to a product or service.
Recurring revenue
Income expected to repeat each period from existing customers.
Churn
The share of customers who leave in a given period.

Selling a base product cheaply and profiting on the supplies it needs.
Two-sided market

Freemium

Franchising

Licensing
Charging others for the right to use intellectual property such as designs or characters.
