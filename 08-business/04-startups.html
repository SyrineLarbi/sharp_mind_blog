Startups. A company built to grow so fast that most of them must fail.

A new café and a new software company are both new businesses. Only one of them is usually called a startup. The difference is not technology or youth. It is ambition and the arrangement that serves it: a plan to grow extremely fast, money from investors who expect most of their bets to fail, and a reward structure built around a small number of huge successes. That arrangement has produced many of the most powerful companies on earth. It has also produced some spectacular frauds, and an enormous number of quiet failures.

Question 1 · What is it?

Defined by growth, not by age or gadgets

The clearest definition comes from the people who fund startups for a living, and it is simpler than most people expect.

Chapter 01

"A company designed to grow fast"

Paul Graham, co-founder of the startup accelerator Y Combinator, put it bluntly in a 2012 essay: a startup is a company designed to grow fast. Being newly founded does not make a company a startup. Neither does working in technology or taking investment. The only essential thing is growth, and everything else about startups follows from it.

Graham suggested that a good growth rate, during the early phase, is around 5 to 7 percent a week. Compounded, 5 percent weekly is more than a tenfold increase in a year. A café cannot grow like that: it can only serve the people who walk past. To grow that fast, a business needs a product that can reach millions of people without needing millions of staff, and a market large enough to absorb it.

Chapter 02

Why most startups are software

Software fits the requirement almost perfectly. Writing a program is expensive, but serving one more user costs very little, as How companies make money explained with gross margins. The product can be distributed worldwide over the internet in a day. Improvements reach every user at once.

Not every startup is software. Biotech startups develop drugs, others build batteries, rockets or food products. But these need far more money and time before they earn, which makes them harder to fund. The more a business can grow without adding physical cost per customer, the better it fits the startup model.

A small business

Aims for steady profit from the start. Grows at the pace its customers and cash allow. Usually funded by the owner and the bank. Success means a good, lasting income.

A startup

Aims for rapid growth first, profit later. Often loses money for years. Usually funded by selling shares to investors. Success means becoming very large, often ending in a sale or a stock-market listing.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Venture capital, funding rounds and the mathematics of a few big wins

The startup machine runs on a particular kind of money with particular expectations. Once you see how investors are paid, the behaviour of startups makes sense.

Chapter 03

What a venture capital fund actually is

A venture capital (VC) fund is a pool of money raised from large investors, such as pension funds, university endowments and wealthy families. They are called limited partners. The fund is managed by a small team, the general partners, who choose which startups to invest in.

A typical fund lasts about ten years. The managers invest during the first few years and spend the rest helping companies grow and waiting for them to be sold or listed. The traditional pay structure is often summarised as "two and twenty": an annual management fee of around 2 percent of the fund, plus around 20 percent of the profits, called carried interest. Terms vary between funds. Venture capitalists get rich only if the fund returns much more than it raised, and that single fact drives what they look for.

Chapter 04

The power law: why one winner pays for everything

Startup outcomes are wildly uneven. Most investments return little or nothing. A few return a few times the money. Very rarely, one returns a hundred times or more. Distributions like this are called a power law: a tiny number of results account for most of the total.

Because of this, a VC investor does not ask "is this company likely to succeed?" Most will not. The question is "if this succeeds, could it become so big that it returns the whole fund?" A solid company that might grow into a comfortable 50-million-dollar business is a bad VC investment, however safe. This is why startups chase enormous markets and why investors push them to grow faster than feels comfortable.

Chapter 05

Funding rounds and dilution

Startups raise money in stages called rounds, each meant to fund the company to the next milestone. The names are conventions, and the amounts have changed a lot over time.

RoundTypical stageTypical investors
Pre-seed / seedAn idea, a prototype, a few early usersFounders, friends, angel investors, accelerators, seed funds
Series AEvidence that customers want it; building a repeatable way to sellVenture capital funds
Series B, C and laterScaling: more markets, more staff, more productsLarger VC funds, growth investors
ExitSale to another company, or listing on a stock exchange (IPO)Buyers, public shareholders

Each round sells new shares, so earlier owners own a smaller percentage. This is called dilution. Suppose a founder owns 100 percent and sells 20 percent at seed, then 20 percent of the enlarged company at Series A. She now owns 64 percent. If each round is raised at a higher valuation (the price the investors put on the whole company), her smaller slice is still worth more. Dilution is fine as long as the pie grows faster than your slice shrinks.

Chapter 06

Accelerators and the founder's first year

An accelerator is a short, intense programme, usually around three months, that gives a group of very early startups a small investment, advice and connections in exchange for a slice of equity. Y Combinator, founded in 2005, made the format famous; its alumni include Airbnb, Stripe and Dropbox. Programmes end with a "demo day", when founders pitch to investors.

The central message of accelerators is focus: talk to users, build what they need, measure growth every week, and ignore distractions like conferences and press. The founder's job in the first year is to find what the next file calls product-market fit. Before that point, almost nothing else matters. After it, almost everything changes.

Chapter 07

Shares for staff: options and vesting

Startups usually cannot match big companies' salaries, so they pay partly in ownership. Employees receive stock options: the right to buy shares later at today's low price. If the company becomes valuable, those options can be worth a great deal. If it fails, they are worth nothing.

Options and founders' shares typically vest, meaning they are earned gradually. In the United States a common pattern is four years, with a one-year "cliff": leave before the first year and you get nothing; after it, a quarter is yours, and the rest arrives month by month. This keeps people committed through the hard years. The details, and their tax treatment, vary a lot by country.

Chapter 08

Unicorns and exits

In 2013 the investor Aileen Lee coined the term unicorn for a privately held startup valued at a billion dollars or more. At the time such companies were rare, which was the point of the name. By the early 2020s there were well over a thousand worldwide, and the word had lost some of its magic.

A private valuation is not cash. It is the price at which the last investor bought a small slice, often with special protections that ordinary shares lack. Investors and employees only get money when there is an exit: an acquisition by a larger company or an initial public offering (IPO), when shares start trading on a stock exchange. Until then, a startup's value is an opinion, not a fact.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where much of modern technology came from, and how it went wrong

A small number of venture-backed companies have shaped how billions of people search, shop, travel and talk. The same machinery also rewards hype.

Chapter 09

A tiny share of companies, a huge share of change

Venture-backed startups are a very small share of all new businesses. Yet many of the companies that define modern life began this way: Apple, Amazon, Google, Facebook, Airbnb and Uber all took venture capital early on, as have most of the newer AI model developers. Research on US public companies has found that firms which once took venture capital account for a large share of the market value and research spending of companies founded in recent decades.

The reason is the model itself. Venture capital is one of the few forms of finance willing to fund ideas that will probably fail but might be enormous. Banks lend against assets and steady cash flow. A startup has neither. Without investors who accept frequent losses, many transformative technologies would have struggled to get money in their early years.

Chapter 10

Why startups cluster

Startups are not spread evenly. Silicon Valley, around San Francisco, remains the largest cluster, with others in places such as New York, London, Boston, Beijing, Shenzhen, Bangalore, Tel Aviv and Paris. Clusters form because the ingredients feed each other: universities produce engineers; experienced founders become investors and mentors; employees from failed startups join new ones; lawyers and bankers specialise.

Governments everywhere have tried to build their own version, with mixed results. Money and office space are easy to supply. Dense networks of experienced people take decades to grow. A tolerant attitude to failure also matters: in places where a failed founder can raise money again, more people are willing to try.

Chapter 11

When "fake it till you make it" becomes fraud

The startup world prizes bold visions and rapid growth, and that creates a temptation to present hopes as facts. Theranos claimed it could run a wide range of medical tests from a few drops of blood. It raised hundreds of millions of dollars and was valued at around 9 billion dollars before journalists and regulators showed the technology did not work as claimed. Its founder, Elizabeth Holmes, was convicted of fraud in 2022.

FTX, a cryptocurrency exchange valued at about 32 billion dollars, collapsed in November 2022 after it emerged that customer funds had been used by an affiliated trading firm. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was convicted of fraud in 2023. In both cases, well-known investors had put in money. Famous backers are not due diligence. Both stories show what happens when a culture that rewards confidence meets weak checks on what is actually true.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful engine with a narrow purpose

The venture model is excellent at one thing: funding long shots that could become giants. It is poorly suited to almost everything else.

Chapter 12

The strongest criticism is about fit, not morality. Venture money is rocket fuel, and rocket fuel is the wrong thing to put in most cars. Many founders who take it would have built healthier, longer-lived companies without the pressure for growth at any cost.

Chapter 14

The lesson of the dot-com bust

The last great startup boom ended in 2000. The Nasdaq index, heavy with technology shares, peaked in March 2000 and lost roughly three quarters of its value over the next two and a half years. Many startups vanished. But the fibre-optic cables, data centres and engineering skills built during the boom stayed, and they became the foundation for the next wave, including companies such as Google and Amazon that survived the crash.

That pattern, a boom that overbuilds followed by a bust that leaves useful infrastructure behind, is a reasonable lens for any period of startup excitement. Both the believers and the sceptics are often right, at different times. The companies that last are the ones that reached real customers who kept paying, which is what the next file is about.

The core idea

A startup is a company designed to grow fast. It is usually funded by venture capital, which expects most investments to fail and relies on a power law in which one huge win pays for the rest. That expectation explains the hunger for big markets, the pressure for speed, and the habit of losing money for years.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Early growth of 5–7 percent a week is Paul Graham's benchmark for a good startup.
  • VC funds typically last about 10 years, with fees often near "2 and 20".
  • Standard US vesting: 4 years with a 1-year cliff.
The common mistake

Treating a private valuation as money in the bank. A valuation is the price of the last small slice sold, often with special terms, and it is only real at an exit.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a venture investor might turn down a company that is very likely to become a solid, profitable business? If you can, you understand the power law.
