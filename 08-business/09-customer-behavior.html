Customer behavior. People rarely buy what they say they want, and usually buy what they bought last time.

Ask people why they chose a product and they will give you a sensible answer about price and quality. Watch them shop and you see something else: habits, half-second glances, decisions shaped by where an item sat on the shelf and what the friend beside them picked up. The study of customer behaviour sits between psychology and business. It tries to explain real choices, not stated ones, and it gives companies a great deal of power. This file explains what it has found, how firms use it, and where the line with manipulation lies.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of real choices, not reported ones

Customer behaviour (often called consumer behaviour) is the study of how people find, choose, use and drop products and services, and why.

Chapter 01

The faster horses nobody asked for

A famous line is attributed to Henry Ford: "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses." There is no good evidence that Ford ever said it. It survives because it captures something true. People are poor reporters of their own wants and reasons. They can describe their frustrations well. They are much worse at predicting what they will buy, and at explaining afterwards why they bought it.

Market researchers meet this gap constantly. Shoppers say in surveys that they care about the environment, then buy the cheaper product. Diners say they want healthy options, then order the burger. This is not hypocrisy. The survey asks the reflective part of the mind, the part that thinks about who we want to be. The shop is run by the fast, automatic part that wants to get home. Volume 35 calls these fast and slow thinking.

So the first rule of the field is simple: trust what people do over what they say. Economists call this the difference between stated preferences and revealed preferences.

Chapter 02

The buying journey

Textbooks since the late 1960s have described buying as a sequence of stages. The names vary, but the idea is stable.

StageWhat happensExample: buying a new phone
NeedSomething triggers the feeling that a change is neededThe battery dies by lunchtime
SearchThe buyer gathers options, from memory and from outsideAsks friends, reads reviews, visits a shop
EvaluationOptions are compared, often on just two or three featuresCamera, price, same brand as before
PurchaseThe choice is made, sometimes changed at the last momentA discount on a rival model tips it
After purchaseThe buyer judges the decision, and talks about itDefends the choice to a sceptical friend

In 2009 the consultancy McKinsey argued that the stages are really a loop. Buyers who are happy often skip the search entirely next time and simply buy again. That loop is the most valuable thing a company can have. The best position in a market is to be bought without being compared.

Chapter 03

Grouping customers: segmentation

No company can treat every customer differently, so it groups them. This is segmentation. The oldest method uses demographics: age, income, gender, location. It is easy, because the data exists, but often weak, because two 45-year-old women with the same income can live completely different lives.

Better methods group people by what they do and why. Behavioural segments use actions: how often someone buys, how much, through which channel. Psychographic segments use attitudes and values: the adventurous traveller, the careful saver. The most useful segmentation is usually the one that predicts behaviour, not the one that sounds most interesting in a presentation.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Habits, shortcuts, jobs and context

Four forces explain most of what buyers do. They repeat what worked. They use quick rules instead of careful analysis. They buy products to do a job. And the situation shapes the choice more than they realise.

Chapter 04

Most buying is habit

The psychologist Wendy Wood asked people to keep diaries of what they did each hour. Roughly four in ten of their everyday actions were habits: done almost daily, in the same place, while thinking about something else. Shopping is full of them. The same supermarket, the same route through it, the same brand of tea.

A habit is triggered by a cue in the environment, such as a time of day, a place or a feeling, and runs without a decision. That has a big business consequence. A habitual customer is not choosing you every week. They are simply not reconsidering. Competitors cannot win them with a slightly better product, because the customer never looks. They have to break the habit first, usually with a free trial, a big discount or a moment when life changes (Chapter 10). The mechanics of habit formation are in Volume 35.

Chapter 05

Shortcuts at the moment of choice

When people do choose, they rarely weigh every feature. They use heuristics, quick rules of thumb that are usually good enough. Several matter especially in shops and apps.

Social proof

If many others chose it, it is probably fine. Star ratings, "bestseller" labels and the queue outside a restaurant all work this way. For new and online products, reviews have become one of the most important inputs to a purchase.

Loss aversion

Losing something feels worse than gaining the same thing feels good. "Don't lose your discount" persuades more than "get a discount". Free trials work partly because, once you have the product, giving it up feels like a loss.

Framing

The same fact lands differently depending on how it is described. In a classic experiment, people rated beef described as "75% lean" more highly than the same beef described as "25% fat". See Volume 35, chapter 07.

Price as a signal of quality

When people cannot judge quality directly, they often assume the more expensive option is better. This is why cutting a price can sometimes reduce sales of wine, perfume or a professional service.

None of these shortcuts is foolish. They save time and work well most of the time. But because they are predictable, anyone who designs the choice can steer it. Chapter 11 returns to what happens when that is abused.

Chapter 06

People hire products to do a job

The Harvard professor Clayton Christensen liked to tell a story about milkshakes. A fast-food chain wanted to sell more of them and asked customers how to make them better: thicker, cheaper, more chocolatey. Small changes made no difference. Then researchers watched who bought milkshakes and when. Many were sold early in the morning, to people driving alone to work. They wanted something that lasted the whole boring drive, could be held in one hand and kept them full until lunch. The milkshake's real competitors were bananas, bagels and boredom, not other milkshakes.

Christensen called this jobs to be done. The question is not "who is our customer?" but "what job is the customer hiring this product to do?" The answer often reveals competitors and improvements that a demographic profile never would. A thicker shake that lasts longer helps the commuter. Making it cheaper does not.

Chapter 07

Context: the shelf, the default and the number of options

Supermarkets put staple items such as milk and bread at the back, so that shoppers pass everything else on the way. Products at eye level sell better than those near the floor, which is why makers pay retailers for good positions. Sweets sit beside the checkout, where waiting children and tired adults meet them. The environment does a great deal of the choosing.

Defaults are the most powerful version of this. Whatever happens if you do nothing, such as an automatic renewal or a pre-ticked box, tends to be what most people end up with. Companies set defaults in their own favour. Governments have learned to use them for pension saving, as the behavioural economics file explains.

The number of options matters too, but less simply than is often claimed. In a well-known 2000 study by Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper, a tasting stand with 24 jams attracted more visitors, but a stand with 6 jams led to far more purchases. The finding became famous as choice overload. Later reviews that combined many similar studies found the effect is real in some conditions and absent in others. On average it is close to zero. Too much choice hurts when the options are hard to compare and the buyer has no clear preference, not as a general rule.

Chapter 08

After the purchase: memory and self-justification

How customers remember an experience shapes whether they come back. Research by Daniel Kahneman and colleagues found that people judge a past experience mostly by two moments: its most intense point and its end. Its length barely counts. This is the peak-end rule. A long hotel stay with one excellent moment and a warm goodbye is remembered more fondly than a smooth stay that ended with a billing argument at checkout.

Buyers also defend their own decisions. The psychologist Leon Festinger described cognitive dissonance, the discomfort of holding two conflicting thoughts, such as "I am sensible" and "I just overpaid". People reduce it by finding reasons the purchase was good. That is why new car owners read adverts for the car they have just bought. Companies help with this on purpose: a thank-you message, a welcome guide, early proof that the product works. The sale is not finished when the money changes hands. It is finished when the buyer feels sure.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Data, life changes, and the limits of persuasion

Understanding customers decides which companies grow. Doing it with modern data gives firms an unusual view into private life, and that has forced regulators to draw lines.

Chapter 09

How companies actually find out

Firms have three main ways of learning about buyers, and each sees something different.

MethodWhat it is good forWhere it fails
Asking: surveys, interviews, focus groupsFinding frustrations, words customers use, ideas for new productsPeople cannot accurately predict or explain their own behaviour
Watching: observation, purchase data, app analyticsWhat people really do, at large scaleShows the what, not the why. Needs careful privacy handling
Testing: experiments, A/B testsProving cause and effect: did this change make people buy more?Only tests what someone thought to try. Finds small improvements, rarely big ideas

An A/B test shows two versions of something, such as a web page, to two random groups of visitors and compares the results. Large online companies run many thousands of these tests each year. It is the most reliable way to learn, because random assignment removes the explanations that usually confuse a business, such as the season, the weather or a competitor's advertising.

Chapter 10

The moments when habits break

If most buying is habit, the best time to win a customer is when their habits are disrupted. Moving house, starting university, getting married, having a child and retiring all scramble routines. New shops, new routes and new needs appear at once.

In 2012 the journalist Charles Duhigg described in The New York Times Magazine how the American retailer Target analysed shopping data to estimate which customers were likely to be pregnant, based on purchases such as unscented lotion and certain supplements. The goal was to reach new parents before rivals did, because a family that settles on a shop for nappies may keep buying everything else there for years. The details of some anecdotes in the story have been questioned, but the strategy it described was real. It also shocked many readers. The same data that lets a company help you can tell it things you have not told anyone.

Chapter 11

When design becomes a trap: dark patterns

A dark pattern is a design that uses predictable shortcuts against the user's own interest. Examples include a subscription that takes one click to join and a dozen steps to cancel, a countdown timer that resets when the page reloads, an extra item quietly added to the basket, or a "no thanks" button worded to make you feel foolish ("No, I don't want to save money").

Regulators now treat these as a consumer-protection issue. The European Union's Digital Services Act, adopted in 2022, bans online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon in 2023, alleging that its Prime subscription was hard to cancel and that people were enrolled without clear consent. The case was settled in 2025. The general principle is spreading: it should be as easy to leave as it was to join.

The line, roughly

Making a good option easy to choose is good design. Hiding information, creating false urgency or making a choice the buyer would regret if they understood it is manipulation. A useful test: would the company be comfortable explaining the design to the customer's face?

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Serving people better, or steering them better

Every insight in this file can be used to help customers or to exploit them. The knowledge is neutral. Its use is not.

Chapter 13

Customers are less foolish than the headlines suggest

Popular books on consumer psychology can leave the impression that shoppers are puppets. That goes too far. Several famous findings, as Chapter 07 showed with choice overload, turned out weaker or more conditional than first reported when other researchers tried to repeat them. This is part of a wider replication crisis in psychology, in which a number of well-known results did not hold up.

The more balanced view is that customers are sensible but busy. They use shortcuts because thinking hard about every purchase would be a poor use of their time. Over repeated purchases they learn, and they punish companies that cheat them, through reviews, complaints and leaving. Tricks work best on one-off purchases, on confusing products and on people under pressure. Over many repeated purchases, companies that are simply good tend to do better than companies that are merely clever.

Chapter 15

When the customer is partly a machine

Much of customer behaviour research is about human limits: small memories, busy minds, a weakness for defaults and stories. A software agent does not get tired at the checkout or feel loss aversion. If people hand routine purchases to such agents, sellers will have to persuade the agent, which probably means clearer prices, better data and fewer tricks.

That would not end the field. People will still choose which assistant to trust, still buy the things that carry meaning for them themselves, and still remember experiences through their peaks and endings. Delegation may move persuasion, not remove it. The question will be whose interest the agent serves: the buyer's, or the company that built it. For the wider question of how people live with these tools, see Psychology of technology.

The core idea

Customers are guided mostly by habit, by quick shortcuts such as social proof and loss aversion, by the job they need done, and by the context of the choice. What they do is a better guide than what they say. The same knowledge can serve them or exploit them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Roughly four in ten everyday actions are habits, in diary studies by Wendy Wood.
  • In the 2000 jam study, 6 options led to more purchases than 24, but later reviews found the average effect is close to zero.
  • Experiences are judged mainly by their peak and their end, not their length.
The common mistake

Building a product from what customers say in a survey. People describe their problems well and predict their own choices badly. Watch and test before you believe.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the morning milkshake's real competitor was a banana? If you can, you understand jobs to be done.
