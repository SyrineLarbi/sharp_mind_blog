Competitive advantage. Why a few companies keep earning high profits while competition wears everyone else down.

Basic economics makes a gloomy prediction. If a business earns unusually high profits, competitors notice, copy it and cut prices, until the extra profit disappears. Most of the time, that prediction comes true. Yet some companies stay unusually profitable for decades. The reason is almost always a competitive advantage: something about the business that rivals cannot easily copy. This file explains what those advantages are, where they come from, why they are worth so much money, and why even the strongest ones eventually weaken.

Question 1 · What is it?

A castle, and the moat around it

A competitive advantage is anything that lets a company earn more than its rivals for a long time. The important words are "for a long time".

Chapter 01

The investor's metaphor

The investor Warren Buffett popularised a simple image. Think of a good business as a castle full of treasure. Its profits attract attackers: competitors who want a share. What protects the castle is a moat, a barrier that makes attacking it expensive or hopeless. Buffett's advice was to look for businesses with wide moats, and to watch whether the moat is getting wider or narrower each year.

The image is useful because it moves attention away from the castle itself. A wonderful product is a castle. If anyone can build the same product next year, it has no moat. Many famous products had no moat at all. The first pocket calculators, the first portable music players and the first fitness trackers were all exciting, and all were copied quickly until prices collapsed. Being good is not the same as being hard to copy.

Chapter 02

How to see an advantage in the numbers

An advantage leaves a fingerprint in a company's accounts. To understand it you need two ideas.

The first is return on invested capital, usually shortened to ROIC. It asks: for every 100 euros the company has put into its business, in factories, equipment, stock, software and so on, how much profit does it make each year? If a company has invested 1,000 million and earns 150 million a year, its return is 15 percent.

The second is the cost of capital: the return investors could reasonably expect elsewhere for taking a similar risk. For a typical large company it is often somewhere around 7 to 10 percent, though it varies with interest rates and risk. It is explained properly in Corporate finance.

Competition pushes returns down towards the cost of capital. At that level, a business is worth only what was put into it. A competitive advantage is whatever keeps returns above the cost of capital for many years, despite rivals trying to take that extra profit away.

Chapter 03

Two basic ways to be better

In his 1985 book Competitive Advantage, Michael Porter of Harvard argued that there are only two basic ways to beat rivals. The first is cost leadership: producing the same kind of thing more cheaply than anyone else, so you can match their prices and still earn more, or undercut them. The second is differentiation: offering something customers value enough to pay more for, at a cost that does not rise as much as the price.

Cost leadership

The Swedish furniture company IKEA designs furniture that packs flat, so it is cheap to ship and store, and customers do the assembly. Customers accept the effort in exchange for low prices. The whole business is built around keeping costs lower than anyone else's.

Differentiation

The French luxury house Hermès sells handbags for which customers wait, and pays craftspeople to make them by hand. Its costs are high, but its prices are far higher. What customers pay for is the rarity, the craft and the name.

Porter added a third option, focus: applying either approach to a narrow market, such as a regional airline or a specialist software firm. He warned against being "stuck in the middle": not the cheapest, not the most special, and so with no reason for customers to choose you. Later writers have argued that some companies do manage both low cost and differentiation, so the rule is a strong tendency rather than a law.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The forces outside, and the moats you can build

Some industries are naturally profitable and some are naturally brutal. Within any industry, a small number of mechanisms let one company protect its position. There are fewer of them than you might think.

Chapter 04

Why some industries are easy and others brutal

Before asking why a company is profitable, ask why its industry is. In a 1979 article Porter set out the five forces that decide how much profit an industry can keep.

ForceThe question it asksWhen it squeezes profits
RivalryHow fiercely do existing firms compete?Many similar rivals, slow growth, high fixed costs
Threat of new entrantsHow easy is it for a newcomer to join?Low start-up costs, no need for scale or licences
Threat of substitutesCan customers meet the same need another way?A video call replacing a business flight
Power of buyersCan customers force prices down?A few huge buyers, such as supermarkets buying from farmers
Power of suppliersCan suppliers force your costs up?One essential supplier with no alternative

Airlines show all five forces at their worst. Rivalry is intense, planes are expensive, fuel suppliers set prices, customers compare fares in seconds online, and governments often keep failing airlines alive. Buffett once joked that a far-sighted capitalist at Kitty Hawk, where the Wright brothers first flew, would have done investors a favour by shooting down the first plane. By contrast, a company with a patented medicine faces almost no rivalry until the patent expires. The same management skill earns very different profits in different industries.

Chapter 05

The short list of real moats

Investors and strategists have tried many times to list the sources of lasting advantage. The lists keep arriving at roughly the same five. The investment research firm Morningstar, for example, uses close to this list when it rates companies.

MoatHow it worksExample
Network effectsThe product gets more valuable as more people use itCard payment networks, online marketplaces
Switching costsLeaving is expensive, slow or risky for the customerBusiness accounting software, a company's core banking system
Cost advantages from scaleBeing bigger makes each unit cheaperWarehouse retailers, chip factories
Intangible assetsBrands, patents, licences and permits that others cannot getCoca-Cola's brand, a drug patent, an airport landing slot
Efficient scaleA market only big enough for one or two firms, so a newcomer would ruin everyoneA regional gas pipeline, a local quarry

Notice what is missing: good management, great products, hard work and being first. They all matter, but on their own none of them stops a determined rival. A moat is a structural feature that would protect the business even if a competitor were just as clever.

Chapter 06

Network effects: the moat that builds itself

The first telephone was useless, because there was nobody to call. Each new telephone made every existing one more valuable. That is a network effect, and it is the most powerful moat in modern business.

Payment cards show a two-sided version. Shops accept Visa and Mastercard because most shoppers carry them. Shoppers carry them because almost every shop accepts them. A new card network faces a chicken-and-egg problem: no shops without cardholders, no cardholders without shops. Marketplaces for rides, holiday rentals or second-hand goods work the same way. Buyers go where the sellers are, and sellers go where the buyers are.

Network effects tend to produce "winner takes most" markets, because the biggest network keeps getting more attractive. But they can also run in reverse. MySpace was the largest social network in the mid-2000s, and News Corporation bought it in 2005 for about 580 million dollars. When users began moving to Facebook, each departure made MySpace less useful for those who stayed. It was sold in 2011 for a small fraction of that price. A network effect is a moat as long as people are arriving, and an accelerating drain once they start leaving.

Chapter 07

Switching costs and scale

Switching costs are everything a customer loses by leaving: money, time, data, training and risk. Changing your phone's case costs nothing. Changing the software that runs a hospital's patient records takes years, costs a fortune and risks lives if it goes wrong. Suppliers of such systems can raise prices steadily, because customers know the pain of moving is greater than the pain of paying. Banks benefit in a smaller way: many people keep a poor current account for years because moving their salary and bills feels like effort.

Economies of scale mean average cost falls as volume rises, usually because a large fixed cost is spread over more units. Building a modern chip factory costs well over ten billion dollars. Only a company selling enormous numbers of chips can spread that cost thinly enough. Warehouse retailers such as Costco use scale differently. Huge volumes on a small range of products let them negotiate low prices, keep their own markup low, and earn much of their profit from membership fees. A smaller rival trying the same thing would lose money on every sale.

Scale has a limit. Above a certain size, extra volume saves little and complexity adds cost. And scale only protects you if your rivals cannot reach the same size. In a growing global market, they often can.

Chapter 08

Abilities that are visible but not copyable

In 1991 the management scholar Jay Barney set out a test now known by the letters VRIO. A resource or ability gives lasting advantage only if it is Valuable, Rare, hard to Imitate, and if the company is Organised to use it. (Barney's original version used slightly different letters, but the idea is the same.)

The most interesting resources are ones that rivals can see clearly and still cannot copy. In the 1980s Toyota's production system was famous, and Toyota allowed competitors to visit its factories. In 1984 it even ran a joint factory in California with General Motors, called NUMMI, where GM managers could learn the methods first-hand. The California plant became one of GM's best. Yet GM took a long time to spread the same results across its other factories. The system was not a set of tools that could be photographed. It was thousands of habits, relationships and shared assumptions, built over decades.

This is sometimes called process power. Its modern form can be seen in the Taiwanese company TSMC, which makes most of the world's most advanced chips for other companies. Rivals with large budgets have struggled to match its manufacturing yields at the leading edge. The know-how lives in its people and processes, not in any single patent.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Moats decide what companies are worth, and sometimes what we pay

For investors, competitive advantage is the main thing they are paying for. For customers and governments, an advantage that becomes too strong turns into a problem.

Chapter 09

Why investors pay for the future, not the present

A company's value is roughly the value today of all the cash it will produce in the future. (How that is calculated is in Company valuation.) Two companies with exactly the same profit this year can therefore be worth very different amounts. If one has a moat that will protect its profits for twenty years, and the other will be copied in three, the first is worth many times more.

This is why stock markets value some companies at thirty or forty times their annual profit, and others at eight or ten. Much of the difference is the market's guess about how long each company's advantage will last. The width of the moat is, in effect, the length of the future that investors are willing to pay for. When a moat shows signs of narrowing, the share price can fall sharply even if this year's profits are still high.

Chapter 10

The ultimate cornered resource

Sometimes a single company controls something the whole economy depends on. The Dutch firm ASML, described in Volume 12, is the only company in the world that makes the extreme ultraviolet lithography machines needed to print the most advanced chips. Each machine is the size of a bus and costs well over a hundred million euros. ASML spent decades and billions developing the technology with partners, and its suppliers are themselves unique.

Advantages like this matter far beyond the company. They become questions of national security. Since 2019 the Dutch government, under pressure from the United States, has restricted ASML's sales of its most advanced machines to China. When a competitive advantage becomes a chokepoint, it stops being only a business matter and becomes a geopolitical one.

Chapter 11

When an advantage becomes a monopoly

A moat protects a company from competitors. Seen from the customer's side, it can also protect the company from having to treat customers well. If there is nowhere else to go, prices can rise and quality can slip. That is why most countries have competition law, called antitrust law in the United States. It does not usually punish a company for being big or successful. It punishes the abuse of a dominant position, or deals that block competition (see Competition law).

The line is hard to draw. In 2024 a US federal judge ruled that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in internet search. A central issue was the large payments Google made to Apple and others to be the default search engine on their devices, reported at about 20 billion dollars in 2022 alone. Google argued that people simply preferred its search engine. The court found that the default deals helped shut rivals out. The European Union took a different route in 2022 with its Digital Markets Act, which sets rules in advance for the largest platforms rather than waiting for case-by-case lawsuits. Whether these approaches will make digital markets more competitive is still being worked out.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every moat leaks eventually

Each kind of advantage has a typical way of failing. Knowing it tells you what to watch for.

Chapter 12

Strengths and weak points of each moat

MoatWhy it is strongHow it typically fails
Network effectsSelf-reinforcing; the leader pulls further aheadA new platform pulls users away and the effect runs backwards. Regulators may force openness
Switching costsSteady, predictable revenue; prices can riseCustomers resent being trapped. A new technology can make switching easy
ScaleHard to beat on priceRivals reach the same size, or a new method makes the old scale irrelevant
BrandCustomers pay more and stay loyalA scandal or a change in taste. Brands built on status are fragile
Patents and licencesLegal protection, sometimes totalThey expire. Drug prices often fall steeply once generic copies arrive
Process know-howInvisible and slow to copyKey people leave. Or the whole process becomes obsolete

There is also a cost to the company itself. A strong moat can make managers comfortable. They raise prices rather than improve the product, and stop listening to customers who have nowhere else to go. The safest-looking advantage is often the one that produces the laziest company, which is exactly the opening that disruption, the subject of topic 20, needs.

Chapter 13

Why most advantages fade

Studies of company returns over long periods find a steady pull towards the average. High-return companies tend to drift down, and low-return companies tend to drift up, over a decade or so. Only a minority keep high returns for very long. Some of this is the statistical effect Volume 39 calls regression to the mean (Why extremes do not last). Most of it is competition doing its work.

The management writer Rita McGrath argued in her 2013 book The End of Competitive Advantage that in fast-moving industries, companies should stop trying to build one permanent advantage. Instead they should expect a series of transient advantages, exploit each while it lasts, and be ready to move on. Others reply that the companies that last longest still tend to have one deep structural moat. Both views fit some industries better than others. A pipeline operator and a mobile game studio live in very different worlds.

Chapter 15

If anyone can build it, what is left to protect?

AI tools make it cheaper to write software, design products and produce content. That sounds like good news for every business, and in one way it is. But anything that becomes cheap for you also becomes cheap for your rivals. When a skill becomes easy, it stops being an advantage. A software company whose only edge was that its product was hard to build may find that edge shrinking.

The likely result is a return to the old, structural moats. Network effects, switching costs, trusted brands, licences and real-world scale are hard to produce with a clever algorithm. The general lesson of this file holds: ask not "is this business good?" but "what would stop a well-funded rival from doing the same thing next year?" If the answer is nothing, the profits will not last, however impressive they look today.

The core idea

A competitive advantage is whatever keeps a company's return on capital above its cost of capital for years, despite rivals. Industry structure (the five forces) sets the background. The real moats are few: network effects, switching costs, scale, intangible assets and efficient scale.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Two basic routes: lower cost or differentiation (Porter, 1985).
  • Five forces shape industry profit (Porter, 1979).
  • MySpace: bought for about 580 million dollars in 2005, sold for a small fraction in 2011.
The common mistake

Confusing a great product with a moat. Good is not the same as hard to copy. Without a barrier, competitors arrive and profits fall back to normal.

Test yourself

Pick a company you use every day. Which of the five moats does it have, if any? And which specific event would make that moat fail?
