Product-market fit. The moment customers start pulling instead of you pushing.

Before a new product finds its market, everything is hard. Every sale needs persuasion, every user drifts away, and the team argues about features. After it finds its market, the problems change completely. Customers arrive by themselves, tell their friends, complain loudly when the service goes down, and the team struggles to keep up. Founders and investors call the line between those two worlds product-market fit. It is the single most important milestone in the life of a new business, and one of the most misunderstood.

Question 1 · What is it?

A good market, and a product that satisfies it

The idea is simple to state and hard to see from the inside. Most teams believe they have it long before they do.

Chapter 01

"The only thing that matters"

The phrase is usually credited to Andy Rachleff, a co-founder of the venture firm Benchmark. It became famous through a 2007 essay by Marc Andreessen, the co-founder of Netscape, titled "The Only Thing That Matters". His definition was short: product-market fit means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market.

Both halves count. A good market has many potential customers with a real, pressing problem and the ability to pay. A product that satisfies it solves that problem well enough that people choose it, keep using it, and would be upset to lose it. A brilliant product in a tiny market is stuck. A large market with a mediocre product is wide open to the next competitor.

Chapter 02

What it feels like: push versus pull

Andreessen described the difference vividly. Without fit, customers are not quite getting value, word of mouth is not spreading, usage is not growing fast, and sales cycles drag on. With fit, customers buy the product as fast as you can make it, usage grows as fast as you can add servers, and money from customers piles up in the bank.

Before fit: pushing

Every customer needs convincing. Free trials end and people quietly leave. Marketing spending brings users who do not stay. The team keeps adding features in the hope that one will finally make it work.

After fit: being pulled

Customers find you through others. They keep using the product without reminders. Support is overwhelmed with requests, not complaints about value. The bottleneck becomes capacity: hiring, servers, stock.

If you have to ask whether you have product-market fit, you almost certainly do not. That is a common saying among founders, and it is broadly true, because real fit is hard to miss. The danger is the grey zone in between, where some customers love the product and most are lukewarm.

Chapter 03

Why the market matters most

In the same essay Andreessen argued that of the three things a startup needs, a team, a product and a market, the market matters most, and he quoted Rachleff's version: when a great team meets a lousy market, the market wins. In a great market, one where people badly need something, they will tolerate a rough early product, and the market pulls a solution out of whoever offers one. In a terrible market, even an excellent team with a good product struggles, because not enough people care.

This explains a pattern that otherwise looks unfair. Weak early versions of successful products are often clumsy: slow, ugly, limited. They succeeded because they met an urgent need that nobody else was meeting. Meanwhile polished products in markets with no urgent need fade away. The polish was never the problem.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Searching, testing and measuring

Nobody plans their way to product-market fit. Teams find it through a loop of learning about customers, building small, measuring what people actually do, and changing course.

Chapter 04

Start with a narrow customer and an urgent problem

The most common early mistake is aiming at everyone. "Small businesses" is not a customer. "Independent dental practices in Germany that still book appointments by phone" is. A narrow group has a shared, specific problem that you can understand deeply and solve fully, and its members talk to each other, so word spreads.

The problem also has to be urgent. Founders sometimes describe the ideal as a problem where the customer's "hair is on fire": they are already trying to solve it, perhaps with an awkward mix of spreadsheets, emails and staff time, and they would pay to stop. A problem people are already spending money or effort on is far better evidence than a problem you think they should care about. Once you dominate a narrow group, you can widen out from there.

Chapter 05

Interviewing customers without fooling yourself

People are polite. Show them your idea and most will say it sounds great, which tells you almost nothing. In his 2013 book The Mom Test, Rob Fitzpatrick argued that good customer questions should be ones that even your mother, who wants to encourage you, could not answer with a kind lie.

Weak questionWhy it misleadsBetter question
"Would you use an app that does this?"Asks about an imagined future; people are optimistic and kind"How do you handle this today?"
"How much would you pay for it?"Hypothetical prices are unreliable"What have you spent trying to fix it?"
"Do you like this idea?"Invites a compliment"When did this last cause you trouble? What happened?"

The principle is to ask about past behaviour, not future intentions. What people have actually done, spent and suffered is solid ground. What they say they will do is mostly noise.

Chapter 06

The minimum viable product

Eric Ries, in The Lean Startup (2011), popularised the minimum viable product (MVP): the smallest thing you can put in front of real customers to test your key assumption. It is not a cheap version of the final product. It is an experiment.

An MVP can be surprisingly basic. A landing page that describes the product and counts how many people try to sign up. A service run by hand behind a simple website, sometimes called a "concierge" or "Wizard of Oz" test, where customers think it is automated. A rough prototype given to ten users. Ries framed the process as a loop: build, measure, learn, as quickly as possible. The speed of the loop matters more than the quality of any single round.

Chapter 07

The 40 percent test

Because fit is a feeling, people have tried to measure it. The best-known survey comes from Sean Ellis, a growth marketer who worked with early Dropbox and others. Ask your active users one question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" The answers are "very disappointed", "somewhat disappointed" and "not disappointed".

Ellis observed that products which went on to grow strongly usually had at least 40 percent of users answering "very disappointed". Below that, growth tended to be a struggle. The threshold is a rule of thumb from experience, not a law of nature. Its real value is in segmenting: find out which users say "very disappointed", what they have in common, and what they value most. Then build for them, not for the lukewarm majority.

Chapter 08

Retention curves: the hardest number to fake

The most trusted evidence is retention: of the people who started using the product in a given week or month, called a cohort, what share are still using it later? Plot it over time and you get a retention curve.

Every product loses many users at first. What matters is the shape. If the curve flattens, a group of people has found lasting value, and growth adds to that base. If it keeps sliding towards zero, every new user eventually leaves, and marketing is filling a leaking bucket. A healthy flat level differs a lot by product: a daily messaging app and an annual tax tool should not be judged by the same line.

Chapter 09

The pivot: changing direction without starting over

When evidence shows the current path is not working, teams pivot: they keep what they have learned and some of what they have built, and change one major element, such as the customer, the problem or the product.

Some famous companies began as something else. Slack started as an internal messaging tool used by the team at Tiny Speck while they built an online game called Glitch. The game failed; the tool became Slack. Instagram began as Burbn, a check-in app with many features. Its founders noticed that users mainly shared photos, cut almost everything else, and relaunched in 2010. A good pivot follows what customers are actually doing, not what the team hoped they would do. A bad pivot is just a change of direction after every disappointment, with nothing learned.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The line between life and death for a new product

Most new products fail not because they were built badly, but because they were built for a need that was too small, too weak or not there at all.

Chapter 10

"No market need": the most common way to die

The research firm CB Insights has collected post-mortems written by founders of failed startups. Across its analyses, lack of market need, building something not enough people wanted, has consistently ranked among the top reasons given, alongside running out of money. The two are often the same story: the money ran out because customers never came.

Founder post-mortems are not a scientific sample, and people may prefer some explanations to others. But the pattern matches what investors and accelerators report. Teams rarely fail because they could not build the product. They fail because the product they built did not matter enough to anyone.

Chapter 11

Premature scaling: pouring fuel before the engine starts

The opposite mistake is spending as if you had fit before you do: hiring a large sales team, buying advertising, expanding to new countries. This is called premature scaling, and investors widely regard it as one of the main killers of funded startups. Money spent acquiring users who do not stay is simply lost.

Quibi is a vivid example. It launched in April 2020 with short-form premium video for phones, raised about 1.75 billion dollars from major investors, and had well-known Hollywood names behind it. It shut down around six months later. Huge spending had been committed before there was evidence that people wanted that format enough to pay. Money can speed up a product people want. It cannot create the wanting.

Chapter 12

Fit can be lost, and it is never universal

Product-market fit is not a trophy you win once. Markets move. Chegg, an American homework-help company with millions of student subscribers, saw its share price plunge in May 2023 after it said that interest in ChatGPT was hurting its growth. A product that fitted one world fitted the next one much less well.

Fit is also specific. A product can fit one customer group and not another, or one country and not the next. Large companies face this every time they launch a new product. Each new market, segment or product line needs its own fit, which is why established firms with huge budgets still fail with new launches. The wider pattern is the subject of Business disruption.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful idea with soft edges

Product-market fit focuses a team brilliantly. It is also vague, easy to misread, and sometimes used as an excuse.

Chapter 13

What the concept gets right, and where it falls short

AspectStrengthWeakness
FocusTells a young team what matters now and what to ignore.Can make teams neglect costs, ethics or law in the rush to grow.
EvidencePushes decisions toward real behaviour: retention, payment, referrals.No single agreed measure; thresholds such as 40 percent are rules of thumb.
Customer-centredForces founders to listen to users rather than their own vision.Users describe today's problems, and may not imagine radically new solutions.
SimplicityEasy to explain to investors, staff and partners.Treats fit as a switch, while in reality it is a matter of degree.
IterationPivots let teams recover from wrong guesses cheaply.Constant pivoting can replace a clear strategy with drift.

One more caution. Some products create needs people did not know they had. Customers asked in 2006 could not easily have described what a modern smartphone would mean to them. Listening to customers is essential, but it means studying their problems, not asking them to design the solution. That distinction is at the heart of Customer behavior.

Four directions, three levels of confidence

DirectionWhere it standsHonest outlook

The deeper point survives every new tool. Product-market fit is a fact about people, not about technology. It is found in the gap between what customers are struggling with and what nobody yet offers, and the only reliable way to see that gap is to watch real behaviour closely. Once the gap is found, the next challenge is telling the rest of the market, which is where Marketing begins.

The core idea

Product-market fit means a good market with a product that satisfies it. Teams find it by targeting a narrow customer with an urgent problem, testing with small experiments, and measuring behaviour, especially retention, rather than opinions. Without fit, spending on growth is wasted.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The Sean Ellis benchmark: at least 40 percent of users "very disappointed" to lose the product.
  • Andreessen's essay "The Only Thing That Matters" appeared in 2007.
  • Quibi raised about 1.75 billion dollars and closed after about six months in 2020.
The common mistake

Treating enthusiasm as evidence. Compliments, sign-ups and downloads are cheap. A flat retention curve and paying customers are not.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a retention curve that flattens matters more than fast sign-up growth? If you can, you understand the difference between pushing and being pulled.
