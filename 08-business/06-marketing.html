Marketing. Deciding whom to serve, what to offer, and how they will hear about it.

Most people think marketing means advertising: the billboard, the video before the video, the email you did not ask for. Advertising is part of it, and the most visible part. But marketing in the full sense starts much earlier. It decides which customers a company is for, what exactly it will offer them, at what price, through which shops or screens, and only then what it will say. Done well, it is the discipline of seeing a business from the customer's side. Done badly, it is noise that people pay to avoid.

Question 1 · What is it?

The customer's side of the business

Marketing is the work of understanding what customers need and arranging the business so that the right people want what it offers, can find it and can buy it.

Chapter 01

Making selling unnecessary

Peter Drucker, perhaps the most influential management writer of the twentieth century, gave marketing its most ambitious definition. He argued that there will always be some need for selling, but that marketing's real purpose is to understand the customer so well that the product fits them and sells itself.

"The aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous."

Peter Drucker · Management: Tasks, Responsibilities, Practices (1973)

The point is the order of thinking. A sales-led company makes a product and then looks for people to sell it to. A marketing-led company starts with a group of customers and their problems, and designs the product, price and distribution around them. Advertising is only the last and loudest step of marketing, not its definition.

Chapter 02

What business are you really in?

In 1960 the Harvard professor Theodore Levitt published a famous article, "Marketing Myopia". Myopia means short-sightedness. His central example was the American railways. They declined, he argued, not because people stopped needing to travel and ship goods, but because the railways thought they were in the railway business rather than the transport business. They let cars, lorries and planes take their customers.

Levitt's lesson is that customers buy a result, not a product. Nobody wants a drill for its own sake; they want a hole in the wall, or really a shelf that holds their books. A company that defines itself by its product gets blindsided when a different product delivers the same result more easily. The idea is one of the roots of Business disruption.

Chapter 03

The marketing mix: the four Ps

In 1960 the American marketing professor E. Jerome McCarthy grouped the decisions a marketer controls into four headings, each starting with P. The framework has been criticised and extended, but it remains the standard first map of the subject.

PThe decisionExample: a new oat-milk brand
ProductWhat exactly is offered: features, quality, packaging, serviceBarista version that foams well for coffee
PriceWhat it costs, discounts, payment termsSlightly above dairy milk, below premium rivals
PlaceWhere and how it is sold and deliveredStart in independent cafés, then supermarkets
PromotionHow people hear about it: advertising, PR, social media, offersBaristas recommending it; social posts of latte art

Service businesses often add three more Ps: people, process and physical evidence (what customers see, such as a clean waiting room). The key lesson is that the Ps must agree with each other. A luxury price sold through a discount shop confuses everyone. Pricing gets its own file in Pricing.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Choosing a target, reaching it, and finding out what worked

Practical marketing is a sequence: decide whom you are for, decide what you stand for in their minds, reach them through the right channels, and measure honestly what changed.

Chapter 04

Segmentation, targeting, positioning

No product suits everyone, so marketers split the market into segments: groups of customers with similar needs or behaviour. Segments can be based on age or income, on location, on attitudes, or most usefully on behaviour, such as how often people buy and why.

Then comes targeting: choosing which segments to serve. Finally, positioning: deciding what the product should stand for in the target customer's mind compared with alternatives. Volvo for many years positioned itself on safety. Low-cost airlines position on price. A positioning is a deliberate sacrifice. To stand for something clearly, a brand has to give up standing for other things.

A simple positioning sentence

For [target customer] who [need], our product is the [category] that [key benefit], unlike [main alternative]. If a team cannot fill this in, it usually has not made the choices that marketing depends on.

Chapter 05

The funnel: from not knowing to buying again

One of the oldest models in marketing is often attributed to the American advertising pioneer E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1900: AIDA, for attention, interest, desire and action. Its modern descendant is the marketing funnel, wide at the top where many people become aware of a product, narrowing as fewer consider it, fewer still buy, and fewer again become loyal.

The marketing funnel
  \  awareness      many people have heard of it      /
   \  consideration    some would think of it        /
    \  purchase           a few buy it              /
     \  loyalty / repeat     fewer come back       /
      \  advocacy              some tell others   /
       \_________________________________________/
        each step loses people; marketing works on every step

The funnel is a simplification. Real customers jump around: they hear about something from a friend, search, read reviews, forget, and come back months later. But it shows why different activities do different jobs. A television advert builds awareness. A search advert catches someone already looking. A good returns policy turns a buyer into a repeat customer.

Chapter 06

Channels: paid, owned and earned

A channel is a route by which a message reaches people. Marketers often sort them into three groups.

Paid media

Attention you buy: television, posters, search ads, social media ads, sponsorships. Fast and controllable, but it stops when the spending stops.

Owned media

Channels you control: your website, app, email list, shop windows, packaging. Cheap to use once built, but you must first attract an audience.

Earned media

Attention others give you: press coverage, reviews, word of mouth, shares. The most trusted, and the hardest to control or buy.

Most effective marketing combines all three. Paid media brings people to owned channels, where a good experience produces earned media. Word of mouth remains one of the most powerful channels of all, because people trust friends more than advertisers, which is the social proof lever from Volume 36.

Chapter 07

How digital advertising works

Digital advertising changed marketing more than anything since television. Two forms dominate. Search advertising shows ads to people based on what they type into a search engine. Someone searching "emergency plumber" is already close to buying, so the ad is very valuable. Advertisers bid in automated auctions for each search, and the platform weighs the bid alongside how relevant and useful the ad seems.

Social and display advertising shows ads based on what platforms know or infer about you: interests, location, behaviour, and similarity to existing customers. It is good at creating demand among people who were not searching. Both forms allow constant experiments, called A/B tests, in which two versions of an ad or page are shown to random groups to see which performs better. The promise of digital marketing was that everything could be measured. As Chapter 09 shows, the reality is more complicated.

Chapter 08

Building the brand versus driving sales now

In 2013 the British researchers Les Binet and Peter Field published The Long and the Short of It, an analysis of hundreds of advertising campaigns submitted to the UK's Institute of Practitioners in Advertising. They distinguished two kinds of marketing. Activation drives sales now: offers, search ads, "buy today" messages. Its effects are quick and short-lived. Brand building creates memories and feelings that make people more likely to choose you in the future. Its effects are slow but long-lasting.

Their data suggested that companies which put all their effort into short-term activation grew less over time than companies balancing the two. They proposed, as a rough guide, around 60 percent on brand and 40 percent on activation, varying by sector. Short-term measures make activation look better than it is, because brand effects appear later, outside the measurement window. Branding is covered in its own file, Branding.

Chapter 09

Measurement: did the marketing cause the sale?

An old line, usually attributed to the American retailer John Wanamaker though the attribution is doubtful, says that half of all advertising money is wasted, and the trouble is nobody knows which half. Digital tools seemed to solve this, because you can track who clicked and who bought. But tracking shows correlation, not causation. Many people who click a search ad for a brand would have bought it anyway.

The honest question is incrementality: how many extra sales happened because of the marketing that would not have happened without it? The cleanest answer comes from experiments: show the ads to a random group and not to a similar control group, then compare. When large advertisers have run such tests, some channels have turned out to produce far fewer extra sales than their tracking reports had suggested. Other methods, such as statistical "marketing mix models" that relate sales to spending over time, have become popular again as tracking has become harder.

The Australian marketing scientist Byron Sharp, in How Brands Grow (2010), argued from large-scale purchase data that brands mostly grow by reaching more buyers, especially light and occasional ones, rather than by deepening loyalty among existing fans. His work is influential and also debated, but it pushed many marketers toward broad reach and being easy to remember and find, rather than narrow targeting alone.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A huge industry that shapes what we know, want and buy

Marketing decides which products succeed, funds much of the media and internet, and influences habits and culture far beyond any single purchase.

Chapter 10

The money that funds the internet

Industry estimates put global advertising spending at around a trillion dollars a year in the mid-2020s, with digital channels now the large majority of it. Much of that money flows to a handful of companies, above all Alphabet (Google and YouTube), Meta (Facebook and Instagram) and Amazon, whose advertising on its own shopping pages has grown into a business worth tens of billions of dollars a year.

That money pays for services billions of people use for free, as explained in Business models. It also funds a large share of journalism, sport and entertainment. When advertising shifts, entire media industries rise or fall with it, as newspapers discovered when classified ads moved online.

Chapter 11

Marketing changes habits, for good and ill

Marketing does not only fight for share within existing demand. It can change what people consider normal. In 1929 the publicist Edward Bernays, working for the American Tobacco Company, arranged for young women to smoke publicly during the New York Easter parade, presenting cigarettes as "torches of freedom". It was a deliberate attempt to break the taboo against women smoking in public.

The same power can serve public goals. Campaigns for seat belts, against drink driving, and for hand-washing and vaccination use marketing methods, and the field is called social marketing. Recognising marketing's influence, many countries restrict advertising for tobacco, and increasingly for alcohol, gambling and unhealthy food aimed at children; the rules vary widely. The tools are neutral. The goal and the honesty of the message are not.

Chapter 12

Marketing for a small business

For a café, a dentist or a local builder, the big theories boil down to a few practical points. Be easy to find, especially in local online search and maps, where many buyers now start. Collect and answer reviews, because they are earned media that strangers trust. Keep in touch with past customers, who are cheaper to keep than new ones are to win. And be clear about who you are for.

The most common small-business mistake is trying to appeal to everyone, which in practice appeals to no one in particular. A shop known for one thing, done well, travels further by word of mouth than a shop that is fine at everything.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Information and choice, at the price of noise and manipulation

Marketing connects people with things that help them. It can also exploit their weaknesses, invade their privacy and flood their attention.

Chapter 14

Where persuasion becomes manipulation

The line is not always sharp, but some practices are widely seen as crossing it. Dark patterns are design tricks that push people into choices they would not otherwise make: a pre-ticked box adding insurance, a cancellation process hidden behind many screens, a fake countdown timer, a "only 1 left" warning that is not true. Regulators in the EU, the UK and the US have increasingly treated such practices as unfair or deceptive.

Privacy is the other front. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation, in force since 2018, requires a legal basis such as consent for much personal-data processing, which is why websites in Europe ask about cookies. Apple's 2021 change requiring iPhone apps to ask permission before tracking users across other apps sharply reduced tracking, and some advertisers reported that their ads became less precise. Every gain in targeting has come with a cost in privacy, and the balance is now set as much by regulators and platforms as by marketers.

Four directions, three levels of confidence

DirectionWhere it standsHonest outlook

AI agents shopping for peopleCompanies are testing assistants that can compare products and complete purchases on a user's behalf.Speculative: if machines choose, marketing would have to persuade software, not people, and that would change the field deeply.

Two older truths are likely to survive all of this. People still buy from brands they remember and trust, and they still tell each other what is good. Google's long effort to phase out third-party tracking cookies in its Chrome browser, which it abandoned in 2024 and 2025, shows how hard it is to predict exactly how the technology of advertising will change. The tools of marketing change every few years. Its basic questions, who is this for and why would they choose it, do not.

The core idea

Marketing is seeing the business from the customer's side: choosing whom to serve (segmentation and targeting), what to stand for (positioning), and setting product, price, place and promotion to fit. Advertising is only the last step. Good marketing balances long-term brand building with short-term activation and measures what it actually caused.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Global advertising spending is estimated at around a trillion dollars a year (mid-2020s).
  • Binet and Field's rough guide: about 60:40 brand building to activation.
  • The four Ps date from McCarthy in 1960, the same year as Levitt's "Marketing Myopia".
The common mistake

Equating marketing with advertising, and clicks with results. A tracked sale is not necessarily a caused sale; many buyers would have bought anyway.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a company that cuts brand advertising might see no drop in sales for months, and then a slow decline? If you can, you understand the long and the short of it.
