Sharp Mind · Business · Topic 10 of 20

Pricing. The most powerful number in a business, and usually the least examined.

Companies spend years designing a product and an afternoon deciding what to charge for it. That is strange, because no other decision moves profit so quickly. A price is not just a cost plus a margin. It is a guess about what buyers will pay, a signal of quality, a way of sorting customers, and a test of what people consider fair. Get it slightly wrong and a good product loses money. Get it badly wrong and customers remember for years.

Question 1 · What is it?

The amount a company asks, and the reasoning behind it

Pricing is the set of decisions about what to charge, how to structure the charge, and when to change it. It is where a company captures the value its product creates.

Chapter 01

Why one percent matters so much

Take a simple company. It sells a product for 100. Each unit costs 60 to make and deliver. Its other costs, rent and salaries, come to 30 per unit at its current volume. That leaves a profit of 10 per unit.

Three ways to improve by one percent
 starting point     price 100   variable cost 60   fixed cost 30   profit 10

 raise price 1%     price 101   cost 60  fixed 30           profit 11.0   (+10%)
 sell 1% more       each extra unit adds 100 - 60 = 40      profit 10.4   (+4%)
 cut fixed cost 1%  fixed 29.7                              profit 10.3   (+3%)

 a 1% price rise, if volume holds, does more than the others combined

The reason is that the whole price increase falls straight to profit, while extra volume brings extra costs with it. A much-cited 1992 analysis by consultants at McKinsey, covering a large sample of companies, put the effect of a 1% price rise at roughly an 11% increase in operating profit for a typical firm. The exact figure depends on the margin. The pattern does not. For most companies, price is the strongest lever on profit, and the catch is the words "if volume holds".

Chapter 02

Three ways to set a price

MethodHow it worksStrengthWeakness
Cost-plusAdd a fixed margin to what it costs youSimple, easy to justifyIgnores what buyers would pay. Leaves money on the table or prices you out
Competitor-basedCharge close to what rivals chargeSafe in crowded marketsAssumes rivals got it right. Invites price wars
Value-basedCharge according to what the product is worth to the buyerCaptures the most profitHard to measure value. Different buyers value it differently

Most companies start with cost-plus because the numbers are on hand. Pricing specialists push towards value-based pricing, because cost sets the floor, but value sets the ceiling. A piece of software that saves a hospital a million a year is not worth what it cost to write. It is worth some share of the million.

Chapter 03

Willingness to pay and elasticity

Every buyer has a maximum they would pay, called their willingness to pay. Above it, they walk away. Below it, they buy and feel they got a bargain. The difference between what they would have paid and what they did pay is the buyer's gain, which economists call consumer surplus.

How strongly sales react to a price change is called price elasticity. If a 10% rise loses you 2% of buyers, demand is inelastic: people need it or have no alternative, as with petrol in the short term, or a medicine. If the same rise loses you 30%, demand is elastic: there are close substitutes, as with one brand of bottled water among many. The broader mechanics are in Supply and demand. The business point is that a company's pricing power comes from having no close substitute, which is why brands, patents and switching costs matter so much (see Competitive advantage).

Question 2 · How does it work?

Different prices for different people, and the psychology underneath

The hardest problem in pricing is that buyers differ. The solutions are versions, bundles, timing and design, plus a firm understanding of how people perceive numbers.

Chapter 04

The single-price problem

Imagine a concert hall. Some fans would pay 300 for a ticket. Students would come at 30. A single price of 100 loses the students and undercharges the fans. Companies would like to charge each person their own maximum. They cannot ask, because everyone would claim to be poor. So they design ways for customers to sort themselves.

Economists call charging different prices for the same thing price discrimination. The word sounds bad, but much of it is ordinary and often helpful. Student and pensioner discounts, cheaper matinee tickets, early-bird offers and coupons all work this way. Coupons, for example, are used mainly by people who are willing to spend time to save money, who are also the most price-sensitive. People who cannot be bothered pay full price. The effort is the filter.

The most common modern form is versioning: offering good, better and best versions. Airlines sell economy, premium economy and business seats on the same plane. Software comes in basic, professional and enterprise tiers. The cost differences are often small. The price differences are large, because they follow willingness to pay, not cost.

Chapter 05

Bundles, blades and subscriptions

Bundling sells several items together for one price, such as a television package or a fast-food meal. It works because buyers value the parts differently. One person values sport highly and films a little, another the reverse. A bundle priced for their combined interest can make more than selling each part separately.

The razor-and-blades model sells the main item cheaply and makes money on what it needs afterwards: printers and ink cartridges, game consoles and games, coffee machines and capsules. It is often credited to the Gillette company, although historians debate how deliberately Gillette used it. Its modern cousin is freemium, where a basic product is free and a minority of users pay for more.

Subscriptions turn a one-off price into a regular payment. Companies love them for predictable revenue. Customers like the low monthly figure. The risk for customers, as the customer behaviour file explains, is that inertia keeps them paying for things they no longer use.

Chapter 06

Dynamic pricing: when the price moves

After the United States removed government control of airline fares in 1978, airlines faced a new problem: a plane seat is worthless once the door closes. In the 1980s, American Airlines and others built systems that changed fares constantly according to how full a flight was and how close to departure. This became known as revenue management or yield management. It is why the person beside you on a plane may have paid three times what you did.

Hotels, car hire and train companies followed. Ride-hailing apps such as Uber raise prices when demand outstrips the number of drivers, which they call surge pricing. The economic case is that higher prices bring more drivers onto the road and ration scarce rides to those who need them most. The public case against it is that it feels like exploiting people at the worst moment, such as in a storm. In 2024 the sale of tickets for the British band Oasis's reunion tour, where prices rose sharply while fans waited online, led the UK competition regulator to investigate. Dynamic pricing is efficient and widely resented at the same time.

Chapter 07

How buyers perceive numbers

People do not judge prices in isolation. They compare them with something.

Anchors

The first number seen sets the frame. A "was 200, now 120" label makes 120 feel cheap, whatever the item is worth. A very expensive item on a menu makes the second most expensive look reasonable. See Volume 35.

Decoys

Adding an option few will choose can change what people pick. In a classroom demonstration popularised by the economist Dan Ariely, a magazine offered online access, print, or print plus online, with print and print-plus-online at the same price. Most students chose the combination. Remove the print-only "decoy" and many switched to the cheaper online option.

Charm prices

A price of 9.99 instead of 10 is read from the left, so it feels closer to 9 than to 10. The effect is small but persistent, which is why it is everywhere. Luxury brands avoid it on purpose, because round numbers signal quality.

Partitioned prices

Splitting a price into a low headline figure plus fees for booking, service or delivery makes the total feel smaller. Several countries now require such fees to be shown up front.

Chapter 08

Fairness: the snow shovel question

In 1986 the economists Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler asked people a simple question. A hardware store has been selling snow shovels for 15 dollars. The morning after a large snowstorm, it raises the price to 20. Is that fair? About four out of five respondents said it was unfair. Economic theory sees nothing wrong: demand rose, so the price did. Ordinary people see a shop taking advantage of a neighbour's need.

Their research found a consistent pattern. People accept price rises that pass on higher costs, but resent rises that exploit higher demand. A price is judged not only by its size but by the reason behind it. That is why companies explain increases by pointing to costs, why a discount is easier to withdraw than a price is to raise, and why the timing of a rise can matter more than its amount.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Profit, reputation, and the line the law draws

Pricing mistakes can lose a company a quarter of its sales in a year. Pricing agreements between rivals can send executives to prison. And prices are where ordinary people feel the economy.

Chapter 09

Two famous mistakes

In 2012 the American department store J.C. Penney hired Ron Johnson, who had built Apple's retail stores. He scrapped most of the store's constant sales and coupons in favour of simple, lower, everyday prices. In theory customers paid about the same. In practice they had lost the pleasure of the bargain, the anchor of a "was" price and the reason to visit. Sales fell by about a quarter in a year, and Johnson was replaced in 2013. Customers were not buying low prices. They were buying the feeling of a good deal.

In 2011 Netflix split its DVD-by-post and streaming services into separate plans. For customers who wanted both, the effective price rose by around 60% at once. Netflix lost about 800,000 subscribers in a single quarter and its share price fell steeply. It recovered, and streaming was the right bet, but the episode showed how a large, sudden rise, badly explained, turns loyal customers into angry ones.

Chapter 10

Price wars, and the crime of agreeing

When products look identical to buyers, price becomes the only difference, and rivals can cut and cut until nobody makes money. Airlines, supermarkets and telecom companies have all lived through such price wars. The usual way out is to make the product less comparable, through service, brand, loyalty schemes or bundles.

The tempting way out is illegal. If rival companies agree among themselves on prices, they form a cartel, and customers pay more. Price-fixing is banned in the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom and most other economies. Penalties include large fines and, in some countries such as the US and the UK, prison for the individuals involved. Note the subtle boundary: companies may watch each other's prices and follow them, but they may not agree on them. That boundary is now being tested by pricing software (Chapter 13).

Chapter 11

Inflation, shrinkflation and the "greed" debate

When costs rise across the economy, companies must decide how to pass them on. One quiet method is shrinkflation: keeping the price but reducing the amount, so that a chocolate bar or a bag of crisps gets smaller. Shoppers notice prices more than weights, which is why it works, and why it causes anger when discovered. From 2024 France required large shops to put a notice next to products that had shrunk without a matching price cut.

During the inflation surge of 2021–2023, a public argument broke out over whether companies used rising costs as cover to widen their profit margins, sometimes called "greedflation". Some studies found that profits contributed noticeably to inflation in certain sectors and periods. Others argued that margins rose mainly because demand was strong and supply was disrupted, and fell back later. The question remains contested, and the answer seems to differ by country and industry. What is clear is that in an inflation, the fairness test from Chapter 08 applies to the whole economy at once.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Each pricing strategy wins something and costs something

There is no correct price, only trade-offs between profit now, volume, loyalty and trust.

One general lesson runs across the table. Pricing is easy to change technically and hard to change psychologically. A price, once seen, becomes the anchor against which every future price is judged, which is why the first price a new product carries matters so much.

Chapter 14

The end of the single price?

For most of history prices were haggled. The fixed price tag, the same for everyone, became common only in the nineteenth century with department stores, partly because large shops could not trust hundreds of clerks to bargain. Algorithmic pricing could take us halfway back: prices set for each buyer, but by a machine rather than a shopkeeper.

Whether that happens depends less on technology than on trust and law. Customers who discover they paid more than a neighbour for the same thing feel cheated, as the fairness research predicts. Laws on data protection, discrimination and competition limit what firms can do. And competitors can win customers by promising one honest price. The technology makes personal pricing possible. The fairness instinct makes it risky. The most likely outcome is personalised offers inside a visible public price, rather than secret individual prices.

The core idea

A price should sit between cost (the floor) and the buyer's value (the ceiling). Because buyers value things differently, companies use versions, bundles, timing and discounts to let customers sort themselves. Buyers judge prices by comparison and fairness, not by the number alone.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A 1% price rise, with volume unchanged, can lift operating profit by around 10% for a typical firm.
  • About four in five people judged the snow-shovel price rise unfair (1986).
  • Netflix lost about 800,000 subscribers in one quarter after its 2011 price change.
The common mistake

Setting prices from costs alone. Customers do not care what it cost you. They care what it is worth to them, and what they paid last time.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a coupon is a form of price discrimination that customers do not resent? If you can, you understand self-selection and fairness together.

The words, in plain words
Margin
The part of the price left after paying for the product's costs.
Cost-plus pricing
Setting the price as cost plus a fixed percentage.
Value-based pricing
Setting the price according to what the product is worth to the buyer.
Willingness to pay
The most a particular buyer would pay before walking away.
Consumer surplus
The gap between what a buyer would have paid and what they actually paid.
Price elasticity
How strongly sales react when the price changes.
Price discrimination
Charging different customers different prices for the same thing.
Versioning
Offering good, better and best versions at different prices.
Bundling
Selling several items together for one price.
Freemium
A free basic product, with paid extras for those who want more.
Dynamic pricing
Prices that change often with demand, time or availability.
Decoy
An option included mainly to make another option look better.
Cartel
Competitors secretly agreeing on prices or output, which is illegal in most countries.
Shrinkflation
Keeping the price while reducing the quantity in the pack.
