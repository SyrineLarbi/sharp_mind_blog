Business disruption. How great companies lose, not by doing things badly, but by doing exactly what made them great.

"Disruption" has become one of the most overused words in business. Every new app claims to be disrupting something. Yet behind the slogan is a precise and surprising idea. Many market leaders are not beaten by better products. They are beaten by worse ones: cheaper, simpler products that the leaders sensibly ignore until it is too late. The leaders usually see the threat coming. They often invented the technology themselves. This file explains why seeing it is not enough, which companies have escaped, and where the idea has been stretched too far.

Question 1 · What is it?

Being overtaken from below

In everyday speech, disruption means any big change. In its original, precise sense, it means something narrower and more interesting: a simpler, cheaper offer that starts at the bottom of a market and climbs.

Chapter 01

The company that invented its own replacement

In 1975 a young engineer at Eastman Kodak called Steven Sasson built the first digital camera. It weighed several kilograms, took about 20 seconds to record a single black-and-white image of 0.01 megapixels, and stored it on a cassette tape. Viewing the picture needed a television. Compared with Kodak's film, it was a toy.

Kodak was then one of the great companies in the world. It dominated photographic film in the United States, and film was extremely profitable: every photograph a customer took meant buying more film and paying for prints. Kodak did develop digital technology over the following decades. But digital photography threatened the business that paid for everything. In January 2012, Kodak filed for bankruptcy protection. Smartphone cameras, built on the kind of technology its own engineer had pioneered, had made film almost irrelevant.

The usual story is that Kodak's managers were foolish. The more useful and more disturbing story is that they behaved rationally at almost every step. That is what makes disruption a subject worth studying.

Chapter 02

The precise meaning, and the loose one

The theory comes from the Harvard professor Clayton Christensen, first in a 1995 article written with Joseph Bower and then in his 1997 book The Innovator's Dilemma. In his sense, disruptive innovation has three features.

  1. It starts out worse than the established product on the measures the market leader's best customers care about.
  2. It is cheaper, simpler or more convenient, so it appeals to people the leader does not serve well: customers who want less, or people who were not customers at all.
  3. It improves over time until it is good enough for mainstream customers, and then takes the market.

The loose meaning, "any big change that hurts incumbents", is far more common. Christensen himself complained about it. In a 2015 article he argued that Uber, for example, was not disruptive in his sense. It did not start at the low end or with non-customers. It started with a service that was, for many riders, simply better than a taxi. The distinction is not academic pedantry. The two kinds of threat need different responses.

ExampleA sharper camera lens, a faster car engineEarly digital cameras, early personal computers

Christensen's research found that established leaders are very good at sustaining innovation, even when it is technically radical. They have the money, the engineers and the customers. Where they fail is with disruptive innovation, even when the technology is simple. The problem is not the technology. It is the business logic around it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Overshooting the customer, and the trap of good management

Disruption works through a gap. Established companies improve their products faster than most customers need, and leave the bottom of the market open to a cheaper rival.

The key observation is that products often improve faster than customers' needs. A market leader keeps adding features for its most demanding customers, because they pay the most. Eventually the product is better than ordinary customers can use, and more expensive than they want to pay. That leaves room at the bottom. A newcomer offers something simpler and cheaper. It is "not good enough" for the leader's best customers, but good enough for the people who were being overserved. Then it improves, and climbs.

Chapter 05

Why listening to your best customers can kill you

Christensen built the theory partly on the history of computer disk drives, an industry that changed so fast it worked like a laboratory. Each new, smaller size of drive was at first worse than the previous one on what mattered most to existing buyers: storage capacity. Makers of large drives for mainframe computers asked their customers whether they wanted the smaller drives. The customers said no. They needed capacity. So the leaders, sensibly, ignored the small drives.

The small drives found new customers, first makers of smaller computers, and then improved until they had enough capacity for everyone. Across several generations, many leading drive makers failed at the transition. The leaders did what every management course recommends: they listened to their best customers, invested where margins were highest and ignored small markets. That was exactly what doomed them.

Three pressures push well-run companies this way.

  • Margins. The disruptive product earns less per unit. Every internal budget meeting favours the high-margin business.
  • Size. A small new market cannot move the growth rate of a giant company. A 10-million-dollar market is exciting for a start-up and irrelevant to a firm with 10 billion in sales.
  • Customers and investors. The people who pay the company's bills want the existing product improved, not replaced.
Chapter 06

Two routes in: the low end and the empty market

Christensen described two main paths.

Low-end disruption

The newcomer serves the least profitable customers of an existing market. In American steel, the "minimills", which melt scrap in electric furnaces, started by making cheap reinforcing bar for concrete, the lowest-value product. The big integrated steelmakers were happy to give it up. The minimills, led by companies such as Nucor, improved and moved up, product by product, into more valuable kinds of steel.

New-market disruption

The newcomer serves people who were not customers at all, because the existing product was too expensive or difficult. In the 1950s Sony's cheap, crackly transistor radios were poor next to large valve radios. But teenagers could afford them and carry them away from their parents. The early personal computer worked the same way: it was weak next to a minicomputer, but individuals could own one.

In both cases, the incumbent feels no pain at first. In the low-end case it is giving up its worst customers, which makes its profit margins look better. In the new-market case it is losing nobody at all. Disruption is dangerous precisely because it does not hurt until it is advanced.

Chapter 07

Disrupting the business model, not the product

Sometimes the product barely changes and the way it is delivered and paid for changes completely. In the late 1990s Blockbuster ran thousands of video rental shops and earned a large share of its income from late fees. Netflix, founded in 1997, mailed DVDs to subscribers for a monthly fee, with no late fees. At first it was a niche service for film enthusiasts willing to wait for the post. According to accounts by Netflix's co-founders, Blockbuster was offered the chance to buy Netflix around 2000 for about 50 million dollars, and declined.

Blockbuster's problem was not that it lacked DVDs or the ability to post them. Its whole business, including thousands of shop leases and its income from late fees, was built around the old model. Copying Netflix meant attacking its own profits. Netflix then moved again, into streaming over the internet, in 2007. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010. A business model is a moat against copying, and a cage when the market moves.

Chapter 08

When the attack comes from above

Not every collapse of a leader is disruption in Christensen's sense. In 2007 the Finnish company Nokia was the world's largest maker of mobile phones. That year Apple launched the iPhone. It was not cheaper or simpler: it was more expensive and, in its own way, more advanced. It attacked from the top of the market, with a better product built around software and a touchscreen. Within a few years Nokia's phone business had collapsed, and in 2013 it agreed to sell it to Microsoft.

This is important because the defences differ. Against classic disruption from below, an incumbent needs a separate, low-cost unit that is allowed to grow without competing for the main business's budget. Against a superior product from above, it needs to compete head-on, and fast, with a better product. Nokia's difficulty was partly that its strengths were in hardware and manufacturing at a moment when the contest moved to software and an ecosystem of apps. Calling every defeat "disruption" hides which mistake was actually made.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Renewal for the economy, pain for the people inside it

Disruption is one of the main ways economies get better and cheaper. It is also one of the main ways jobs, skills and whole towns lose their purpose.

Chapter 09

Creative destruction

Long before Christensen, the Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter described the same force on a bigger scale. In his 1942 book Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy, he called it creative destruction: the process by which new products, methods and companies constantly replace old ones. For Schumpeter, this was not a side effect of capitalism. It was its essential fact.

Consumers are the main winners. Digital photography made pictures almost free. Streaming gave households vast libraries for the price of a few rentals. Cheap steel, cheap computing and cheap communication all arrived through waves in which new firms beat old ones. The largest companies today are, to a large degree, different names from the largest companies of fifty years ago. An economy where leaders could never be displaced would be one where prices stayed high and improvement slowed.

Chapter 10

The cost that does not show in the statistics

The benefits of disruption are spread thinly across millions of consumers. The costs fall heavily on particular people and places. Rochester, in New York State, was home to Kodak's headquarters and much of its workforce. As film declined, the company's local employment fell from tens of thousands to a small fraction of that. Skilled workers found that their experience, in film chemistry for example, was worth far less than before.

Economists broadly agree that the total gains from this kind of change outweigh the losses. They also increasingly agree that the losses are real, concentrated and slow to heal, and that people displaced in mid-career often do not get back to similar pay. This tension sits behind many of today's political arguments about trade, technology and automation. It is explored further in Work and society.

Chapter 11

How some leaders escaped

Disruption is not destiny. Several established companies have survived waves that killed their peers, and their methods are instructive.

MethodWhat it meansExample
A separate unitGive the new business its own team, budget and freedom, far from the main business's rulesIBM built its first personal computer, launched in 1981, in a small separate unit in Florida, away from its mainframe culture
Cannibalise yourselfLaunch the product that replaces your own best seller before a rival doesApple's iPhone absorbed the functions of its own very successful iPod
Change the model in stagesRun the old and new businesses in parallel, then shiftNetflix moved from posted DVDs to streaming. A clumsy 2011 attempt to split the two businesses angered customers and was quickly reversed, but the shift succeeded
Find the job, not the productAsk what customers are really trying to get done, and serve that, whatever the technologyChristensen's later "jobs to be done" idea

The common thread is a willingness to accept lower margins and smaller revenue now in order to own the future, which is exactly what normal management systems are designed to prevent. The leaders who escape usually do so because someone at the very top protects the new business from the old one.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful lens with a blurry edge

The theory explains some of the most famous corporate failures. It is weaker at predicting the next one, and the word has been stretched until it sometimes means nothing.

Chapter 12

ConsumersLower prices, more convenience, products for people who were priced outSometimes lower quality, at least at first; sometimes a new dominant firm

PlacesNew clusters of growth elsewhereTowns built around one industry can decline for decades

The overall effect on living standards has been strongly positive over the long run. The effect on particular groups at particular moments can be harsh. Both statements are true at the same time, which is why debates about disruption so often talk past each other.

Chapter 13

The critics, and what the theory cannot do

In 2014 the Harvard historian Jill Lepore published a sharp critique in The New Yorker. She argued that some of Christensen's case studies were selected and interpreted to fit the theory, and that several companies described as disrupted had in fact recovered. Researchers have since debated how well the theory predicts outcomes, with mixed results. Christensen responded and defended the core of the idea.

Two fair criticisms stand out. First, the theory is easier to apply looking back than looking forward. Many cheap, simple products start at the low end and simply stay there. It is hard to know in advance which "toy" will grow up. Second, the popular use of the word has become a marketing claim. Start-ups describe themselves as disruptors to attract investors, often with no low-end or new-market foothold at all. Used carefully, the theory is one of the most useful ideas in business. Used carelessly, it is a slogan.

Chapter 15

Is AI a disruptive innovation?

By Christensen's strict test, the answer is mixed. Much of AI is being adopted first by established companies to improve existing products, which is sustaining innovation, and the incumbents may well capture most of the gain. But some uses fit the classic pattern closely. An AI legal or accounting assistant is worse than a skilled professional, but far cheaper and always available. It first serves people and small businesses who could never afford the professional at all. That is new-market disruption, and the history in this file says it is exactly the kind of threat that firms charging by the hour tend to underestimate.

The deeper lesson of the theory applies regardless. The question to ask about any new technology is not "is it better than what we have?" but "is it good enough for someone we are not serving, and is it getting better fast?" If both answers are yes, the time to respond is while it still looks like a toy. Related questions about how jobs may change are in How work may change because of AI.

The core idea

Disruptive innovation starts worse but cheaper or simpler, wins customers the leader ignores, and improves until it is good enough for everyone. Leaders fail not from stupidity but because margins, size and their best customers all tell them, rationally, to ignore it.

The common mistake

Calling every big change "disruption". A better product attacking from above is a different threat from a cheaper one rising from below, and it needs a different response.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the disk drive leaders failed by listening to their best customers? If you can, you understand why good management can be the cause of disruption, not the cure.

The words, in plain words
Disruptive innovation
A cheaper or simpler offer that starts at the bottom of a market or with non-customers, then improves and takes over.
Sustaining innovation
Improvement of existing products for existing customers.
Incumbent
An established company that already leads a market.
Overshooting
When products improve faster than most customers need.
Low-end disruption
Entering by serving an existing market's least demanding, least profitable customers.
New-market disruption
Entering by serving people who could not use or afford the product before.
Business-model disruption
Changing how a product is delivered and paid for, rather than the product itself.
Cannibalisation
When a company's new product takes sales from its own existing one.
Creative destruction
Schumpeter's term for new firms and methods constantly replacing old ones.
Jobs to be done
The idea that customers "hire" a product to get a specific task done.
Non-consumers
People who need something but do not buy any existing product for it.
