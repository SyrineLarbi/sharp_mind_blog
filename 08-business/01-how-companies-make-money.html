How companies make money. Sell something for more than it costs you, and get paid before the bills arrive.

Every company, from a street food stall to a trillion-dollar technology group, runs on the same simple sum: money in, minus money out. What makes business interesting is everything hidden inside that sum. Which costs grow when you sell more and which do not. How much of each sale you keep. When the cash actually arrives. A company can sell billions of dollars of goods and lose money on every one. Another can sell almost nothing physical and keep most of what it earns. Once you can read the sum, you can understand almost any company in a few minutes.

Question 1 · What is it?

Revenue, costs, profit, and the cash that ties them together

Four words carry most of the subject. People mix them up constantly, and most bad business judgements start with that confusion.

Chapter 01

A coffee cart, done properly

Imagine you run a coffee cart outside a train station. You sell 300 cups a day at 3 euros each. That is 900 euros a day of revenue: the total money customers pay you. It is also called sales or turnover, and it is the "top line", because it sits at the top of the accounts.

Each cup needs coffee beans, milk, a paper cup and a lid. Say that costs 0.80 euros. Those are variable costs, because they rise and fall with every cup you sell. Then there are costs you pay whether you sell one cup or a thousand: the licence for your spot, the loan on the espresso machine, your assistant's wages. Those are fixed costs. Say they come to 400 euros a day.

Now the sum. 300 cups times 2.20 euros kept on each cup (3.00 minus 0.80) gives 660 euros. Take away the 400 of fixed costs and you have 260 euros of profit a day, before tax. Profit is the "bottom line". Every business on earth is a more complicated version of this calculation. The rest of this file is about the complications.

Chapter 02

Revenue is not profit, and profit is not cash

The first trap is treating revenue as success. A headline says a company "made" 10 billion dollars. Usually that means it sold 10 billion dollars of goods. If those goods cost 11 billion to make and deliver, the company did not make money at all. It lost a billion. Revenue tells you how big a business is. Profit tells you whether it works.

The second trap is subtler. Accountants record a sale when it happens, not when the money arrives. If your coffee cart starts supplying an office on 60-day payment terms, your accounts show the profit today, but the cash comes in two months later. Meanwhile you still have to pay for beans and wages this week. Cash is the money actually in the bank. A profitable company can run out of it and fail. This is common enough to have a name in small-business circles: growing broke.

The rule to remember

Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality. It is an old saying among accountants and small-business owners, and it compresses this whole chapter into eight words.

Chapter 03

The income statement, read from top to bottom

Large companies publish the same sum in a standard format called the income statement (in Britain often the profit and loss account, or P&L). It moves from the top line to the bottom line in steps, and each step answers a different question.

LineWhat it meansThe question it answers
RevenueEverything customers paid for goods and servicesHow big is the business?
Cost of goods soldThe direct cost of making or delivering what was soldWhat does each sale cost to fulfil?
Gross profitRevenue minus cost of goods soldIs the product itself worth making?
Operating expensesSalaries, rent, marketing, research, administrationWhat does it cost to run the company?
Operating profitGross profit minus operating expensesDoes the business work, before finance and tax?
Interest and taxPayments to lenders and to governmentsHow much goes to others with a claim?
Net profitWhat is left for the ownersWhat did the shareholders actually earn?

Each profit line divided by revenue gives a margin: the share of each euro of sales that survives that step. If revenue is 100 and gross profit is 40, the gross margin is 40 percent. Margins let you compare a tiny company with a giant, because they turn absolute numbers into proportions. The full accounts, including the balance sheet and the cash flow statement, are explained in Finance · Financial statements.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Margins, scale, timing and the economics of one customer

Two companies with the same revenue can be completely different machines. The differences come from four places: how much each sale keeps, how costs behave as you grow, when cash moves, and what each customer is worth.

Chapter 04

Gross margin: the first and most revealing number

Gross margin tells you how much room a business has. It varies enormously by industry, and the variation is not an accident. It reflects what the customer is really paying for.

A supermarket sells goods made by other people. It adds convenience and choice, but shoppers can compare prices easily and switch stores. Gross margins are thin, and after paying staff, rent and energy, large grocers commonly keep only one to four cents of net profit per euro of sales. They survive on enormous volume and fast-moving stock.

A software company is the opposite. Writing the program is expensive, but once it exists, selling one more copy costs almost nothing. Mature software businesses often report gross margins of 70 to 90 percent. Apple shows both models inside one company: in recent years its hardware has run at gross margins in the mid-to-high 30s percent, while its services, such as the App Store, iCloud and payments, have run at roughly 70 percent or more (as of 2024). A high gross margin usually means customers are paying for something hard to copy: a brand, a patent, a network, or code.

Chapter 05

Fixed costs, variable costs and the magic of scale

Go back to the coffee cart. If sales rise from 300 to 400 cups, revenue rises by a third. But fixed costs stay at 400 euros. So profit jumps from 260 to 480 euros, an increase of more than 80 percent. If sales fall to 200 cups, profit falls to 40 euros. A one-third change in sales produced a much bigger change in profit, in both directions.

This effect is called operating leverage. The more of a company's costs are fixed, the more its profit swings with its sales. Airlines are a famous example. A plane flying half empty costs nearly as much as a full one: the fuel, crew, aircraft lease and airport fees barely change. The last few passengers on each flight are almost pure profit. That is why airlines fight so hard on filling seats, and why a drop in travel, as in 2020, turns them from profit to heavy losses almost overnight.

The point where revenue first covers all costs is the break-even point. Below it, each sale reduces a loss. Above it, each sale adds profit. For high-fixed-cost businesses such as software, pharmaceuticals, telecoms and car factories, the race is to get past break-even and then sell as much as possible, because each extra unit is so cheap to supply.

Chapter 06

Timing: getting paid before you pay

Money tied up in the daily running of a business is called working capital. It includes stock sitting in warehouses and invoices that customers have not yet paid. Every euro stuck there is a euro the company had to find from somewhere else, often a loan.

The cleverest companies flip this around. In the 1990s Dell became known for building computers only after customers had ordered and paid, while paying its own component suppliers weeks later. Amazon's retail business has long worked in a similar way: it often collects from shoppers before it pays the sellers and suppliers whose goods it sold. In both cases, the suppliers were in effect lending the company money for free. The faster the company grew, the more cash it had, which is the reverse of "growing broke".

The measure is the cash conversion cycle: the number of days between paying for inputs and collecting cash from customers. Most manufacturers have a positive cycle and need finance to cover the gap. A business with a negative cycle can fund its own growth.

Chapter 07

Unit economics: the profit on one customer

For many modern businesses, especially subscriptions and apps, the useful question is not "is the company profitable this year?" but "does each customer, over their whole relationship with us, bring in more than they cost?" This is called unit economics.

Two numbers matter. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what you spend in advertising and sales to win one new customer. Lifetime value (LTV) is the profit that customer brings in before they leave. If a meal-kit company spends 90 euros to win a customer who orders for three months and brings in 60 euros of gross profit, it loses 30 euros on every customer it adds. Growing faster just loses money faster.

Healthy unit economics

Lifetime value is several times acquisition cost, and the company earns back the acquisition cost within a year or so. Growth creates value, so spending on marketing makes sense.

Broken unit economics

Customers cost more to win than they ever pay back, often because they leave quickly (high churn). More growth deepens the hole. Discounts and free trials can hide this for years.

Unit economics is a projection, not a fact, because nobody knows how long today's customers will stay. Optimistic guesses about lifetime value have justified a great deal of unprofitable growth. A careful reader asks how the company measured it.

Chapter 08

Two roads to profit: thin and fast, or fat and slow

Profit is margin times volume. A company can win by keeping a little on a vast number of sales, or a lot on a few. Both work, but they demand opposite habits.

Costco, the American warehouse retailer, is the classic volume player. It keeps markups on its goods unusually low and charges customers an annual membership fee. Its published accounts show membership fees equal to well over half of its net profit in recent years, so it is often described as earning its profit from memberships while selling goods close to cost. That keeps members loyal and prices low, which brings more members.

Luxury houses such as Hermès sit at the other end. They sell relatively few items, at very high prices, with operating margins far above most manufacturers. Scarcity is part of the product. Selling too many would damage the very thing customers pay for. Neither road is better. The danger is the middle: neither the cheapest nor the most desirable. The strategist Michael Porter called this being "stuck in the middle", and it is covered in Business strategy.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Profit is a signal, a test and a source of power

Understanding how companies earn explains why some survive recessions and others do not, why investors tolerate years of losses, and why your employer makes the decisions it makes.

Chapter 09

What profit is for

In a market economy, profit does a job beyond enriching owners. It is a signal. High profits in an industry tell other firms that customers value something and are not being served well enough, so competitors move in. Losses tell a company that it is using resources, such as people, materials and money, to make things customers do not value at that price. Adam Smith's observation that the baker serves you out of regard for his own interest is the starting point of this idea (see The Wealth of Nations).

Profit is also the main source of investment. Companies that earn well can fund new factories, research and hiring from their own cash, without asking banks or shareholders. That is why investors care so much about return on capital: how much profit a company earns for each euro invested in it. A business earning 25 percent a year on its capital can grow from its own pocket. One earning 3 percent, below what a safe government bond pays, is quietly destroying value even if it reports a profit.

Chapter 10

When growth came before profit

For roughly the decade after 2010, interest rates in rich countries were extremely low. Money was cheap, and investors were willing to fund companies that grew fast while losing money, on the theory that size would eventually bring profit. Some bets paid off spectacularly. Amazon reported thin or negative profits for years while it built warehouses and its cloud business. Today Amazon Web Services, a minority of Amazon's revenue, has produced well over half of its operating profit in most recent years.

Others never got there. WeWork, which leased offices long-term and rented them out short-term, was valued privately at around 47 billion dollars in early 2019. Its planned stock market listing collapsed later that year when investors read its accounts closely, and it filed for bankruptcy protection in the United States in 2023. Uber, after years of large losses, reported its first full year of operating profit in 2023. The lesson is not that losses are always bad. It is that losses must be buying something that will later earn. When rates rose sharply in 2022, investors became much less patient about which was which.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every way of earning has a weak point

There is no perfect profit structure. Each one buys strength in one kind of world and weakness in another.

Chapter 13

When the numbers mislead

Profit is measured by accounting rules, and rules involve judgement. When does a long contract count as revenue? How quickly does a machine wear out? Honest companies make these choices conservatively. Others stretch them. At the extreme is fraud: Enron, an American energy trader, used complicated off-balance-sheet companies to hide debt and inflate profits, and collapsed in 2001. The scandal brought down its auditor, Arthur Andersen, and led to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, which tightened rules on corporate accounts in the United States.

Short of fraud, watch for adjusted numbers. Many companies present a version of profit that leaves out certain costs. A common one is EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It can be useful for comparing businesses, but it ignores the real cost of replacing equipment. The investor Warren Buffett has long been a critic of leaning on it. When a company's favourite profit number keeps excluding more costs, ask why. Cash flow, the money that actually moved, is harder to dress up, which is why experienced readers check it first.

Chapter 15

The shift from things to ideas

A century ago, a company's value sat mostly in what you could touch: factories, land, railway lines, stock. Today the most valuable companies in the world mostly own things you cannot touch: software, patents, brands, data and networks of users. Accountants call these intangible assets, and traditional accounts handle them badly. Money spent building a factory is recorded as an asset. Money spent writing software or building a brand is often recorded as an expense, which makes the company look less profitable today than it really is.

This changes how companies earn. Intangibles are expensive to create and almost free to copy internally, so they reward scale even more than factories did. The winners can become very large very fast, and the gap between the leader and everyone else tends to widen. That trend sits behind many current debates, from competition law to the taxation of digital services, and it connects directly to the topics ahead: business models, competitive advantage and disruption.

The core idea

A company makes money when revenue exceeds costs, but the shape matters as much as the total. Gross margin shows how valuable the product is, fixed costs decide how profit swings with sales, cash timing decides whether growth needs outside money, and unit economics shows whether each new customer adds or destroys value.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Large grocers typically keep about 1–4 cents of net profit per euro of sales.
  • Mature software firms often have gross margins of 70–90 percent.
  • A 15 percent global minimum tax for large multinationals began applying in the EU from 2024.
The common mistake

Confusing revenue with profit, and profit with cash. A company can grow its sales, show a profit and still run out of money, because customers pay later than the bills arrive.

Test yourself

Can you explain why an airline's profit falls much faster than its ticket sales in a downturn? If you can, you understand fixed costs, operating leverage and break-even together.

The words, in plain words
Revenue
All the money customers pay a company for what it sells. The "top line".
Profit
What is left after all costs are subtracted from revenue. The "bottom line".
Cash flow
Money that actually comes into and leaves the bank account in a period.
Cost of goods sold
The direct cost of producing or delivering what was sold.
Gross margin
Revenue minus direct costs, as a share of revenue.
Fixed costs
Costs that stay the same whatever the volume of sales, such as rent.
Variable costs
Costs that rise with each unit sold, such as raw materials.
Operating leverage
The way high fixed costs make profit swing more than sales.
Break-even point
The sales level at which revenue exactly covers all costs.
Working capital
Money tied up in stock and unpaid customer invoices, minus what the company owes suppliers.
Unit economics
The profit or loss on a single customer or unit over its whole life.
Customer acquisition cost
What it costs in marketing and sales to win one new customer.
Return on capital
Profit earned for each unit of money invested in the business.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: a profit measure that leaves out some real costs.
