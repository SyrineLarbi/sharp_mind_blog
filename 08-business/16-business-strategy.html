Sharp Mind · Business · Topic 16 of 20

Business strategy. Not a list of ambitions, but a short list of things you have decided not to do.

Ask ten managers for their company's strategy and most will give you a goal: grow revenue, become the leader, delight customers. Those are wishes. A strategy is something different and much rarer. It is a clear account of the main problem a business faces, a chosen way of dealing with it, and a set of actions that fit together. Above all, it involves giving things up. This file explains how real strategies are built, how to recognise the fake ones, and why the subject has become harder in a world that refuses to stay predictable.

Question 1 · What is it?

Choosing a position, and paying for it

Strategy answers two questions: where will we compete, and how will we win there? The answers only count if they rule something out.

Chapter 01

The airline that said no

For decades Southwest Airlines, in the United States, was the textbook case. While other airlines tried to offer everything, Southwest refused. It flew one type of aircraft, the Boeing 737, so pilots, mechanics and spare parts were interchangeable. It flew point to point between cities rather than through big central hubs, so planes spent less time on the ground. For most of its history it served no meals and assigned no seats.

Each refusal annoyed some customers. Together they made a machine that could turn a plane around at the gate faster and fly more hours per day than its rivals, at lower cost. Southwest was profitable for decades in an industry famous for bankruptcies. Its strategy was visible less in what it did than in the long list of things it chose not to do.

The story has a modern twist. In 2024, under pressure from investors and changing customer tastes, Southwest announced it would end its famous open seating and offer assigned and premium seats. Whether that is a sensible update or the loss of a distinctive position is exactly the kind of question strategy is about.

Chapter 02

What strategy is not

In a 1996 article called "What Is Strategy?", the Harvard economist Michael Porter drew a line that is still the clearest in the field. He separated operational effectiveness, doing the same things as your rivals but better, from strategy, doing different things, or the same things in a different way. Cutting waste, adopting best practice and using good software are all operational effectiveness. They are necessary. But rivals can copy them, so they rarely give a lasting edge.

The strategist Richard Rumelt, in his 2011 book Good Strategy Bad Strategy, listed the common fakes. They are worth learning, because they fill most corporate slide decks.

Fake strategyWhat it looks likeWhy it fails
Fluff"We will leverage our synergies to deliver customer-centric value"Big words that hide the absence of a thought
A goal in disguise"Grow 20% a year and become number one"Says where you want to be, not how you will get there
A wish listTwelve "strategic priorities", all equally importantIf everything is a priority, nothing is
Avoiding the problemA plan that never names the main obstacleYou cannot solve a problem you have not stated

The test is simple. If the opposite of your strategy is obviously stupid, you do not have a strategy. "We will serve customers well" fails, because no one plans to serve them badly. "We will serve only small businesses, and turn away large ones" passes, because a reasonable rival might do the opposite.

Chapter 03

Three levels of choice

Strategy happens at three levels, and confusion often comes from mixing them up.

Corporate strategy

Which businesses should the company be in at all? Should a food company own a restaurant chain? Should it sell its pet food division? This is where mergers, acquisitions and sales of divisions are decided.

Business strategy

Within one business, how do we compete? Which customers do we serve, with what offer, and why will they choose us over the alternatives? This is the level most people mean by "strategy".

Functional strategy

How does each department, such as marketing, operations or technology, support the business strategy? A low-price strategy needs a very different supply chain from a luxury one.

The levels have to fit. A company that promises premium quality in its advertising while its operations team is told to cut costs at any price does not have three strategies. It has none, and its customers will notice first.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A diagnosis, a guiding choice, and actions that fit

Good strategy has a simple core, a handful of useful tools, and an honest acknowledgment that the plan will change once it meets reality.

Chapter 04

The kernel: three parts every real strategy has

Rumelt's most useful idea is that every good strategy has a kernel with three parts.

  1. A diagnosis. What is really going on? What is the main obstacle? A good diagnosis simplifies a messy situation into something you can act on.
  2. A guiding policy. The overall approach chosen to deal with that obstacle. It is like a signpost: it does not say every step, but it rules out many directions.
  3. Coherent actions. A set of steps that support each other and carry out the policy, with money and people actually assigned to them.

Imagine a mid-sized bookshop chain in the 2010s. A weak "strategy" would say: grow sales and improve the customer experience. A real one starts with a diagnosis: we cannot beat online sellers on price or range, but people still enjoy browsing and asking for advice. The guiding policy follows: compete on discovery and atmosphere, not on price or completeness. The coherent actions follow too: let each shop's staff choose its stock, add events and a café, stop matching online discounts. Something close to this path was taken by some booksellers in that decade, including the British chain Waterstones.

Most of the value is in the diagnosis. Once you have honestly named the problem, the choices often become obvious. Most bad strategy comes from skipping it, because naming the problem is uncomfortable.

Chapter 05

Where to play, how to win

A. G. Lafley, who ran Procter & Gamble, and the strategy professor Roger Martin wrote Playing to Win in 2013. They describe strategy as a chain of five linked choices.

The strategy cascade (Lafley and Martin)
  1. WINNING ASPIRATION   what does winning mean for us?
            |
  2. WHERE TO PLAY        which customers, products, regions, channels?
            |
  3. HOW TO WIN           why will those customers choose us there?
            |
  4. CAPABILITIES         what must we be excellent at to win that way?
            |
  5. MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS   what measures, structures and habits keep it going?

  Each choice must support the one above it. A break anywhere breaks the chain.

The two middle questions do most of the work. Where to play is the choice of battlefield. How to win is the reason customers will pick you on that battlefield. They must match: winning on price in a luxury market makes no sense. Martin's favourite tool for testing these choices is the question in Volume 02, "What would have to be true?". Instead of arguing about which option is right, the team lists what would have to be true for each option to work, then checks the items it is least sure about.

Chapter 06

The standard tool kit, and what each tool is for

Business schools teach a handful of frameworks. Each is a way of organising thinking, not a machine that produces answers.

ToolWhat it doesUseful forWatch out for
SWOTLists internal Strengths and Weaknesses, and external Opportunities and ThreatsA first conversationLists without choices. It rarely tells you what to do
Five forces (Porter, 1979)Asks why an industry is profitable or notDeciding whether a market is worth enteringExplained in Competitive advantage
Ansoff matrix (1957)Sorts growth options: existing or new products, for existing or new marketsSeeing how risky a growth plan isNew product and new market at once is the riskiest box
Growth-share matrix (BCG, about 1970)Sorts a company's businesses by market growth and market shareDeciding where to invest and what to sellIts labels ("cash cows", "dogs") can become self-fulfilling

The growth-share matrix, from the Boston Consulting Group, is the best known. It calls a business with high share in a slow market a cash cow: it produces money without needing much investment. A business with high share in a fast market is a star. Low share in a fast market is a question mark. Low share in a slow market is a dog. The logic is to use the cows' cash to fund the stars and the most promising question marks, and to sell or close the dogs. It was hugely influential in the 1970s and is now seen as too simple, but its core question, which businesses fund which, is still central to corporate finance.

Chapter 07

Planned strategy and the strategy that happens

In 1959 Honda arrived in the United States planning to sell large motorcycles to compete with Harley-Davidson and British brands. The big bikes leaked oil and broke down under long American rides. Meanwhile, the Honda staff were riding small 50cc motorbikes, the Super Cub, around Los Angeles on errands. People kept asking where to buy one. Honda switched its focus, and the Super Cub became a huge success, sold through sports shops rather than motorcycle dealers.

The Canadian scholar Henry Mintzberg used cases like this to separate deliberate strategy, planned in advance, from emergent strategy, a pattern that forms from many decisions as the company learns what works. (How exactly the Honda story unfolded has been debated by historians of the company, but the general lesson is widely accepted.) Most real strategies are a mix. The plan sets a direction, and the market edits it. The skill is to notice the edit quickly, as Honda did, rather than defend the original plan.

Chapter 08

Planning for several futures at once

In the early 1970s a planning team at the oil company Shell, led by Pierre Wack, stopped trying to forecast the oil price. Instead it wrote several detailed stories, called scenarios, about different futures. One explored what would happen if oil-producing countries cut supply and prices jumped. When the 1973 oil crisis came, Shell's managers had already thought through that world. Shell is often said to have responded faster than its rivals as a result, though how large that advantage was is hard to measure.

The point of scenario planning is not to guess which future will happen. It is to make a strategy that survives several of them, and to agree in advance on the warning signs that one is arriving. It is a cure for the most common planning error: building everything on a single forecast.

Chapter 09

Making the competition irrelevant

In their 2005 book Blue Ocean Strategy, W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne argued that companies often fight in crowded "red oceans", where rivals compete on the same features until profits disappear. The alternative is to create a "blue ocean": a new market space. Their main example was Cirque du Soleil. Circuses were in decline. Instead of competing on star performers and animal acts, it removed the animals, which were expensive and increasingly unpopular, and added elements from theatre and dance. It could then charge theatre prices to adults.

The method has four actions: what to eliminate, what to reduce, what to raise and what to create. It is a useful way to think about trade-offs. Its critics point out that blue oceans do not stay blue for long. Once a new space proves profitable, rivals arrive, and then the question becomes how to defend the position, which is the subject of the next topic.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Focus is scarce, and every company spends it somewhere

Every company has a strategy, even if nobody wrote it down. It is wherever the money, the best people and the leadership's attention actually go. The question is only whether it was chosen on purpose.

Chapter 10

Four boxes that saved a company

When Steve Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, the company was close to running out of money. It sold a confusing range of computers, printers and other devices. Jobs drew a simple grid on a whiteboard: consumer and professional on one side, desktop and portable on the other. Apple would make one great product for each of the four boxes, and stop almost everything else. Many projects were cancelled and many people left.

Apple's later success had many causes, but the first step was this act of subtraction. It shows why strategy matters even for companies that are not in crisis. Money, talented people and management attention are limited. Spread across twenty priorities, each one gets too little to succeed. Concentrated on four, they can win.

Chapter 11

The gap between strategy and what actually happens

A strategy that lives only in a presentation changes nothing. It becomes real only through thousands of daily decisions: which customer gets the scarce product, which project gets the engineer, which request gets a polite no. For that to happen, people several levels down must understand the strategy well enough to apply it without asking.

Surveys of managers regularly find that many of them cannot name their company's top priorities. The exact figures vary widely between studies, but the pattern is consistent. This is why the "management systems" step in the Lafley and Martin cascade matters so much. If the budget, the bonuses and the measures reward something other than the strategy, people will follow the budget, the bonuses and the measures.

Chapter 12

Strategy for whom? The purpose debate

Behind every strategy sits a question: what is the company for? In 1970 the economist Milton Friedman wrote a famous essay in The New York Times Magazine arguing that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits, within the rules of the game. On this view, called shareholder primacy, managers work for the owners, and society's other goals are best pursued by governments and by individuals with their own money.

The opposing view, stakeholder capitalism, says a company should serve employees, customers, suppliers and communities as well as shareholders. In 2019 the Business Roundtable, a group of chief executives of large US companies, published a statement endorsing this broader purpose. Supporters saw a real shift. Critics on one side called it public relations with no change in behaviour. Critics on the other side argued that managers accountable to everyone are accountable to no one. The debate became sharper in the early 2020s with arguments over environmental, social and governance (ESG) investing. Both positions are held by serious people, and how the question is answered varies by country and by law. Large German companies, for example, must give employees seats on their supervisory boards.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Commitment against flexibility

The central tension in strategy is that choices are only valuable if you stick to them, and only safe if you can change them.

Chapter 13

What a clear strategy buys, and what it costs

What a clear strategy gives you

  • Focus: resources concentrated where they can win.
  • Speed: people can decide without asking, because the trade-offs are known.
  • Fit: activities that support each other are hard for rivals to copy as a set.
  • A shared story: staff, investors and customers know what to expect.

What it costs you

  • Lost options: the markets you said no to may turn out to be the big ones.
  • Rigidity: a strong commitment can blind a company to change.
  • False confidence: a neat framework can make a guess look like analysis.
  • Internal pain: every "no" disappoints someone with power.

Formal planning has its own balance sheet. Mintzberg's 1994 book The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning argued that elaborate yearly planning cycles often became rituals that produced documents rather than thinking. Supporters of planning answer that without some formal process, the loudest voice wins. The sensible middle is light planning, honest diagnosis, and frequent review. A strategy should be firm enough to say no today, and open enough to be revised when the evidence changes.

Chapter 14

Why the frameworks look better than they work

Strategy has a measurement problem. You cannot rerun history to see what would have happened with a different choice. So the field learns mainly from case studies, and case studies favour survivors. A company that made a bold bet and won is a case study. Ten companies that made the same bet and lost are forgotten. Volume 39 explains this trap as survivorship bias.

This does not make strategy useless. It means frameworks are aids to thinking, not proofs . They help you ask better questions. They cannot tell you the future, and a strategy presented with too much certainty should make you more suspicious, not less.

Chapter 16

When everyone has the same analysis

There is an interesting consequence if AI makes strategic analysis cheap. If every company can produce a competent market study in an afternoon, analysis stops being an advantage. Companies using the same tools on the same public data may also reach the same conclusions, and crowd into the same opportunities. That is exactly the red ocean that strategy is meant to avoid.

The likely result is that the old core of strategy becomes more valuable, not less. A distinctive diagnosis, private knowledge of customers, and the willingness to make a choice others will not make are hard to automate. The tools will change the work of strategy. They are unlikely to change its heart, which is deciding what to give up.

The core idea

Strategy is a diagnosis of the main problem, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions that carry it out. It answers where to play and how to win, and it only counts if it rules options out.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Three parts of Rumelt's kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.
  • Five choices in the Lafley and Martin cascade.
  • Four product boxes at Apple in 1997, after cutting most of the range.
The common mistake

Calling a goal a strategy. "Become number one" is a wish. A strategy says how, and what you will stop doing to get there.

Test yourself

Take your own organisation's stated strategy. Is its opposite obviously stupid? If it is, can you rewrite it as a diagnosis plus a choice that a sensible rival might reject?
