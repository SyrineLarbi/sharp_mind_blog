Human resources. How organisations find, pay, judge and keep the people they depend on.

Most employees meet HR at three moments: when they are hired, when something goes wrong, and when they leave. In between it is easy to see it as paperwork and policy. But the decisions HR shapes, who gets hired, how pay is set, how performance is judged, decide more about a company's results than almost anything else, because people are usually its largest cost and its hardest asset to copy. This file explains what the function does, what research says works, and why it is so often distrusted.

Question 1 · What is it?

The function that manages the employment relationship

Human resources (HR) is the part of an organisation responsible for its people: how they join, how they are paid and developed, how they are treated, and how they leave.

Chapter 01

From welfare officer to "people function"

In the factories of the late 1800s, some employers hired "welfare secretaries" to look after workers' housing, health and morals, partly out of conscience and partly to keep unions away. As companies grew and labour laws spread in the early twentieth century, this became personnel management: hiring, records, pay and handling disputes.

Between 1924 and 1932, researchers at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory near Chicago, studied how lighting and rest breaks affected output. Output seemed to rise with almost any change, and the team, later led by the Harvard researcher Elton Mayo, concluded that attention, group feeling and being consulted mattered as much as physical conditions. Later analysts have questioned how strong the original data really was. But the studies launched a lasting idea: workers are not just hands to be timed, but people whose motivation affects results.

From the 1980s the name human resource management spread, with the claim that people should be managed as a strategic asset, not just administered. Today many companies prefer "People" or "People and Culture". The renaming says something real, and also something about how much the function wants to be liked.

The easiest way to see the whole job is to follow one employee through their time at a company.

Underneath all of these sits compliance: obeying employment law on contracts, working hours, discrimination, health and safety, and data. In many companies this legal work takes most of HR's time, which is one reason it is so often seen as the department of "no".

Chapter 03

Whose side is HR on?

A common piece of workplace advice is "HR is not your friend". It is unkind but partly accurate. HR is employed by the company. Its job includes protecting the organisation from legal risk, and when an employee's interests and the company's clash, HR usually represents the company.

Yet the best HR work serves both sides at once, because a company that treats people fairly keeps them longer, gets more from them and is sued less. In 1997 the American professor Dave Ulrich proposed a model that many large companies adopted. HR splits into business partners who advise managers on people questions, centres of expertise for specialist topics such as pay or learning, and shared services that handle routine administration. The model made HR more strategic. Critics say it also moved HR further from ordinary employees, who now meet a portal and a call centre rather than a person.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Choosing, paying, judging and keeping people

HR's core tasks are full of decisions that feel like common sense and are often wrong. Research has a lot to say about which methods work.

Chapter 04

Hiring: what actually predicts performance

For decades, researchers in industrial and organisational psychology have measured how well different selection methods predict how people later perform in the job. An influential 1998 review by Frank Schmidt and John Hunter combined many studies. A large 2022 re-analysis led by Paul Sackett corrected some of its statistical assumptions and lowered several estimates, but the broad ranking held.

MethodWhat it isHow well it predicts
Structured interviewEvery candidate gets the same job-related questions, scored against a set guideAmong the best in recent estimates
Work sampleDoing a realistic piece of the job, such as writing code or handling a mock customerGood
Cognitive ability testsTests of reasoning and problem-solvingUseful, but lower in the newer estimates than once believed, and can disadvantage some groups
Unstructured interviewA free conversation, different for each candidateWeaker than structured, and prone to bias
Years of experienceHow long someone has done similar workWeak beyond the first few years
Handwriting analysis, astrologyStill used by a few firmsNo useful prediction

The single most practical finding is that structure beats intuition. Asking every candidate the same questions about real situations ("tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news to a client") and scoring the answers against a guide works much better than a pleasant chat.

Chapter 05

Why the free-flowing interview misleads

Unstructured interviews feel informative, which is why managers love them. But they invite every bias in the library. Interviewers form a view in the first minutes and then ask questions that confirm it (Volume 35). They prefer people who resemble themselves, went to similar schools or share their hobbies. They mistake confidence and fluency for competence. And they over-read a single conversation, which may simply have been a good or bad day for the candidate.

There is a second problem: interviewers rarely find out whether they were right. Nobody tracks the candidates they rejected, and the ones they hired are judged by others. So the feeling of being a good judge of people is almost never tested. Companies that take hiring seriously use panels, score answers independently before discussing them, and compare interview scores with later performance.

Chapter 06

Pay: structure, fairness and transparency

Most larger organisations group jobs into levels and set a pay band for each: a range from minimum to maximum, based partly on what other employers pay for similar work. Pay can include a base salary, bonuses linked to results, shares in the company and benefits such as pensions and health cover. Together these are called total reward.

People care intensely about pay relative to others, not just its level. Discovering that a colleague doing the same job earns much more is one of the fastest ways to lose an employee's trust. That is why pay secrecy has come under pressure. Several US states now require salary ranges in job adverts, and the European Union's Pay Transparency Directive, adopted in 2023, requires member states by June 2026 to give job applicants pay information, let employees ask about average pay for their category, and make larger employers report gender pay gaps. Pay that cannot be explained will increasingly have to be.

Chapter 07

Performance reviews, and why companies keep redesigning them

The traditional system is the annual review: a manager rates each employee once a year, often on a five-point scale, and the rating drives bonuses and promotion. At General Electric under Jack Welch, from the 1980s, managers had to place staff on a "vitality curve": the top 20%, the vital middle 70%, and the bottom 10%, who were often asked to leave. Critics called it "rank and yank". Microsoft used a similar "stack ranking" until 2013, and former staff said it made colleagues compete instead of cooperate.

From the early 2010s many large companies, including Adobe and Deloitte, moved away from annual ratings towards frequent, informal check-ins. Deloitte reported that its old process consumed close to two million hours a year. The trouble with ratings is partly statistical. A single year's performance mixes skill with luck, and as Volume 39 explains, extreme results tend to drift back towards average the next year. Rating people once a year often rewards luck and punishes bad luck, while the real purpose, helping people improve, gets lost in the argument about the number.

Chapter 08

Engagement, and the manager who makes or breaks it

Engagement means how involved and committed people are in their work. The polling company Gallup surveys it worldwide. In its reports from the mid-2020s, only around a fifth to a quarter of employees counted as engaged. Gallup's own research suggests that most of the difference between teams comes from the immediate manager.

Google tested this in its own company. In a project called Oxygen, it set out partly to show that managers did not matter much, and found the opposite: teams with better managers performed better and kept people longer. The qualities that counted were ordinary ones, such as being a good coach, not micromanaging, and caring about people's careers. A later Google study, Project Aristotle, found that the strongest predictor of team effectiveness was psychological safety, a term developed by the Harvard professor Amy Edmondson for a shared belief that it is safe to speak up, admit mistakes and ask questions. Much of HR's real influence lies in how managers are chosen and trained. Why people want to work at all is the subject of Psychology · Motivation.

Chapter 09

The law: very different rules in different countries

HR operates inside employment law, and the law varies enormously. The details change often, so only the general shapes are given here.

PlaceThe general approachEffect on HR
United States"At-will" employment in almost every state: either side can usually end the job at any time, for any lawful reason. Strong anti-discrimination laws.Fast hiring and firing. Legal risk centres on discrimination and retaliation claims.
United KingdomProtection against unfair dismissal after a qualifying period of employment, a threshold that was under political reform in 2025.Dismissals need a fair reason and a fair process.
FranceDismissal needs a "real and serious cause". Detailed labour code and collective agreements by sector.Dismissal is slow and costly. Much negotiation with employee representatives.
GermanyWorks councils can be set up in workplaces with five or more employees. In large companies, employees elect up to half of the supervisory board.Many decisions on hours, layoffs and monitoring must be agreed with employee representatives.

Neither model is simply better. Easy dismissal lets companies adjust and hire more freely, but leaves workers insecure. Strong protection gives security and long-term loyalty, but can make firms reluctant to hire. Economists still debate how much these rules change total employment, as opposed to who gets the secure jobs.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

People are the cost, the asset and the risk

For many companies, especially in services, staff costs are the largest item in the budget. The quality of people decisions shows up in results, and their failures show up in court and in the news.

Chapter 10

The hidden price of people decisions

When an employee leaves, the company pays far more than the cost of an advert. There is the time spent recruiting, the months before a new person is fully productive, the knowledge that walked out of the door, and the extra load on colleagues. Estimates of the total vary widely by job and study, so a single figure would be misleading. But for skilled roles the cost is commonly put at a large fraction of a year's salary, and sometimes more.

Bad hires are worse than slow ones. A person who is wrong for a role can damage customers, morale and colleagues before anyone acts. This is why good companies treat hiring as one of a manager's most important tasks rather than an interruption. Every hire is a bet of hundreds of thousands in salary over a few years, often made after a few hours of conversation.

Chapter 11

When HR fails: culture as a risk

In February 2017 Susan Fowler, a former engineer at the ride-hailing company Uber, published a blog post describing sexual harassment by a manager and an HR department that, she wrote, had repeatedly failed to act because the manager was a "high performer". The post led to an external investigation, the dismissal of a number of employees and, within months, the resignation of the company's chief executive.

Similar stories have repeated across industries, especially after the #MeToo movement from 2017. The pattern is consistent. Complaints are handled as legal risks to contain rather than problems to fix, powerful people are protected, and the culture learns that speaking up is dangerous. A company's real values are shown by what it tolerates from its best performers. HR cannot fix this alone. It needs leaders who back it, and independent channels so that complaints about senior people do not go only to those people's allies.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What a strong HR function gives, and what it costs

The same practices that bring fairness and consistency can also bring bureaucracy and distance. Most of HR's reputation problem lives in this tension.

The pattern across the table is that process protects against the worst decisions but can crowd out good judgement. The best HR functions keep firm rules where fairness and law demand them, and leave room for managers to treat people as individuals elsewhere.

Chapter 14

HR when some of the work is done by machines

AI tools already screen CVs, write job adverts, answer staff questions about leave and benefits, and summarise engagement surveys. The routine administration of HR is likely to shrink. The harder questions grow. Which tasks should move to software, and what happens to the people who did them? How do junior employees learn a job when the simple tasks they once learned on are automated? Who is responsible when an algorithm makes an unfair decision about a person?

These are classic HR questions about fairness, skills and trust, applied to a new tool. The more work machines do, the more the human parts of work, judgement, relationships and learning, need deliberate management. For the wider picture of how jobs may change, see How work may change because of AI and Human-AI collaboration.

The core idea

HR manages the whole employment relationship: planning, hiring, pay, performance, relations and leaving, all inside employment law that differs by country. It serves both the company and its people, and the two are best served together. Structure beats intuition in hiring, and managers drive most of what employees experience.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Gallup's mid-2020s surveys found only about a fifth to a quarter of employees worldwide engaged.
  • GE's "vitality curve" sorted staff into 20 / 70 / 10.
  • The EU Pay Transparency Directive must be applied by June 2026.
The common mistake

Trusting your gut in a job interview. A pleasant, unstructured chat mostly measures how much a candidate resembles you, and you will rarely find out whether you were right.

Test yourself

Can you explain why rating employees once a year can reward luck? If you can, you have connected performance reviews with regression to the mean.

The words, in plain words
Human resources (HR)
The function that manages people in an organisation, from hiring to leaving.
Personnel management
The older, more administrative name for the same function.
HR business partner
An HR specialist who advises a particular part of the business on people questions.
Onboarding
Bringing a new employee into the company and helping them become effective.
Structured interview
An interview with the same job-related questions for everyone, scored against a guide.
Work sample
A test in which candidates do a realistic piece of the job.
Pay band
The salary range set for a level of job.
Total reward
Everything an employee receives: salary, bonus, shares and benefits.
Performance management
The ways an organisation sets goals, judges results and gives feedback.
Engagement
How involved and committed people feel in their work.
Psychological safety
A shared belief in a team that it is safe to speak up and admit mistakes.
At-will employment
The US rule that either side can usually end a job at any time, for any lawful reason.
Works council
An elected body of employees with legal rights to be consulted on, or agree to, certain decisions.
People analytics
Using data about employees to understand and improve decisions about them.
