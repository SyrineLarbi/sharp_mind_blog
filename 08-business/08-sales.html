Sharp Mind · Business · Topic 08 of 20

Sales. Turning someone's interest into a decision, one conversation at a time.

The popular picture of a salesperson is a fast talker who will not take no for an answer. The reality, in most serious businesses, is closer to a doctor taking a history: careful questions, a diagnosis, then a recommendation the buyer can defend to their own boss. Nothing in a company happens until something is sold, yet selling is one of the least understood jobs in it. This file explains the machinery behind it, the psychology, and the incentives that make it go right or badly wrong.

Question 1 · What is it?

The job of helping someone decide to pay

Selling is any effort that moves a specific buyer from interest to commitment. It ranges from a shop assistant's two minutes to a two-year campaign for a government contract.

Chapter 01

The cash register company that invented modern selling

Merchants have always sold, but the organised sales force is a fairly recent invention. One of its founders was John H. Patterson, who ran the National Cash Register Company in Dayton, Ohio, from the 1880s. His product was hard to sell. Shopkeepers did not think they needed a machine to record money, and some felt insulted by the suggestion that their clerks stole.

Patterson's answer was method. He gave his salesmen a written script, sent them to a training school, assigned each one a territory, set targets known as quotas, and paid them commission on what they sold. Much of what a modern sales department does, from sales training to monthly targets, can be traced to his company. One of his salesmen, Thomas Watson, later ran IBM and built its famous sales culture on the same ideas.

The lasting insight is that selling can be studied, taught and managed like any other process. It is not just a gift some people are born with.

Chapter 02

Many kinds of selling

Selling a sandwich and selling a hospital software system are both "sales", but they have little in common. The two big splits are who is buying and how complicated the decision is. B2C (business-to-consumer) means selling to individuals. B2B (business-to-business) means selling to organisations, where several people must usually agree.

People who sell by phone and video are called inside sales. Those who travel to meet customers are field sales. After the deal, an account manager often looks after the customer and tries to keep and grow the relationship.

Chapter 03

Sales is not marketing

The two are often confused, and in many companies they quarrel. A simple division helps. Marketing works on many people at once: it creates awareness, builds the brand and produces interested prospects. Sales works on one buyer at a time: it turns a particular person's interest into a signed order.

The handover between them is where things break. Marketing complains that sales ignores the prospects it sends. Sales complains that the prospects are useless. The common fix is a shared definition of a good prospect and a shared target, so that both teams are paid for revenue, not for activity. The wider marketing toolkit is in Business · Marketing.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A pipeline, a diagnosis, and a decision

Behind every sales team sits a funnel of numbers and a set of conversations. The numbers tell a manager where deals are lost. The conversations decide whether they are won.

Chapter 04

The pipeline

Every possible deal moves through stages, and at each stage some drop out. The full list of deals in progress is the pipeline. Picture it as a funnel.

A simple B2B funnel, with illustrative numbers
 LEADS            200   people who showed some interest
   \              /
  QUALIFIED      60    have the need, the money and the authority
     \          /
    MEETINGS    30     a real conversation about their problem
       \      /
     PROPOSALS 15      a written offer with a price
         \  /
         WON  5        signed

 win rate from lead to deal: 5 / 200 = 2.5%
 to win 10 deals next quarter, you need about 400 leads,
 or a better conversion rate at one of the stages

The power of the funnel is that it turns a vague worry ("sales are down") into a precise question ("we have enough meetings, but proposals are not converting"). A sales manager's main tool is not motivation. It is knowing which stage is leaking. Software that records every contact, called a CRM (customer relationship management system), exists largely to keep this funnel accurate.

Chapter 05

Qualification: deciding whom not to chase

The most expensive mistake in selling is spending months on a buyer who was never going to buy. So good teams qualify early: they check whether a prospect is real. An old checklist, long associated with IBM, is BANT. Does the buyer have a budget? Do you have the person with authority to say yes? Is there a genuine need? Is there a timeline, a reason to decide soon?

Modern versions add questions such as "what happens if they do nothing?" and "who else must agree?" In a large organisation, a purchase may need approval from the user, the technical team, finance, the legal department and a senior sponsor. A seller who talks only to the enthusiastic user often loses at the last step, when someone they never met says no.

Qualification also protects the buyer. A seller who walks away from a poor fit saves both sides months of wasted effort, and is more likely to be trusted next time.

Chapter 06

Discovery: questions beat pitches

In the 1970s and 1980s the British researcher Neil Rackham and his team watched about 35,000 sales calls to see what successful sellers actually did. His 1988 book, SPIN Selling, reported a surprise. In large, complex sales, the techniques taught for small sales, such as pushing for the order early, often reduced success. What separated the best sellers was the questions they asked.

Rackham grouped those questions into four kinds. Situation questions gather facts. Problem questions find what is going wrong. Implication questions explore what that problem costs ("if the machine breaks down, how long is the line stopped?"). Need-payoff questions let the buyer describe the value of solving it in their own words. The point is that a buyer believes what they say themselves far more than what a seller tells them.

Another school

In 2011 the researchers Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson argued in The Challenger Sale that the most successful B2B sellers do more than diagnose. They teach the customer something new about their own business and are willing to push back. The two views are compatible: understand the buyer's problem first, then bring an insight they did not have.

Chapter 07

Objections, negotiation and the close

An objection is a reason the buyer gives for not going ahead: too expensive, not now, we already have a supplier. Beginners argue with objections. Experienced sellers ask about them, because an objection is information. "Too expensive" may mean "I cannot see the value", "I do not have the budget this year", or "I need a better deal to look good to my boss". Each calls for a different response.

Negotiation follows once both sides want the deal and are discussing terms. The classic advice from Getting to Yes is to focus on interests rather than positions: a buyer demanding a lower price may really need lower monthly payments, which can be solved without cutting the price at all.

The close is asking for the decision. The 1992 film of David Mamet's play Glengarry Glen Ross made "Always be closing" famous as a sales motto. It was meant as a portrait of a toxic office, not a manual. In complex sales, a better habit is to agree a clear next step at the end of every meeting. Deals are rarely won by a clever final line. They are won by a series of small commitments, as Small yes, large yes explains.

Chapter 08

After the signature

For many businesses, especially those selling subscriptions, the first sale is only the start. If a customer pays monthly and leaves after three months, the company may have lost money winning them. Two numbers capture this. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what it costs to win a customer, including sales salaries and advertising. Customer lifetime value (LTV) is the profit that customer brings over the whole relationship. A healthy business needs LTV well above CAC.

So modern sales teams care about churn, the rate at which customers leave, and about expansion, selling more to existing customers. This is sometimes called "land and expand": win a small first contract, prove the value, then grow. Keeping a customer is usually far cheaper than finding a new one, which is why account managers and customer success teams have become as important as the people who win new business.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Revenue, incentives, and a buyer who has changed

Sales decides whether good products survive. How it is paid decides how honestly it behaves. And the buyer today arrives knowing far more than before.

Chapter 09

Good products do not sell themselves

Engineers and founders often believe that a better product will win on its merits. Sometimes it does. More often a decent product with a strong sales team beats a better product without one. Buyers are busy, cautious and loyal to what they already have. Switching suppliers carries risk for the person who recommends it, and nobody wants to be blamed for a failed change.

This is why founders of new companies are told that they must sell personally in the early days. Each conversation with a buyer teaches them what the market needs, which objections matter, and what customers will pay. Selling is the fastest way a young company learns whether its idea is real, which links directly to Product-market fit.

Chapter 10

You get what you pay for: the incentive problem

Salespeople are usually paid a base salary plus a variable part linked to results. Their expected total pay if they hit target is called on-target earnings. This makes sense: it rewards effort that is hard to observe directly. But it also means the pay plan is the real sales strategy. People sell what they are paid to sell, in the way they are paid to sell it.

The clearest warning came from the American bank Wells Fargo. Staff there faced aggressive targets for the number of products each customer held. In 2016 regulators fined the bank after it emerged that employees had opened millions of accounts that customers had never asked for. Thousands of employees were fired, the chief executive left, and the bank paid billions in later settlements. The employees were not uniquely dishonest. They were responding to a system that measured the wrong thing and punished people who missed the number.

The general rule

When a measure becomes a target, people find ways to hit the target without achieving the goal. Sales is where this happens fastest, because the targets are explicit and the money is immediate.

Chapter 11

The buyer who already knows

Twenty-five years ago a buyer often needed a salesperson to learn what a product did and what it cost. Today they read the website, the reviews, the comparison sites and forum discussions first. Research by the advisory firm Gartner has suggested that B2B buyers spend only a small part of their buying process, roughly a sixth, actually talking to suppliers, and that time is split between every supplier they consider.

That has changed the job. A seller who only repeats the brochure adds nothing. The value now lies in the things a website cannot do: understanding the buyer's particular situation, helping several people inside a company agree, reducing the risk of a bad decision, and making the purchase easy to approve. The shift to video calls during the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this, and much B2B selling has stayed remote.

Chapter 12

Trust as the real product

Selling has a bad reputation partly because some of it deserves one: pressure tactics, false deadlines, hidden fees, and the door-to-door and timeshare sellers who appear in consumer-protection reports. Many countries give buyers a cooling-off period, a few days to cancel certain contracts signed at home or far from a shop, precisely because pressure works in the moment and is regretted later.

In repeat businesses, the incentives point the other way. A seller who will meet the same customer next year, and whose name travels among buyers who talk to each other, gains more from being trusted than from any single trick. In the long run, the best salespeople sell their reliability as much as the product. Volume 08's review of Give and Take describes why generous professionals often end up at the top.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

People who sell, or products that sell themselves

Companies can lean on a human sales team or let customers sign up on their own. Each route has real strengths and real costs.

Most mature companies mix these. A software firm might let small customers sign up online, use inside sales for mid-sized companies, and keep a field team for the largest accounts. The right choice depends mainly on the size of the deal: the more a customer will pay, the more a company can afford to spend on a human being to win them.

Chapter 15

What stays human

If machines can answer every factual question, what is left for a salesperson? Probably the parts that were always the hardest. Finding out what a buyer is really worried about. Getting five people with different interests to agree. Taking responsibility when something goes wrong after the contract is signed. Being someone the customer is willing to call.

There is a second, quieter effect. When every inbox is full of perfect automated messages, a genuine recommendation from someone the buyer trusts becomes rarer and more valuable. Automation makes the average sales message cheaper and the trusted relationship more precious. The future of selling probably looks less like a call centre and more like advice. For the broader picture of how AI changes jobs like this one, see How work may change because of AI.

The core idea

Selling is a process, not a personality. Deals move through a pipeline, are qualified early, are won mainly through diagnosis and questions, and are worth only as much as the customer's lifetime value. How sellers are paid shapes how they behave.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Neil Rackham's research behind SPIN Selling (1988) studied about 35,000 sales calls.
  • A healthy business needs customer lifetime value well above the cost of winning the customer.
  • B2B buyers spend only a small part of their buying time, by one estimate about a sixth, talking to suppliers.
The common mistake

Talking instead of asking. A pitch delivered before the problem is understood sounds like every other pitch, and the buyer has already read it on the website.

Test yourself

Can you explain why Wells Fargo's employees opened accounts nobody wanted, without calling them simply dishonest? If you can, you understand how sales incentives work.
