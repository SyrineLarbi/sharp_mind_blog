Branding. The promise a company makes, stored in someone else's head.

Most people think a brand is a logo, a colour and a slogan. Those are only the visible tools. The brand itself is what a stranger expects before they have tried the product: that this cola tastes a certain way, that this car is safe, that this bank will still exist next year. That expectation is built slowly, lives in memory, and is worth more than the factories of many famous companies. It can also be destroyed in an afternoon.

Question 1 · What is it?

A shortcut that buyers carry around with them

A brand is a name with expectations attached. The company designs the name and the signs. The customer supplies the expectations, and they are the part that matters.

Chapter 01

From a burn mark to a promise

The word comes from burning. Farmers marked cattle with a hot iron so that anyone could see who owned the animal. Craftsmen did something similar with their goods. Potters in the ancient world pressed marks into jars, and medieval guilds stamped their work so that a buyer far away knew which workshop to praise or blame.

The modern brand appeared when goods began to travel far from the people who made them. In a village you knew the baker. In an industrial city of the 1800s you bought flour, soap and beer from factories you would never see. A mark on the package replaced the face of the maker. When Britain opened its first register of trade marks in 1876, the first one entered belonged to the Bass brewery: a plain red triangle. It told a drinker in a distant pub that this bottle came from the same place as the last good one.

That is still the core of it. A brand is a promise of consistency from someone you cannot see. The logo is just the signature on the promise.

Chapter 02

What a brand does for the buyer

It is easy to treat branding as something companies do to customers. In fact brands survive because they save buyers real effort. A large supermarket stocks tens of thousands of products. Nobody has time to judge each one from scratch, so people lean on names they recognise.

Chapter 03

The vocabulary: brand, product, company

A product is the thing itself: a shampoo, a bank account, a phone. A brand is the name and the set of memories attached to it. The two are not always the same size. One company can own many brands, and one brand can cover many products.

How a company arranges its names is called brand architecture. At one extreme is the branded house: one master name on everything, as with Virgin's airlines, trains and phone services, or Samsung's televisions and fridges. At the other is the house of brands: a parent most shoppers never notice, such as Procter & Gamble or Unilever, owning dozens of brands that each stand alone. The branded house is cheaper to build, because every advertisement helps everything. The house of brands is safer, because a scandal in one brand does not stain the others, and it lets one owner sell both a cheap and a premium version without confusing anyone.

Two more words are worth having. Brand identity is what the company wants people to think. Brand image is what they actually think. The gap between the two is where most branding work, and most branding failure, takes place.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Memory, availability and meaning

A brand works if it comes to mind at the moment of buying, if it is easy to find, and if it means something useful. Most branding effort is a slow campaign on those three fronts.

Chapter 04

Being thought of: mental availability

Picture someone thirsty at a petrol station. They will not compare every drink in the fridge. A few names come to mind almost instantly, and the choice is made among those. Researchers at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute in Australia, whose findings Byron Sharp popularised in the 2010 book How Brands Grow, call this mental availability: the chance that a brand is noticed or recalled in a buying situation.

This connects directly to a bias described in Volume 35. Things that come to mind easily feel more common, more popular and safer. Familiarity is itself persuasive. A brand that people have seen many times, even without paying attention, feels trustworthy for no better reason than that it feels known.

To be recalled, a brand needs distinctive assets: signs that point to it and to nothing else. Colours (the blue box of the jeweller Tiffany), shapes (the curved Coca-Cola bottle), sounds, characters and slogans all count. Companies guard them fiercely. The chocolate maker Cadbury spent years in the courts trying to own a particular purple, with mixed results, because a colour that shoppers link to one firm is worth a great deal.

Chapter 05

Being found: physical availability, and an odd law

Memory is only half of it. A brand also has to be easy to buy: on the shelf, in the app, in stock, at the top of the search results. Sharp calls this physical availability. A beloved brand that is missing from the shop loses the sale to whatever is there.

The research group also found a pattern so regular that they call it a law. In almost every category they studied, from toothpaste to banks, small brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal. They named it double jeopardy. The practical lesson upset a lot of marketing thinking. Big brands are big mainly because many people buy them occasionally, not because a small group adores them. So growth usually comes from reaching more light buyers, not from deepening the love of a few fans.

A live disagreement

Not everyone accepts this. Many brand strategists argue that emotional connection and a clear difference from competitors still matter a great deal, especially for premium and luxury goods. The sensible reading is that both are true in different places: reach and recall drive most everyday purchases, while meaning and difference justify higher prices.

Chapter 06

Meaning something: positioning

Being remembered is not enough if the memory is vague. Positioning is the choice of the one or two ideas a brand wants to own in the buyer's mind. For decades the Swedish carmaker Volvo has stood for safety. That single association does a lot of work. A parent buying a family car does not need to read crash statistics, because the brand has already done the summarising.

The American marketing professor Kevin Lane Keller described the value of a brand as the difference people's knowledge makes to how they respond. He called it brand equity: the extra willingness to choose, recommend, forgive or pay because of the name. Equity is built from associations that are strong (easily recalled), favourable (actually wanted) and, ideally, unique.

Good positioning is also a list of things the brand will not be. A budget airline that starts promising luxury confuses everyone. The discipline is to give up attractive options in order to stay clear.

Chapter 07

Why it takes years, and why short-term thinking wins anyway

Memories form through repetition and fade without it, as the library's memory file explains. So brands are built slowly and must be maintained. The British researchers Les Binet and Peter Field, analysing hundreds of advertising campaigns submitted to effectiveness awards, argued in 2013 that there are two different jobs. Brand building works slowly, through broad emotional advertising, and lifts sales and prices for years. Activation, such as a discount or a "buy now" message, gives a quick spike that soon disappears.

Their widely quoted rule of thumb is to spend roughly 60% on brand and 40% on activation, though the right split varies by category. The trouble is measurement. Activation shows up in next week's figures. Brand building shows up years later and is hard to prove. Managers judged quarterly tend to starve the brand without noticing, because the damage appears only after they have moved on.

Chapter 08

The taste test: brands change the experience itself

From 1975 PepsiCo ran the "Pepsi Challenge", inviting shoppers to taste two unlabelled colas. Many preferred Pepsi. Yet Coca-Cola kept selling more. One explanation is that a small sip favours the sweeter drink. The deeper one is that people do not drink a liquid alone. They drink the brand too.

Later research supports this. In a 2004 brain-imaging study, people's preferences between the two colas shifted when they were told which one they were drinking, and their brain activity changed with the label, not just with the liquid. Similar results appear with wine, where the same wine is rated higher when people are told it is expensive. A brand does not only describe an experience. It partly creates it. That is the reason a brand can be worth more than the factory that makes the product.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Money, trust, and who really owns the name

Brands decide which products people choose and how much they will pay. They also decide who survives a crisis, and they turn out to belong partly to the public.

Chapter 09

A brand is an asset you cannot see on the balance sheet

Imagine two identical bottles of painkiller, made in similar factories with the same active ingredient. One carries a famous name and costs three times as much. Many people still buy it. That gap, the price premium, is the most direct way a brand earns money. A strong brand also makes advertising more efficient, wins shelf space from retailers, attracts staff, and makes new products easier to launch.

Accounting struggles with this. Under the main accounting rules, a company cannot put the value of a brand it built itself on its balance sheet. A brand only appears there when it is bought, as part of the price paid above the value of the physical assets, recorded as goodwill or as an intangible asset. So a company's most valuable possession is often invisible in its own accounts. Consultancies such as Interbrand and Kantar publish yearly rankings that try to estimate brand values. Their methods differ and their numbers disagree, but they agree that the most valuable brands, such as Apple, Google and Microsoft in recent years, are worth hundreds of billions of dollars each.

Chapter 10

Trust in a crisis: the poison and the speech

In 1982 seven people died in the Chicago area after taking Tylenol capsules that someone had laced with cyanide. The maker, Johnson & Johnson, had not caused it. It still recalled about 31 million bottles nationwide, stopped advertising, and returned the product in new tamper-resistant packaging. The brand recovered much of its market within about a year. The episode is still taught as the model response: put the customer's safety above the quarter's profit, visibly and fast.

The opposite lesson came in 1991 from Gerald Ratner, who ran a large British chain of jewellery shops. In a speech to business leaders he joked that some of his products were "total crap". The newspapers repeated it everywhere. Customers who had bought gifts there felt mocked, and the company's value fell by hundreds of millions of pounds. The products had not changed. Only what people felt about buying them had. "Doing a Ratner" entered British English as shorthand for wrecking your own reputation with a careless sentence.

Chapter 11

When customers turn out to own it

In 1985 Coca-Cola replaced its century-old formula with a sweeter one, "New Coke", which had beaten the original in blind taste tests. The public reaction was furious. People phoned and wrote in by the hundreds of thousands, and in less than three months the company brought the old recipe back as "Coca-Cola Classic". The tests had measured taste. They had not measured attachment.

Smaller versions of this happen often. In 2010 the clothing retailer Gap changed its logo and reversed the change within about a week after an online backlash. The lesson is uncomfortable for managers. A company legally owns its trade marks, but the meaning of the brand is held by the public, and the public can refuse a change. In recent years this has extended to politics. In 2023 the American beer Bud Light lost a large share of its sales after a promotion with a transgender influencer drew a boycott from some customers, and it lost its place as the country's best-selling beer. Whatever one thinks of the campaign, it showed that a brand seen as taking sides can lose customers on one side or the other.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Useful shortcuts, and expensive illusions

Brands make markets work better in some ways and worse in others. The same feature, a name people trust without checking, produces both.

Chapter 13

Where branding misleads

The most common problem is not lying but implication. A cereal box shows fields and sunshine, and the buyer infers something about health that the label never actually claims. A newer version is greenwashing: suggesting that a product is good for the environment with vague words such as "natural" or "eco-friendly" and a picture of a leaf. Regulators have begun to act. The European Union adopted rules in 2024 that restrict generic environmental claims unless the company can prove them.

A quieter fact is that many supermarket own-label products are made in the same factories as branded ones, sometimes to very similar recipes. Discount chains such as Aldi and Lidl have built large businesses on this. Part of what a brand premium buys is reassurance, not a different product. That can be worth paying for. It is worth knowing when you are paying for it.

Finally, a brand is a target. Its value invites counterfeits, and its fame means that one mistake by one employee can travel around the world in hours. The same memory structures that make a brand powerful make a scandal stick to it.

Chapter 15

Does a brand matter to a machine?

If an assistant reads every review and specification before recommending a kettle, the shortcut a brand provides might seem less necessary. Some forecasters expect exactly that: a shift of power from the most famous name to the best-documented product. Others point out that the assistant learns from what people have written, and people write mostly about famous brands. Reputation may simply move from human memory into the training data.

Two things seem likely to hold either way. First, for anything bought on trust, such as medicine, insurance, banking or food, reputation will stay central, because the risk of a bad choice is too high to leave to a list of features. Second, the oldest rule of branding still applies. A brand is a promise of consistency. The companies that keep it, quietly, for decades, will keep the most valuable thing they own.

The core idea

A brand is the set of expectations a name triggers in a buyer's memory. It works when it is recalled at the moment of choice (mental availability), easy to buy (physical availability), and means something useful (positioning). It can change how a product is experienced, which is why it is worth so much.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Britain's first registered trade mark, in 1876, was the Bass red triangle.
  • A widely used rule of thumb splits marketing spend about 60:40 between brand building and short-term activation.
  • New Coke lasted less than three months before the old formula returned in 1985.
The common mistake

Confusing the logo with the brand. Redesigning the signature does not change the promise, and the promise lives in customers' heads, where the company cannot simply overwrite it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why blind taste tests can mislead a company about its own product? If you can, you understand that a brand is part of the experience, not a label on top of it.

The words, in plain words
Brand
A name, and everything people expect when they meet it.
Trade mark
The legal protection for a name, logo or other sign, so that others cannot copy it.
Brand equity
The extra value a brand adds: how much more likely people are to choose it, or pay for it, because of the name.
Positioning
The one or two ideas a brand chooses to own in buyers' minds, such as "safe" or "cheap".
Mental availability
How likely a brand is to come to mind when someone is about to buy.
Physical availability
How easy a brand is to find and buy: in stock, on the shelf, on the screen.
Distinctive assets
Colours, shapes, sounds and characters that point to one brand only.
Double jeopardy
The pattern that small brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are slightly less loyal.
Brand architecture
How a company organises its names: one master brand, or many separate ones.
Price premium
The extra amount people pay for a branded product over a similar unbranded one.
Goodwill
The accounting entry for the part of a purchase price that pays for invisible assets such as a brand.
Greenwashing
Suggesting a product is environmentally friendly without solid evidence.
