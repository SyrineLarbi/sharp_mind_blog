Corporate finance. Three decisions every company makes: what to invest in, how to pay for it, and what to do with what is left.

Corporate finance sounds like a world of spreadsheets and jargon. Underneath, it is a small set of questions that any household also faces. Is this purchase worth what it costs, once you count the wait for the benefit? Should I pay for it with savings or with a loan? And when money is left over, should I spend it, save it or give it back? Companies answer the same questions at much larger scale, with other people's money. This file explains how they do it, why cash matters more than profit, and why borrowing is both the most useful and the most dangerous tool in business.

Question 1 · What is it?

The money side of every business decision

Corporate finance is the study of how companies raise money, spend it on investments, and return it to the people who provided it. Its central goal is to create more value than the money costs.

Chapter 01

A profitable company that ran out of money

Imagine a small furniture maker that wins a big order from a hotel chain. The order is profitable: the hotel will pay 500,000 euros for furniture that costs 350,000 to make. The owner is delighted. But the hotel pays 90 days after delivery, as large customers often insist. Meanwhile the wood supplier wants payment in 30 days, and the staff want wages every month. For three months the company pays out money it has not yet received. If the bank refuses an extra loan, the company can fail while making a profit.

This is not a rare story. It is one of the most common ways small businesses die. A much larger version happened in 2018 when Carillion, a big British construction and services company, collapsed with heavy debts and a long line of unpaid suppliers, despite having reported profits not long before. Profit is an opinion produced by accounting rules. Cash is a fact. A company survives on cash.

Chapter 02

Three decisions, one goal

Almost everything in corporate finance belongs to one of three decisions.

1. The investment decision

Which projects should we spend money on? A new factory, a new product, a software system, an acquisition. This is called capital budgeting.

2. The financing decision

Where does the money come from? From the company's own profits, from borrowing (debt), or from selling a share of ownership (equity)?

3. The payout decision

When there is cash the business cannot use well, how should it go back to the owners? As dividends, as share buybacks, or should it be kept?

A fourth, everyday job sits underneath all three: managing working capital, the money tied up in stock, unpaid customer bills and unpaid supplier bills. The furniture maker's problem was a working capital problem.

The usual goal given in textbooks is to maximise the value of the company for its owners. That is not the same as maximising this year's profit. A company can raise this year's profit by cutting research, which may destroy value over ten years. The deeper debate about whether companies should serve only owners is covered in Business strategy.

Chapter 03

The two ideas everything rests on

The first idea is the time value of money. A euro today is worth more than a euro in five years. You could invest today's euro and earn interest, and there is always some risk that the future euro never arrives. So future money must be discounted, reduced, to compare it with money now. Volume 38 shows the mirror image of this, compounding: money growing forward in time. Discounting is the same calculation run backwards.

The second idea is the cost of capital. The money a company uses is never free. Lenders want interest. Shareholders want a return too, even though nobody sends them a bill, because they could have put their money somewhere else with similar risk. That forgone alternative is called the opportunity cost. A project is only worth doing if it earns more than the investors who fund it could have earned elsewhere. The cost of capital is the bar every investment must clear.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Discounting, borrowing and giving cash back

The tools are simpler than they look. One method for judging investments, one number for the cost of money, and one big trade-off between debt and equity.

Chapter 04

Is the project worth it? Net present value

Suppose a company can buy a machine for 1,000 today. It will bring in an extra 400 a year for three years, then wear out. In total that is 1,200, a gain of 200. But the 400s arrive later, so they are worth less than 400 today. To compare fairly, we discount each one by the cost of capital.

Net present value, worked through
  cost of capital 8%                        cost of capital 12%
  year 1   400 / 1.08   = 370               year 1   400 / 1.12   = 357
  year 2   400 / 1.08^2 = 343               year 2   400 / 1.12^2 = 319
  year 3   400 / 1.08^3 = 318               year 3   400 / 1.12^3 = 285
                         -----                                     -----
  value today           1,031               value today             961
  minus cost today     -1,000               minus cost today     -1,000
  NPV                    + 31   DO IT       NPV                    - 39   DON'T

  Same machine, same cash. Only the cost of money changed the answer.

The result is the net present value, or NPV: the value today of all the money a project will bring in, minus what it costs. If NPV is positive, the project earns more than the cost of capital and adds value. If it is negative, the investors would have done better elsewhere. Notice that the same machine is a good idea at 8 percent and a bad one at 12. That is why rising interest rates cancel so many business investments: nothing about the project changed except the price of money.

Two other measures are common. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which NPV would be exactly zero. For this machine it is about 9.7 percent, so the project makes sense whenever capital costs less than that. The payback period asks simply how long until the money comes back: here, two and a half years. Payback is crude, because it ignores what happens afterwards, but many managers like it because it is easy to understand and favours quick, safe returns.

Chapter 05

What the money costs: the blended rate

A company is usually funded by a mix of debt and equity. Each has its own cost. Lenders might charge 5 percent. Shareholders, who take more risk because they are paid only after the lenders, expect more, perhaps 10 percent. The company's overall cost of capital is the average of the two, weighted by how much of each it uses. It is called the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC.

There is one twist. In most countries, interest on debt can be deducted from taxable profit, so the government effectively pays part of it. With a 25 percent tax rate, a 5 percent loan really costs the company about 3.75 percent. So a company funded 60 percent by shareholders at 10 percent and 40 percent by loans at an after-tax 3.75 percent has a WACC of about 7.5 percent. Every project it considers must clear that bar.

Where does the shareholders' 10 percent come from? Nobody quotes it. Finance estimates it with models such as the capital asset pricing model, which says investors want a bigger reward for shares that swing more than the market as a whole. The details belong in Risk and return. The honest point is that the cost of equity is an estimate, and small changes in it can swing a decision.

The key difference is who is first in line. Lenders are paid first and have a fixed claim. Shareholders are paid last and have an open claim on everything left over. Debt is cheaper because it is safer for the lender, and riskier for the company for exactly the same reason.

Chapter 07

Leverage: how borrowing magnifies everything

Borrowing to fund a business is called leverage, because like a lever it multiplies force, in both directions. Take a business that costs 100 and normally earns 10 a year. (Taxes are ignored to keep it simple.)

How it is fundedGood year: earns 10Bad year: earns 2
All equity: owners put in 100Owners earn 10 on 100 = 10%Owners earn 2 on 100 = 2%
Leveraged: owners put in 40, borrow 60 at 5%10 minus 3 interest = 7 on 40 = 17.5%2 minus 3 interest = minus 1 on 40 = −2.5%

The business is identical in both rows. Only the funding changed, and the owners' results became far more extreme. In a good year, leverage nearly doubles their return. In a bad year, it turns a small profit into a loss, and if bad years continue the interest still has to be paid. This is the basic mechanism behind many corporate collapses, and behind financial crises. Before 2008 some investment banks were funded with roughly 30 euros of borrowing for every euro of their own capital. A loss of about 3 percent on their assets was enough to wipe out the owners. (More in Financial crises.)

Chapter 08

Does the mix even matter? The famous puzzle

In 1958 two economists, Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller, published a surprising result. In an idealised world, with no taxes, no cost of going bankrupt and perfect information, it does not matter how a company is funded. The value of a business depends on the cash its assets produce, not on how that cash is divided between lenders and owners. Cutting a pizza into more slices does not make more pizza. Both men later won the Nobel prize in economics, partly for this work.

The result is useful precisely because the real world breaks its assumptions. Each broken assumption tells you why funding does matter.

  • Taxes favour debt, because interest is tax-deductible. This pushes companies to borrow more.
  • Bankruptcy is expensive: lawyers, lost customers, staff who leave, forced sales at low prices. This pushes companies to borrow less.
  • Information is unequal. Managers know more than investors. When a company sells new shares, investors suspect managers think the shares are overpriced. So companies tend to prefer their own profits first, then debt, and new shares last.

The result is the trade-off view of funding: borrow enough to get the tax benefit, but not so much that the risk of financial distress outweighs it. Stable businesses such as utilities, with predictable income, can safely carry much more debt than a young technology company whose income could disappear.

Chapter 09

Giving cash back: dividends and buybacks

When a company produces more cash than it can invest at a return above its cost of capital, the textbook answer is to give it back. It can pay a dividend, a cash payment for each share. Or it can do a share buyback: buy its own shares on the market and cancel them, so each remaining shareholder owns a slightly larger slice.

In theory the two are close to equivalent. In practice they differ. Dividends are sticky: companies hate to cut them, because a cut signals trouble. Buybacks are flexible and can be stopped quietly. Taxes on the two often differ too. Since the early 1980s, when US rules made buybacks safer from legal challenge, they have become the main way large American companies return cash. Investors look closely at this decision, because a company that keeps cash it cannot use well tends to waste it, often on overpriced acquisitions, the subject of the next topic.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Survival, growth and the price of patience

Finance decides which companies survive a bad year, which ideas get funded, and how far ahead a business can afford to look.

Chapter 10

Cash flow as a competitive weapon

In the 1990s Dell sold computers directly to customers and built each one only after it was ordered and paid for. It paid its component suppliers weeks later. The result was a negative cash conversion cycle: customers' money arrived before Dell had to pay its own bills. Every extra sale brought in cash instead of consuming it. Its rivals, who built computers in advance and sold them through shops, had money tied up in stock for weeks.

The same principle explains why some large retailers and online marketplaces can grow so fast. Their suppliers in effect fund their growth. It is also why the furniture maker in Chapter 01 struggled: it was on the wrong side of the same arrangement. Who pays whom, and when, can matter as much as the price itself.

Chapter 11

When money is cheap, and when it isn't

From the 2008 crisis until 2022, interest rates in most rich countries were very low, sometimes below zero. Capital was cheap, so the bar in Chapter 04 was low. Projects with distant, uncertain payoffs, such as loss-making technology start-ups, looked attractive, because future profits were barely discounted. Companies also borrowed heavily, because debt cost so little.

When inflation returned and central banks raised rates sharply in 2022 and 2023, the arithmetic changed. The same distant profits were now worth much less today. Valuations of young growth companies fell steeply, and many firms had to refinance old cheap debt at higher rates. The cost of capital is not just a number in a spreadsheet. It sets how patient a whole economy can afford to be. The causes of these rate changes are covered in Interest rates.

Chapter 12

The short-termism argument

A long-running debate asks whether public companies, watched by investors every quarter, invest too little for the long term. Critics point to managers who cut research or maintenance to hit a quarterly profit target, and to large buyback programmes at times when shares were expensive. In 2023 the United States introduced a 1 percent tax on share buybacks, partly in response to this criticism.

Defenders answer that shareholders are not as short-sighted as claimed. They point out that markets have given very high valuations to companies, such as Amazon in its early years, that reported little profit while investing heavily. They also argue that returning cash to investors lets it flow to younger firms that need it. The evidence is genuinely mixed, and the answer probably varies by company and by how managers are paid.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful tools with hidden assumptions

The methods of corporate finance are logical and widely used. Their weakness is that they are only as good as the guesses fed into them.

Chapter 13

What the toolkit does well, and where it misleads

Tool or practiceStrengthWeakness
Net present valueA clear rule that compares any projects fairly, including the time value of moneyDepends entirely on forecasts. Optimistic managers can make any project look good
The cost of capitalForces every project to beat what investors could earn elsewhereThe cost of equity is an estimate. A small change swings the answer
Using debtCheap, tax-efficient, owners keep controlMagnifies losses and can force bankruptcy in a downturn
Issuing sharesNo fixed payments; safe in a bad yearExpensive, dilutes existing owners, often read as a bad sign
BuybacksFlexible way to return spare cashCan be done at high prices, or used to flatter earnings per share and executive bonuses

The biggest weakness is subtle. Discounting makes far-off benefits look small. A project whose benefits arrive in 25 years is worth very little today at a normal cost of capital. That is mathematically correct, but it can push companies away from patient investments in research, skills or climate resilience that matter a great deal. The spreadsheet does not lie, but it answers only the question you asked it.

Chapter 15

The part that will not be automated

Software can already build a financial model faster than any analyst. What it cannot do is supply the honest assumptions the model depends on. Will customers really pay this price? Will the competitor respond? How bad could the bad year be? Every major corporate failure in the history of finance involved numbers that looked fine and assumptions that were wrong.

So the future of corporate finance is likely to look like its past, with better tools. The core discipline remains: count cash rather than profit, respect the cost of money, and never borrow so much that one bad year can end the company. That discipline is simple to state and hard to keep, especially when times are good and everyone else is borrowing.

The core idea

Corporate finance makes three decisions: what to invest in, how to fund it, and what to return to owners. A project adds value only if it earns more than the cost of capital, measured with net present value. Debt is cheaper than equity but magnifies both gains and losses.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A typical large company's cost of capital is often around 7–10%, varying with rates and risk.
  • At about 30 to 1 leverage, a 3% fall in asset values wipes out the owners.
  • Modigliani and Miller's funding puzzle dates from 1958.
The common mistake

Confusing profit with cash. Profitable companies go bust when cash runs out, usually because customers pay later than bills fall due.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the same project can be worth doing at 8 percent and not worth doing at 12? If so, you understand discounting, the cost of capital and why rate rises cancel investments.
