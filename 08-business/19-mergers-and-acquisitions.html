Mergers & acquisitions. The fastest way to grow a company, and one of the most reliable ways to waste its money.

Building a new business takes years. Buying one takes months. That simple fact makes acquisitions irresistible to ambitious chief executives, and every year companies spend trillions of dollars buying each other. Some of these deals have transformed whole industries. Many others have destroyed huge amounts of value, usually for the shareholders of the company that did the buying. This file explains how deals work from first idea to final integration, who tends to win and lose, and why intelligent people keep paying too much.

Question 1 · What is it?

One company becoming part of another

An acquisition is a purchase. A merger is, in theory, a union of equals. In practice, almost every deal has a buyer and a bought, whatever the press release says.

Chapter 01

The deal of the century

In January 2000, at the peak of the internet boom, the online service AOL agreed to combine with the media group Time Warner, owner of CNN, Warner Bros. films and Time magazine. The deal valued Time Warner at well over 150 billion dollars, one of the largest in history. The logic sounded powerful: old media's films, news and cable networks would meet new media's millions of internet subscribers. Commentators called it the future of entertainment.

Within months the internet bubble burst. AOL's dial-up business started shrinking as broadband spread, and its advertising income collapsed. The two corporate cultures clashed badly. In 2002 the combined company reported a loss of around 99 billion dollars, mostly from writing down the value of what it had bought, then one of the largest annual losses in corporate history. The companies separated in 2009. A deal that looked like strategic genius on paper became the standard example of how acquisitions go wrong.

Chapter 02

The vocabulary of deals

In an acquisition, one company buys control of another, usually by buying a majority of its shares. The buyer is called the acquirer and the company bought is the target. In a merger, two companies combine into one, and shareholders of both receive shares in the new company. People often say "M&A" for the whole field. Deals are also sorted by how the two businesses relate.

TypeWhat it joinsTypical reasonExample of the kind
HorizontalTwo competitors in the same businessScale, cost savings, less competitionTwo supermarket chains combining
VerticalA company and its supplier or customerControl of supply, lower costs, securityA carmaker buying a battery maker
AdjacentRelated but different businessesSell more to the same customers, add skillsA software firm buying a security start-up
ConglomerateUnrelated businessesSpread risk, use spare cashAn industrial group buying a hotel chain

Conglomerate deals were fashionable in the 1960s, when companies such as ITT bought hotels, bakeries and insurers. They fell out of favour, because shareholders can spread their own risk by holding different shares. They do not need a company to do it for them, and certainly not to pay a premium for the privilege.

Chapter 03

Good reasons and bad reasons to buy

Reasons that can create value

  • Cost savings: closing duplicate offices, factories and systems.
  • Capabilities: buying a technology or skill that would take years to build.
  • Market access: entering a country or customer group quickly.
  • Better owner: the target is badly run, and the buyer can run it better.

Reasons that usually destroy it

  • Empire-building: bigger companies tend to pay their bosses more.
  • Hiding slow growth: buying revenue to disguise a stalled core business.
  • Fear of missing out: buying because rivals are buying.
  • Spare cash: money the company cannot think how else to use.

The bad reasons have something in common. They benefit the managers, or soothe their anxiety, more than they benefit the owners. Economists call this an agency problem: the people running a company (the agents) do not always act in the interests of the people who own it. Many acquisitions are driven by a mixture of both columns, which is why deals are so hard to judge from the outside.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From a quiet conversation to a combined company

A large deal passes through a long sequence of steps, involving bankers, lawyers, regulators and thousands of employees. The price is decided early. The value is decided much later, in the integration that most outsiders never see.

Due diligence is the buyer's investigation of what it is buying. Teams of accountants, lawyers and specialists go through the target's accounts, contracts, lawsuits, tax positions, technology and key staff. Its purpose is to find problems before the price is final. Its weakness is that it is easy to check what is written down and hard to check what matters most: whether customers will stay, whether key people will leave, and whether the two cultures can work together.

Chapter 05

The price: premium and synergies

If a target company's shares trade at 50 euros, a buyer cannot usually take control by offering 50. Shareholders already own something worth 50, so they need a reason to sell. Buyers typically offer a premium, often somewhere between 20 and 40 percent above the recent share price, although it varies widely. This is the price of control.

How can paying 30 percent more than market value make sense? Only through synergies: extra value that exists only when the two companies are combined. Cost synergies come from removing duplication, such as two head offices, two sets of systems or two sales forces visiting the same customers. Revenue synergies come from selling more, for example by offering the target's products to the buyer's customers.

The critical arithmetic

A deal creates value for the buyer only if the synergies are worth more than the premium paid, plus the costs of the deal and the integration. If the buyer pays away all the synergies in the premium, the target's shareholders capture the whole gain, and the buyer has done a great deal of work for nothing. Cost synergies are usually more reliable than revenue synergies, which often fail to appear.

Chapter 06

Paying with cash, shares or borrowed money

A buyer can pay in cash, from its own reserves or from new borrowing, or in its own shares, giving the target's shareholders part ownership of the combined company. The choice sends a signal. Paying cash says the buyer is confident: it keeps all the future gains for its own shareholders. Paying in shares shares the risk, and sometimes suggests the buyer thinks its own shares are expensive.

A special form is the leveraged buyout, or LBO. A private equity firm buys a company using a large amount of borrowed money, and the debt is loaded onto the company that was bought. The idea, using the leverage logic explained in Corporate finance, is that modest improvements in the business produce large returns on the small amount of equity. The most famous early example was the 1989 buyout of the food and tobacco group RJR Nabisco by the firm KKR, for about 25 billion dollars, told in the book Barbarians at the Gate. Supporters say LBOs force discipline on lazy managers. Critics say they load good companies with debt that leaves them fragile. Both have happened.

Chapter 07

Friendly and hostile deals

Most deals are friendly: the target's board agrees and recommends the offer to its shareholders. A hostile takeover happens when the buyer goes directly to the shareholders against the board's wishes, offering to buy their shares at a premium. In 2010 the American food company Kraft bought the British chocolate maker Cadbury after its first offer was rejected. The deal caused such a public reaction in Britain that the UK's takeover rules were tightened afterwards.

Boards have developed defences. The best known is the poison pill, invented by the American lawyer Martin Lipton in the early 1980s. If a hostile buyer passes a certain shareholding, other shareholders gain the right to buy new shares cheaply, making the takeover far more expensive. Supporters say such defences protect companies from short-term raiders. Critics say they protect managers from losing their jobs. Rules on what defences are allowed differ a lot between countries: the UK, for example, generally restricts boards from taking defensive action without shareholder approval, while US states often allow more.

Chapter 08

The regulators' veto

Large deals need permission from competition authorities, such as the US Federal Trade Commission and Department of Justice, the European Commission and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority. Their main question is whether the deal will reduce competition enough to harm customers through higher prices, lower quality or less innovation. (The legal background is in Competition law.)

Most deals pass. Some are changed, with the buyer forced to sell parts of the business. A few are blocked or abandoned. In 2019 the European Commission blocked the combination of the train makers Siemens and Alstom. In 2022 the chip designer Nvidia abandoned its planned 40-billion-dollar purchase of Arm after objections from regulators in several countries. Microsoft's purchase of the video game company Activision Blizzard, for about 69 billion dollars, was completed in 2023 only after a long fight and a restructured deal to satisfy the British regulator. Governments may also block foreign purchases on national security grounds, a power many countries have widened in recent years.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Deals reshape industries, and decide who wins

Acquisitions are how many industries consolidate, how big companies renew themselves, and how founders and investors get paid. They are also a test of whether managers are spending owners' money wisely.

Chapter 09

When buying works brilliantly

Between 2006 and 2012 Disney bought three companies: the animation studio Pixar for about 7.4 billion dollars, Marvel Entertainment for about 4 billion, and Lucasfilm, owner of Star Wars, for about 4 billion. Each brought characters and stories that Disney then used across films, theme parks, merchandise and later its streaming service. Most observers consider these some of the most successful acquisitions in media history.

Two features stand out. Disney bought creative capabilities it did not have, and it largely let the bought companies keep their own creative cultures. Pixar's leaders were put in charge of Disney's own animation studio, rather than the other way round. Another striking case is Facebook's purchase of Instagram in 2012 for about 1 billion dollars, when Instagram had only a few employees. It became one of the most valuable parts of the company. That deal also shows the other side: years later, US competition regulators challenged it in court, arguing that Facebook had bought a rising rival to remove a threat.

Chapter 10

Who actually gains?

Studies of share prices around the announcement of deals tell a fairly consistent story. The target's shareholders usually do well. They receive the premium. The buyer's shareholders, on average, gain little or nothing, and in many large deals they lose. Measured over longer periods, a large share of acquisitions fail to deliver the value promised. Figures of "70 to 90 percent fail" are often quoted, but they depend heavily on how failure is defined, and serious estimates vary widely. The safe summary is that the average deal is good for the seller and a coin toss, at best, for the buyer.

Averages hide an important pattern. Some research suggests that companies doing many small, related acquisitions, as a regular habit, tend to do better than companies making rare, transformational bets. Small deals are easier to integrate, mistakes are cheaper, and the company learns how to do it. The giant "deal of the century" is where the risk concentrates.

Chapter 11

The merger of equals that wasn't

In 1998 the German carmaker Daimler-Benz and the American carmaker Chrysler announced a "merger of equals". Within a few years it was clear that the German side was in charge, and many senior Chrysler managers had left. The companies differed in pay, in decision-making, in how formal they were and in how they thought about cars. The promised savings from sharing parts and technology came slowly. In 2007 Daimler sold most of Chrysler to a private equity firm for a small fraction of what it had paid.

The lesson is that integration is where value is made or lost, and culture is the hardest part of integration. Merging computer systems is difficult but well understood. Merging two groups of people who each think their way is right is not. The best acquirers decide before the deal closes who will run what, which systems will survive, and how they will keep the target's best people, because the best people are the ones with the most options to leave.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Speed and scale, bought at a premium

An acquisition buys time. The cost is a premium paid in advance for benefits that arrive later, if at all.

For society, the balance is also mixed. Deals can move assets to better owners, rescue failing companies and give founders and early investors a reward that encourages new start-ups. They can also reduce competition, raise prices and lead to large job losses in the merged company. Whether a particular deal is good for the wider economy depends far more on its details than on any general rule.

Chapter 13

Why smart buyers overpay

If buyers lose on average, why do they keep buying? Part of the answer is the winner's curse. When several bidders compete for the same company, the winner is the one who estimated its value most optimistically. On average, the most optimistic estimate is too high. Winning the auction is, in itself, evidence that you may have paid too much.

In 1986 the finance economist Richard Roll proposed the hubris hypothesis: chief executives who have been successful come to believe they can see value others miss, and overpay as a result. Later research found that deals by very confident chief executives were more likely to destroy value. Add the momentum of a deal process, where bankers are paid mainly if the deal happens and nobody wants to walk away after months of work, and the pressure all runs one way. Volume 07 explains the overconfidence behind this (Thinking, Fast and Slow).

The discipline that helps

Experienced buyers set a walk-away price before talks begin, write it down, and stick to it. They also ask an outsider to argue the case against the deal. The best deal is sometimes the one you did not do.

Chapter 15

Buying the people, not the company

A newer kind of deal has appeared in the AI industry. Instead of buying a start-up outright, a large company hires most of its leaders and researchers, and pays a licence fee for its technology. In 2024 Microsoft did this with the AI start-up Inflection, and Google made a similar arrangement with Character.AI. Supporters call these practical ways to bring scarce talent in quickly. Critics, including some regulators, argue they are acquisitions in all but name, structured to avoid the usual merger review. Competition authorities in the UK and the US looked into such arrangements.

This points to a broader trend. In industries where value lives in people and ideas, rather than factories, the old tools of M&A fit awkwardly. You can buy a company's shares, but you cannot buy its people's loyalty, and they can leave the week after the deal closes. That has always been true. It matters more as the most valuable companies own less and less that can be counted on a balance sheet.

The core idea

An acquisition creates value for the buyer only if the synergies exceed the premium paid plus all the costs. Targets' shareholders usually win. Buyers, on average, gain little. Value is decided by integration, and culture is its hardest part.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Buyers often pay a premium of about 20–40% over the share price.
  • AOL Time Warner reported a loss of around 99 billion dollars in 2002.
  • Disney paid about 15 billion dollars in total for Pixar, Marvel and Lucasfilm.
The common mistake

Judging a deal by its strategic story. Almost every deal has a good story. The question is whether the price leaves room for the buyer to gain anything.
