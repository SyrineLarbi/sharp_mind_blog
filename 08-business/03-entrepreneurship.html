Entrepreneurship. Acting on an opportunity before anyone can prove it is one.

The popular picture of an entrepreneur is a young genius in a garage who sees the future and bets everything on it. The reality is broader and more ordinary. Most entrepreneurs open a shop, a restaurant, a consultancy or a building firm. Most are not young. Most do not bet everything, and many of the successful ones are cautious. What they share is a willingness to organise people and money around an idea whose outcome nobody can know in advance. That is a rarer and more important skill than it sounds.

Question 1 · What is it?

Carrying uncertainty that others will not

Economists have argued for three centuries about what an entrepreneur actually contributes. Their answers still give the best definition.

Chapter 01

Three old answers to a simple question

In an essay written around the 1730s, the Irish-French economist Richard Cantillon described the entrepreneur as someone who buys at a known price today in order to sell at an unknown price later. A farmer pays rent and wages now without knowing what the harvest will fetch. That person carries the uncertainty so that others, such as the labourers, can be paid a fixed amount.

In 1921 the American economist Frank Knight sharpened the point. He separated risk, where you can calculate the odds, such as a dice roll or an insurance table, from uncertainty, where nobody can calculate them, such as whether people will want a product that does not exist yet. Risk can be insured. Uncertainty cannot. For Knight, profit is the reward for bearing uncertainty.

Then the Austrian-American economist Joseph Schumpeter added the dramatic version. Entrepreneurs, he argued, are the people who introduce new combinations: new products, new methods, new markets. In doing so they make old businesses obsolete. In his 1942 book Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy he called this creative destruction. Put the three together and you get a working definition: an entrepreneur organises resources around an uncertain opportunity and accepts the consequences if it fails.

Chapter 02

Not one kind of founder, but several

The word covers very different activities. Mixing them up leads to bad advice, because what works for one kind can ruin another.

Researchers in the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor, a long-running international survey, distinguish people who start a business to pursue an opportunity from people who do so because they have no better option. In many lower-income countries, a large share of adults run their own small business, mostly out of necessity. A high rate of self-employment is not the same as a dynamic economy. What matters for growth is the small minority of new firms that become larger employers.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From an idea to a business that pays its way

Starting a business is less about a single brilliant idea than about a series of small decisions under uncertainty: what to try, how much to risk, where the money comes from, and when to change course.

Chapter 03

Where opportunities come from

Good opportunities rarely arrive as a flash of inspiration. They usually come from noticing a change that others have not yet acted on. In his 1985 book Innovation and Entrepreneurship, the management writer Peter Drucker listed sources such as unexpected successes or failures, gaps between how things are and how they should be, changes in population, shifts in how people see things, and new knowledge.

Concrete examples help. An ageing population creates demand for home care and accessible housing. A new regulation forces thousands of companies to file data in a new way, and someone builds the tool. A cheaper technology, such as smartphones with cameras and GPS, makes a service possible that was too expensive before. Most founders start from a problem they know well, often from their own work, not from a blank page. Knowing an industry's frustrations from the inside is a real advantage over a clever outsider.

Chapter 04

How expert founders actually think

Business schools long taught entrepreneurship like a planning exercise: set the goal, research the market, write a detailed plan, raise money, then execute. In the late 1990s the researcher Saras Sarasvathy studied experienced founders and found that many of them did almost the opposite. She called their logic effectuation.

Planning logic (causation)

Start with a goal and work out the means to reach it. Predict the market, then build for that prediction. Works well when the future is reasonably knowable, such as opening another branch of a proven chain.

Founder logic (effectuation)

Start with what you have: who you are, what you know, whom you know. Risk only what you can afford to lose. Bring in partners who commit early, and treat surprises as material, not failures. Works when the future is unknowable.

The key idea is affordable loss. Instead of asking "how much could I win?", which nobody can answer for a new idea, ask "how much am I willing to lose to find out?" That keeps each experiment small enough to survive if it fails. Good founders are not reckless gamblers. They are skilled at making many small, survivable bets.

Chapter 05

Testing before building

The biggest waste in a new business is building something nobody wants. So experienced founders look for evidence cheaply and early. They talk to potential customers about their problems before describing a solution. They take pre-orders, run a simple website to see whether anyone signs up, or deliver the service by hand to a few customers before automating it.

The aim is to test the riskiest assumption first. For a new restaurant it may be whether people in that street will pay that price. For a software tool it may be whether companies will switch from the spreadsheet they already use. Customers' polite enthusiasm is weak evidence. Their money, time or effort is strong evidence. This approach was popularised for startups by Eric Ries in The Lean Startup (2011), and it is covered in Product-market fit.

Chapter 06

Where the first money comes from

Most new businesses are funded mainly by the founder's own savings, sometimes with help from family and friends, and often with a bank loan or a credit card. This is called bootstrapping when the business grows from its own revenue. Only a tiny share of new firms ever raise money from professional investors.

A loan must be repaid but leaves you owning the whole business. Equity, selling a share of ownership, need not be repaid but gives away part of every future profit and some control. The choice shapes the whole venture, which is why Startups treats venture capital separately.

Chapter 07

How often new businesses survive

The most quoted myth is that "nine out of ten new businesses fail in the first year". The real figures are less dramatic but still sobering. United States government data on new business establishments have shown for decades that roughly one in five closes within the first year, and about half within five years. Figures in Europe are broadly similar, though definitions vary.

Closing is not always failing. Some owners sell, retire or move on to a better opportunity. Survival also differs by sector: restaurants and retail have harder odds than, for example, healthcare services. The useful conclusion is not "don't start". It is "start in a way you can survive losing". That is effectuation's affordable loss again.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

New firms create jobs, competition and progress

Entrepreneurship is how economies try new ideas and retire old ones. Its effects reach far beyond the founders themselves.

Chapter 08

It is young firms, not small firms, that create jobs

Politicians often say small businesses create most jobs. Research by the economist John Haltiwanger and colleagues, using detailed US data, refined that claim in an important way. Once you account for age, size matters much less. It is young firms, a few years old, that create a disproportionate share of new jobs. Most of them stay small or close. A small number grow very fast, and those "high-growth" firms account for much of the net job creation.

This has practical consequences. Policies that help every small business equally, regardless of age or ambition, spend a great deal on firms that will never grow. Policies that make it easy to start, grow, and, when necessary, close a business cleanly tend to support the dynamism that matters. Countries differ widely here, for example in how harshly bankruptcy laws treat failed founders.

Chapter 09

Creative destruction in practice

Schumpeter's idea is visible in any long list of companies. Many of the largest companies of the 1950s have shrunk, merged or vanished, and many of today's largest did not exist forty years ago. New entrants brought new products and methods. Incumbents, the established firms, either adapted or declined.

This churn is uncomfortable. Workers lose jobs in declining firms and towns lose employers. But economists broadly agree that a large part of long-run productivity growth comes from resources moving from weaker firms to stronger ones, including new ones. An economy where no business ever dies is usually an economy where few good new ones are born. The pattern is explored further in Business disruption and Economics · Productivity.

Chapter 10

The myth of the twenty-year-old founder

Famous examples such as Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg, who started their companies at around 20, created the idea that great founders are very young. In 2018 the economists Pierre Azoulay, Benjamin Jones, J. Daniel Kim and Javier Miranda looked at millions of US business founders using administrative data. They found the average age of founders of the fastest-growing new firms was about 45. A 50-year-old founder was much more likely to build a top-performing company than a 30-year-old.

The likely reasons are experience, networks, industry knowledge and savings. The lesson is not that young founders cannot win. It is that the famous cases are memorable precisely because they are unusual, which is survivorship bias in action (see The ones you cannot see).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Freedom and upside, paid for in risk and stress

For the individual, entrepreneurship is a trade. For society, it is mostly a gain, with some real costs.

Chapter 12

Optimism: the fuel and the danger

Surveys of new founders consistently find that most believe their own chances of success are much higher than the average for businesses like theirs. Psychologists call this overconfidence, and Daniel Kahneman described entrepreneurs as a group especially prone to it. Without it, perhaps few people would start at all, and society would lose the occasional great success.

The cost is borne by individuals. Overconfident founders put in more money than they can afford, ignore early warning signs and persist too long. The healthy version is optimism about the destination combined with realism about each step: set a limit on what you will risk, decide in advance what evidence would make you stop, and stick to it. The behavioural side of these mistakes is in Psychology · Decision-making.

Chapter 14

When starting is easy, what becomes scarce?

If anyone can launch a website, a shop or an app in a weekend, launching stops being an advantage. The scarce things become the ones that cannot be downloaded: deep knowledge of a customer's problem, trust, distribution (a reliable way to reach buyers), and the persistence to keep improving after the first excitement fades.

That points back to the older definition. Entrepreneurship was never really about having the tools. It was about carrying uncertainty that others would not, and organising people around a bet that might fail. Tools change what it costs to place the bet. They do not change who is willing to place it, or who learns fastest from the result. The next file follows the most extreme version of that bet: the company designed from day one to grow very fast.

The core idea

An entrepreneur organises resources around an uncertain opportunity and accepts the consequences. Good founders make many small, survivable bets, test the riskiest assumption first, and treat customers' money, not their compliments, as evidence. For economies, young high-growth firms drive a large share of job creation.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Roughly 1 in 5 new US businesses closes within a year, and about half within five years.
  • The average founder of the fastest-growing US startups was about 45 (Azoulay and colleagues, 2018).
  • Schumpeter named creative destruction in 1942.
The common mistake

Thinking entrepreneurship means a brilliant idea plus a huge gamble. Most successful founders limit what they risk and change course as evidence arrives.
