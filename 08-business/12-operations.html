Operations. The unglamorous machine that decides whether a promise is kept.

Strategy decides what a company sells and marketing persuades people to want it. Operations is everything that happens next: making it, checking it, moving it, serving it, on time and at a cost that leaves a profit. It rarely makes headlines until it fails. Yet some of the biggest economic changes of the last 250 years, from the pin factory to the Toyota production line, were operations ideas. They are also why a queue at a supermarket can go from short to hopeless in a few minutes.

Question 1 · What is it?

Turning inputs into something a customer will pay for

Operations management is the design and running of the processes that produce a company's goods or services. Every organisation has it, from a car plant to a hospital to a café.

Chapter 01

The pin factory

In 1776 the Scottish philosopher Adam Smith opened The Wealth of Nations with a small workshop that made pins. One worker alone, he wrote, could perhaps make twenty pins a day, and possibly not even one. But in a workshop where the job was split into about eighteen steps, one person drawing the wire, another straightening it, another cutting, another sharpening, ten workers made around 48,000 pins a day.

Smith gave three reasons. Each worker grew skilled at one step. Nobody wasted time switching between tasks. And simple, repeated steps invited the invention of machines to do them. How work is divided and arranged can matter more than how hard people work. That is the founding idea of operations, and it underlies the economy-wide story told in Productivity.

Chapter 02

What operations covers

Every operation takes inputs (materials, information, customers, staff, machines), transforms them, and produces outputs. A factory transforms steel into cars. A hospital transforms sick patients into healthier ones. A bank transforms an application into a loan. The textbook vocabulary describes operations along four dimensions, often called the four Vs.

DimensionHigh endLow endWhy it matters
VolumeA burger chain serving millions a dayA small restaurantHigh volume allows standard steps and machines, and lowers cost per unit

Chapter 03

What "good" means: five objectives that pull against each other

An operation can be judged on five things. Cost: how cheaply it works. Quality: whether the output is right. Speed: how long the customer waits. Dependability: whether promises about time are kept. Flexibility: how easily it adapts to different requests or volumes.

The difficulty is that no operation can be best at all five at once. A clinic with spare doctors waiting is fast and flexible but expensive. A factory running every machine at full capacity is cheap but slow to respond. Part of a company's strategy is choosing which objectives to win on, and part of operations is accepting that the others will be only good enough. The rest of this file is largely about how clever designs, especially Toyota's, managed to soften some of these trade-offs.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Lines, waste, bottlenecks, queues and stock

A century of ideas, each one fixing the blind spot of the last. Together they make up most of what operations managers know.

Chapter 04

The stopwatch and the moving line

Around 1900 the American engineer Frederick Winslow Taylor timed workers with a stopwatch, broke each job into motions and redesigned them to remove wasted effort. His 1911 book, The Principles of Scientific Management, argued that managers should plan the work and workers should carry it out exactly. Output rose sharply. So did workers' resentment of being treated as parts of a machine.

In 1913 Henry Ford's factory at Highland Park, Michigan, added the moving assembly line. Instead of workers walking to the car, the car moved past the workers. The time to assemble a Model T chassis fell from over twelve hours to about an hour and a half. Cars that had been luxuries became affordable for ordinary families. The work was so monotonous, however, that staff turnover was huge, and in 1914 Ford roughly doubled pay to five dollars a day to keep people. Mass production solved the problem of cost and created the problem of people.

Chapter 05

The Toyota way: just in time, and stopping the line

After the Second World War, Toyota could not copy Ford. Japan's car market was small and varied, and money for big stocks of parts was scarce. Under the engineer Taiichi Ohno, the company built a different system over several decades. Two ideas sit at its centre.

The first is just-in-time: make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. Parts arrive at the line shortly before they are used. Each step "pulls" parts from the one before, often signalled by a card called a kanban, instead of pushing out as much as possible. The second is jidoka, often translated as automation with a human touch. It traces back to a loom invented by the company's founder, Sakichi Toyoda, that stopped by itself when a thread broke. On a Toyota line, any worker who sees a problem can pull a cord, the andon, to stop the line until it is fixed.

Stopping a whole line for one fault sounds expensive. That is the point. Low stock and a stopped line make problems impossible to ignore, so they get fixed at the root instead of being hidden by spare parts. Ohno listed the forms of waste the system attacks.

The seven wastes, as Toyota taught them
 overproduction   making more, or sooner, than anyone needs
 waiting          people or parts standing idle
 transport        moving things more than necessary
 over-processing  doing more work than the customer values
 inventory        stock that ties up money and hides problems
 motion           people reaching, walking, searching
 defects          anything that must be fixed or thrown away

In the West this approach became known as lean, a label popularised by the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World, based on a large MIT study of car factories around the world.

Chapter 06

Quality: build it in, don't inspect it in

The old way to ensure quality was to inspect finished goods and throw out the bad ones. The American statistician W. Edwards Deming argued this was wasteful. Most defects, he said, come from the system, not from careless workers, so the system must be improved. He lectured to Japanese managers from 1950, and Japan's main quality award is still named after him.

Deming's tools were statistical. Every process varies a little. The job is to tell normal variation from a real problem and to reduce variation over time. In the 1980s the American company Motorola turned this into Six Sigma, a programme with a target of no more than about 3.4 defects per million opportunities. Training belts, project methods and dashboards followed. The lasting principle is simpler than the jargon. Quality is cheaper when it is designed into the process than when it is checked at the end.

Chapter 07

Bottlenecks: the slowest step sets the pace

Picture a kitchen. The chef can prepare twenty dishes an hour, the oven can cook fifteen, and the waiters can serve thirty. The restaurant serves fifteen, however hard the chef and waiters work. A system can go no faster than its slowest step, its bottleneck.

The Israeli physicist Eliyahu Goldratt made this idea famous in The Goal, a 1984 business novel about a struggling factory manager. His theory of constraints says: find the bottleneck, make sure it never stands idle, organise everything else around it, then expand it, and look for the next one. Its most counter-intuitive lesson is that improving a step that is not the bottleneck achieves nothing except a bigger pile of half-finished work in front of the step that is.

Chapter 08

Queues: why "nearly full" means "very slow"

Every operation that serves customers has queues: tills, call centres, emergency departments, computer servers. Queues behave in a way that surprises almost everyone. When arrivals are random, the waiting time does not grow steadily as a server gets busier. It grows slowly at first and then explodes.

The lesson is hard for cost-focused managers. A team or a machine kept busy close to 100% of the time will produce long, unpredictable waits, because there is no slack to absorb random bursts. A related rule, proved by John Little in 1961 and known as Little's Law, links three numbers in any stable system: the average number of items inside equals the rate at which they arrive times the average time each spends inside. If a clinic sees 10 patients an hour and each stays 2 hours, there are 20 patients in the building on average. To cut the crowd, cut the time.

Chapter 09

Inventory and the bullwhip

Inventory is stock: raw materials, half-finished goods and finished products. It protects against surprises, but it costs money to hold, takes space, and can go out of date. The art is holding enough, in the right places.

When companies in a chain each order on the basis of the orders they receive, small changes in what customers buy grow into large swings further back. A shop sees sales rise a little and orders a bit extra to be safe. The wholesaler sees a jump in orders and orders more again. The factory sees a boom. Then everyone has too much and cuts orders sharply. This is the bullwhip effect, studied by Jay Forrester at MIT in the 1950s and 1960s. Procter & Gamble famously found it in nappies: babies used them at a steady rate, yet orders from retailers swung wildly. Sharing real sales data along the chain is the main cure, because it replaces guesses about guesses with the actual demand.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Same people, different system

Operations explains why some companies win for decades on cost and quality, why services feel fast or slow, and why the world ran out of things in 2021.

Chapter 10

The factory that changed its system, not its workers

General Motors' car plant in Fremont, California, had a dreadful reputation by the early 1980s: poor quality, high absenteeism, bitter relations between managers and the union. GM closed it in 1982. In 1984 it reopened as NUMMI, a joint venture between GM and Toyota, run on Toyota's system. Most of the workers were rehired from the old plant, with the same union.

Within a few years the plant's quality was among the best in GM's whole network. The people had not changed. The system had: teams, training, the andon cord, managers who responded when it was pulled. Many problems blamed on workers turn out to be problems with the design of the work. GM struggled for years to copy the lessons in its other plants, which shows that the hard part of lean is the culture and management habits, not the tools. NUMMI closed in 2010, and the factory was later taken over by Tesla.

Chapter 11

Services are operations too

Operations is not only factories. In services, the customer is often inside the process, which makes everything visible and variable. Fast-food chains run kitchens like small assembly lines, with standard steps and timings. The American airline Southwest built its low costs partly on turning planes around at the gate much faster than rivals, so that each aircraft flew more trips a day and earned more.

Hospitals have adopted lean ideas to reduce waiting and errors, mapping each patient's path and removing steps that add delay but no care. The results are mixed but often real. In services, the waiting is part of the product, and the queue rules from Chapter 08 apply directly. An emergency department running at near full capacity will have long waits, however hard its staff work.

Chapter 12

When lean became fragile

Just-in-time works brilliantly when supply is reliable. In 2020 and 2021 it was not. When the COVID-19 pandemic began, many carmakers cancelled orders for computer chips, expecting sales to collapse. Demand returned quickly, but chipmakers had given the capacity to makers of laptops and phones. Carmakers around the world lost production of millions of vehicles. In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for six days, delaying hundreds of ships, a story told in Volume 12.

Toyota itself was reported to have coped better than many rivals, partly because after the 2011 earthquake in Japan it had begun keeping larger stocks of critical parts. That is the real lesson. Lean was never meant to mean zero stock of everything. It meant no stock that does not earn its place. Many companies had cut buffers to save money without asking which ones were protecting them. Since then "just in case" has joined "just in time" in the vocabulary.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Efficiency against resilience, control against people

Every operations system buys something with something else. The main trade-offs have stayed remarkably stable for a century.

Chapter 13

Weighing the approaches

ApproachAdvantagesDisadvantages
Division of labour and standard workHuge gains in output and cost. Easy to train new staff.Repetitive, deskilled jobs. Workers disengage.
Mass productionVery low cost per unit at high volume.Inflexible. Large stocks. Poor at variety.
Lean and just-in-timeLess waste and stock. Problems surface and get fixed. Higher quality.Vulnerable to supply shocks. Demands discipline and trust that many firms lack.
Six SigmaFewer defects. Decisions based on data.Can become bureaucratic, with certificates valued over results.
Running at full capacityLowest cost per unit while demand is steady.Long, unpredictable waits. No room for surprises.
Holding buffers ("just in case")Resilience in a crisis. Reliable delivery.Money tied up. Waste if the crisis never comes.

The deepest trade-off is between efficiency and resilience. Slack looks like waste on a normal day and like wisdom on a bad one. Managers judged on this year's costs are pulled towards too little of it, which is why regulators now push some industries, such as banking and pharmaceuticals, to hold more.

Chapter 15

What stays the same

New tools change the details, but the underlying rules in this file are not technological. Queues will still explode near full capacity. The slowest step will still set the pace. A chain that shares guesses instead of data will still whip from shortage to glut. And measuring the wrong thing will still make people optimise the wrong thing.

The human side looks just as durable. Toyota's real advantage was never the kanban card. It was a culture in which workers were expected to spot problems and managers were expected to listen. The best operations treat every employee as a problem-solver, not a pair of hands, and that idea leads directly to the next topic, Human resources.

The core idea

Operations turns inputs into outputs while trading off cost, quality, speed, dependability and flexibility. The big ideas are division of labour, the moving line, Toyota's just-in-time and stopping to fix problems, bottlenecks, and the rule that queues explode near full capacity. Efficiency and resilience pull against each other.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Smith's ten pin-makers produced about 48,000 pins a day (1776).
  • Ford's moving line cut Model T chassis assembly from over 12 hours to about 90 minutes (1913).
  • In a simple random queue, going from 80% to 95% busy multiplies the average wait by about five.
The common mistake

Trying to keep every person and machine 100% busy. Full utilisation is not efficiency for the system. It produces queues, delays and piles of half-finished work.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the NUMMI plant improved with mostly the same workers? If you can, you understand why operations focuses on the system rather than on blame.

The words, in plain words
Operations management
Designing and running the processes that make and deliver a company's goods or services.
Division of labour
Splitting work into separate steps done by different people.
Assembly line
A production method in which the product moves past fixed workstations.
Just-in-time
Producing and delivering only what is needed, when it is needed.
Kanban
A card or signal telling the previous step to supply more parts.
Andon
A cord or button any worker can use to stop the line when there is a problem.
Lean
The Western name for Toyota-style production, focused on removing waste.
Six Sigma
A data-driven programme to reduce defects and variation.
Bottleneck
The slowest step, which limits the output of the whole system.
Utilisation
The share of time a person or machine is busy.
Little's Law
Items in a system equal the arrival rate times the average time each spends inside.
Inventory
Stock of materials, parts or finished goods.
Bullwhip effect
Small changes in customer demand growing into large swings in orders further back in a chain.
