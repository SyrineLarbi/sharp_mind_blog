Sharp Mind · Business · Topic 15 of 20

Leadership. Getting people to go somewhere hard, willingly, and to keep going when it gets worse.

Most people picture a leader as a person: a confident figure at the front of the room, a famous founder, a general. Researchers who study the subject picture something else. They see a relationship. Leadership exists only when other people choose to follow, and they make that choice again every day. This file explains what makes them choose it: direction, trust, the right use of power, and a feel for what a situation needs. It also explains why we give leaders too much credit when things go well, and too much blame when they go badly.

Question 1 · What is it?

A relationship, not a job title

You can be appointed a manager. You cannot be appointed a leader. Leadership is what happens when people follow you further than your formal authority could push them.

Chapter 01

Twenty-eight men on the ice

In 1915 the ship Endurance was trapped and then crushed by sea ice in the Weddell Sea, off Antarctica. Its 28 men, led by the explorer Ernest Shackleton, were stranded on drifting ice with no radio contact and no hope of rescue. Their original goal, to cross Antarctica on foot, was gone. Shackleton replaced it within hours with a new one: everyone gets home alive.

Over the next year and a half he kept routines going, so that days had a shape. He kept the most pessimistic men close to him, in his own tent, so their gloom could not spread. He gave up his own gloves and rations at moments when the men could see it. When the ice broke up, they sailed small open boats to a bare island. From there Shackleton and five others crossed some 1,300 kilometres of the Southern Ocean in a lifeboat to reach South Georgia, then crossed its mountains on foot to a whaling station. Every one of the 28 men survived.

The expedition failed at what it set out to do. It is still taught as one of the great cases of leadership, because it shows the core of the subject with nothing else in the way. There was no salary to pay, no promotion to offer and no law to enforce. There was only a direction that people believed in and a person they trusted to get them there.

Chapter 02

Leading, managing and being in charge

Three ideas get mixed up, and separating them clears up most confusion.

IdeaWhat it meansWhere it comes fromExample
AuthorityThe formal right to give instructionsThe organisation chart, a contract, the lawA shift supervisor can assign who works on Saturday
ManagementMaking a complex system run reliably: plans, budgets, staffing, controlSkill and processGetting 400 orders shipped correctly every day
LeadershipGetting people to commit to a direction, especially a changeOther people's willingnessPersuading a team to abandon a product they love for a better one

The Harvard professor John Kotter put the difference neatly in a 1990 article: management copes with complexity, leadership copes with change. A large company without management falls into chaos. A large company without leadership runs smoothly in the wrong direction. Most real jobs need both, and the same person usually switches between them many times a day.

Another useful split comes from Ronald Heifetz, also at Harvard. He separates technical problems, which experts can solve with known methods, from adaptive problems, which need the people involved to change their own habits, values or loyalties. Fixing a broken server is technical. Getting two merged teams to stop competing is adaptive. A common failure is to treat an adaptive problem as a technical one: to issue a new procedure when what is needed is for people to change how they think.

Chapter 03

A century of theories, each correcting the last

Ideas about leadership have moved in a clear sequence. Each one fixed a weakness in the one before. None of them is completely wrong, and none is the whole story.

ApproachMain claimWhat it got rightIts weakness
"Great man" and traits (19th to mid-20th century)Leaders are born with special qualitiesSome traits, such as intelligence, confidence and drive, do correlate modestly with emerging as a leaderThe correlations are weak, and the same traits fail in different settings
Behaviour (1940s–60s)What leaders do matters more than who they areBehaviour can be learned and measuredNo single behaviour works everywhere
Situational (1960s–70s)The best style depends on the people and the taskExplains why a great wartime leader can fail in peacetimeHard to apply in the moment
Transformational (from 1978)Great leaders change what followers want, not just what they doCaptures inspiration and meaningCan slide into hero worship
Servant and shared (1970 onward)The leader serves the team, and leadership can be spread across many peopleFits knowledge work and expert teamsCan blur who is accountable

The word transformational comes from the historian James MacGregor Burns, who in 1978 contrasted it with transactional leadership. A transactional leader trades: do this work and you get this reward. A transformational leader raises people's sense of purpose so that they want the goal for its own sake. Most effective leaders do both. The pay cheque is the floor. The purpose is what produces the extra effort that no contract can specify.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Direction, trust and power, adjusted to the moment

Strip away the slogans and leaders do a small number of things. They set a direction, they earn enough trust that people follow it, they use power carefully, and they read what each situation needs.

Chapter 04

The three jobs: direction, alignment, commitment

One practical way to judge whether leadership is happening in a group is to ask three questions. Do people agree on where they are going? Is their work coordinated, so that the pieces fit? Are they committed, putting in effort beyond the minimum? If the answer to all three is yes, leadership is working, whoever is doing it. If not, something is missing, whatever the titles say.

Direction sounds simple and is not. "Be the best in our market" is not a direction, because it does not tell anyone what to do on Monday. A real direction is specific enough to rule things out. In Shackleton's case, "everyone survives" told the men to drop scientific work, to carry food rather than equipment, and to keep the group together at almost any cost. A good direction is one that makes trade-offs easier, not one that sounds inspiring.

Alignment is mostly communication, repeated far more than feels natural. Leaders consistently overestimate how well their message has landed. They have thought about it for months. Their teams heard it once, in a meeting, between other things. A common rule among experienced executives is that by the time you are sick of saying something, people are just beginning to hear it.

Chapter 05

Two dimensions that keep coming back

In the late 1940s and 1950s, researchers at Ohio State University asked thousands of employees to describe their bosses. Out of many questions, two broad dimensions kept appearing. They called them initiating structure, which means organising the work, setting goals and deadlines, and consideration, which means showing respect, listening and caring about people's wellbeing. Researchers at the University of Michigan found something similar at about the same time.

Decades of later research have kept those two axes, under many names: task and people, results and relationships, challenge and support. The lesson is that they are separate, not two ends of one scale. A leader can be high on both, low on both, or high on one only.

Kim Scott's idea of "radical candor", summarised in Volume 08, is a modern version of the top right box: care personally and challenge directly at the same time. Most bad bosses fail on one axis, not both. The "pleasant drift" boss is liked and ineffective. The "driver" gets results this quarter and loses the best people by next year.

Chapter 06

Trust, and the team that reported more mistakes

In the 1990s the Harvard researcher Amy Edmondson studied hospital nursing teams. She expected the best-led teams to make fewer medication errors. The data seemed to show the opposite: the better-led teams reported more errors. The explanation was that they were not making more mistakes. They were willing to admit them. In teams with harsh leaders, errors were hidden, so nobody learned from them.

Edmondson called the missing ingredient psychological safety: a shared belief in a team that you will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with a question, a concern or a mistake. Later, Google studied about 180 of its own teams in a project it called Aristotle. Of all the factors it looked at, psychological safety was the one most strongly linked to team effectiveness.

Psychological safety is not niceness, and it is not low standards. It means the team can say "I think this is wrong" without fear. Leaders build it in small, visible ways: by asking questions they do not know the answer to, by admitting their own errors first, and by thanking the person who brings bad news instead of punishing them. A leader who shoots the messenger soon has no messengers, and then flies blind.

Chapter 07

Where a leader's power comes from

In 1959 the social psychologists John French and Bertram Raven described five bases of power, the reasons people do what someone else wants. The list has held up well.

Base of powerWhy people complyHow long it lasts
Legitimate"You have the title, so you have the right to ask"As long as the title, and only for what the title covers
Reward"You can give me something I want"As long as the rewards keep coming
Coercive"You can punish me"Compliance without commitment, and resentment
Expert"You know what you are doing"Durable, and it works across the organisation chart
Referent"I respect you and want to be like you"The most durable, and the basis of real leadership

The first three come with the job. The last two have to be earned. This is why a new manager who leans only on the title often struggles, and why a respected engineer with no title can steer a whole department. Formal power gets compliance. Earned power gets commitment. Volume 36 shows the dark side of legitimate power in Milgram's obedience experiments: people will do a great deal simply because someone in authority tells them to.

Chapter 08

Reading what the moment needs

The situational school's lasting insight is that there is no single best style. In 2000 the psychologist Daniel Goleman published a widely read article in the Harvard Business Review describing six styles. Three are worth knowing by name.

Directive

"Do what I tell you." Right in a genuine crisis, such as a fire, a cash emergency or a safety failure, when there is no time to discuss. Damaging as a default, because people stop thinking.

Coaching

"Try this, and let's look at what happened." Right for developing capable people who want to grow. Slow, and useless with people who do not want to learn.

Democratic

"What do you all think?" Right when the team knows more than the leader, and when buy-in matters. Frustrating when a decision is urgent and meetings drag on.

The same idea appears in the "situational leadership" model of Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard, which matches style to how competent and committed a person is at a particular task. A new hire needs clear instructions. An experienced expert needs to be left alone with a goal. Treating the expert like the new hire feels like an insult. Treating the new hire like the expert feels like abandonment. The skill is not having a style. It is switching style on purpose.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

People leave managers, cultures follow leaders

Leadership is the single biggest influence on how most people experience work. It also shapes culture, which outlasts any strategy. But its effect on company results is smaller, and harder to measure, than the business press suggests.

Chapter 09

The boss is the job

For most employees, "the company" is mostly one person: their direct manager. That person decides their tasks, their workload, their feedback and often their pay rise. The polling company Gallup has estimated that managers account for about 70 percent of the variation in how engaged teams are at work. The old saying is that people join companies and leave managers. It is a simplification, but the data points in that direction.

Google once tried to prove the opposite. Around 2008, a team of its engineers suspected that managers added little value in a company of skilled engineers. The internal research project, called Oxygen, found the reverse. Teams with better managers had higher satisfaction and lower turnover. The behaviours of the best managers were not mysterious. The first one on Google's list was being a good coach. Others included not micromanaging, showing interest in people's success, communicating clearly and having a clear vision.

Why this matters to you

If you manage anyone, your behaviour is a large part of their working life, whether you notice it or not. The small things carry the most weight: whether you are available, whether you notice good work, and how you react to bad news. The wider system of hiring, pay and reviews is covered in Human resources.

Chapter 10

Culture: what happens when the leader is not in the room

A company's culture is the set of unwritten rules about how things are really done: what gets rewarded, what gets punished, what people are afraid to say. Leaders shape it less by what they announce than by what they do, especially under pressure. Staff watch who gets promoted, which mistakes are forgiven and what happens to the person who disagrees with the boss.

Two well-known examples show this. At Toyota, any production worker can pull a cord, the andon cord, to stop the assembly line when they see a problem. Supervisors come to help, not to blame. That single rule tells every worker that quality matters more than output and that their judgment counts. It is leadership built into a system.

The second is Microsoft. When Satya Nadella became chief executive in 2014, the company was widely described as internally competitive and slow. Nadella made culture a central theme, drawing openly on the psychologist Carol Dweck's idea of a growth mindset, the belief that abilities can be developed (see Mindset in Volume 09). He talked about moving from "know-it-alls" to "learn-it-alls". Microsoft's later success also owed a great deal to cloud computing and a well-timed bet on AI, so it is hard to say how much was culture. But insiders and outsiders broadly agree the culture did change.

Chapter 11

Do chief executives really matter that much?

This is a genuinely contested question. On one side, there are clear cases where a change at the top changed a company's direction: Steve Jobs's return to Apple in 1997 is the usual example. On the other side, much of a company's performance is driven by its industry, the economy and luck. Studies that try to measure the "CEO effect" reach different answers, depending on the method, from small to substantial.

What researchers do agree on is a bias in how we see it. In 1985 the organisational researcher James Meindl and colleagues described the romance of leadership: people tend to explain an organisation's success or failure by its leader, more than the evidence supports. When a company does well, the chief executive appears on magazine covers. When the market turns, the same person is a fool. The real cause, often, was the market both times.

Leaders matter most in moments of real choice: a crisis, a big bet, a change of direction, a culture that has gone bad. They matter less in the long stretches when the business largely runs on its existing position. Volume 39 explains why results drift back towards average (Why extremes do not last), which is one reason a new chief executive hired after a terrible year often looks brilliant in the next.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every leadership strength has a shadow

The qualities that make people follow you are the same ones that can make them stop thinking. Strong leadership is valuable and dangerous for the same reasons.

QualityWhen it helpsWhen it hurts
CharismaEnergises people, attracts talent, carries a team through hard timesPeople follow the person instead of the evidence. The organisation collapses when the person leaves
DecisivenessEnds paralysis, essential in a crisisFast, confident decisions on things the leader does not understand
Strong visionGives direction and makes trade-offs easierIgnores warning signs that the vision is wrong

The pattern is that a strength used everywhere becomes a weakness. The answer is rarely to drop the strength. It is to build checks around it: a deputy who is expected to disagree, a board that asks hard questions, a habit of asking "what would make us wrong?"

Chapter 13

Hubris, and the books that aged badly

The most serious risk is the leader whose success has gone to their head. Psychologists use the word hubris, from ancient Greek drama, for excessive pride that leads to a fall. People who score high on narcissism are often chosen as leaders, because they seem confident and bold. They can also take reckless risks and punish dissent. Volume 37 describes this group of traits (Three traits that travel together), and Volume 09 summarises Ego Is the Enemy.

There is a quieter trap in how we learn about leadership. In his 2001 bestseller Good to Great, Jim Collins described "Level 5" leaders who combined personal humility with fierce determination. It is a useful idea. But some of the companies he praised later struggled badly. The electronics retailer Circuit City went bankrupt in 2009, and the mortgage giant Fannie Mae needed a government rescue in 2008. The lesson is not that Collins was foolish. It is that studying only successful companies makes their habits look like causes, when luck and timing played a large part. Volume 39 calls this the problem of the ones you cannot see (survivorship bias).

Read leadership books carefully

A story of one leader's success is not evidence that their method works. Ask how many people did the same thing and failed. If nobody counted them, the book is a story, not a study.

Chapter 15

What machines cannot take from the job

Think about what management software is good at: tracking tasks, measuring output and spotting patterns in numbers. That is the management half of Kotter's split. As those tools improve, the administrative part of a manager's day is likely to shrink. What remains is the human part: deciding what matters when the numbers conflict, earning trust, handling conflict, and helping people through change.

There is a real risk here, too. When workers are managed by an algorithm they cannot question, they lose the things Chapter 06 showed matter most: a voice, and a person who listens. Several countries are already debating rules on "algorithmic management". The likely future is not leaderless organisations. It is organisations where leadership becomes more purely human, because the routine part has been automated. The broader effect on jobs is covered in How work may change because of AI.

The core idea

Leadership is a relationship: people choosing to follow a direction. It works through three things: a clear direction that makes trade-offs easier, trust that lets people speak up, and earned power rather than title alone, all adjusted to what the situation needs.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Managers explain about 70% of the variation in team engagement (Gallup's estimate).
  • Five bases of power: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, referent (French and Raven, 1959).
  • All 28 men of Shackleton's Endurance expedition survived (1914–1916).
The common mistake

Confusing leadership with being in charge. A title gives you compliance. Only trust and competence give you commitment.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the best-led nursing teams reported more errors, not fewer? If you can, you understand psychological safety and why it matters more than a leader's charisma.
