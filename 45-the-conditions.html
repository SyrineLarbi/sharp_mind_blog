The conditions that matter. Blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, heart attack, stroke — what they actually are.

A small number of conditions account for most of the deaths and most of the disability in the world, they share most of their causes, they develop silently over decades, and almost everyone can describe them only in slogans. This volume explains the actual mechanism of each — because once you can see the mechanism, the advice stops being a list of rules and starts being obvious.

Part I

The plumbing

Chapter 01

Blood pressure, and why it is called the silent one

Blood pressure is the force the blood exerts against the artery walls. The higher number is the push during a heartbeat; the lower number is the pressure that remains between beats, when the vessels are merely holding the column of blood. Both matter, and the lower one is often the more informative in younger people.

Pressure is not inherently bad — you need it to move blood. What causes damage is pressure that stays high for years, because arteries are not rigid pipes. They are living, elastic tubes with a delicate inner lining, and sustained excess force does three things: it thickens and stiffens the vessel walls, it injures that inner lining in a way that starts the process in the next chapter, and it makes the heart work against greater resistance until the muscle thickens and eventually fails at the job.

The reason it kills so many people is entirely in one word. It produces no symptoms. Not usually a headache, not dizziness, not a flushed face — those are folklore. For decades it does quiet damage to arteries, to the heart, to the kidneys and to the small vessels in the brain and the eyes, and the first sign is frequently the event it caused.

Which means the only way to know is to measure. And measurement has its own subtleties worth knowing: it varies through the day, it rises in medical settings for a great many people, one high reading is not a diagnosis, and the readings that best predict outcomes are the ones taken repeatedly, at rest, often at home.

What moves it: weight, salt intake, alcohol, activity, sleep — including untreated sleep apnoea, which is a common and badly under-recognised cause — and stress, though less than people assume. Medication works well, and the widespread habit of stopping it once the numbers look good is exactly backwards: the numbers look good because of it.

Chapter 02

Cholesterol — the part everyone gets wrong

Cholesterol is not a poison and not a waste product. It is an essential building material: your cell membranes are made partly of it, several hormones are built from it, and your liver manufactures most of what you have because it is that important. You could not live without it.

The problem is transport. Cholesterol does not dissolve in blood, so it travels wrapped in particles, and it is the particles rather than the cholesterol that matter. Some of them deliver cholesterol out to tissues; some carry excess back to the liver. The popular "bad" and "good" labels are a rough shorthand for those two directions, and the shorthand causes more confusion than it resolves.

The delivering particles become a problem when there are too many and when the artery lining is already injured — by high pressure, by smoking, by high blood sugar, by inflammation. Damaged lining lets particles into the wall, where they are trapped, oxidised, and attacked by immune cells that arrive to clear them and then die there. The accumulating mess of fat, dead cells and debris is a plaque.

Two corrections worth making explicitly, because both are widely believed and both are wrong. Cholesterol in food is not the main driver of cholesterol in blood for most people — the liver adjusts its production in response, which is why eggs were exonerated. And the danger is not simply "high cholesterol"; it is the combination of particle burden with injured arteries, which is why the same number means different things in different people and why doctors calculate overall risk rather than treating a number in isolation.

Chapter 03

How a heart attack actually happens

The intuitive picture is a pipe slowly blocking until nothing gets through. That is not usually what happens, and the real mechanism explains a great deal that the intuitive one cannot.

A plaque grows in the artery wall over years, covered by a fibrous cap. Large, stable plaques can narrow an artery considerably and cause chest pain on exertion — the heart muscle asking for more blood than a narrowed vessel can supply — which is a warning, and a treatable one.

But a heart attack usually begins when a plaque ruptures. The cap tears, the contents are exposed to flowing blood, and the body does exactly what it does with any injury: it forms a clot. The clot, not the plaque, blocks the artery — and it does so in minutes.

Which explains the thing that confuses everyone: why people who "had no warning" have heart attacks. The plaques most likely to rupture are often not the largest ones, and a modest plaque that tears is more dangerous than a big one that holds. A clear stress test last year is not a guarantee.

Downstream of the blockage, heart muscle starts dying within minutes. It does not grow back — the scar is permanent — which is why the entire emergency system is built around time, and why the phrase used in cardiology is that time is muscle.

What it actually feels like — and why people wait

The Hollywood version — sudden, dramatic, clutching the chest — is one presentation. Many are a heavy pressure, tightness or squeezing in the centre of the chest, building over minutes, often spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back, with sweating, nausea, or breathlessness. It is frequently described as discomfort rather than pain, which is precisely why it gets dismissed.

Women, older people and people with diabetes more often present without classic chest pain — instead with breathlessness, unusual fatigue, nausea or jaw and back discomfort — and are consequently diagnosed later and treated worse. This is a well documented failure and worth knowing about in advance.

Call an ambulance rather than driving. Do not wait to see if it passes. The most common reason people die is delay caused by not wanting to make a fuss, and no emergency department is annoyed by a false alarm of this kind.

Chapter 04

Stroke — the same disease, in the brain

A stroke is the brain's version of the same event, and it comes in two kinds that look identical from outside and require opposite treatments — which is why nobody should ever be given anything at home and why imaging comes first in hospital.

The common kind is a blockage: a clot, often thrown from elsewhere, stops blood reaching part of the brain. The less common kind is a bleed: a vessel ruptures, often because of long-standing high pressure. Roughly four in five are blockages.

Brain tissue is exquisitely intolerant of losing blood supply, and cells begin dying quickly. Around the dying core is a zone of tissue that is starved but not yet dead, and everything in emergency stroke care is aimed at saving that zone — which is only possible within a window of hours, and the window narrows continuously.

The recognition test is deliberately crude so that anyone can apply it under stress. Face — has one side drooped, can they smile evenly? Arms — can they raise both and hold them? Speech — is it slurred, are the words wrong, can they repeat a simple sentence? Time — if any of these, call emergency services immediately and note when symptoms started, because that time determines which treatments are possible.

Also worth knowing: sudden numbness or weakness on one side, sudden confusion, sudden loss of vision, sudden severe unexplained headache, or sudden loss of balance. And a transient episode — symptoms that resolve within minutes or an hour — is not a relief. It is a warning that a full stroke is substantially more likely in the following days, and it is an emergency in its own right.

Part II

The metabolic side

Chapter 05

Diabetes, and what insulin resistance means

Glucose in the blood is fuel, and it must be kept in a fairly narrow range: too little starves the brain, too much damages vessels and nerves. Insulin is the hormone that tells cells to take glucose in, and both types of diabetes are failures of that system in completely different ways.

Type 1 is an autoimmune condition: the immune system destroys the cells that make insulin. It usually appears young, it is not caused by diet or lifestyle, and insulin is not a treatment choice but a requirement for life. The persistent public confusion between the two types causes real harm to people with type 1, who are routinely blamed for a condition they could not have prevented.

Type 2 is different. Insulin is still produced, but cells respond to it poorly — insulin resistance — so the body produces more and more to achieve the same effect. For years the compensation works and blood sugar stays normal, which is why the condition develops silently over a decade or more. Eventually the insulin-producing cells cannot keep up, and blood sugar rises.

The damage is done by sugar in the blood behaving as a slow corrosive. It harms the smallest vessels first — in the eyes, the kidneys and the nerves, which is why the classic complications are sight loss, kidney failure and the nerve damage that leads to unnoticed foot injuries. And it damages the large vessels too, which is why diabetes is a major cardiovascular condition: people with type 2 are far more likely to have a heart attack or stroke, and that is what most of them actually die of.

Two things worth carrying. The warning signs — unusual thirst, passing urine frequently, unexplained weight loss, persistent fatigue, blurred vision, wounds healing slowly — are easily attributed to being busy or getting older. And type 2 is, in many people, substantially reversible in its earlier stages through weight loss and activity, which is one of the more hopeful findings in modern medicine and not widely enough known.

Chapter 06

Cancer, in one chapter

Volume 28 covered this; here is the practical distillation. Cancer is not one disease. It is what happens when a cell accumulates enough damage to its control instructions that it divides without stopping, ignores the signals telling it to die, recruits a blood supply, and eventually invades elsewhere. Different tissues produce different diseases with different behaviour, which is why "a cure for cancer" is not a coherent target.

Most of it is accumulated random damage plus time, which is why age is by far the largest risk factor and why the number of cases rises as populations live longer. Some is inherited. Some is caused by specific exposures — tobacco above all, and by a wide margin — and some by infections that are now vaccine-preventable.

The practical part is the warning signs, which are unglamorous and worth knowing: a lump that persists, a mole that changes, unexplained weight loss, unusual bleeding, a cough or hoarseness lasting weeks, a change in bowel habit lasting weeks, difficulty swallowing, a sore that does not heal. Almost all of these turn out to be something else — which is the point, because the cost of checking is small and the benefit of catching something early is enormous.

Part III

What actually helps

Chapter 07

The shared roots

The striking thing about everything in this volume is how much of it has the same causes. These are not six separate problems requiring six separate strategies; they are largely one problem expressed in different organs.

Smoking is the largest single avoidable cause of nearly all of them — it damages artery lining, raises pressure, promotes clotting and causes cancer directly. Stopping produces measurable benefit within weeks and substantial benefit within years, at any age.

Weight, and particularly abdominal fat, drives insulin resistance, pressure and inflammation together. Fat around the organs behaves differently from fat under the skin, which is why waist measurement predicts risk better than weight alone.

Activity improves insulin sensitivity, pressure, cholesterol particles and mood simultaneously, and the largest benefit is in the step from doing nothing to doing something — not in the step from a lot to more.

Sleep affects all of it, and untreated sleep apnoea in particular drives high pressure and is frequently missed.

Diet matters most in its overall pattern rather than in any single ingredient, and the pattern that keeps performing well in evidence is unglamorous: plenty of vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts and fish; limited ultra-processed food, refined sugar and processed meat. Alcohol is worth naming honestly — the old story about moderate drinking being protective has not held up well, and the current evidence points toward less being better.

And the one people forget: chronic stress and social isolation both show up as measurable cardiovascular risk, at a size comparable to the well-known physical factors.

Chapter 08

Numbers to know, and what to ask

You do not need to memorise thresholds — they differ by country, by age and by your other risks, and they get revised. What is worth knowing is which numbers exist and that you are entitled to know yours.

Blood pressure. A cholesterol panel, including the particle breakdown rather than only the total. Blood sugar, ideally the measure that reflects the last few months rather than a single morning. Waist measurement. And your family history, which you can only get by asking relatives and which changes what any of the other numbers mean.

The crucial framing is that these are not pass or fail marks. Risk is continuous, calculated from all of them together, and a single value outside a range is not a verdict — a point Volume 42 made and which applies here especially, because these are the numbers people most often panic about in isolation.

So the questions to bring: what is my overall risk, not just this number? What would change it most, in my case? What happens if we wait and measure again? And what are the alternatives to starting a medication now? A good clinician will welcome all four, because they are the questions they are already weighing.

Scope

This volume explains mechanisms so you can follow a conversation and ask better questions. It is not medical advice, does not know your situation, and contains no dosing or threshold guidance deliberately. Nothing here should delay calling emergency services for chest discomfort, stroke signs, difficulty breathing or sudden severe headache.

Reference

What to carry forward

The mechanism

High pressure injures the artery lining; particles enter the wall and build a plaque; the plaque ruptures and a clot forms in minutes. That is a heart attack, and it is why people with no warning have one — the dangerous plaques are often not the biggest.

The two silent ones

Blood pressure causes no symptoms — not headaches, not dizziness — and does decades of damage before the event it causes. Type 2 diabetes is silent for years while the body compensates, and the damage is sugar acting as a slow corrosive on small vessels first.

Recognition

Stroke: Face, Arms, Speech, Time — and note when symptoms started. Heart attack: often pressure rather than pain, and frequently without classic chest pain in women, older people and people with diabetes. A transient episode is a warning, not a reprieve.

The shared roots

These are largely one problem in different organs: smoking, abdominal weight, inactivity, poor sleep, dietary pattern — plus chronic stress and isolation. Know your pressure, cholesterol panel, blood sugar, waist and family history, and ask for overall risk rather than reacting to one number.
