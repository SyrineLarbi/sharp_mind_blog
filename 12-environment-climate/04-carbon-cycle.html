Carbon cycle. The planet's bookkeeping, and the account we keep overdrawing.

Every carbon atom in your body was once in the air, and before that perhaps in a seashell, a volcano or a dinosaur. Carbon never disappears. It only moves between stores: the atmosphere, living things, soils, the ocean and rock. Some of those moves take a season and some take a hundred million years. The climate problem is, at bottom, a story about moving carbon from the slowest store into the fastest one, faster than the rest of the system can cope with.

Question 1 · What is it?

Stores and flows

The carbon cycle is the movement of carbon between the places it is kept. Think of it as a set of bank accounts with money constantly transferred between them.

Chapter 01

The journey of one atom

Follow a single carbon atom. Today it is in the air, part of a molecule of carbon dioxide. In spring a leaf takes it in, and sunlight powers a reaction that builds it into sugar. A caterpillar eats the leaf. A bird eats the caterpillar, burns the sugar for energy, and breathes the atom back out as CO2. That loop took a few months.

Now a different path. The atom dissolves into the sea, and a tiny plankton builds it into a shell of calcium carbonate. The plankton dies and its shell sinks to the sea floor. Over millions of years layers of shells are pressed into limestone. Eventually that rock may be pushed up into a mountain, weathered by rain, or dragged into the Earth's interior and released by a volcano. That loop took a hundred million years.

The same atom can take a quick route or a geological one, and the planet's climate depends on the balance between them.

Chapter 02

Where the carbon is kept

Scientists measure carbon stores in gigatonnes of carbon (GtC). A gigatonne is a billion tonnes. The figures below are rounded, and the land figures in particular are uncertain.

The table hides the key point. The atmosphere is one of the smallest stores, so a modest transfer into it changes it a lot. Moving one per cent of the ocean's carbon into the air would raise atmospheric CO2 by close to half.

Chapter 03

The fast cycle and the slow cycle

It helps to split the system in two. The fast cycle links the air, living things, soils and the surface ocean. Enormous amounts of carbon go round it: land plants take up on the order of a hundred gigatonnes a year, and almost the same amount comes back through breathing, decay and fire. The ocean swaps a similar quantity with the air in both directions.

The slow cycle links the fast cycle to rock. Volcanoes add carbon from deep in the Earth, and the weathering of rock and the burial of shells and dead matter take it out. These flows are tiny per year, but over millions of years they decide how much carbon the fast cycle has to play with.

Fossil fuels are a short-cut between the two. Coal is the remains of ancient swamp forests, and oil and gas mostly of ancient marine plankton. The slow cycle buried them over many millions of years. Burning them moves slow-cycle carbon into the fast cycle in a few generations. Humans now release around 10–11 GtC a year from fossil fuels and land clearing, which is roughly a hundred times what all the world's volcanoes release.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Leaves, water and stone

Three different mechanisms move carbon: life on land, the chemistry and biology of the ocean, and the very slow reactions of rock. Each works on a different clock.

Chapter 04

The land breathes

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants use sunlight to join CO2 and water into sugar, releasing oxygen. Respiration is the reverse: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria break sugar down for energy and release CO2. Most carbon a plant takes in is released again within a few years, as leaves fall and rot, roots die and animals eat.

The carbon that stays longer is in wood and especially in soil. Soil holds more carbon than all plants and the atmosphere together. It is made of dead roots, leaves and microbes, broken down slowly. Cold and wet conditions slow that breakdown, which is why peat bogs and the frozen soils of the Arctic, called permafrost, have accumulated huge amounts over thousands of years.

The balance between growth and decay shifts with the seasons. The Northern Hemisphere has most of the world's land, so when its forests leaf out in spring, the CO2 level in the whole atmosphere dips, and in autumn it rises again. The planet visibly inhales and exhales once a year.

Chapter 05

The ocean's two pumps

The ocean takes up carbon in two ways.

The chemical pump

CO2 dissolves in seawater and reacts with it, forming carbonic acid, which turns mostly into bicarbonate. Cold water dissolves more gas, as any fizzy drink shows when it goes flat in the warm. In the cold far north and around Antarctica, surface water absorbs CO2 and then sinks, carrying it into the deep sea, where it stays for centuries.

The biological pump

Microscopic plants in the sunlit surface, phytoplankton, take up CO2 as land plants do. When they die or are eaten, some of the carbon sinks as a slow drizzle of dead matter and droppings, sometimes called "marine snow". A small fraction reaches the sea floor and is buried.

Together the pumps keep the deep ocean far richer in carbon than the surface. The deep sea is the planet's largest active carbon reservoir. The surface ocean, however, is quick to reach a balance with the air, and the deep water mixes up only slowly. The ocean is a huge sink with a narrow doorway. That doorway limits how fast it can absorb what we add.

Chapter 06

The rock thermostat

On the longest timescales, carbon is controlled by rock. Rainwater is slightly acidic because it contains dissolved CO2. When it falls on rocks such as granite and basalt, it slowly dissolves them. The products wash into rivers and the sea, where they help shell-building organisms lock carbon into limestone. This process is called weathering.

Weathering runs faster when the climate is warm and wet. So if volcanoes push CO2 up and the planet warms, weathering speeds up and draws CO2 down. If the planet cools, weathering slows and volcanic CO2 builds up again. This feedback is thought to have kept Earth habitable for billions of years. Its weakness, for us, is the speed. It works over hundreds of thousands of years, so it will eventually clean up our emissions, but not on any timescale that matters to a human society.

Chapter 07

Where our carbon goes

Every year the Global Carbon Project, an international group of scientists, publishes a budget of human emissions and where they end up. The picture has been consistent for decades.

Human CO2 each year, where it ends up (recent averages, rounded)
 emitted by fossil fuels + land clearing   100%
 |
 +--> stays in the ATMOSPHERE ............. about 45%   (raises CO2 ~2-3 ppm a year)
 |
 +--> absorbed by LAND (plants, soils) .... about 30%   (varies a lot year to year)
 |
 +--> absorbed by the OCEAN ............... about 25%   (makes seawater more acidic)

So nature is doing us a large favour. Roughly half of everything we emit is removed each year by the land and the sea. Without that, the CO2 level would be far higher than it is. The share that stays in the air is called the airborne fraction, and it has stayed fairly steady even as emissions have grown, which means the sinks have grown too. They have grown because higher CO2 pushes more into the ocean and, up to a point, fertilises plant growth.

The land sink is the jumpy one. In dry, hot years, especially during El Niño events (a periodic warming of the tropical Pacific that shifts rainfall worldwide), tropical forests grow less and burn more. In 2023, a hot El Niño year with huge fires in Canada, scientists reported that the land sink weakened sharply. Whether that was a one-off or an early warning is still being studied.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fingerprints, acid, and a warning from the deep past

The carbon cycle explains how we know the extra CO2 is ours, why the ocean is changing chemically, and what might happen if the natural sinks start to fail.

Chapter 08

How we know the extra carbon is ours

Carbon comes in slightly different forms called isotopes, which differ in weight. Most is carbon-12. About one per cent is the heavier carbon-13. A tiny trace is radioactive carbon-14, made in the upper atmosphere by cosmic rays, which decays over thousands of years.

Fossil fuels have two signatures. Plants prefer the lighter carbon-12, so fossil carbon, made from ancient plants, is low in carbon-13. And it is millions of years old, so all its carbon-14 has decayed. In the 1950s the chemist Hans Suess noticed that carbon-14 in the air was being diluted, as if by carbon with no carbon-14 at all. Since then, the share of both carbon-13 and carbon-14 in atmospheric CO2 has kept falling, exactly as expected if the new carbon is fossil.

Two more checks agree. The oxygen level in the air is falling slightly, in the amount that burning would consume. And the extra CO2 matches, in quantity, the fuel recorded as burned. Volcanoes cannot explain it: they emit far too little, and their carbon does not have the plant signature. The rise in CO2 is the most firmly attributed fact in climate science.

Chapter 09

The ocean's bill: acidification

The ocean's absorption of CO2 has a cost. Dissolved CO2 forms an acid. The average acidity of surface seawater has increased since industrialisation: its pH, a scale where lower means more acidic, has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1. That sounds small, but the pH scale is logarithmic, so the change means roughly a 30% increase in acidity. The sea is still slightly alkaline, not acid, but it is moving in that direction.

The chemistry matters to anything that builds a shell or skeleton out of calcium carbonate: corals, oysters, mussels, sea urchins and some plankton. As the water absorbs CO2, it has less of the dissolved carbonate those animals need. Oyster hatcheries in the US Pacific Northwest suffered large die-offs of larvae in the late 2000s when corrosive deep water welled up, and now monitor seawater chemistry and adjust it. This connects to the Biodiversity and Ocean pollution files.

Chapter 10

When sinks turn into sources

The biggest worry is that the stores now absorbing carbon could start releasing it, adding to human emissions without anyone burning anything. Three are watched closely.

  • Permafrost. Arctic soils hold an enormous amount of frozen organic matter. As they thaw, microbes can digest it and release CO2 and methane. This is happening already in places. How much and how fast are uncertain; most estimates suggest a gradual addition over the century rather than a sudden burst.
  • Tropical forests. The Amazon has been a carbon sink. Studies measuring air from aircraft suggest that parts of the southeastern Amazon, heavily cleared and burned, now release more carbon than they absorb. See Deforestation.
  • The ocean. Warmer water holds less gas, and a more stratified ocean, with warm water sitting on top of cold, mixes less. Both could slow the ocean sink over time.

These are feedbacks: warming causes changes that cause more warming. They are why climate scientists talk about the sinks as something that cannot be taken for granted.

Chapter 11

A rehearsal 56 million years ago

The rock record contains a natural experiment. About 56 million years ago, at an event called the Palaeocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), a huge amount of carbon entered the air and ocean over a few thousand years. Its source is still debated, with volcanic activity and the release of methane from the sea floor among the candidates. Global temperatures rose by roughly 5 °C or more. The deep ocean became more acidic and many sea-floor species died out.

Two lessons follow. First, the rock thermostat did eventually bring things back, but it took more than a hundred thousand years. Second, our current rate of carbon release appears to be faster than at the PETM, probably by a large factor. That comparison is debated in its details, since dating ancient sediments precisely is hard. But the direction of the lesson is not: the system can absorb carbon, and it can recover, but only on its own clock.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A generous system with hard limits

The carbon cycle has been a buffer for us, and it has been a trap. Both sides matter for policy.

Chapter 13

Why a tree is not a lump of coal

A common idea is that planting trees can cancel out burning fossil fuels. It sounds like simple arithmetic: one tonne released, one tonne absorbed. But the carbon cycle shows why the two are not equal. Fossil carbon was locked away for millions of years. Carbon in a forest is in the fast cycle and can return to the air through fire, drought, pests or a change of land use. Moving carbon from a permanent store to a temporary one is not neutral, even if the tonnes match on paper.

That is the core of the criticism of many carbon offsets, in which a company pays for trees or forest protection to balance its emissions. Investigations have found that some forest-protection credits claimed to save forest that was never under threat, and that some planted forests have burned. Supporters reply that forests are worth protecting anyway and that better rules can fix the abuses. Both sides tend to agree on one point: forests are a complement to cutting fossil emissions, not a substitute for it.

Chapter 15

The long tail

Here is the point that most surprises people. If all CO2 emissions stopped tomorrow, the CO2 level would start to fall slowly, as the ocean and land kept absorbing. But the temperature would stay roughly where it is for a long time, because the ocean's slow warming and the slow fall of CO2 roughly cancel. According to the IPCC, somewhere around 15–40% of a pulse of CO2 is still in the air after a thousand years.

That has two consequences. Warming is effectively irreversible on human timescales unless we actively remove carbon. And every tonne counts in the same way, whether it was emitted in 1950 or 2050, because the air keeps a running total. The carbon cycle is not a bin that empties every night. It is a ledger that remembers.

The core idea

Carbon moves between stores: air, plants, soils, ocean and rock. A fast cycle (life and the surface ocean) swaps carbon yearly; a slow cycle (rock) works over millions of years. Burning fossil fuels moves slow-cycle carbon into the fast cycle. About half stays in the air, the rest goes into land and ocean.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Humans emit about 10–11 GtC a year, roughly 100 times what volcanoes do.
  • About 45% stays in the air, about 30% goes to land, about 25% to the ocean.
  • Surface ocean acidity is up about 30% since industrialisation.
The common mistake

Treating a tonne stored in a forest as equal to a tonne left in the ground. Forest carbon is temporary; fossil carbon was permanent.
