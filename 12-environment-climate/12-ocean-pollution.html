Ocean pollution. The sea was treated as infinite. It turned out to be a place with an address.

For most of history the ocean seemed too big to damage. Anything poured into it would simply vanish. That belief was wrong in a specific way. Some pollutants do dilute and break down. Others concentrate as they move up the food chain, starve coastal waters of oxygen, or last for centuries. Ocean pollution is not one problem but several, with different sources, different harms and very different records of success.

Question 1 · What is it?

Anything people add to the sea that harms it

Ocean pollution means substances or energy introduced by people that damage marine life, human health or the uses of the sea. The list is longer than most people expect.

Chapter 01

"Dilution is the solution"

For a long time engineers had a saying: the solution to pollution is dilution. Pour waste into enough water and it becomes harmless. Cities piped raw sewage into the sea. Ships washed their oil tanks at sea. Governments dumped industrial waste, munitions and even barrels of radioactive waste in deep water until international agreements stopped it, mainly between the 1970s and the 1990s.

The saying is half right, and the half that is wrong explains most of this subject. Dilution works for substances that break down quickly and that living things do not accumulate, such as some organic waste in a well-mixed open sea. It fails for three kinds of pollutant. Some do not break down, like many plastics and industrial chemicals. Some are concentrated by living things, like mercury. And some act as fertiliser, so a little dilution does not neutralise them but spreads them to where they do harm. The ocean is large, but it is not well mixed: most pollution stays in coastal waters, where most marine life and most fishing also are.

Chapter 03

Most of it starts on land

The image of ocean pollution is a tanker spill. The reality is a river. A commonly cited estimate is that around four fifths of marine pollution comes from land, carried by rivers, pipes, rain and wind. Fertiliser washes off fields into streams. Plastic waste blows from open dumps into rivers. Mercury from burning coal falls from the air onto the sea.

This matters for solutions. Pollution from a single pipe or ship, called point-source pollution, is relatively easy to regulate: you can inspect it, fine its owner and fit a filter. Pollution spread across millions of fields, roads and homes, called diffuse or non-point pollution, has no single owner and no single place to fix. Most of the successes in this file are point-source problems. Most of the remaining difficulties are diffuse ones.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Four ways the sea is harmed

Each pollutant has its own mechanism. Understanding the mechanism tells you why the harm appears where it does, sometimes far from the source and years later.

Chapter 04

How fertiliser suffocates the sea

Algae, like crops, are limited by nutrients. In many coastal waters, nitrogen and phosphorus are in short supply, which keeps algae in check. Add them, and algae explode. That process is called eutrophication, from Greek words meaning "well fed".

Dead zones are worst in summer, when warm, fresh river water sits on top of colder, saltier sea water and the layers do not mix, so oxygen from the surface cannot reach the bottom. Scientists have identified hundreds of coastal dead zones around the world. The one in the Gulf of Mexico, fed by the Mississippi River and the farms of the American Midwest, often covers well over 10,000 square kilometres in summer. The Baltic Sea, nearly enclosed and surrounded by farms and cities, has some of the largest areas of low-oxygen water anywhere.

Some blooms are toxic in themselves. "Red tides" and other harmful algal blooms produce poisons that kill fish, accumulate in shellfish and can sicken people who eat them. Not all are caused by pollution, but nutrient run-off makes many of them more frequent and severe.

Chapter 05

Biomagnification: why big fish carry mercury

Some pollutants are absorbed by living things faster than they are removed. Mercury is the classic case. Coal burning, gold mining and some industries release mercury. In water, bacteria convert part of it into methylmercury, a form that animals absorb easily and excrete slowly.

Plankton take in a little. Small fish eat a great deal of plankton and keep most of the mercury. Bigger fish eat many small fish. At each step up the food chain, the concentration rises. This is biomagnification. By the time you reach a tuna, a swordfish or a shark, the concentration can be many thousands or even millions of times higher than in the surrounding water.

The same process works for a family of industrial chemicals called persistent organic pollutants, which include PCBs (once used in electrical equipment) and the insecticide DDT. They dissolve in fat rather than water, so they collect in the blubber of seals, dolphins and whales. The animals at the top of the food chain, including people, receive the dose. That is why many health authorities advise pregnant women and young children to limit the large predatory fish they eat, while still recommending fish in general.

This is general education, not medical or dietary advice. Fish advice varies by country and species; check your national food safety authority's guidance.

Chapter 06

What happens to spilled oil

Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of compounds. When it spills, the lighter parts evaporate within days. Some dissolves, and some is broken down by bacteria, which can digest oil. Waves whip part of it into a sticky water-in-oil mix nicknamed "chocolate mousse". The heaviest parts form tar balls that wash ashore or sink. This set of changes is called weathering.

The damage depends on where the oil goes. On the open sea, much of it disperses. On a sheltered rocky coast or a salt marsh, oil can persist for decades: traces of oil from the 1989 Exxon Valdez spill in Alaska were still found beneath beach gravel more than twenty years later. Oil kills birds by matting their feathers, so they lose insulation and buoyancy, and poisons fish eggs and larvae.

Responders can contain oil with floating booms, skim it, burn it, or spray dispersants, chemicals that break the slick into tiny droplets that mix into the water. Dispersants are controversial. They protect coastlines and seabirds but push oil into the water column, where fish and plankton live. During the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill in the Gulf of Mexico, dispersants were injected at the wellhead, a mile underwater, on a scale never tried before. Scientists still debate the net effect.

Chapter 07

Noise: the pollution you cannot see

Light travels poorly in water, but sound travels far and fast. Whales and dolphins use sound to find food, navigate and communicate across great distances. Many fish use it too. Human activity has filled the sea with sound: ship engines and propellers, seismic air guns used to search for oil and gas, pile-driving for offshore wind farms and harbours, and military sonar.

Low-frequency noise from shipping increased greatly over the twentieth century in busy ocean regions. Researchers have shown that whales change their calls and behaviour in noisy water, and that stress hormones in right whales fell when shipping briefly paused in Canada's Bay of Fundy after the September 2001 attacks. Military sonar has been linked to mass strandings of beaked whales, including a well-documented event in the Bahamas in 2000. Noise is unusual among pollutants: it stops the moment the source stops. Quieter ship propellers and slower speeds reduce it directly.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Poisoned towns, burning wells and empty water

Ocean pollution matters because billions of people depend on the sea for food and income, and because its worst episodes have shown how slowly the damage heals. They also produced most of the laws we now rely on.

Chapter 08

Minamata: the town that named a disease

In the 1950s, in the fishing town of Minamata in southern Japan, cats began to behave strangely and die. Then people fell ill, with numbness, trembling, loss of speech and sight, and in severe cases death. Babies were born with severe disabilities, even when their mothers seemed healthy. The disease was officially recognised in 1956. The cause was methylmercury discharged into Minamata Bay by a chemical factory owned by the Chisso company, which had built up in the fish and shellfish that local families ate every day.

It took years for the company and the government to accept responsibility, and the discharges continued into the late 1960s. Thousands of people were eventually recognised as victims, and compensation disputes lasted decades. In 2013 a global treaty to reduce mercury pollution was signed and named the Minamata Convention. Biomagnification is not a laboratory abstraction; it was first understood through a poisoned town.

Chapter 09

The spills that wrote the rules

EventWhat happenedWhat changed

Exxon Valdez, 1989A tanker hit a reef in Prince William Sound, Alaska, spilling tens of thousands of tonnes of crude on a remote, cold coast.The US Oil Pollution Act of 1990, which phased in double-hulled tankers.
Deepwater Horizon, 2010An offshore drilling rig exploded in the Gulf of Mexico, killing 11 workers. Oil flowed from the sea floor for 87 days, the largest marine spill in US history.Tighter rules on offshore drilling safety and well control in the US, and multibillion-dollar penalties for BP.

The rules worked. According to the tanker industry's pollution body, ITOPF, the number of large oil spills from tankers has fallen by more than 90% since the 1970s, even though far more oil is shipped. Double hulls, better navigation and port inspections did most of the work. It is one of the clearest success stories in environmental regulation. Big spills now come more often from drilling and pipelines than from tankers.

Chapter 10

The cost to food and livelihoods

According to the FAO, fish and other aquatic foods provide an important part of the animal protein eaten by around three billion people. Pollution affects that supply in several ways. Dead zones push fish and shrimp out of fishing grounds, and in severe years kill them. Toxic blooms close shellfish beds. Oil spills close fisheries for months: after Deepwater Horizon, a large area of the Gulf of Mexico was closed to fishing at the peak of the spill. Sewage pollution can make shellfish unsafe to eat.

Coastal tourism suffers too. Beaches close after sewage overflows or toxic algae; and since 2011 huge mats of a floating seaweed called sargassum have washed onto Caribbean and West African beaches in many years. Why the sargassum blooms began is debated: nutrients from rivers such as the Amazon, dust from the Sahara, and changes in ocean circulation have all been proposed, and it is probably a combination.

Chapter 11

Plastics: the newest, most visible problem

Plastic is the pollutant most people now associate with the sea. A widely cited 2015 study estimated that several million tonnes of plastic entered the ocean from land in 2010, mostly from countries where waste collection had not kept up with fast-growing consumption. Later estimates differ, sometimes widely, but agree that the amount is in the millions of tonnes a year.

The so-called Great Pacific Garbage Patch, between Hawaii and California, is not a floating island you could walk on. It is a vast area where currents gather debris, mostly small fragments spread through the water, along with larger objects. A large share of the mass of the big pieces there is discarded fishing gear. Plastics harm wildlife through entanglement and swallowing, and break down into microplastics, particles smaller than five millimetres, now found from the surface to the deepest trenches. The full story, including what recycling can and cannot do, is in the next file, Plastic pollution.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What has worked, and what has not

Ocean pollution has no "advantages". The honest comparison is between approaches: which fixes worked, at what cost, and why some problems resist them.

Chapter 13

Why the remaining problems are hard

The pattern in the table is clear. Concentrated sources with identifiable owners have been largely controlled. Diffuse sources and long-lived chemicals have not. A tanker owner can be fined. Millions of farmers each contributing a little nitrogen cannot be policed the same way, and measures that reduce run-off, such as planting buffer strips by streams, using less fertiliser or restoring wetlands, cost farmers money or yield.

Long-lived chemicals add a time problem. PCBs were banned in most countries in the 1970s and 1980s, yet a 2018 study suggested that PCB contamination threatened the long-term survival of many killer whale populations, especially near industrial coasts. A newer family, the PFAS or "forever chemicals", used in non-stick coatings, firefighting foams and waterproofing, is now being found in seawater and marine animals worldwide. What we ban today, the ocean keeps for decades.

Finally, the open ocean beyond any country's control is a commons, a shared resource that no one owns. Each country gains from using it and bears only part of the cost of damaging it. That is why treaties matter so much in this field, and why they move so slowly.

Chapter 15

The sea can recover

The evidence of recovery is real when sources are cut. After leaded petrol was phased out, lead levels in the surface waters of the North Atlantic fell markedly. After DDT was banned in many countries in the 1970s, the eggshells of ospreys, pelicans and eagles thickened again and their populations recovered. In the Black Sea, a large dead zone on the northwestern shelf shrank in the 1990s after fertiliser use collapsed along with the Soviet economy; it was an unplanned experiment, but it showed how quickly coastal waters respond when nutrient inputs drop.

The lesson for conversation is precise. Ocean pollution is not a single trend going one way. Some problems, such as tanker spills and lead, have been largely solved. Some, such as nutrients and plastics, are still growing. And some, such as forever chemicals and deep-sea mining, are only beginning. The difference between them is mostly whether there is a clear source, a substitute, and the political will to act.

The core idea

Ocean pollution is several problems: nutrients that create dead zones, oil, persistent chemicals that build up through food chains, plastics, sewage and noise. Most comes from land. Point sources have largely been controlled; diffuse and long-lived pollutants remain the hard part.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Around four fifths of marine pollution is commonly estimated to come from land.
  • Large tanker spills are down by more than 90% since the 1970s.
  • The Gulf of Mexico dead zone often exceeds 10,000 km² in summer.
The common mistake

Thinking the ocean is too big to pollute, or that tanker spills are the main problem. The bigger, quieter harm arrives by river.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a tuna can contain far more mercury than the water it swims in, and why a fertilised field hundreds of kilometres inland can kill fish at sea? If so, you understand the two mechanisms that make ocean pollution more than a dilution problem.
