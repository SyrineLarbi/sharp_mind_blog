Sustainable cities. The most efficient way humans have found to live is also the place where most pollution starts.

More than half of humanity now lives in cities, and the share keeps growing. Cities burn most of the world's energy and produce most of its carbon dioxide. They also make life efficient in a way nothing else does: in a dense city, a home is smaller and easier to heat, shops are walking distance away, and one train carries the people who would fill a motorway. A sustainable city is one that uses those advantages on purpose. This file explains the main levers, from streets and buildings to trees and drains, and the hard trade-offs that come with each.

Question 1 · What is it?

A city that works for people now without failing people later

A sustainable city keeps its residents healthy, housed and moving while using far less energy, land and water, and while staying safe as the climate changes.

Chapter 01

The bus that behaved like a train

In the early 1970s, Curitiba in southern Brazil was a fast-growing city with little money. Under the architect and mayor Jaime Lerner, it did not build an expensive underground railway. Instead it gave buses their own lanes on main roads, so they never sat in traffic. Later it added raised tube-shaped stations where passengers paid before boarding and stepped in at floor level, like on a train. Buses filled and emptied in seconds.

This system, later called bus rapid transit (BRT), delivered much of the speed of a metro for a fraction of the cost. The city also zoned its densest buildings along the bus corridors, so that homes and jobs grew where transport already was. Bogotá in Colombia built its own large system, TransMilenio, in 2000, and BRT has since spread to well over a hundred cities. Curitiba showed that a sustainable city is mostly a matter of design and political will, not wealth.

Chapter 02

What "sustainable" covers

The term is broad, but it usually includes five goals, which the UN groups in its Sustainable Development Goal 11, "sustainable cities and communities".

GoalWhat it means in practice
Low emissionsClean transport, efficient buildings, clean electricity and heating
Clean air and waterFewer exhaust fumes and chimneys; treated sewage; healthy rivers
Efficient use of land and resourcesCompact growth instead of sprawl; less waste; reuse of materials
ResilienceCoping with heat, floods and drought (see Climate adaptation)
Fairness and liveabilityAffordable homes, safe streets, green space and services for everyone, not only the rich

The last goal is often forgotten in talk about green technology. A city that is green but unaffordable pushes people out to distant suburbs, where they drive more. Housing is a climate issue.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The five big levers

A city's environmental footprint comes mainly from how people move, how buildings are heated, cooled and built, and how land, water and waste are managed.

Chapter 03

Density: why a flat in a city beats a house in the suburbs

The single most important fact about urban sustainability is that density, done well, saves energy. Flats share walls, so they lose less heat. Smaller homes need less heating, cooling and furniture. Shops, schools and jobs close together mean shorter trips, many of them on foot. And only dense places have enough passengers to support frequent public transport.

The difference shows up in data. In the United States, residents of dense New York City produce much lower carbon emissions per person than the national average, mainly because many of them do not drive. Spread-out cities built around the car, such as Houston or Atlanta, show the opposite. Density is not the same as tall towers. Paris and Barcelona are among the densest cities in Europe, mostly built of six- to eight-storey blocks.

Why sprawl happens

Sprawl is low-density growth spreading outward, with homes far from jobs and shops. It is often encouraged by rules: zoning that allows only detached houses on most land, minimum parking requirements, and cheap land at the edge. Changing those rules is one of the least expensive sustainability tools a city has.

Chapter 04

Moving people, not cars

Transport is usually the largest or second-largest source of a city's emissions, and the main source of its air pollution and noise. The sustainable approach has a simple order: first make walking and cycling easy, then public transport, and only then cleaner cars.

Copenhagen is the famous example. In the 1960s and 1970s its streets were filling with cars, like everywhere else. The city built separated cycle lanes, gave bicycles their own traffic lights, and narrowed some roads. Today close to half of trips to work or education in the city are made by bike. The reason is not that the Danes are unusually virtuous. It is that cycling became the fastest and easiest way to get around.

Pricing helps too. Singapore introduced a charge for driving into its centre in 1975. London followed in 2003, Stockholm made its charge permanent in 2007 after a trial and a referendum, and New York began charging drivers entering lower Manhattan in January 2025. These schemes cut traffic in the zone, speed up buses, and raise money for public transport.

One lesson is counter-intuitive. Building more road lanes rarely cures congestion. New capacity makes driving easier, so more people drive, and within a few years the roads fill again. This effect is called induced demand. The widening of the Katy Freeway in Houston to more than twenty lanes in places, completed in 2011, is a widely cited case: travel times at rush hour later got worse, not better.

Chapter 05

Buildings: running them and making them

Buildings account for a large share of global energy use and carbon emissions, roughly a third when the energy used to run them and the emissions from making their materials are counted together. There are two kinds of carbon.

Operational carbon

The energy used every day to heat, cool, light and power a building. It falls with insulation, good windows, shading, efficient appliances, heat pumps (see Volume 11) and clean electricity. Copenhagen, for example, heats almost all its buildings through a shared network of hot-water pipes, called district heating.

Embodied carbon

The emissions released to make and transport the materials and build the building, before anyone moves in. Cement and steel dominate (see Volume 15). As buildings get more efficient to run, embodied carbon becomes a larger share of their total footprint.

This second kind of carbon explains a growing movement among architects. The greenest building is often the one that already exists. Renovating an old building usually emits far less than demolishing it and building a new efficient one. Where new buildings are needed, some cities now encourage mass timber, large engineered wooden beams and panels, which can replace part of the concrete and steel.

Chapter 06

Green and blue: trees, parks and water

Cities are hotter than the countryside around them. Dark roofs and roads soak up sunlight all day and release heat at night, while engines and air conditioners add more. This is the urban heat island effect. On a still summer night a large city can be several degrees warmer than nearby fields, which matters a lot during heatwaves, when people cannot cool down while they sleep.

Trees are one of the best answers. They shade streets and cool the air as they release water from their leaves. Parks, green roofs and light-coloured surfaces also help. Planners call this network of parks, street trees and green spaces green infrastructure, and the rivers, ponds and canals blue infrastructure.

Water is the other half. Concrete and asphalt stop rain soaking into the ground, so a storm becomes a flood in minutes. After a cloudburst in July 2011 flooded much of Copenhagen, the city adopted a plan to turn some streets and parks into channels and basins that hold storm water safely. In 2015 China launched a programme of sponge cities, using permeable pavements, wetlands and green spaces to soak up and store rain in dozens of pilot cities. The idea is that a city should absorb water like a sponge, not shed it like a roof.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where most people live, and most emissions start

Whether the world meets its climate goals, and whether billions of people breathe clean air and stay safe in heatwaves, will be decided largely in cities.

Chapter 07

The numbers that make cities central

The UN has long estimated that about 55% of people live in urban areas, rising towards two-thirds by 2050. The exact share depends on how a "city" is defined, and newer UN figures using a stricter, internationally comparable definition put it somewhat differently. On any definition, nearly all of the world's population growth in the coming decades will happen in cities and towns, mostly in Africa and Asia. The social side of this is covered in Urbanization and Megacities.

Cities are also where most energy is used. The IPCC estimates that urban areas account for roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of energy-related carbon dioxide emissions, much of it from the electricity, heating and transport that city life depends on. Networks such as C40, founded in 2005, link mayors of major cities to share policies and set climate targets, sometimes moving faster than their national governments.

Chapter 08

The cities still to be built

The biggest opportunity is not rebuilding Paris or London. It is the cities of Africa and South Asia that will grow enormously this century. Much of the housing, roads and pipes they will need does not yet exist. Whatever is built in the next few decades will lock in emissions and living patterns for a century or more, because buildings and street layouts last that long.

About a billion people today live in informal settlements, often called slums, without secure housing, clean water, sanitation or drainage. For these residents, a sustainable city means first having basic services at all. Many experts argue that upgrading such neighbourhoods in place, by adding water, drains, paths and legal titles, is better than clearing them, which often pushes people to the even more distant edge of the city.

Chapter 09

Paris and the fifteen-minute city

Paris under Mayor Anne Hidalgo, first elected in 2014, became the best-known test of rapid change in a large, rich city. It turned the riverside expressway along the Seine into a park, removed most car traffic from the Rue de Rivoli, built hundreds of kilometres of cycle lanes, and raised parking charges for large cars after a public vote in 2024. Cycling in the city grew sharply. In 2025, after years of investment in sewers and treatment for the Olympic Games, Parisians could swim in parts of the Seine again.

Paris also adopted the idea of the fifteen-minute city, promoted by the researcher Carlos Moreno: most daily needs, such as shops, schools, doctors and parks, should be within a fifteen- minute walk or bike ride of home. The idea is old in spirit; it describes how many towns worked before the car. It became politically controversial in some countries, where critics wrongly claimed it meant restricting where people could travel. In practice it means bringing services closer, not locking anyone in.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Green for whom?

Most sustainable city policies bring clear benefits. They also create winners and losers, and ignoring that is the fastest way to make them fail.

Chapter 11

Two traps: green gentrification and showcase projects

Green gentrification is the problem of success. When a neglected neighbourhood gets a new park, tram line or clean river, it becomes more desirable. Property prices and rents rise, and long-standing residents, often poorer, may be forced out. New York's High Line, an old elevated railway turned into a park, is often cited: it was a huge success, and property values around it rose sharply. Cities that want to avoid this pair greening with protections such as social housing.

Vienna is the usual example of how housing and sustainability can work together. A large share of its residents live in city-owned or subsidised housing, which keeps rents affordable in a dense, well-served city with excellent public transport.

The second trap is the showcase. Gleaming new eco-districts and futuristic planned cities attract investment and headlines, but they usually house few people. Most of the gains come from changing the ordinary city that already exists: its bus lanes, building codes, parking rules and drains.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Electric, cooler, and probably more contested

Some changes are already well under way. Others depend on politics and on whether fast-growing cities can skip the car-centred stage. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 13

Technology helps, but streets decide

New technology will play a role: sensors that time traffic lights, apps that combine buses, bikes and trains, and data that shows where heat or flooding will hit. These are the subject of Smart cities. Self-driving cars may help or hurt: if they are shared, they could cut car ownership; if they are private, they could make long commutes more comfortable and encourage more sprawl.

But the history of cities suggests that the biggest changes are physical and political. The width of a street, the price of parking, the rules about what can be built where, and the frequency of the bus decide more than any app. Curitiba, Copenhagen and Paris changed through decisions by mayors who were prepared to argue for them. That is encouraging, because it means the tools already exist. It is also a challenge, because each city has to win the argument for itself.

The core idea

Cities produce most emissions but are also the most efficient way to live. A sustainable city uses density, walking, cycling and public transport, efficient buildings, green and blue infrastructure and affordable housing to cut energy use and stay safe as the climate changes. The hardest part is politics and fairness, not technology.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Roughly half or more of people live in urban areas, depending on definition, rising further by 2050.
  • Urban areas produce about two-thirds to three-quarters of energy-related CO₂ (IPCC).
  • About a billion people live in informal settlements.
The common mistake

Thinking that more roads cure traffic, or that a sustainable city needs futuristic technology. Extra lanes fill up again (induced demand), and the proven tools are mostly old ones: bus lanes, cycle paths, density and good buildings.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a resident of Manhattan may have a lower carbon footprint than a resident of a leafy suburb, even though the suburb looks greener? If so, you understand density.

The words, in plain words
Density
How many people or homes there are per area of land.
Sprawl
Low-density growth spreading outward from a city, usually built around the car.
Bus rapid transit (BRT)
Buses with their own lanes and train-like stations, giving metro-like service at lower cost.
Induced demand
The extra traffic created by building more road space.
Congestion charge
A fee for driving into a busy area at busy times.
Operational carbon
Emissions from running a building: heating, cooling, lighting.
Embodied carbon
Emissions from making materials and constructing a building.
District heating
A network that delivers hot water from central plants to heat many buildings.
Urban heat island
The extra warmth of a city compared with the surrounding countryside.
Green infrastructure
Parks, trees and green roofs used to cool, clean and drain a city.
Sponge city
A city designed to absorb and store rain rather than letting it flood.
Fifteen-minute city
A plan to put daily needs within fifteen minutes' walk or cycle of home.
Green gentrification
When environmental improvements raise prices and push out poorer residents.
Informal settlement
A neighbourhood built without formal permission or services, often called a slum.
