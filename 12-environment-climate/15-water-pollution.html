Water pollution. Everything flows downhill, and so does everything we put in it.

Water is the great solvent and the great carrier. It dissolves salts, metals and chemicals, and it carries soil, sewage and germs along with it. For most of history that made rivers the obvious place to get rid of waste: the river took it away. The trouble is that someone else always lives downstream. Water pollution has killed more people than any war, through diseases such as cholera. It is also a field with real victories, where rivers once declared dead now have fish, seals and swimmers again.

Question 1 · What is it?

Anything that makes water unsafe for life or use

Water pollution is any substance or change, added by people, that harms the living things in water or makes it unsafe for drinking, farming, fishing or swimming.

Chapter 01

The pump handle and the Great Stink

In the summer of 1854, cholera swept through Soho in central London and killed hundreds of people in a few days. Most doctors believed diseases like cholera spread through bad air, or "miasma". A doctor called John Snow had a different idea. He marked each death on a map and found that they clustered around one public water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded local officials to remove the pump's handle, and the outbreak, already declining, ended. Later it emerged that the well sat close to a leaking cesspit.

Four years later, in the hot summer of 1858, the River Thames, full of the city's sewage, smelled so bad that Parliament hung curtains soaked in chemicals over its windows. The "Great Stink" finally pushed the government to act. The engineer Joseph Bazalgette built a network of large sewers that carried waste far downstream of central London. It was built to stop the smell, but it also helped end cholera in the city, which shows how much public health owes to engineering.

Chapter 03

Pipes and fields: point and non-point sources

Pollution experts divide sources into two kinds. A point source comes out of one identifiable place, such as a factory outfall pipe or a sewage works. It is relatively easy to measure, license and fine. A non-point source is spread over a wide area: rain washing fertiliser off thousands of fields, oil off roads, or soil off building sites. No single pipe is to blame.

This distinction explains the history of water policy in rich countries. Laws from the 1970s onward cleaned up point sources dramatically. Non-point pollution, especially from farming, is now the bigger problem, and it is much harder to control, because it means changing the behaviour of millions of separate land users.

Question 2 · How does it work?

How each pollutant does its damage

Different pollutants harm water in different ways. Some poison directly, some starve water of oxygen, and some build up quietly through the food chain.

Chapter 04

Too much food: eutrophication and dead zones

Algae, the tiny plants of lakes and seas, are usually held back by a lack of nutrients, mainly nitrogen and phosphorus. Add fertiliser runoff or sewage and you remove that limit. The result is a chain of events called eutrophication, from the Greek for "well fed".

The best-known example is in the Gulf of Mexico, where the Mississippi River carries runoff from the American farm belt. Each summer a low-oxygen zone forms off Louisiana, often covering more than 10,000 square kilometres. The Baltic Sea has some of the world's largest dead zones. Scientists have counted hundreds of such areas around the world's coasts. A dead zone is the ocean end of a problem that starts on a farm hundreds of kilometres away.

Some algal blooms are directly toxic. In August 2014, a bloom in Lake Erie produced a toxin called microcystin near the water intake for Toledo, Ohio. About half a million people were told not to drink or even boil their tap water for several days.

Chapter 05

Oxygen: the number that tells you if a river is alive

Fish breathe oxygen dissolved in water. Cold, fast-flowing water holds plenty; warm, still water holds much less. When sewage or other organic waste enters a river, bacteria start to digest it, and they use oxygen to do so. The amount of oxygen they need is called the biochemical oxygen demand, or BOD. High BOD means the waste will strip oxygen from the river.

This is how sewage killed the Thames. In 1957 scientists from London's Natural History Museum declared the tidal river "biologically dead": in long stretches there was almost no oxygen and no fish. Better sewage treatment changed that. By the 2000s the Thames supported over a hundred species of fish, and seals and porpoises are regularly seen in the estuary. Rivers can recover, sometimes within decades, once the oxygen comes back.

Chapter 06

Poisons that climb the food chain: the Minamata story

From the 1930s, a chemical factory owned by the Chisso company released waste containing mercury into Minamata Bay in southern Japan. In the bay, bacteria turned the metal into methylmercury, a form that living things absorb easily and do not get rid of. Plankton took it in, small fish ate the plankton, larger fish ate the small fish, and people ate the larger fish.

At each step the concentration rose, a process called biomagnification. In 1956 doctors officially identified a new disease: people, cats and birds were suffering numbness, loss of control of their bodies, and brain damage. Babies were born with severe disabilities even when their mothers showed few symptoms. Thousands of people were eventually recognised as victims. The disease was named after the town, and in 2013 a global treaty to control mercury, the Minamata Convention, took its name too.

Biomagnification is why large predatory fish such as swordfish and some tuna carry more mercury than small fish, and why health agencies in many countries give pregnant women advice on which fish to limit.

Chapter 07

Groundwater: the hidden reservoir

About half of the world's people depend on groundwater, water stored underground in porous rock and gravel called aquifers, for at least part of their drinking water. Groundwater moves very slowly, sometimes only metres a year. That slowness is a strength when it filters out germs. It is a weakness when pollution gets in, because it can stay for decades.

The largest case of groundwater poisoning was not caused by industry at all. From the 1970s, aid agencies and the government of Bangladesh drilled millions of shallow tube wells to give people water free of the germs in surface ponds. It worked against diarrhoea. But in the 1990s, tests showed that many wells drew water with high levels of arsenic, released naturally from the sediments underground. Tens of millions of people were exposed. The World Health Organization described it as the largest mass poisoning of a population in history. Solving one water problem without testing for others can create a new one.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Health, ecosystems, and trust in the tap

Unsafe water still kills on a large scale. In rich countries, the fights are now over older pipes, overflowing sewers and chemicals that refuse to break down.

Chapter 08

The global burden

According to the WHO and UNICEF monitoring programme, roughly 2 billion people in the early 2020s did not have safely managed drinking water, and about 3.5 billion lacked safely managed sanitation, meaning a toilet whose waste is safely contained or treated. Diarrhoeal disease linked to unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene kills hundreds of thousands of people a year, many of them young children. The public health side is covered in Sanitation and Clean water.

A large share of the world's sewage is still released without proper treatment. UN estimates in the early 2020s suggested that close to half of household wastewater is not safely treated. A much-repeated claim that "80%" of wastewater is untreated is older and poorly sourced, so it is best avoided.

Chapter 09

Rich countries are not finished

Three recent stories show that wealthy countries still struggle.

Flint, 2014

To save money, the city of Flint, Michigan, switched its water supply to the Flint River without adding a standard chemical that stops pipes corroding. The water dissolved lead from old pipes, and children were exposed. Residents' complaints were dismissed for over a year. Flint became a symbol of how pollution and neglect fall on poorer communities.

Sewage in British rivers

England's water companies use storm overflows that release untreated sewage into rivers and the sea when pipes are overloaded. Official data showed hundreds of thousands of spill events a year in the early 2020s. Public anger about this became a major political issue.

Forever chemicals

PFAS are thousands of related chemicals used in non-stick pans, waterproof clothing and firefighting foam. They barely break down and are now found in water and blood almost everywhere. In 2023, the company 3M agreed to pay over 10 billion dollars to settle claims from US water utilities.

These cases share a feature. The technology to provide safe water existed in every case. What failed was maintenance, money or oversight. Water pollution is often less a scientific problem than a governance problem.

Chapter 10

The river that caught fire

In June 1969, oil and debris on the surface of the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, caught fire. It was not a big fire, and it was not even the first: the river had burned several times before, including a much worse fire in 1952. But it came at the right moment. Magazines published dramatic photographs, public concern about the environment was rising, and the story became a national symbol.

Within a few years the United States created the Environmental Protection Agency (1970) and passed the Clean Water Act (1972), which set out to make rivers fishable and swimmable. Europe followed its own path. After a fire at a chemical warehouse near Basel in 1986 sent toxic water into the Rhine and killed huge numbers of fish, the countries along the river agreed a joint programme to restore it. Salmon, which had vanished from the Rhine, have since been reintroduced in small numbers. Disasters that people can see often unlock the laws that fix the damage they cannot see.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What works, and what it costs

The tools for cleaning water are well known. The arguments are about who pays, how fast, and how strict to be.

Chapter 11

The main approaches, weighed

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
Sewage treatment plantsProven; turned dead rivers into living ones.Expensive to build and run; old networks overflow in heavy rain.
Permits and fines for point sourcesClear responsibility; easy to monitor.Only as strong as enforcement; fines can be cheaper than compliance.
Farm rules and incentivesTackle the biggest remaining source: fertiliser and manure runoff.Politically hard; farmers face costs and lost yields. The Dutch nitrogen protests from 2019 show how fierce this can become.
Nature-based fixes (wetlands, riverside strips of trees)Cheap; filter nutrients and sediment; help wildlife and flooding too.Need land; slower; results vary by site.
Banning chemicals at sourceCheapest in the long run: stop it entering at all. Phosphate detergent bans helped many lakes.Slow, contested chemical by chemical; replacements can be just as bad.

One general lesson stands out. Prevention is far cheaper than cleanup. Removing PFAS from drinking water needs costly filters for decades. Not releasing them in the first place would have cost a fraction of that. The same is true of fertiliser: keeping it on the field saves farmers money and saves fishermen downstream.

Chapter 13

The lesson of a century of cleanup

Water pollution history repeats a pattern. A new substance is introduced because it is useful. It spreads widely. Years later, evidence of harm builds up. The harm is denied or played down, then eventually regulated, and the substance is replaced, sometimes by a cousin that later turns out to be harmful too. This happened with lead, with certain pesticides and now with PFAS.

The other pattern is more hopeful. Every major river cleanup started when people decided that a river was a place to live beside, not a drain. London, Cleveland, the Rhine countries and many others reached that decision after a crisis. In July 2025, Paris reopened parts of the Seine to public swimming for the first time in about a century, after years of investment in sewers and treatment. The question for the coming decades is whether the rest of the world can reach the same point without waiting for its own cholera summer or burning river.

The core idea

Water pollution comes in kinds: germs, nutrients, organic waste, metals, synthetic chemicals, sediment and heat. Nutrients cause eutrophication and dead zones; organic waste uses up oxygen; metals like mercury biomagnify up the food chain. Point sources have largely been tamed in rich countries; diffuse farm runoff has not.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water in the early 2020s.
  • About 3.5 billion lacked safely managed sanitation.
  • The Gulf of Mexico dead zone often exceeds 10,000 km² in summer.
The common mistake

Picturing water pollution as factory pipes. Today the biggest source in many rich countries is farmland, and the biggest killer worldwide is still untreated sewage.

The words, in plain words
Pathogen
A germ that causes disease, such as a bacterium, virus or parasite.
Eutrophication
Over-enrichment of water with nutrients, causing algae to grow and oxygen to fall.
Algal bloom
A sudden, dense growth of algae, sometimes toxic.
Dead zone
An area of water with too little oxygen for most animals to survive.
Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)
How much oxygen bacteria need to break down the waste in water.
Point source
Pollution from one identifiable place, such as a pipe.
Non-point source
Pollution spread over a wide area, such as runoff from fields.
Biomagnification
The rise in concentration of a poison at each step up the food chain.
Methylmercury
A form of mercury that living things absorb easily; the cause of Minamata disease.
Aquifer
Underground rock or gravel that holds water and can be tapped by wells.
PFAS
A family of thousands of very persistent man-made chemicals, nicknamed "forever chemicals".
Storm overflow
An outlet that releases untreated sewage into rivers when sewers are overloaded.
