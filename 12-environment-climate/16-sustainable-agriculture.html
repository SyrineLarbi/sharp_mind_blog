Sustainable agriculture. Feeding everyone this year without borrowing from the harvests of the future.

Farming is the largest thing humans do to the planet. It uses roughly half of all the land that is not ice or desert, most of the fresh water we take from rivers and aquifers, and it produces about a quarter to a third of greenhouse gas emissions. It is also the reason eight billion people can eat. Sustainable agriculture asks a simple question with hard answers: how can farming keep producing enough food, decade after decade, without wearing out the soil, draining the water, wiping out wildlife and heating the climate?

Question 1 · What is it?

Farming that can keep going

"Sustainable" is used so loosely that it can mean almost anything. In farming it has a fairly precise core: producing enough food while keeping the natural systems farming depends on in working order.

Chapter 01

When the sky turned black

In the early 1930s, a long drought hit the southern Great Plains of the United States. For decades before, settlers had ploughed up the deep-rooted native grasses that held the soil in place and planted wheat. When the rain failed, there was nothing to hold the dry soil. Strong winds lifted it into huge dust storms. On one day in April 1935, remembered as "Black Sunday", a wall of dust rolled across the plains and turned day into night. Dust from the plains reached as far as the East Coast.

The Dust Bowl drove hundreds of thousands of people from their farms. It also taught a lesson that every farming culture eventually learns. Soil is not just dirt. It is a living, slowly built resource, and it can be lost in a few seasons. In 1935 the United States created a Soil Conservation Service to teach farmers to plough along contours, plant windbreaks of trees, and keep the ground covered.

Chapter 02

Three goals at once

Most definitions of sustainable agriculture rest on three aims, which pull in different directions.

Enough food

Yields high enough to feed a growing population, at prices poor people can afford. A system that protects nature but leaves people hungry is not sustainable either.

A healthy environment

Soil that stays fertile, water that stays clean and plentiful, wildlife that survives, and emissions low enough not to destabilise the climate.

Viable livelihoods

Farmers who can make a living. A method that works in theory but bankrupts the people who use it will not spread.

Every argument about farming is, at heart, an argument about how to balance these three. Keep that in mind and the debates in this file become much easier to follow.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The farm as a set of cycles

A farm moves nutrients, water and carbon around. Sustainable methods try to keep those cycles closed, so less is lost and less has to be added from outside.

Chapter 03

The Green Revolution, and the bill that came later

To understand where farming is going, start with where it came from. In the 1940s and 1950s the American plant breeder Norman Borlaug, working in Mexico, developed wheat varieties with short, sturdy stems. Tall wheat given lots of fertiliser grows heavy heads of grain and falls over. Borlaug's "semi-dwarf" wheat stayed upright and turned fertiliser into grain. In the 1960s these seeds, and similar rice varieties from the Philippines, spread to India, Pakistan and much of Asia, together with irrigation, synthetic fertiliser and pesticides. Borlaug won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.

This Green Revolution roughly tripled world cereal yields over the following decades. Famines that experts had predicted for South Asia did not happen on the scale feared. It is one of the great achievements of the twentieth century.

But it left bills. Heavy fertiliser use led to runoff and dead zones (see Water pollution). Free or cheap electricity for pumps let farmers in places such as India's Punjab draw groundwater faster than rain refills it, so water tables have been falling. Growing the same crops on the same fields year after year encouraged pests and weakened soils. The Green Revolution solved the problem of too little food by creating problems of too much of everything else. Sustainable agriculture is largely an attempt to keep its yields while paying off those bills.

Chapter 04

Looking after the soil

Healthy soil is about half mineral grains and half spaces filled with air and water, with a small but crucial share of organic matter: decaying plant material and the vast community of bacteria, fungi and worms that live on it. Organic matter holds water like a sponge, stores nutrients, and binds soil grains into crumbs that resist wind and rain. Ploughing, bare ground and erosion destroy it.

The main methods to protect soil are old ideas, now backed by research:

MethodWhat it meansWhy it helps
Crop rotationGrowing different crops on a field in a sequence over the yearsBreaks pest and disease cycles; different roots and needs balance the soil

Chapter 05

Using less, more precisely

A large share of the nitrogen fertiliser applied to fields worldwide is not taken up by crops. It washes into water or escapes into the air, partly as nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas far stronger than carbon dioxide. Losing it is bad for the environment and bad for the farmer, who paid for it.

Precision agriculture tries to put exactly what each part of a field needs, where and when it needs it. Tractors guided by satellite positioning apply fertiliser at different rates across a field, using soil maps and images from drones or satellites. Drip irrigation delivers water through pipes straight to plant roots instead of flooding the whole field, cutting water use a great deal. China achieved a notable result with a simpler approach: a large programme in the 2000s and 2010s that gave millions of smallholder farmers local advice on how much fertiliser they really needed raised yields while cutting fertiliser use.

Integrated pest management applies the same logic to pests. Instead of spraying on a schedule, farmers monitor fields, encourage natural predators, rotate crops, and use pesticides only when pests pass a damage threshold. In East Africa, a method called "push-pull" plants a repellent plant among maize and an attractive grass around the edge, which draws a damaging moth away from the crop.

Chapter 06

Animals: the biggest lever

The most important number in food and the environment comes from a large 2018 study by Joseph Poore and Thomas Nemecek, which pulled together data from about 38,000 farms. It found that livestock and their feed use more than three-quarters of the world's farmland but supply less than a fifth of its calories. Beef and lamb have by far the largest land and climate footprints per gram of protein. Cattle and sheep digest grass with the help of microbes in their stomachs, and this releases methane, a powerful greenhouse gas.

This does not mean all animal farming is bad. Grazing animals can turn grass on land unsuitable for crops into food, and manure can return nutrients to the soil. But it explains why changes in diet, especially in rich countries that eat a lot of meat, can make a bigger difference than almost any change in farming method. Feed additives that reduce cattle methane, plant-based and fermented alternatives, and better grazing management are all being tried.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Land, water, wildlife and climate all run through the farm

Almost every environmental problem in this category, from deforestation to dead zones, leads back to how we grow food.

The emissions range is wide because studies draw the boundary differently: some count only the farm, others include land clearing, transport, packaging and cooking. Either way, the world cannot meet its climate goals by cleaning up energy alone. Food has to change too. The links to forests and wildlife are in Deforestation and Biodiversity.

Chapter 08

Sri Lanka: when good intentions moved too fast

In April 2021, the government of Sri Lanka banned imports of synthetic fertilisers and pesticides, aiming to make the country's farming fully organic. The move was also driven by a shortage of foreign currency. Farmers had little time to prepare, and organic fertiliser was not available in anything like the amounts needed.

Yields of rice, the staple food, fell sharply in the following season, and the tea industry, a major export, was hit too. The government reversed much of the ban within about seven months. The episode fed into the country's wider economic crisis of 2022. The lesson is not that organic farming fails. It is that a whole farming system cannot be switched overnight, and that soil fertility lost by removing fertiliser must be replaced from somewhere.

Chapter 09

The people who grow most of the food

Most of the world's farms are small. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that the large majority of the world's roughly 600 million farms are under two hectares, about the size of three football pitches. Many smallholders in Africa and South Asia are poor themselves, and they face falling soil fertility, little access to credit, and growing climate shocks.

For them, sustainable agriculture is not a lifestyle choice. It is about whether the land will still feed the family in twenty years. Methods that cost little, such as cover crops, legumes and agroforestry, often fit them well. Methods that need expensive machines or certification often do not. A sustainable food system has to work for a farmer with one hectare and no tractor, not only for a large farm in Iowa or Brazil.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The arguments that do not go away

Most methods have clear benefits. The genuine disagreements are about yields, land and labels.

Chapter 11

Land sparing or land sharing?

One of the most important debates in conservation is easy to state. Should we farm some land very intensively, to get high yields, and leave the rest wild? This is land sparing. Or should we farm more gently everywhere, with hedges, wildflowers and fewer chemicals, so that wildlife can live on the farms themselves? This is land sharing.

Studies in several countries have found that many species, especially those that need large undisturbed areas, do better under land sparing. But it only works if the spared land is actually protected. Otherwise higher yields can simply make farming more profitable and encourage more clearing. Land sharing supports pollinators and the species that live alongside people. Most experts now suggest a mix that depends on the place. The honest answer is that the best farm for nature depends on what happens to the land next to it.

Chapter 13

The hardest part is politics

Farmers are few in rich countries but politically powerful, and food prices affect everyone. When governments push too fast, they meet resistance. In the Netherlands, a 2019 court ruling forced the government to cut nitrogen pollution, and plans to reduce livestock numbers set off large farmer protests and the rise of a new farmers' party. In 2024, after protests by farmers across Europe, the European Commission withdrew a proposal to halve pesticide use by 2030.

The deeper problem is that the price of food does not include most of its environmental costs. A farmer who pollutes a river pays nothing for the damage downstream, while one who plants a buffer of trees bears the cost alone. Many economists argue that public farm subsidies, which are very large in the EU, US, China and India, should increasingly pay farmers for clean water, healthy soil and wildlife, not only for producing more. Some countries have started down that path. Whether they go further is a political choice, not a technical one.

The good news is that many of the most sustainable changes also save farmers money: using less fertiliser, losing less soil, wasting less water. The challenge is mostly the cost and risk of switching, and that is something good policy can share.

The core idea

Sustainable agriculture means producing enough food while keeping soil, water, wildlife and climate healthy and farmers viable. The Green Revolution solved hunger at a large environmental cost. Today's methods (rotation, cover crops, less tillage, precision inputs, less meat) try to keep the yields and pay off the bill.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Farming uses about half of habitable land and about 70% of freshwater withdrawals.
  • Livestock use over three-quarters of farmland for less than a fifth of calories.
  • Roughly a third of food is lost or wasted.
The common mistake

Treating "organic" as the same as "sustainable". Organic has real benefits but usually lower yields, so it can need more land for the same food. What you eat often matters more than how it was farmed.

The words, in plain words
Green Revolution
The spread of high-yield crop varieties, fertiliser and irrigation from the 1960s onward.
Soil organic matter
The living and decaying material in soil that holds water and nutrients.
Crop rotation
Growing different crops on the same field in sequence.
Cover crop
A crop grown mainly to protect and feed the soil between main harvests.
No-till farming
Planting without ploughing, to keep the soil undisturbed.
Legume
A plant, such as beans or clover, whose root bacteria take nitrogen from the air.
Agroforestry
Combining trees with crops or livestock on the same land.
Precision agriculture
Using data and machines to apply water, fertiliser and pesticide exactly where needed.
Integrated pest management
Controlling pests by monitoring and natural methods first, and chemicals only when needed.
Nitrous oxide
A strong greenhouse gas released from fertilised soils.
Regenerative agriculture
A loose term for farming focused on rebuilding soil health.
Land sparing
Farming some land intensively so other land can stay wild.
Land sharing
Farming gently everywhere so wildlife can live on farmland.
