Plastic pollution. The problem is not that plastic breaks. It is that it never quite finishes breaking.

Plastic is one of the most useful materials ever invented. It is cheap, light, clean, waterproof and strong, and it keeps food fresh and medicine sterile. Those same qualities are what make it a pollutant. A material built to resist water, sunlight and bacteria does not go away when we throw it out. It moves, it fragments, and it spreads into places nobody intended: the deep ocean, farm soil, rain, and human blood. This file explains where plastic goes, what it does when it gets there, and which fixes work.

Question 1 · What is it?

A miracle material in the wrong place

Plastic pollution is plastic that has escaped the systems meant to hold it, and is now in the environment, where it lasts for a very long time.

Chapter 01

From billiard balls to everything

The first fully synthetic plastic, Bakelite, was invented by Leo Baekeland in 1907. It was hard, heat-resistant and good for telephones and radios. But plastic only became a part of daily life after the Second World War, when chemical companies that had grown during the war needed new markets. Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC and polystyrene moved from factories into kitchens, shops and hospitals.

The scale since then is hard to picture. A widely cited 2017 study by Roland Geyer, Jenna Jambeck and Kara Lavender Law estimated that humans had made about 8.3 billion tonnes of plastic by 2015, and that about half of it was made in just the previous thirteen years. Production is now well over 400 million tonnes a year. The single largest use is packaging, which is designed to be used once and thrown away within minutes or days.

That is the root of the problem in one sentence. We use a material that lasts for centuries to make products that last for minutes.

Chapter 02

Big pieces, small pieces, invisible pieces

Scientists sort plastic pollution by size, because size decides where it goes and what it can harm.

CategorySizeExamplesMain harm
MacroplasticLarger than about 5 mmBottles, bags, fishing nets, food wrappersEntangles and is swallowed by animals; blocks drains; litters land and beaches
MicroplasticSmaller than 5 mmFragments of bottles, fibres from clothes, tyre dust, factory pelletsEaten by small animals; carries chemicals; spreads through soil, water and air
NanoplasticSmaller than about 1 micrometre (a thousandth of a millimetre)The smallest breakdown productsSmall enough to cross into cells and tissues; hardest to measure

Microplastics come in two kinds. Primary microplastics are made small on purpose, such as the tiny beads once used in face scrubs, or the lentil-sized pellets, called nurdles, that factories melt to make products. Secondary microplastics come from bigger items breaking down: a bottle in the sun, a synthetic jumper in a washing machine, a car tyre on the road. Most microplastic in the environment is secondary.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Where plastic goes after the bin

Most people imagine plastic waste as a simple line from shop to bin to dump. In reality it splits into several paths, and only one of them is the loop people hope for.

Chapter 03

The four destinations

Every piece of plastic waste ends up in one of four places. It is recycled, burned (ideally in a plant that captures energy and cleans the smoke), buried in a managed landfill, or mismanaged, which means dumped, burned in the open, or lost into the environment.

Two lessons come out of this picture. First, recycling is the smallest path, not the main one. Volume 15 explains why: sorting is hard, mixed plastics are nearly worthless, and new plastic is often cheaper than recycled plastic. Second, the "mismanaged" share is the one that becomes pollution. It is not spread evenly. It is largest where waste collection is weakest: in fast-growing cities in parts of Asia and Africa, where many people have no rubbish collection at all.

Chapter 04

Rivers are the conveyor belts

Plastic dropped in a street does not stay there. Rain washes it into drains, drains run into rivers, and rivers carry it to the sea. A 2015 study led by Jenna Jambeck estimated that between about 5 and 13 million tonnes of plastic entered the ocean from land in 2010. Later estimates differ, but all agree the figure is in the millions of tonnes each year.

Research since then suggests that the flow comes from a very large number of rivers, many of them small rivers through dense cities, rather than from a few giant ones. That matters for solutions. Stopping plastic at the source, on land, works far better than trying to collect it at sea, where it has already spread out.

The garbage patch myth

The "Great Pacific Garbage Patch" is not a floating island you could walk on. It is a huge area where ocean currents, called gyres, collect floating debris. Most of it is scattered pieces, many of them small, spread through the water. By weight, a large share is lost fishing gear. Satellites cannot see it as a solid mass, because there is no solid mass to see. The sea floor and coastlines may hold far more plastic than the floating patches. The ocean side of the story is in Ocean pollution.

Chapter 05

Breaking down, but never away

Paper and food scraps are eaten by bacteria and fungi, which turn them back into carbon dioxide, water and soil. Most plastics are not. Their long carbon chains are unfamiliar to microbes, because they did not exist in nature until a century ago. So plastic does not biodegrade in any useful time. It fragments. Sunlight makes the surface brittle. Waves, sand and heat crack it. A bottle becomes flakes, flakes become grains, grains become dust.

Each split makes more pieces, with more surface area, and smaller pieces travel further. That is why microplastics have now been found in Arctic sea ice, in snow near the summit of Everest, and in sediment at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the deepest place in the ocean. Fragmenting turns a visible litter problem into an invisible contamination problem.

Chapter 06

The sources nobody pictures

When people think of plastic pollution, they picture bottles on a beach. But some of the largest sources of microplastic do not look like litter at all.

Tyres

Car tyres are made partly of synthetic rubber and plastics. Every kilometre driven sheds fine dust from the tread. Rain washes it from roads into rivers. Studies across several countries rank tyre wear among the largest sources of microplastic. Electric cars do not avoid this; being heavier, they can shed somewhat more.

Clothes

Polyester, nylon and acrylic shed tiny fibres, especially in the first washes. Many pass through sewage plants. Those that are caught end up in sewage sludge, which is often spread on farmland as fertiliser, moving the plastic from water to soil.

Pellets and paint

Nurdles spill during transport, sometimes by the billion when a container ship loses cargo. Paint on buildings, roads and ship hulls also flakes into the environment. These sources are less visible, and they are harder to regulate.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Animals, chemicals, people and climate

The harms of plastic range from obvious and well proven to subtle and still uncertain. Keeping them separate is the key to talking about plastic sensibly.

Chapter 07

The harm we can see: wildlife

The best-documented harm is to animals. Seabirds, turtles, whales and fish swallow plastic, mistaking it for food. A turtle can confuse a floating bag with a jellyfish. Albatross parents feed plastic fragments to their chicks, and dead chicks on remote Pacific islands have been found with their stomachs full. Plastic in the gut can block digestion and make an animal feel full while it starves.

Entanglement is the other big harm. Lost or abandoned fishing nets, often called ghost gear, keep catching fish, seals, turtles and dolphins for years after they are lost. This is one reason many scientists say the fishing industry deserves more attention than drinking straws, even though straws get far more headlines.

Chapter 08

The harm we are still measuring: human health

Microplastics are now in us. Researchers have reported them in human blood, lungs, placentas, breast milk and arteries. We take them in through food, drinking water (bottled water often contains more particles than tap water) and the air, especially indoors, where clothes, carpets and furniture shed fibres.

What this does to health is genuinely unknown. A 2024 study in the New England Journal of Medicine found that patients whose artery plaque contained micro- and nanoplastics had a higher risk of heart attack, stroke or death over the following few years. That is an association, not proof that the plastic caused the harm, and it needs confirming. Measuring tiny particles is also difficult, and some laboratory methods can overcount or be contaminated by plastic in the lab itself.

The chemicals in plastic are a separate and better-studied concern. Plastics contain additives: substances that make them soft, coloured, fire-resistant or stable. Some, such as certain phthalates (softeners) and bisphenol A (BPA), can interfere with hormones. Such substances are called endocrine disruptors. The European Union banned BPA in baby bottles in 2011 and has since moved to restrict it more widely in food contact materials. The honest summary is the one Volume 15 gives: "we do not know" is not the same as "it is fine", and it is not the same as cause for panic.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. The practical steps that most experts consider sensible are modest: avoid heating food in plastic containers not meant for it, and replace scratched or ageing food containers.

Chapter 09

The harm people forget: climate and the poor

About 99% of plastic is made from fossil fuels: oil and natural gas. Making, shipping and disposing of it releases greenhouse gases at every step. The OECD estimated that plastics were responsible for about 3–4% of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2019, more than aviation. As oil demand for transport may fall with electric cars, oil companies see plastics and petrochemicals as a major growth market. This is why plastic is a climate issue, not only a litter issue.

The burden also falls unevenly. In 2018 China, which had been importing a large share of the rich world's plastic scrap, banned most of those imports under a policy known as "National Sword". The scrap was redirected to countries such as Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand and Turkey, some of which then restricted imports too. Much of it was never recycled. It was dumped or burned in the open, close to where people live. Open burning releases toxic smoke, and it is common wherever there is no rubbish collection. In many poorer cities, most of the plastic that does get recycled is collected by informal waste pickers, who do valuable work for very little money and with little protection.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Plastic is not the villain. Throwaway plastic is.

Any honest discussion has to weigh what plastic does for us against what it costs, and compare it with real alternatives, not imaginary perfect ones.

Chapter 11

Why the obvious swaps are not always better

Replacing plastic with another material sounds simple. It often is not. Lifecycle studies, which add up all the impacts of a product from raw materials to disposal, regularly find that a paper bag or a cotton tote has a larger climate and water footprint than a thin plastic bag if each is used only once. A cotton bag may need to be reused many dozens or even hundreds of times to come out ahead on climate, and more on some other measures. Glass bottles are heavy, so they need more fuel to transport.

This does not mean plastic bags are good. A plastic bag that ends up in a river does damage that a paper bag does not. It means the real answer is usually reuse, not substitution. Whatever you carry your shopping in, the greenest bag is the one you already own.

"Bioplastics" need similar care. The term covers plastics made from plants, and plastics that can biodegrade, which are not the same thing. Many plant-based plastics do not biodegrade at all. Many "compostable" plastics break down only in industrial composting plants at high temperatures, not in a garden or the sea. In a 2019 University of Plymouth experiment, some bags labelled biodegradable could still carry a load of shopping after three years in soil or seawater.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Treaties, taxes, deposits and enzymes

Some fixes have a long record of working. Others are promising but unproven. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 12

What already works

The clearest successes come from small price signals and simple systems. Ireland introduced a levy on plastic bags in 2002, and their use fell by about 90% within months. England's 5p charge, introduced in 2015, cut bag sales in large supermarkets by more than 95% over the following years. Some countries went further with bans. Rwanda banned plastic bags in 2008, and Kenya introduced one of the world's strictest bans in 2017.

Deposit return schemes are another proven tool. You pay a small extra amount for a bottle or can and get it back when you return it. Germany's scheme returns well over 90% of covered bottles, and so do several Nordic schemes. The deposit turns an empty bottle into money, so people return it, or pick it up off the street.

Above all, the biggest lever is waste collection. A large share of ocean plastic comes from places where no one collects rubbish at all. Basic, reliable collection in fast-growing cities would likely do more for the oceans than any number of straw bans in Europe. The European Union's Single-Use Plastics Directive, which banned items such as plastic cutlery, plates and straws from 2021, targets the items most often found on European beaches. It is useful, but it is not the main global lever.

Chapter 14

The question underneath: less, or better managed?

Behind every plastic debate sits one disagreement. One side says the problem is management: plastic is valuable, and the task is to collect it, recycle it and keep it out of nature. The other side says the problem is volume: production is expected to keep rising for decades, and no waste system anywhere has ever kept up with that growth. On this view, the only lasting fix is to make less, especially less single-use packaging.

Both sides have a point. Better collection is urgent where none exists, and less throwaway plastic is needed where collection already works but recycling does not. The sensible position is "use less, and manage the rest properly", in that order. It is the same hierarchy Volume 15 ends on: reduce, then reuse, then recycle.

The core idea

Plastic pollution is plastic that has escaped waste systems. Because plastic fragments instead of biodegrading, it spreads from visible litter into microplastics found almost everywhere. The proven harms are to wildlife; the effects on human health are still uncertain; and plastic's fossil-fuel origin makes it a climate issue too.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Over 400 million tonnes of plastic are made each year; packaging is the largest use.
  • Under one tenth of plastic waste is recycled worldwide (OECD, 2019).
  • Plastics cause roughly 3–4% of global greenhouse gas emissions.
The common mistake

Thinking that recycling solves the problem, or that switching to paper or "bioplastic" does. Reuse and reduction beat substitution, and collection matters more than any single ban.

The words, in plain words
Polymer
A long chain molecule made of many repeating units. All plastics are polymers.
Microplastic
A piece of plastic smaller than 5 millimetres.
Nanoplastic
A piece smaller than about a thousandth of a millimetre, small enough to enter cells.
Nurdle
A small plastic pellet, the raw form in which plastic is shipped to factories.
Fragmentation
Breaking into smaller and smaller pieces without turning back into natural substances.
Biodegradation
Being broken down by living organisms into water, carbon dioxide and natural material.
Gyre
A huge circular system of ocean currents that collects floating debris.
Ghost gear
Lost or abandoned fishing nets and lines that keep trapping animals.
Additive
A chemical added to plastic to change its properties, such as colour or flexibility.
Endocrine disruptor
A substance that can interfere with the body's hormone system.
Mismanaged waste
Waste that is dumped, burned in the open or lost, rather than collected and treated.
Deposit return scheme
A system where you pay a small deposit on a container and get it back when you return it.
Extended producer responsibility
Rules that make the companies selling a product pay for its collection and recycling.
