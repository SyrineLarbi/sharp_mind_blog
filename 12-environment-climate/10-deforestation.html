Sharp Mind · Environment & Climate · Topic 10 of 20

Deforestation. Forests are rarely destroyed for their own sake. They are cleared to make room for something.

The usual picture of deforestation is a chainsaw. The more accurate picture is a cattle ranch, a soy field or a palm oil plantation, seen years later. Most forest is cleared because the land is worth more, to someone, as farmland. That makes deforestation an economic problem as much as an ecological one, and it explains both why it is so hard to stop and why some countries have managed to slow it dramatically.

Question 1 · What is it?

Losing forest, and the words for it

Deforestation means turning forest permanently into something else. That simple definition hides important distinctions, and confusing them is the source of many misleading headlines.

Chapter 01

An old story with a new location

Forests cover about 31% of the world's land, roughly 4 billion hectares, according to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) in 2020. A hectare is 100 by 100 metres, about the size of a large football pitch. Since the end of the last ice age, around 10,000 years ago, the world has lost roughly a third of its forests. About half of that loss happened in the last century or so.

For most of history, the clearing happened in what are now rich countries. Much of Europe was once forest. By around 1900, Britain had only a few per cent of its land under trees; today it has about 13%. The eastern United States was largely cleared for farms in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, then partly grew back as farming moved west and people moved to cities. China cleared forests for centuries and has planted vast areas since the 1980s.

Today almost all net forest loss happens in the tropics: the Amazon and the rest of South America, the Congo Basin and West Africa, and Southeast Asia. That shift matters for the argument about who should pay to stop it. Rich countries cleared their forests to grow; tropical countries point out that they are being asked not to.

Net forest lossDeforestation minus new forest gained elsewhereLoss in Brazil partly offset, on paper, by plantations in China

The FAO estimated that about 10 million hectares a year were deforested between 2015 and 2020, down from about 16 million a year in the 1990s. Because forest also grew back or was planted elsewhere, net loss was smaller, about 4.7 million hectares a year over 2010–2020.

The warning in that second number is important. A new plantation does not replace an old rainforest. A rainforest that has stood for thousands of years holds far more carbon and far more species than a young stand of eucalyptus or pine. Net figures can make the problem look smaller than it is for nature, even if they are accurate for area.

Chapter 03

Primary forest: the part you cannot replace

Ecologists distinguish primary forest, which has never been cleared or heavily logged in modern times, from secondary forest, which has grown back after disturbance. Primary tropical forest is the richest land ecosystem on Earth. A single hectare of Amazonian forest can contain hundreds of tree species. A hectare of European forest typically has a handful.

Secondary forest is valuable too. It can recover much of its carbon within a few decades. But its full community of species, including old giant trees, specialist insects and animals, can take centuries to return, if it returns at all. That is why monitoring groups track the loss of tropical primary forest separately. According to Global Forest Watch, run by the World Resources Institute, tropical primary forest loss was roughly 3–4 million hectares a year for much of the past decade, and it rose sharply in 2024, largely because of fires during a record hot, drought-affected year.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Roads, fire, cattle and commodities

Forests are rarely cleared in one step. There is a sequence, and at each stage someone earns money. Knowing the sequence is the key to knowing where to intervene.

Chapter 04

The frontier, step by step

In the Amazon, the classic pattern looks like this.

How a forest frontier moves (typical Amazon pattern)
  1. ROAD        a highway or logging road opens access to the interior
        |
  2. LOGGING     valuable trees taken; the forest is thinned and drier
        |
  3. FIRE        remaining vegetation is cut and burned in the dry season
        |
  4. PASTURE     grass planted; cattle graze, often at low density
        |
  5. CROPS       years later, flatter land may be converted to soy or maize
        |
  6. TITLE       cleared land can be sold or legalised, sometimes after illegal clearing

Step 1 matters most. Studies across the tropics find that most deforestation happens within a few kilometres of a road. In the Brazilian Amazon, maps from space show the famous "fishbone" pattern: a highway as the spine, and side roads as ribs, each one lined with clearings.

Step 6 is the hidden engine. Where land ownership is unclear, clearing forest can be a way to claim land. Cleared, occupied land is worth more than standing forest, and if titles are later granted, the speculator profits. This is why land registration and enforcement are central to controlling deforestation, not just tree protection.

Chapter 05

What the land is cleared for

The FAO estimates that agriculture drives the great majority of deforestation, roughly nine tenths worldwide. Different regions have different drivers.

This table explains why deforestation is linked to global trade. A large share of the beef, soy, palm oil and cocoa produced on recently cleared land is exported. A steak in one country, or a chocolate bar in another, can carry forest loss inside it. That is the logic behind laws that try to stop such goods being imported, discussed in Chapter 15.

Chapter 06

Fire, edges and peat

Healthy tropical rainforest rarely burns on its own. It is too wet. But logging and clearing make it drier, and people use fire to clear land. When a dry year comes, especially during an El Niño (a periodic warming of the Pacific that brings drought to parts of the tropics), fires escape into standing forest. Each fire leaves the forest drier and more flammable, so the next one burns more.

Forest edges also suffer. Where forest meets pasture, wind, heat and light reach inside, large trees die faster, and the forest dries. Research in the Amazon has found such edge effects reaching tens to hundreds of metres into the forest. Breaking a forest into fragments therefore damages much more than the area cleared.

In Indonesia the special problem is peat: waterlogged soil made of partly decayed plants, many metres deep, holding huge amounts of carbon. Draining peat swamps for plantations dries them, and dried peat can smoulder underground for weeks. The fires of 1997–98 and 2015 covered Southeast Asia in a thick haze for months, closing schools and airports, and released very large amounts of carbon.

Chapter 07

Watching from space

Forty years ago, nobody really knew how fast forests were disappearing. Today satellites see almost every patch. Brazil's space research institute, INPE, has measured Amazon clearing every year since 1988 with a system called PRODES, and since 2004 has run a faster alert system, DETER, that flags new clearing within days. In 2013 a team led by the geographer Matthew Hansen published global maps of tree cover change at a resolution of 30 metres, based on the Landsat satellites. That data now powers Global Forest Watch, which anyone can use.

Satellites have limits. They see tree cover, not ownership or legality. They can confuse a harvested plantation with rainforest clearance, and they find it harder to see degradation under an intact canopy. But they changed the politics. When clearing is visible within days, it becomes possible to send inspectors, cut off credit and name buyers.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Carbon, rain, species and people

Forests do several jobs at once. Losing them affects the global climate, regional rainfall, most of the world's land species, and the people who live in and from them.

Chapter 08

Carbon going up in smoke

A tropical forest stores carbon in its trunks, roots and soil. When it is cut and burned, most of that carbon returns to the air as CO2. Land-use change, mostly tropical deforestation, is responsible for roughly a tenth of human CO2 emissions in recent years, according to the Global Carbon Project. If tropical deforestation were a country, its emissions would rank among the largest in the world.

There is a second loss. A standing forest absorbs carbon as it grows; a cleared one does not. So deforestation both adds carbon and removes a sink. The Carbon cycle file explains why some parts of the southeastern Amazon now appear to release more carbon than they absorb.

Chapter 09

Forests that make rain

Trees pull water from the soil and release it from their leaves as vapour, a process called transpiration. In the Amazon, a large share of rainfall is water that has already been through trees once or several times. Moist air from the Atlantic rains on the eastern forest, the trees send the water back up, it falls again further west, and so on across the continent. Brazilian scientists call these moving bands of moisture "flying rivers". Some of that moisture eventually helps water farms and cities in southern Brazil and neighbouring countries.

That leads to a serious concern. If enough forest is lost, the recycling could weaken so much that parts of the rainforest can no longer sustain themselves and turn into a drier, savanna-like landscape. The ecologists Thomas Lovejoy and Carlos Nobre suggested in 2018 that this tipping point might come at 20–25% deforestation of the Amazon, combined with warming and fire. Around a fifth of the original Brazilian Amazon has already been cleared. The threshold is contested: some scientists think the change would be gradual and regional rather than sudden, and the exact number is uncertain. But the direction of the risk is widely accepted.

Chapter 10

Species and people

Tropical forests cover a small share of the land but hold, by most estimates, more than half of the world's land species. Many live only in small areas. When a forest is cleared, they lose their home, and when it is cut into fragments, populations become isolated and more likely to die out. The Biodiversity file explains why habitat loss is the largest driver of species decline.

Forests are also home to people. Hundreds of millions of people depend directly on forests for food, fuel, medicine and income, including many indigenous peoples. In the Amazon, research using satellite data has repeatedly found that deforestation rates are lower inside indigenous territories with recognised land rights than in comparable land outside them. That finding has made secure land rights for indigenous communities a mainstream conservation policy, not only a human rights issue.

Chapter 11

The Brazil experiment

Brazil offers the clearest proof that deforestation responds to policy, in both directions.

Clearing in the Brazilian Amazon peaked in 2004 at nearly 28,000 square kilometres in a year, almost the size of Belgium. The government then launched an action plan. It created protected areas, used the new DETER alerts to send environmental police quickly, blacklisted the municipalities with the most clearing, and cut off farm credit to properties that broke the law. Traders agreed a "soy moratorium" in 2006, refusing to buy soy grown on newly cleared Amazon land. By 2012 annual clearing had fallen by roughly four fifths, while beef and soy production kept growing.

Then it rose again. Enforcement budgets were cut, and from 2019 the government of Jair Bolsonaro openly favoured development of the Amazon. By 2021 annual clearing was back above 13,000 square kilometres. After Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva returned to office in 2023 and restored enforcement, clearing fell again, to around 6,000 square kilometres in the year to mid-2024. The same satellites, the same forest and the same farmers produced very different results depending on political will.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Who gains from clearing, and who pays

For a non-technology topic, the honest question is: what does clearing deliver, and what does it cost? The gains are real and local. The costs are larger, and spread widely.

Chapter 12

The ledger

AspectWhat clearing deliversWhat it costs
FoodLand for beef, soy, palm oil and cocoa, much of it exported.Much cleared land becomes low-productivity pasture; some is abandoned within years.
IncomeJobs and export earnings for farming regions; land for poor families.Gains often go to a few large landholders and speculators.
DevelopmentRoads, towns and services in remote areas.Violence over land, and loss of territory for indigenous and traditional communities.
Climate—About a tenth of human CO2 emissions; loss of a carbon sink.
Water—Weaker rainfall recycling, more erosion, silted rivers, worse floods downstream.
Species—The largest single cause of species loss on land.

The pattern is the core of the problem. The benefits are private and immediate. The costs are shared and delayed. A rancher who clears a hectare gains pasture now. The lost rainfall, carbon and species are spread across a region, a planet and future decades. Economists call this an externality, a cost that falls on people outside the transaction.

Chapter 13

Is planting trees the answer?

Tree-planting campaigns are popular, and some have been large. The Bonn Challenge, launched in 2011, aims to restore hundreds of millions of hectares of degraded land. Many countries and companies have announced plans to plant billions of trees.

The evidence is mixed. Planting the wrong species in the wrong place, such as trees on natural grasslands or fast-growing plantations in place of native forest, can harm biodiversity and water supplies. Many planted seedlings die. And as the Carbon cycle file explains, new trees take decades to store what an old forest held. Ecologists generally agree on an order of priorities: protect existing forest first, let degraded forest regrow naturally where possible, and plant carefully where needed. Natural regrowth is often cheaper and richer in species than planting.

A success story

Costa Rica had one of the highest deforestation rates in Latin America in the mid-twentieth century. From the 1990s it paid landowners to protect and restore forest, funded partly by a fuel tax, and banned the conversion of forest to other uses. Forest cover rose from roughly a quarter of the country in the 1980s to over half today. Ecotourism became a major industry.

Chapter 15

What actually works

Decades of experience suggest a short list of policies that reliably reduce clearing. First, enforcement backed by monitoring, as Brazil showed in the late 2000s. Second, clear land rights, especially for indigenous and local communities, which removes the incentive to clear in order to claim. Third, pressure from buyers: traders, supermarkets and importing countries refusing goods linked to recent clearing. Fourth, making farming more productive on land already cleared, so that more food can be grown without moving the frontier. Brazil's pastures often hold about one animal per hectare, far below what well-managed grassland can support.

None of these is a technology problem. All are questions of politics and money. The encouraging fact is that the world has examples of every one of them working. The discouraging fact is that each has also been reversed when political priorities changed. Forest protection is never finished; it has to be renewed by every government.

The core idea

Deforestation is the permanent conversion of forest to another use, today mostly in the tropics, and mostly for farming: cattle, soy, palm oil and cocoa. It releases carbon, weakens rainfall recycling, and is the biggest cause of species loss on land. It responds strongly to enforcement, land rights and buyers' demands.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Forests cover about 31% of land (FAO, 2020).
  • About 10 million hectares a year deforested in 2015–2020, down from about 16 million in the 1990s.
  • Brazil cut Amazon clearing by about four fifths between 2004 and 2012.
The common mistake

Thinking a new plantation cancels out a lost rainforest. Area can be replaced quickly; carbon and species cannot.
