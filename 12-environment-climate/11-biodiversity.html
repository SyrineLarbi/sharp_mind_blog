Biodiversity. The variety of life is not decoration. It is the machinery.

Biodiversity sounds like a list of animals to feel sorry for. It is better thought of as the parts inventory of the living world: genes, species and whole ecosystems that pollinate crops, clean water, build soil, store carbon and supply medicines. Most of those parts have never been named, and many are disappearing faster than they can be counted. Understanding biodiversity means understanding what holds that machinery together and what is pulling it apart.

Question 1 · What is it?

Variety at three levels

Biodiversity, short for "biological diversity", is the variety of life in all its forms. It is measured in genes, in species and in ecosystems, and much of it is still unknown.

Chapter 01

More life than anyone has counted

Scientists have formally described about two million species. About half of them are insects; beetles alone account for hundreds of thousands. Each year, thousands of new species are named, from deep-sea fish to frogs to fungi.

How many exist in total is unknown. A widely cited 2011 estimate put the number of species of plants, animals and fungi at around 8.7 million, which would mean that most have never been described. Bacteria and other microbes could add vastly more, depending on how a "species" of microbe is defined. The honest summary is that we have named a minority of the life on Earth, and we are losing some of it before we meet it.

The word itself is recent. "Biodiversity" became widely used after a conference in Washington in 1986 and a book of the same name edited by the biologist E. O. Wilson in 1988. By 1992 it was in the title of a global treaty, the Convention on Biological Diversity, signed at the Rio Earth Summit.

Chapter 02

Genes, species, ecosystems

Genetic diversityVariation within a speciesThe thousands of local varieties of riceThe raw material for adapting to disease and climate

Ecosystem diversityThe variety of habitats and communitiesForest, wetland, grassland, reef, desertEach provides different services and holds different species

People usually think only of the middle row. The other two matter as much. A species with very little genetic variation, such as the cheetah, is more vulnerable to disease. A landscape with only one type of ecosystem holds only the species suited to it. Biodiversity is not just how many kinds of life there are, but how different they are from one another.

Chapter 03

Where it is concentrated

Life is not spread evenly. The most striking pattern is the latitudinal gradient: the number of species rises from the poles to the equator. A patch of rainforest in Ecuador or Borneo can hold more tree species than the whole of Canada. Coral reefs are the equivalent in the sea. Explanations include warmth, steady climate over long periods, and abundant energy from sunlight, but scientists still debate the relative weight of each.

In 1988 the British ecologist Norman Myers proposed the idea of biodiversity hotspots: regions with very many species found nowhere else, which have also lost most of their original habitat. Madagascar, the Atlantic Forest of Brazil, the tropical Andes and the islands of Southeast Asia are examples. Hotspots cover a small fraction of the land but hold a large share of its unique plants and animals, which made them a natural priority for conservation money.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Webs, keystones, and what pulls them apart

Species do not live in isolation. They eat, pollinate, shelter and infect each other. That web is why losing one species can change a whole ecosystem, and why variety tends to make ecosystems more stable.

Chapter 04

Keystones: the species that hold up the arch

In the 1960s the American ecologist Robert Paine ran a simple experiment on the rocky shore of Washington State. He kept removing one predator, a starfish, from a stretch of coast and left a neighbouring stretch alone. Without the starfish, mussels took over and crowded out most other species. The number of species on his plot collapsed. Paine called such a species a keystone, after the central stone that holds an arch together.

Another classic case comes from the Pacific coast. Sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp, the giant seaweed that forms underwater forests. Where hunters wiped out otters for their fur, urchins multiplied and stripped the kelp, leaving bare "urchin barrens". Where otters returned, kelp forests came back, and with them the fish that shelter in kelp. This chain of effects running down a food web is called a trophic cascade. One species can control the shape of an entire ecosystem.

The best-known land example is more debated. Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995. They hunt elk, and many accounts say that fewer, more cautious elk let willows and aspens recover along rivers. Later research found that the effect is real in places but smaller and more patchy than the popular story suggests, with other causes, such as other predators and human hunting, also at work.

Chapter 05

Why variety brings stability

Do ecosystems with more species work better? From the 1980s the American ecologist David Tilman and colleagues tested this in Minnesota by sowing grassland plots with different numbers of plant species and measuring them for decades. Plots with more species generally produced more plant material and kept producing it more steadily through droughts. Many experiments since have found similar patterns.

The reason is often compared to a financial portfolio. Different species respond differently to drought, heat, pests and disease. When one does badly, another does well. A system with many species has insurance: some of its parts are ready for whatever comes next. That does not mean every species is essential. Many ecosystems have several species doing similar jobs. But each lost species removes a backup, and ecologists cannot always tell in advance which loss will matter.

Chapter 06

The five big drivers of loss

In 2019 the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), the biodiversity equivalent of the IPCC, published its first global assessment. It ranked the direct causes of nature's decline, from largest to smallest overall impact.

RankDriverWhat it meansExample
1Land and sea use changeConverting habitat to farms, cities, roads, or trawled seabedRainforest cleared for cattle; wetlands drained
2Direct exploitationHunting, fishing, logging and collecting beyond what populations can sustainOverfishing; the bushmeat and wildlife trades
3Climate changeShifting temperatures, rainfall and ocean chemistryCoral bleaching; species moving uphill and poleward
4PollutionChemicals, fertiliser run-off, plastics, light and noiseDead zones at river mouths; pesticides harming insects
5Invasive speciesSpecies moved by people to places where they do harmRats on seabird islands; cane toads in Australia

The order matters for policy. Climate change gets most of the attention, and its share will grow. But today, on land, the main threat is simply losing habitat, and in the sea it is fishing. The same assessment estimated that humans have significantly altered about three quarters of the land surface and two thirds of the ocean area.

Chapter 07

Islands, and the way small populations fail

Most recorded extinctions in the last five centuries happened on islands. Island species often evolved without predators, so they had no defence when people brought rats, cats, pigs and snakes. The brown tree snake, accidentally brought to the Pacific island of Guam in the years after the Second World War, wiped out most of the island's native forest birds within a few decades.

Small populations anywhere face a trap. With few individuals, a bad year, a disease or simple bad luck can wipe them out. Inbreeding reduces genetic diversity and health. Fewer animals mean fewer births, which makes the population smaller still. Ecologists call this downward spiral the extinction vortex. It explains why conservation tries to act while populations are still reasonably large, and why connecting fragments of habitat with corridors matters: it lets small groups mix and breed.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Measured loss, contested labels, real services

The decline of biodiversity is well documented, and often misreported. Why it matters to people is easier to show: food, medicine and resilience all depend on it.

Chapter 08

How decline is measured, and misread

Two measures dominate the news.

The IUCN Red List

The International Union for Conservation of Nature assesses species one by one and classifies their risk of extinction, from "least concern" to "critically endangered". As of 2024, more than 45,000 of roughly 170,000 assessed species were classed as threatened. The share varies by group: around four in ten amphibians, about a quarter of mammals, and roughly one in eight birds.

The Living Planet Index

Run by WWF and the Zoological Society of London, it tracks tens of thousands of monitored populations of vertebrates (animals with backbones). Its 2024 report found an average decline of 73% in those populations since 1970. The steepest falls were in Latin America and the Caribbean, and in freshwater species.

A common misreading

The Living Planet Index does not mean that 73% of all wild animals have disappeared. It is an average of percentage changes across populations. A few populations collapsing by 99% can pull the average down a lot even if many others are stable. Some scientists criticise the index for exactly this. It is still a useful signal of direction, but it should be quoted carefully.

The IPBES assessment estimated that around one million species of animals and plants face extinction, many within decades, without action to reduce the drivers. That figure is also an estimate built from extrapolation, not a count, but it has been widely accepted as a reasonable order of magnitude.

Chapter 09

Is this a sixth mass extinction?

The fossil record shows five mass extinctions, moments when a large share of all species, by convention roughly three quarters or more, vanished in a geologically short time. The largest, at the end of the Permian about 252 million years ago, wiped out most marine species. The most famous, 66 million years ago, followed an asteroid impact and ended the age of non-bird dinosaurs.

Many scientists argue that we are entering a sixth. Estimates of current extinction rates are tens to hundreds of times higher than the average background rate in the fossil record, and still rising. Others point out that recorded extinctions since 1500, around 900 species, are far below the three-quarters mark, and that calling it a mass extinction already risks overstatement. Both sides agree on the direction; they disagree on the label. The precise position is that the current rate, if it continued for centuries, would produce a mass extinction, and that most of the damage so far is to populations rather than whole species.

Chapter 10

What nature does for us

Ecologists call the benefits people get from nature ecosystem services. Some are obvious and some invisible until they fail.

  • Pollination. According to IPBES, about three quarters of the main types of food crop benefit to some degree from animal pollinators, mainly bees, but also flies, butterflies, birds and bats. Fruits, nuts and many vegetables depend on them. Staple grains such as wheat, rice and maize are pollinated by wind.
  • Medicines. Aspirin descends from a compound in willow bark. Penicillin came from a mould. The cancer drug paclitaxel (taxol) was found in the Pacific yew, and the malaria drug artemisinin in sweet wormwood, work that earned the Chinese scientist Tu Youyou a Nobel Prize in 2015. A blood pressure drug, captopril, was developed from the venom of a Brazilian viper.
  • Clean water and soil. Forests and wetlands filter water. New York City has long protected the forested watersheds in the Catskill mountains partly to avoid building an expensive filtration plant. Soil is a living system of fungi, bacteria and animals that recycle nutrients.
  • Protection. Mangroves and coral reefs reduce the force of waves and storm surges on coasts.
Chapter 11

The banana that disappeared

Until the 1950s the banana sold in Western shops was a variety called Gros Michel. Bananas for export are grown from cuttings, so every plant is a genetic copy. When a soil fungus causing Panama disease spread through plantations, nothing stopped it, and the industry switched to a different variety, the Cavendish. Today the Cavendish faces its own strain of the same disease, known as Tropical Race 4, which has spread across several continents.

The Irish potato famine of the 1840s had a similar root. Much of Ireland depended on a narrow range of potato varieties, which a disease destroyed. Genetic diversity is insurance, and farming without it is a bet that nothing new will come along. That is why seed banks such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway store hundreds of thousands of crop varieties, and why the wild relatives of crops, often found in threatened habitats, are valuable for breeding resistance to pests and heat.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Conserving versus converting

Biodiversity itself has no "disadvantages". The real trade-offs are between using land and sea for people now and keeping them rich in life, and between different ways of conserving.

Chapter 13

Why save a species nobody uses?

Arguments for conservation come in two families. Instrumental arguments say nature is valuable because of what it does for people: food, medicine, clean water, climate stability, tourism. These arguments are powerful with finance ministries. But they have a weakness. If a species has no known use, or if a road would earn more than a forest, the instrumental case can lose.

Intrinsic arguments say living things have value in themselves, regardless of use, and that wiping out a species that evolved over millions of years is a wrong in its own right. Many religious and indigenous traditions, and many people's ordinary moral intuitions, hold some version of this view. Most conservation policy uses both families at once.

There is a third, practical point. We often do not know what a species does until it is gone. Captopril came from a snake venom that nobody would have listed as a "service". The precautionary case for keeping as many parts as possible is that the machinery is too complex to know which parts are spare.

Chapter 15

Recovery is possible

The story is not only of loss. When the main pressure is removed, many species recover. After the international moratorium on commercial whaling took effect in 1986, humpback whale populations grew strongly in many oceans. European bison, extinct in the wild after the First World War, were bred from zoo animals and now roam forests in Poland and other countries. Removing rats from islands, including the large sub-Antarctic island of South Georgia, has allowed seabird colonies to return. Beavers, once hunted out of Britain, have been reintroduced and are changing rivers and wetlands again.

These cases share a pattern. Recovery happens when a specific driver is stopped: hunting is banned, a predator removed, habitat protected. They also show the limit. They work species by species and site by site, while the main drivers, habitat loss and overuse, operate at the scale of the whole economy. Turning local recoveries into global ones depends on how food is grown, how fishing is managed and how land is planned, the subjects of the Sustainable agriculture and Deforestation files.

The core idea

Biodiversity is the variety of life at three levels: genes, species and ecosystems. Species are linked in webs, some keystone species shape whole ecosystems, and variety gives insurance against change. The main drivers of loss are habitat conversion and overexploitation, followed by climate change, pollution and invasive species.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 2 million species described; perhaps 8.7 million plants, animals and fungi in total.
  • About 1 million species at risk of extinction (IPBES, 2019).
  • Monitored vertebrate populations fell by an average of 73% since 1970 (Living Planet Index, 2024).
The common mistake

Reading "73% decline" as "73% of animals are gone", or assuming climate change is already the main threat. Today it is habitat loss and overuse.

Can you explain, with the sea otter example, how removing one species can change a whole ecosystem? If so, you understand why biodiversity is about connections, not just counts.

The words, in plain words
Biodiversity
The variety of life: genes within species, species, and ecosystems.
Species
A group of organisms that can breed with each other and produce fertile offspring (roughly; definitions vary, especially for microbes).
Ecosystem
A community of living things and their physical environment, working as a system.
Keystone species
A species whose effect on its ecosystem is much larger than its numbers suggest.
Trophic cascade
A chain of effects down a food web, such as otters, urchins and kelp.
Ecosystem services
The benefits people get from nature, such as pollination and clean water.
Biodiversity hotspot
A region with many unique species that has lost most of its original habitat.
Invasive species
A species moved by people to a new place, where it spreads and causes harm.
Extinction vortex
The downward spiral by which small populations become smaller and more vulnerable.
Mass extinction
The loss of a large share of all species, conventionally around three quarters, in a short geological time.
Red List
The IUCN's global assessment of the extinction risk of species.
IPBES
The intergovernmental body that assesses the state of biodiversity, like the IPCC for climate.
Environmental DNA (eDNA)
Traces of genetic material left in water, soil or air, used to detect which species are present.
