Greenhouse gases. A few molecules in ten thousand set the temperature of the world.

Ninety-nine per cent of the air is nitrogen and oxygen, and for heat they might as well not be there. The planet's temperature is set by a handful of gases that together make up a tiny fraction of the atmosphere. Each one works differently, comes from different places, and stays for a different length of time. Understanding that cast of characters is the difference between talking about climate in slogans and talking about it in specifics.

Question 1 · What is it?

Trace gases with an outsized job

A greenhouse gas is any gas that lets sunlight pass but absorbs the heat the Earth sends back out. There are only a few that matter, and none of them is common.

Chapter 01

Thirty-three degrees of difference

Do a simple calculation. Take the amount of sunlight that reaches the Earth, subtract the part that clouds, ice and deserts reflect straight back to space, and ask how warm a ball at this distance from the Sun would need to be to radiate the rest away. The answer is about minus 18 °C. The real average temperature at the surface is about plus 15 °C. The gap of roughly 33 degrees is the work of greenhouse gases.

So the first fact to hold on to is that the greenhouse effect is natural and necessary. Without it, the oceans would freeze. The climate problem is not that the effect exists. It is that human activity has thickened it quickly, in about two centuries, after thousands of years in which it was fairly stable.

The name is a slightly misleading picture. A real greenhouse stays warm mainly because its glass stops warm air from rising away. The atmosphere works differently: it stops radiation from escaping, not air. A better image is a blanket. A blanket does not make heat. It slows the rate at which your body loses it, so you settle at a warmer temperature for the same heat production.

And ozone high in the stratosphere, the "ozone layer", is a separate story about ultraviolet protection that people often confuse with climate. Chapter 11 untangles the two.

Chapter 03

Why nitrogen and oxygen do nothing

It looks odd that the two main gases in the air play no part. The reason is the shape of their molecules. Nitrogen (N2) and oxygen (O2) are pairs of identical atoms. When such a pair vibrates, the electric charge stays perfectly balanced. Infrared light, which is what heat radiation is, can only grab hold of a molecule whose vibration shifts its charge from one side to the other.

Molecules with three or more atoms, or with two different atoms, can do that. Carbon dioxide is a straight line, O=C=O. When it bends, like a person doing a sit-up, its charge swings off-centre, and that bending motion matches the frequency of a large share of the heat the Earth emits. Greenhouse gases are simply the molecules that are shaped to wobble at the right frequency. Methane, water and nitrous oxide all qualify. This is basic physics, measured in laboratories since the nineteenth century.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Catch, re-emit, and push the exit higher

The mechanism has three layers: what one molecule does, what a whole column of air does, and how gases with different strengths and lifetimes are compared.

Chapter 04

Two kinds of light

The Sun is very hot, so most of its energy arrives as visible light and near-infrared, at short wavelengths. The Earth is cool by comparison, so it glows at long wavelengths, in the thermal infrared that night-vision cameras see. Greenhouse gases are mostly transparent to the first kind and partly opaque to the second. Energy comes in easily and leaves with difficulty. That asymmetry is the whole trick.

When a CO2 molecule absorbs a packet of infrared energy, it almost immediately bumps into neighbouring molecules and shares the energy as heat. Molecules in that warmed air also emit infrared, in every direction, some of it back down. The air is constantly absorbing and re-emitting, passing heat upward step by step, like a bucket chain in which some buckets are passed backwards.

Chapter 05

"But isn't CO2 already saturated?"

A common objection goes like this. Near the ground, there is already enough CO2 to absorb nearly all the infrared at its main frequency. Adding more cannot absorb what is already absorbed. So more CO2 should make no difference.

The premise is partly true and the conclusion is wrong, and the reason is worth understanding. What matters is not whether heat is absorbed near the ground. It is the height from which heat finally escapes to space. Think of the greenhouse gases as a fog. Heat escapes from roughly the level where the fog thins enough for radiation to get out. Adding more gas raises that level. Higher air is colder, and colder air radiates less energy. So the planet sends less heat to space than it receives, and it warms until the escape level is warm enough to restore the balance.

There are also the edges of the absorption band. Even where the centre is "full", the frequencies on either side are only partly absorbed, and they keep filling in as concentration rises. The upshot is that each extra unit of CO2 has a little less effect than the last one. The effect is roughly logarithmic: every doubling of concentration adds about the same amount of warming. That is a slowing curve, not a ceiling. It is also why scientists summarise the response as climate sensitivity, the long-term warming from a doubling of CO2. The UN's climate science panel, the IPCC, put the likely range at 2.5 to 4 °C in its 2021 assessment, with a best estimate of about 3 °C.

Measured from space

This is not only theory. Satellites measuring the infrared leaving the Earth see the characteristic "bites" taken out of the spectrum at the frequencies CO2 and methane absorb, and comparisons over decades show those bites deepening. The cooling of the upper atmosphere while the lower warms, mentioned in Volume 30, is the same physics seen from another side.

Chapter 06

Strength versus staying power

Gases differ on two separate dials. One is how strongly each molecule traps heat. The other is how long the molecule stays in the air. Methane is far stronger than CO2 per kilogram, but it breaks down in about a decade. CO2 is weaker but does not break down at all. It is gradually absorbed by oceans, plants and rocks, and that takes centuries for most of it and many thousands of years for the last part.

To compare them, scientists use the global warming potential (GWP): how much heat one kilogram of a gas traps over a chosen period, relative to one kilogram of CO2. The choice of period changes the answer dramatically.

Multiply the tonnes of each gas by its 100-year GWP and you get a single number, "tonnes of CO2 equivalent", or CO2e. That is how national emissions and company footprints are reported. On that basis, recent global emissions are roughly three quarters CO2, about a fifth methane, and a few per cent each for nitrous oxide and fluorinated gases. Chapter 13 explains why that single number hides an important difference.

Chapter 07

Water vapour, the amplifier

Water vapour is the largest single contributor to the natural greenhouse effect. People sometimes take that as a reason to ignore CO2. It is the opposite. Water vapour cannot build up on its own. If you added a lot to the air tomorrow, it would rain out within days. The amount the air can hold is set by temperature: roughly 7% more for every degree Celsius of warming.

So water vapour behaves as a feedback, not a driver. CO2 and the other long-lived gases warm the air a little, the warmer air holds more water, and the extra water traps more heat. This roughly doubles the warming that CO2 would cause alone. In a thermostat analogy, CO2 turns the dial and water vapour is the boiler that responds. Clouds, also made of water, are more complicated: some trap heat and some reflect sunlight, and their net effect in a warmer world is one of the larger remaining uncertainties.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Measured precisely, traced to their sources

Greenhouse gases are among the best-measured things in science. That precision is why we know where they come from, and why we have already fixed part of the problem once.

Chapter 08

The curve on the volcano

The physics was worked out early. In the 1850s the American scientist Eunice Foote and the Irish physicist John Tyndall each showed experimentally that carbon dioxide and water vapour absorb heat. In 1896 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius calculated, by hand, that doubling CO2 would warm the planet by several degrees.

The measurement came later. In 1958 the American chemist Charles David Keeling began measuring CO2 on Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii, far from local pollution. The record has run ever since. It shows two things at once: a steady upward climb, and a yearly zigzag. The zigzag is the Northern Hemisphere breathing. In spring and summer its forests grow and pull CO2 out of the air; in autumn and winter leaves decay and it comes back. The planet's breath is visible in a single line on a graph, and on top of it sits the human signal, rising every year.

Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice extend the record back about 800,000 years. Across that whole span, CO2 moved between roughly 180 ppm in ice ages and 280–300 ppm in warm periods. The present level of over 420 ppm is far outside that range, and it was reached in a geological instant. The chemical fingerprint of the extra carbon, covered in the Carbon cycle file, shows it comes from fossil fuels.

Chapter 09

Where the gases come from

Each gas has its own economy behind it, which is why there is no single solution.

Carbon dioxide

Mostly from burning fossil fuels: for electricity, heat, transport and industry. Fossil sources released roughly 37 billion tonnes of CO2 in 2024, and clearing land, mainly tropical forest, adds several billion more. Cement adds a specific share because making it releases CO2 from limestone as well as from fuel.

Methane

About 60% of today's emissions are human-caused. The big sources are the stomachs of cattle and sheep, where microbes digest grass and release methane; flooded rice paddies; leaks and venting from oil, gas and coal production; and rotting waste in landfills. Natural wetlands supply most of the rest.

Nitrous oxide

Mostly from farming. When nitrogen fertiliser or manure is spread on fields, soil bacteria turn some of it into N2O. The fertiliser that feeds half the world, described in Volume 12, carries this climate cost.

Fluorinated gases

Industrial chemicals with no natural source. They are used as refrigerants, in insulating foams, and in high-voltage switchgear in electricity grids (SF6). Small in volume, huge per kilogram, and they leak when equipment is serviced or thrown away.

The temperature effect follows the totals. The IPCC's 2021 assessment attributed about 1.1 °C of warming by the 2010s to human activity, and attributed roughly 0.5 °C of that to methane alone, with CO2 responsible for most of the rest. Air pollution particles, discussed in Chapter 12, have masked part of the total. In 2024 the global average temperature was, for the first time in a calendar year, about 1.5 °C above the late-nineteenth-century level.

Chapter 10

The methane puzzle

Methane levels rose steeply through the twentieth century, nearly flattened in the early 2000s, then started climbing again around 2007. The rise accelerated around 2020. Scientists still argue about why, and it is a good example of an open question inside a settled field.

One clue is the chemistry of the carbon itself. Methane from microbes, in wetlands, cattle stomachs and landfills, contains slightly more of the lighter form of carbon than methane from fossil sources. Since the renewed rise, atmospheric methane has been getting lighter, which points towards biological sources. That could mean more livestock, more rice, or tropical wetlands producing more as they warm. That last possibility would be a feedback: warming producing more of a warming gas. Others argue that fossil-fuel leaks are larger than official figures show, and that changes in the chemistry that destroys methane in the air also matter. The honest summary is that the cause of the recent surge is contested, and probably a mix.

Chapter 11

The gases we already managed

In the 1970s scientists showed that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), the refrigerants and spray-can propellants of the time, were destroying the stratospheric ozone layer, the high-altitude ozone that blocks harmful ultraviolet light. A seasonal "hole" over Antarctica was reported in 1985. Two years later, in 1987, governments signed the Montreal Protocol, which phased CFCs out. It was eventually ratified by every country in the United Nations, and the ozone layer is now slowly recovering.

There was a climate bonus. CFCs are also extremely strong greenhouse gases, so the Montreal Protocol has avoided a significant amount of warming. But the replacements, hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), are safe for ozone and still potent for heat. In 2016 the Kigali Amendment added a phase-down of HFCs to the same treaty.

Keep the two problems separate. The ozone hole is not the cause of global warming, and fixing it was not a climate policy. What the story shows is that a treaty can remove a whole class of gases when substitutes exist and the industry is concentrated. CO2 is harder because it comes from almost everything.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Life support, and a bill that keeps arriving

The gases themselves are neither good nor bad. The question is the amount, the speed of change, and what we get in exchange for emitting them.

Some researchers argue it also contributed to the unusually high temperatures of 2023 and 2024 by removing a reflective haze over busy shipping lanes. How large that contribution was is still debated, but nobody serious suggests keeping the pollution.

Chapter 13

The trouble with a single number

CO2-equivalent is useful because it lets a country add everything into one total. But it blends two kinds of gas that behave very differently. A tonne of CO2 keeps adding to warming for centuries. A tonne of methane causes a sharp burst of warming that fades within a few decades. If methane emissions stay constant, methane's warming effect stays roughly constant too. If CO2 emissions stay constant, temperature keeps rising.

That difference fuels a real argument. Farm groups in some countries say that a herd of steady size is not adding new warming, so treating its methane like CO2 overstates its impact. Critics reply that the herd is still holding the temperature higher than it would otherwise be, and that cutting methane is the cheapest way to shave warming in the near term. Researchers have proposed alternative metrics that track this better. Neither side is denying physics. They are disagreeing about accounting and fairness, which is why the choice of metric is a policy fight, not a technical footnote.

Chapter 15

What to watch

If you want to follow this subject without drowning in headlines, watch three numbers. The annual increase in CO2 at Mauna Loa, which will keep rising until emissions fall substantially, because the concentration only stabilises at net zero. The methane trend, which tells you whether the fastest lever is being pulled or whether natural feedbacks are starting to push. And global CO2 emissions from fossil fuels, which have been close to a plateau. Whether they bend downward in the second half of the 2020s is the single most important question in climate policy.

One more point for conversation. The temperature responds to the total amount of CO2 ever emitted, not to this year's amount. That makes the problem like filling a bath with a slow drain. Turning the tap down a little does not stop the water rising. Only turning it almost off does.

The core idea

Greenhouse gases are molecules shaped to absorb outgoing infrared heat while letting sunlight through. Adding more raises the height from which heat escapes to colder air, so the planet must warm to rebalance. Gases differ in strength and lifetime: methane is strong and brief, CO2 weaker but effectively permanent.

The numbers worth knowing
  • CO2: about 280 ppm before industry, about 420–425 ppm in 2024.
  • Methane traps about 80 times more heat than CO2 over 20 years, about 28–30 times over 100 years, and lasts about 12 years.
  • The natural greenhouse effect is worth about 33 °C.
The common mistake

Mixing up the ozone hole and global warming, or thinking water vapour makes CO2 irrelevant. Water vapour amplifies warming; it does not start it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why adding CO2 still warms the planet even if the air near the ground already absorbs most heat at CO2's frequency? If so, you understand the mechanism better than most people who argue about it.

The words, in plain words
Greenhouse gas
A gas that lets sunlight through but absorbs and re-emits the heat the Earth radiates.
Infrared
Light with wavelengths too long to see; the form in which warm objects lose heat.
ppm / ppb
Parts per million / parts per billion: how many molecules of a gas in every million or billion molecules of air.
Radiative forcing
The imbalance between energy coming in and going out caused by a change, such as more CO2.
Climate sensitivity
The long-term warming caused by doubling CO2; likely 2.5–4 °C.
Feedback
A response to warming that adds to it (or reduces it), such as more water vapour in warmer air.
Global warming potential (GWP)
How much heat a kilogram of a gas traps over a set period compared with a kilogram of CO2.
CO2 equivalent (CO2e)
Emissions of all gases converted into one total using their GWPs.
Keeling Curve
The continuous record of CO2 measured at Mauna Loa since 1958.
Fluorinated gases
Man-made industrial gases such as HFCs and SF6; tiny in quantity, very strong per kilogram.
Montreal Protocol
The 1987 treaty that phased out ozone-destroying chemicals; the Kigali Amendment (2016) added HFCs.
Net zero
The point where any remaining emissions are balanced by removals, so the total in the air stops rising.
