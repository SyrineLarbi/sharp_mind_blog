Sharp Mind · Environment & Climate · Topic 19 of 20

Climate adaptation. Cutting emissions decides how bad it gets. Adaptation decides how many people it hurts.

Even if every country stopped emitting greenhouse gases tomorrow, the world would still face decades of change from the warming already under way. Seas will keep rising for centuries. Heatwaves, heavy downpours and droughts are already more frequent in many regions. Adaptation is everything people do to live with those changes: warning systems, stronger buildings, new crops, insurance, and sometimes moving away. It is less glamorous than solar panels, but for billions of people it is the part of climate policy that decides whether they live safely.

Question 1 · What is it?

Living with the change we can no longer avoid

Climate policy has two halves. One tries to slow the change. The other prepares for the change that is coming anyway.

Chapter 01

The country that learned from one night

On the night of 31 January 1953, a severe storm over the North Sea pushed a huge surge of water against the coast of the Netherlands just as the tide was high. Dykes, the earth walls that protect land below sea level, broke in dozens of places. By morning, large parts of the south-west of the country were under water. More than 1,800 people died in the Netherlands, and hundreds more in England and Belgium.

The Dutch response took decades. The Delta Works, a system of dams, sluices and storm barriers, closed off or protected the estuaries of the south-west. The last major piece, a giant movable barrier near Rotterdam called the Maeslantkering, was completed in 1997. The Dutch also wrote safety standards into law, with the most important areas protected against storms expected only once in thousands of years. That is adaptation in its clearest form: a society deciding, after a disaster, how much risk it is willing to live with, and building to that standard.

Chapter 02

Mitigation and adaptation

Two words organise all of climate policy.

Mitigation

Reducing the cause: cutting greenhouse gas emissions, or removing them from the air. It works globally and slowly. A tonne of carbon dioxide not emitted in Japan helps Brazil as much as Japan, and the benefits arrive over decades.

Adaptation

Reducing the harm: adjusting how we live so that the changes hurt less. It works locally and often quickly. A sea wall protects the town behind it, starting the year it is built.

For a long time, many campaigners were wary of talking about adaptation. They feared it would sound like giving up on cutting emissions. That has changed, because the warming is now visible. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the UN body that assesses climate science, is clear that both are needed, and that they are linked: the less we mitigate, the more we will have to adapt, and beyond some point adaptation stops being possible at all.

Chapter 03

Risk has three parts

Climate scientists describe the risk to a place or a person as the result of three things.

Mitigation mostly works on the hazard. Adaptation works on all three. Moving homes off a floodplain reduces exposure. Warnings, shelters, better housing and health care reduce vulnerability. This explains a fact that surprises many people. The death toll from a disaster depends at least as much on the society it hits as on the strength of the storm.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The adaptation toolkit

Adaptation is not one technology. It is a wide set of tools, from concrete walls to text messages, and the best plans combine several.

Chapter 04

Early warnings: the cheapest life-saver

Bangladesh, a low, flat, densely populated delta, is one of the most cyclone-exposed countries on Earth. In 1970 the Bhola cyclone killed somewhere between 300,000 and 500,000 people, one of the deadliest storms in recorded history. Another cyclone in 1991 killed well over 100,000.

Since then Bangladesh has built thousands of raised cyclone shelters and trained tens of thousands of volunteers who go house to house with megaphones and flags when a storm approaches. Forecasts have improved enormously. When Cyclone Sidr, a very strong storm, struck in 2007, it killed a few thousand people: still a tragedy, but a small fraction of earlier tolls. Later strong cyclones have killed far fewer. Bangladesh did not become rich in those years. It became prepared.

Warnings work for heat and floods too. In 2022 the UN Secretary-General launched an initiative called Early Warnings for All, aiming for every person on Earth to be covered by a warning system by 2027. Many poorer countries still lack basic weather stations and forecast services, which is why the gap is large.

Chapter 05

Hard defences and room for the river

The classic response to flooding is to build higher: dykes, sea walls, levees and barriers. London's Thames Barrier, a row of rotating steel gates across the river, became operational in 1982 and is closed when surges threaten the city. Hard defences protect valuable land very well, up to the level they were designed for.

But walls have a weakness, first described by the American geographer Gilbert White in the 1940s, and now called the levee effect. When a wall makes an area feel safe, more people and buildings move in behind it. If the wall is ever overtopped or breaks, the damage is far greater than if nobody had built there. Volume 23 tells how this played out in New Orleans.

The Netherlands, the world's master of dykes, has itself moved toward a different idea. After near floods on its rivers in the 1990s, it launched a programme called Room for the River. Instead of only raising dykes, it moved some of them back, dug side channels and lowered floodplains, so that high water can spread out safely. Some farms were bought out. The idea is to work with the water rather than only against it.

Chapter 06

Nature as infrastructure

Some of the most effective defences are alive. Mangroves, salt-tolerant trees that grow along tropical coasts, slow waves and storm surges with their tangled roots and trap sediment, so the shore can build up as the sea rises. Coral reefs break waves before they reach the beach. Wetlands upriver soak up floodwater like a sponge and release it slowly. These are called nature-based solutions.

Their advantages are real: they can be cheaper than concrete, they repair themselves, and they also support fish, wildlife and the people who depend on them. Their limits are real too. Mangroves cannot stop the largest surges, they take years to grow, and they need space that is often wanted for shrimp farms, ports or housing. Many coastal countries, including Vietnam and the Philippines, now combine planted mangroves with conventional dykes.

Chapter 07

Heat: the quiet killer

Heatwaves kill more people than any other kind of extreme weather in many countries, but they leave no ruined buildings, so they get less attention. The European heatwave of August 2003 is estimated to have caused tens of thousands of extra deaths, many of them elderly people alone in hot city apartments.

The response is a heat action plan: forecasts trigger alerts, people check on elderly neighbours, cooling centres open, outdoor work hours change, and hospitals prepare. France built such a system after 2003. In South Asia, the city of Ahmedabad in India launched the region's first heat action plan in 2013 after a deadly heatwave in 2010, and many Indian cities and states have copied it. Longer term, cities can plant trees, paint roofs white and redesign buildings to stay cool, as the next file, Sustainable cities, explains.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. In a heatwave, older people, babies, people with heart or kidney disease and outdoor workers face the highest risk, and local health warnings should be followed.

Chapter 08

Farms, water and money

Adaptation also happens quietly in fields and finance departments. Farmers switch to crops and varieties that tolerate drought or heat, change planting dates, and store water in ponds or underground. Cities fix leaking pipes, recycle wastewater and plan for dry years. The farming side is in Sustainable agriculture.

Money is part of the toolkit. Insurance spreads the cost of a disaster across many people and many years. A newer form, parametric insurance, pays out automatically when a measured trigger is reached, such as a hurricane of a given strength passing within a set distance, or rainfall below a set level. It is fast because there is no need to assess every damaged house. Caribbean governments created a shared insurer of this kind in 2007, which has made payouts within weeks of hurricanes and earthquakes.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fairness, money and limits

Adaptation decides who is hurt by climate change. It raises hard questions about who pays, and about places that cannot be protected at all.

Chapter 09

Those who emitted least are hit hardest

In the summer of 2022, extreme monsoon rains and melting glaciers flooded large parts of Pakistan. Around a third of the country was under water at the peak, more than 1,700 people died, and damage and losses were estimated at around 30 billion US dollars. Pakistan has produced a tiny share of historical greenhouse gas emissions.

This pattern repeats across the world. Poorer countries are often in hotter regions, depend more on farming, and have less money for defences. The countries least responsible for climate change are often the most exposed to it and the least able to pay for adaptation. That is why adaptation is at the centre of international climate negotiations. At the 2022 climate summit in Egypt, countries agreed to create a fund for "loss and damage", the harm that adaptation cannot prevent. It was set up at the following summit in Dubai, though the money pledged so far is small compared with the need.

Chapter 10

The gap between need and money

The UN Environment Programme publishes a yearly Adaptation Gap Report. Its recent estimates suggest that developing countries need a few hundred billion dollars a year for adaptation this decade, while international public finance for adaptation has been a small fraction of that, in the tens of billions. At the 2021 summit in Glasgow, rich countries pledged to roughly double adaptation finance by 2025 compared with 2019.

The economic case is strong. A 2019 report by the Global Commission on Adaptation, led by figures including former UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon, argued that investing in early warnings, resilient infrastructure, drought-tolerant farming, mangrove protection and water management could yield benefits several times larger than the costs. Adaptation is often one of the best investments a government can make, and yet it is chronically underfunded, because the benefits are disasters that do not happen, and nobody holds a ceremony for a flood that was avoided.

Chapter 11

The limits: when staying is no longer possible

The IPCC distinguishes soft limits, where adaptation is possible but not currently affordable or acceptable, from hard limits, where no adaptation can prevent the harm. A coral reef cannot adapt to water that stays too hot. A low island cannot build walls against a sea that rises faster than it can afford to build. The human body also has a limit: in heat combined with very high humidity, sweat stops cooling the body. Research suggests the danger point for healthy people may be lower than the theoretical limit once thought.

For some communities the answer is managed retreat: planned relocation away from danger. In 2016 the United States gave a federal grant to relocate the residents of Isle de Jean Charles in Louisiana, a shrinking island, often described as the country's first federally funded climate relocation. In 2014 the Pacific nation of Kiribati bought land on an island in Fiji, partly as a food security measure and potential future refuge. In 2023 Tuvalu and Australia signed a treaty that includes a path for a limited number of Tuvaluans to move to Australia each year. Jakarta, which is sinking because of groundwater pumping as well as rising seas, is a major reason Indonesia is building a new capital on the island of Borneo.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Good adaptation, and adaptation that backfires

Done well, adaptation saves lives cheaply. Done badly, it moves risk onto others or makes things worse later. The second has a name: maladaptation.

Chapter 12

The options weighed

OptionStrengthsWeaknesses and risks
Early warning systemsCheap; save many lives fast; work for many hazards.Save lives more than property; need trust and a way for people to act on them.
Sea walls and leveesProtect valuable land very reliably up to their design level.Expensive; can push erosion onto neighbours; encourage building in risky places (the levee effect).
Nature-based solutionsCheap, self-repairing, good for wildlife and livelihoods.Slower; limited against the biggest events; need space.
Air conditioningSaves lives in heatwaves.Uses a lot of electricity, can raise emissions, heats the street outside, and is unaffordable for many.
InsuranceSpeeds recovery; prices reveal risk.Insurers may withdraw from risky areas, leaving people unprotected. Some large insurers stopped writing new home policies in parts of California and Florida in the 2020s.
Managed retreatPermanently removes people from danger.Painful, costly and culturally wrenching; often resisted, sometimes for good reasons.

Maladaptation happens when a response increases risk in the long run or for someone else. A sea wall that protects one beach while washing away the next village's is maladaptation. So is a dam that saves a city's water while drying out farmers downstream, or building new homes on a floodplain because a new levee makes it look safe. Good adaptation asks not only "does this protect us?" but "who does it shift the risk onto, and what happens when the design limit is passed?"

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

More adaptation, harder choices

Adaptation will grow whatever happens to emissions. The open questions are how fair, how fast and how far. Sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 13

Chapter 14

Adaptation is mostly about ordinary decisions

The image of adaptation is a giant barrier. The reality is mostly ordinary decisions made with the future climate in mind. A school built on higher ground. A building code that requires shading and ventilation. A drainage pipe sized for tomorrow's rainfall instead of yesterday's. A farmer who plants a different variety. A hospital with a backup generator above flood level.

Engineers and planners used to design using past weather records, assuming the climate would stay the same. That assumption no longer holds. The central shift in adaptation is planning for the climate of the coming decades, not the climate of the past. Because adaptation and mitigation are linked, every tenth of a degree of warming avoided makes this planning cheaper and keeps more places within reach of protection.

The core idea

Adaptation reduces the harm from climate change by lowering exposure and vulnerability. Its tools range from early warnings and heat plans to sea walls, mangroves, new crops, insurance and, as a last resort, managed retreat. It works locally and quickly, but it has limits, and it is underfunded where it is most needed.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bangladesh's cyclone deaths fell from hundreds of thousands (1970) to a few thousand (2007).
  • The 1953 North Sea flood killed more than 1,800 people in the Netherlands.
  • Developing countries' adaptation needs are estimated at a few hundred billion dollars a year, far above current funding.
The common mistake

Thinking that adaptation means giving up on cutting emissions, or that walls alone are enough. The two halves of climate policy depend on each other, and walls can make risk worse if they encourage building behind them.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the same cyclone can kill a hundred people in one country and a hundred thousand in another? If so, you understand hazard, exposure and vulnerability.

The words, in plain words
Adaptation
Adjusting how we live and build to reduce harm from climate change.
Mitigation
Reducing the cause of climate change, mainly by cutting emissions.
Hazard
The physical event, such as a flood, storm or heatwave.
Exposure
The people and things located where the hazard strikes.
Vulnerability
How easily those people and things are harmed.
Resilience
The ability to absorb a shock and recover quickly.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level pushed onshore by a storm's winds and low pressure.
Levee effect
The tendency for flood defences to attract building, increasing losses when they fail.
Nature-based solution
Using ecosystems such as mangroves or wetlands to reduce risk.
Heat action plan
A coordinated local plan that triggers protective steps when a heatwave is forecast.
Parametric insurance
Insurance that pays automatically when a measured trigger, such as wind speed, is reached.
Maladaptation
A response that increases risk later or shifts it onto others.
Managed retreat
Planned relocation of people and buildings away from danger.
Loss and damage
Harm from climate change that adaptation cannot prevent.
