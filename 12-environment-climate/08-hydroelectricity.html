Hydroelectricity. The oldest clean power, and the most argued-over.

For most of the twentieth century, "renewable electricity" meant one thing: water falling through a turbine. It still produces more of the world's power than any other single low-carbon source. It can be switched on in minutes, it stores energy as well as producing it, and a good dam runs for a century. It has also flooded valleys, moved tens of millions of people, blocked fish and sediment, and set countries against each other over rivers. Both halves of that story are true.

Question 1 · What is it?

Sunlight, lifted and dropped

Hydroelectricity is electricity generated by water flowing downhill through a turbine. Behind that simple idea are three quite different kinds of power station.

Chapter 01

A battery the Sun recharges

Where does the energy in a river come from? From the Sun. Sunlight warms the oceans, water evaporates, winds carry the vapour over land, and it falls as rain or snow on hills and mountains. At that moment the water has been lifted, sometimes thousands of metres. Gravity then pulls it back down to the sea. A hydroelectric plant catches part of that fall on the way down.

So hydropower is really solar power, stored by the weather. That is why it is renewable: as long as the Sun shines and rain falls, the "battery" is recharged. It is also why it depends on climate. A dry year is a smaller recharge.

The physical principle is old. Water wheels ground grain in the Roman world and powered the first cotton mills of the Industrial Revolution. The new step, in the late nineteenth century, was to connect the wheel to an electrical generator. In 1878 Cragside, a country house in northern England, became one of the first homes lit by hydroelectricity. In the 1890s a large plant at Niagara Falls sent alternating current to the city of Buffalo, about 40 kilometres away, which helped prove that electricity could be generated in one place and used in another.

The first two are sources of energy. The third is a store. It does not make electricity overall; it shifts it in time. That difference becomes important in Chapter 07.

Chapter 03

How big it is

In the mid-2020s, hydropower supplied roughly 14–15% of the world's electricity. That is more than nuclear power and, until around 2024, more than wind and solar combined. Installed capacity worldwide is roughly 1,400 gigawatts. (A gigawatt is a billion watts, roughly the output of a large power station.)

The giants are mostly recent. China's Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze, fully operating since 2012, has 22,500 megawatts of capacity, the largest of any power station of any kind. Next come Baihetan, also in China, and Itaipu on the border of Brazil and Paraguay, which for years produced more electricity in a year than any other plant because its river flows strongly all year round. The Hoover Dam in the United States, a symbol of the 1930s, is small by comparison, at about 2,000 megawatts.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Head, flow, and a spinning shaft

The engineering is simple in principle and demanding in practice. Two numbers decide how much power a site can give, and three designs of turbine cover nearly every river.

Chapter 04

Two numbers: head and flow

The power available from falling water depends on two things. The head is the height the water falls, from the surface of the reservoir to the turbine. The flow is how much water passes per second. Power is roughly proportional to head multiplied by flow.

The rule of thumb
  power (kilowatts)  ~  10  x  flow (cubic metres per second)  x  head (metres)  x  efficiency

  example:  100 m3/s falling 100 m, at 90% efficiency
            10 x 100 x 100 x 0.9  =  90,000 kW  =  90 MW
            (enough for a small city's average demand)

This explains why mountains matter. A small stream falling a long way down a Norwegian or Swiss mountainside can give as much power as a broad, slow river that falls only a few metres. It also explains the dam. A dam does two jobs: it raises the head by lifting the water level, and it stores the flow so that rain in spring can be used in winter.

The conversion is remarkably efficient. A large modern hydro turbine and generator turn around 90% of the water's energy into electricity. A coal or gas plant turns roughly a third to a little over a half of its fuel's energy into electricity, and the rest is lost as heat.

Chapter 05

Three turbines for three kinds of river

Water enters the power station through a large pipe called a penstock, spins a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator: a coil spinning inside magnets, which produces electric current. Engineers choose the turbine to suit the site.

Pelton wheel

For very high head and small flow, typical of mountains. Jets of water strike a ring of cup-shaped buckets, like a high-pressure hose hitting a wheel.

Francis turbine

The most common design, for medium head. Water spirals in around the edge and flows inward through curved blades. Three Gorges and Itaipu use it.

Kaplan turbine

For low head and large flow, such as wide rivers. It looks like a ship's propeller with blades that can change angle as the flow changes.

After the turbine, the water leaves through the tailrace back into the river below. Nothing is burned and nothing is consumed. The same water can pass through several power stations on its way to the sea, and many rivers, such as the Columbia in North America or the Yangtze, have a staircase of dams for exactly that reason.

Chapter 06

Why grid operators love it

An electricity grid must match supply and demand every second. When millions of kettles go on at once, something must produce more power immediately. A coal plant needs hours to change output much. A nuclear plant prefers to run flat out. A reservoir hydro plant can go from idle to full output in minutes, simply by opening gates.

That flexibility is worth more than the energy itself. Hydro plants are used to follow demand through the day, to cover sudden failures elsewhere, and to restart the grid after a blackout, because they can start without power from outside. As more solar and wind come onto grids, this ability becomes more valuable, not less. A reservoir is a store of energy that can be released at will, which is exactly what weather-dependent sources lack.

Chapter 07

Pumped storage: the water battery

Pumped storage takes this a step further. When electricity is plentiful and cheap, for example at midday on a sunny day or on a windy night, pumps push water from a lower reservoir up to an upper one. When power is scarce and expensive, the water runs back down through turbines. Usually the same machines work as pumps in one direction and generators in the other.

Some energy is lost on the round trip. Typically around three quarters to four fifths of the electricity used for pumping comes back. But that is good for storage at this scale. Pumped storage holds most of the world's grid energy storage measured in stored energy, although batteries are growing very fast and now dominate new short-duration installations. Wales has a well-known example, Dinorwig, built inside a mountain in the 1980s and able to reach full output in well under a minute. The grid batteries described in Volume 11 are its chemical competitor.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Nations built on rivers, and rivers fought over

For some countries hydropower is the whole electricity system. For others it is a source of national pride, regional tension, or vulnerability to drought.

Chapter 08

Countries that run on water

Norway produces roughly nine tenths of its electricity from hydropower, thanks to high mountains, heavy rain and snow, and deep valleys. That cheap, clean power attracted energy-hungry industry such as aluminium smelting. Paraguay, with its half of Itaipu, generates far more than it uses and exports most of it to Brazil. Brazil itself, Canada, and the province of Quebec in particular, rely heavily on large dams. In many African countries, such as Ethiopia, Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, hydro supplies a large share of the power that exists.

For these places, hydropower has delivered low-carbon electricity for decades, long before climate policy existed. It is a reminder that a clean grid is not only a modern ambition. Geography gave some countries a head start.

Chapter 09

Three Gorges: the scale of a single decision

The Three Gorges Dam shows the whole argument in one project. It was planned for decades and built mostly in the 1990s and 2000s. It produces as much electricity in a year as several large coal plants, displacing a great deal of coal burning. It was also designed to control the floods of the Yangtze, which have killed huge numbers of people in the past, and it made the river navigable for large ships far inland.

The costs were equally large. The reservoir, more than 600 kilometres long, submerged towns, farmland and archaeological sites. Around 1.3 million people were relocated. The reservoir has had problems with landslides along its banks, water pollution and the trapping of sediment that once nourished the river downstream. Supporters and critics still disagree about how to weigh these, and the dam has become a reference point in every later debate about mega-dams. Worldwide, the World Commission on Dams estimated in 2000 that large dams had displaced roughly 40 to 80 million people during the twentieth century.

Chapter 10

Water across borders

Most big rivers cross borders, and a dam upstream changes what flows downstream. That makes hydropower a matter of foreign policy.

  • The Nile. Ethiopia built the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, its largest power project, and declared it complete in 2025. Ethiopia sees it as essential for electricity and development. Egypt, which depends on the Nile for almost all its water, has seen it as a threat, especially during the years the reservoir fills and in future droughts. Sudan sits in between. Years of negotiations did not produce a binding agreement on how the reservoir would be operated in dry years.
  • The Mekong. Dams in China and Laos have changed the river's flood pulse and sediment. Downstream, in Cambodia and Vietnam, fisheries and the fertile delta depend on both. How much of the recent decline in fish catches and delta sediment is due to dams, and how much to other causes such as sand mining and drought, is debated.
Chapter 11

When dams fail, and when rivers run dry

A dam holds back an enormous amount of energy, so failure is catastrophic. In 1963, at Vajont in northern Italy, a landslide fell into a reservoir and sent a wave over the dam that killed around 2,000 people. The dam itself held; the site was the mistake. In 1975 the Banqiao dam in China collapsed during an extreme typhoon, together with other dams downstream. Deaths numbered in the tens of thousands, and some estimates including later famine and disease are far higher. In 2023 two ageing dams above the city of Derna in Libya collapsed during a storm, and thousands died.

The quieter risk is drought. In 2022 a heatwave and drought in Sichuan, a Chinese province that depends heavily on hydropower, forced factories to close to save electricity. In 2024 drought in southern Africa lowered Lake Kariba, the reservoir shared by Zambia and Zimbabwe, so far that both countries suffered long daily power cuts. A grid built on rain inherits the weather's risks, and climate change is making rainfall more variable in many regions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Clean, flexible, long-lived, and never small in its effects

The advantages are mostly about electricity. The disadvantages are mostly about rivers and people. That is why engineers and ecologists often see the same dam differently.

Chapter 13

The hidden costs

Methane. When a valley is flooded, the trees and soil under the water rot without oxygen and produce methane, a strong greenhouse gas described in the Greenhouse gases file. Most reservoirs emit modest amounts. But some shallow tropical reservoirs that flooded large forests for little power, Balbina in the Brazilian Amazon being the usual example, have been estimated to emit as much per unit of electricity as fossil fuel plants, or more. Low-carbon is typical, not guaranteed.

Sediment. Rivers carry sand and silt that build and feed deltas and beaches. A dam traps it. After the Aswan High Dam was completed on the Nile in 1970, the floods that had fertilised Egyptian fields for millennia stopped, farmers came to rely on artificial fertiliser, and parts of the Nile Delta coast began to erode. Over decades, sediment also fills the reservoir and slowly reduces its storage.

Fish. Salmon, eels and many river fish migrate up and down rivers to breed. A dam blocks them. Fish ladders, which are stepped channels, and fish lifts help some species but rarely restore the full run. In the Columbia River basin, the decline of salmon has been linked in part to dams and is still a political issue.

Chapter 15

Taking dams down

In rich countries the newest trend is removal. Many small and medium dams were built a century ago for mills or small power plants that no longer earn their keep, and some have become unsafe. On the Elwha River in Washington State, two dams were removed between 2011 and 2014, in what was then the largest dam removal in US history. Salmon returned to the upper river within a few years, and sediment rebuilt the beach at the river mouth. On the Klamath River, on the California–Oregon border, four dams were removed in 2023 and 2024, an even larger project.

Europe has also removed thousands of small barriers in recent years. The direction is clear in some places: keep and upgrade the dams that give a lot of power, remove the ones that give little. Meanwhile, in countries that still lack electricity, building new hydropower remains attractive. The global picture is therefore mixed, and the most useful question about any dam is not "is hydro good?" but "is this dam, on this river, worth what it costs?"

The core idea

Hydroelectricity captures the energy of water falling from where rain lifted it. Power depends on head (height) times flow. Reservoir plants can store energy and release it on demand, and pumped storage acts as a giant battery, which makes hydro very valuable on grids with solar and wind. The costs fall on rivers and people.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 14–15% of world electricity in the mid-2020s.
  • Three Gorges: 22,500 MW, about 1.3 million people relocated.
  • Large turbines convert about 90% of the water's energy into electricity.
The common mistake

Assuming all hydro is carbon-free and harmless, or that all dams are disasters. The answer depends on the site: a mountain plant and a shallow tropical reservoir are very different things.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a pumped storage plant that loses a fifth of its energy is still useful? If you can, you understand the difference between producing energy and moving it in time.

The words, in plain words
Head
The height the water falls between the reservoir surface and the turbine.
Flow
The volume of water passing per second, in cubic metres.
Reservoir
The artificial lake behind a dam.
Run-of-river
A plant that uses a river's natural flow with little storage.
Pumped storage
Pumping water uphill when power is cheap and letting it flow down through turbines when power is needed.
Penstock
The large pipe that carries water from the reservoir to the turbine.
Turbine
A wheel with blades that water pushes round; it turns the generator.
Generator
A machine that turns spinning motion into electricity using magnets and coils.
Capacity (MW, GW)
The maximum power a plant can produce at once; a megawatt is a million watts, a gigawatt a billion.
Flexibility
How fast a power source can raise or lower its output.
Sediment
Sand, silt and mud carried by a river; it builds deltas and is trapped by dams.
Fish ladder
A stepped channel that lets migrating fish get past a dam.
