Sharp Mind · Environment & Climate · Topic 14 of 20

Air pollution. The most deadly pollution is the one you cannot see.

Every day you breathe roughly ten thousand litres of air. You cannot choose not to. That makes air pollution unlike almost any other health risk: it reaches everyone in a place, rich and poor, smokers and non-smokers, babies and the old. Most of the harm comes from particles far too small to see, which travel deep into the lungs and from there into the blood. Air pollution is also one of the great success stories of environmental policy. Many cities have cleaned their air dramatically, and we know exactly how they did it.

Question 1 · What is it?

A mixture of particles and gases that harm the living

Air pollution is any substance in the air, in high enough amounts, that harms people, animals, plants or buildings. It is not one thing but a small family of culprits.

Chapter 01

Four days in London

On 5 December 1952, a cold, still mass of air settled over London. Millions of homes were burning cheap coal to keep warm, and power stations and factories were burning more. Normally wind would have carried the smoke away. That week it did not. The smoke mixed with fog into a yellow-black blanket so thick that buses had to be led by men walking in front with torches, and cinemas closed because people could not see the screen.

The Great Smog lasted about four days. At the time, officials linked about 4,000 extra deaths to it. Later studies of death records put the true toll much higher, at around 10,000 or more over the following months. The deaths were not from an accident or a leak. They came from ordinary daily life, concentrated. Four years later, Britain passed the Clean Air Act of 1956, which created smoke-free zones and moved homes away from burning raw coal.

The word for what London suffered, smog, is a blend of "smoke" and "fog". It became the word for dirty city air in general.

One distinction matters a lot. Primary pollutants come straight out of a chimney or exhaust pipe. Secondary pollutants are formed in the air when primary pollutants react with each other, with water or with sunlight. Ozone is entirely secondary, and a large share of PM2.5 is too. That is why air can be dirty far from any obvious source.

Chapter 03

Outdoor and indoor

When rich-country readers think of air pollution, they think of traffic. But globally, a huge share of the harm happens inside homes. About 2 billion people still cook with wood, charcoal, crop waste, dung, coal or kerosene, often on open fires or simple stoves with poor ventilation. The smoke inside can be far worse than on the busiest city street. Women and young children, who spend the most time near the fire, breathe the most of it. Household air pollution is one of the largest health risks in the world, and almost entirely a problem of poverty.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From exhaust pipe to bloodstream

To understand air pollution you need two stories: how the air traps and transforms pollutants, and how the body reacts when it breathes them in.

Chapter 04

The lid on the city: temperature inversions

Normally air gets cooler as you go up. Warm air near the ground rises, carrying pollution upward and spreading it out. Sometimes, especially on cold, calm winter nights, the pattern flips. A layer of warm air sits above cooler air near the ground. This is called a temperature inversion. The cool air cannot rise through the warm layer, so it stays put, and everything emitted into it builds up.

Inversions explain London in 1952, and they explain why valley cities and basin cities, such as Los Angeles, Mexico City, Santiago and Tehran, often suffer badly. The emissions on a smoggy day are usually no greater than on a clear day. The difference is the weather. Volume 30 explains the wider weather engine (Weather is one engine).

Chapter 05

Sunshine smog: the Los Angeles puzzle

In the 1940s and 1950s, Los Angeles had terrible smog, but it was different from London's. It was worst on bright, sunny afternoons, not foggy winter mornings. It stung the eyes and cracked rubber. And Los Angeles did not burn much coal.

A chemist at the California Institute of Technology, Arie Haagen-Smit, solved the puzzle in the early 1950s. He showed that the smog was made in the air. Nitrogen oxides and unburned fuel vapours from car exhausts, which chemists call volatile organic compounds (VOCs), react together in strong sunlight and form ozone and other irritating chemicals. This is called photochemical smog. His discovery shifted the blame from factories to the car, and it led California to set some of the world's first limits on vehicle exhaust. It is why cars later got catalytic converters, devices in the exhaust that use metals such as platinum to turn the worst gases into less harmful ones.

Chapter 06

What tiny particles do inside you

Your airways are a filter. Big particles get caught in the nose and throat and are coughed or swallowed away. Particles of PM10 size get further but are mostly trapped in the upper airways. PM2.5 is different. It is small enough to reach the tiny air sacs at the bottom of the lungs, where oxygen passes into the blood, and the smallest particles can cross into the blood itself.

Once there, the particles trigger inflammation, the body's defence reaction. Short bursts of inflammation are useful. Constant inflammation from breathing dirty air every day is not. It stiffens and damages blood vessels, makes blood more likely to clot, and raises blood pressure. That is why most deaths from air pollution are from heart attacks and strokes, not lung disease. Air pollution also causes lung cancer, chronic lung disease and pneumonia in children. In 2013 the World Health Organization's cancer agency classified outdoor air pollution as a cause of cancer in humans, in the same top category as tobacco smoke, although the risk to any one person is much smaller.

Evidence also links polluted air to low birth weight, to slower lung growth in children, and, less certainly, to dementia. Research on the brain is active and the links there are less settled.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. People with asthma, heart disease or lung disease should follow their own doctor's guidance on high-pollution days.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The largest environmental risk to human health

Air pollution kills more people each year than many diseases that get far more attention, and it does so quietly, through illnesses that are rarely blamed on the air.

Chapter 07

The scale of the harm

The World Health Organization estimates that outdoor and household air pollution together cause about 7 million early deaths a year. Other research groups give somewhat different numbers, but every serious estimate is in the millions. That makes air pollution one of the leading risk factors for death worldwide, alongside high blood pressure, smoking and poor diet.

It also reaches almost everyone. The WHO reported that about 99% of the world's population lives where air quality does not meet its guidelines. In 2021 the WHO tightened its guideline for PM2.5 to an annual average of 5 micrograms per cubic metre, half its previous level, because new studies showed harm even at low levels. There is no known safe level of PM2.5; every reduction seems to bring health gains.

The burden is very uneven. The worst outdoor air today is mostly in South Asia, parts of East Asia, the Middle East and Africa. In northern India, winter brings a toxic mix of traffic, industry, burning rubbish, and the smoke of farmers burning crop stubble after the harvest, all trapped by inversions. Pollution in Delhi in some winters reaches many times the WHO guideline.

Chapter 08

A name on the certificate

Statistics can make harm feel abstract. One case changed that in Britain. Ella Adoo-Kissi-Debrah was a nine-year-old girl who lived near a busy road in south London and suffered severe asthma attacks. She died in 2013. In 2020, after a long campaign by her mother, a coroner ruled that air pollution had made a material contribution to her death. It was the first time in the UK that air pollution was named as a cause of death of an individual person. The case added pressure for stronger action in London.

The same pattern of unfairness appears in many countries. Busy roads, factories and ports are more often near poorer neighbourhoods, and people there are less able to move away. Those who contribute least to pollution often breathe the most of it.

Chapter 09

How Beijing and others cleaned their air

The good news is that air pollution can be fixed, and fixed quickly. In January 2013 Beijing suffered air so bad that the international press called it an "airpocalypse". The government responded with a national action plan that closed or cleaned coal plants, moved homes and factories from coal to gas or electricity, and restricted old vehicles. Within a decade, average PM2.5 in Beijing fell by more than half. It is still above WHO guidelines, but the change is one of the fastest improvements in air quality ever recorded.

Other successes are older. The United States created its Environmental Protection Agency and passed its Clean Air Act in 1970. Since then the main pollutants in American air have fallen sharply while the economy grew several times larger. A 1990 amendment set up a market for sulphur dioxide: power plants received permits and could trade them. Emissions fell faster and more cheaply than expected, and acid rain, which had been killing lakes and forests, declined.

The removal of lead from petrol is another victory. Leaded petrol was introduced in the 1920s to stop engines knocking. Lead is a nerve poison that harms children's developing brains. Countries phased it out from the 1970s onward, and in 2021 Algeria, the last country still selling it for cars, stopped. Leaded petrol for road vehicles is now gone worldwide.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The trade-offs of cleaning the air

Cleaning air is one of the best deals in public policy, but it is not free, and some of its side effects are surprising.

Chapter 11

The uncomfortable twist: dirty air was cooling the planet

Some air pollution, especially sulphur dioxide, forms tiny droplets called aerosols that reflect sunlight back to space. They also help clouds form and make them brighter. That means pollution has been masking part of the warming caused by greenhouse gases. Scientists estimate the masking effect is substantial, though its exact size is one of the larger uncertainties in climate science.

Cleaning the air removes some of this mask. A recent example is shipping. In 2020 the International Maritime Organization cut the sulphur allowed in ship fuel from 3.5% to 0.5%. That was good for the health of people living near ports and shipping lanes. Several studies suggest it also slightly added to warming, through fewer bright clouds over busy shipping routes, though how much is debated.

Nobody serious argues for keeping pollution to cool the planet: it kills millions a year. But the twist explains why cutting air pollution and cutting greenhouse gases need to happen together. Doing the first without the second unmasks warming that was already on its way.

Chapter 13

Seeing the air: the measuring revolution

For most of history you could only guess how dirty the air was. Today satellites map nitrogen dioxide and fine particles over the whole planet every day. When many countries locked down in early 2020, satellite images showed nitrogen dioxide falling sharply over cities within weeks, a dramatic natural experiment. Cheap sensors costing a few hundred dollars now let schools and citizens measure their own street.

Measurement changes politics. The United States embassy in Beijing published hourly readings from a monitor on its roof from 2008, and those numbers, spread on social media, helped build the public pressure that led to China's clean air plan. When people can see the numbers, it becomes harder to say that nothing can be done.

Indoor air is the next frontier. In homes in rich countries, gas cookers, wood burners, cleaning sprays and poor ventilation can make indoor air dirtier than the street outside. In poor countries, the switch to clean cooking remains the single largest prize. Air pollution is a solvable problem. It is solved slowly, street by street and stove by stove, and quickly only when governments decide it matters.

The core idea

Air pollution is a mix of particles and gases from burning and from chemistry in the air. The deadliest part is PM2.5, fine particles that reach deep into the lungs and blood and cause mainly heart attacks and strokes. Weather, especially inversions, decides how bad a given day is. Strong policy has cleaned the air in many cities, and quickly.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 7 million early deaths a year from outdoor and household air pollution (WHO).
  • About 99% of people live where the air fails WHO guidelines.
  • WHO guideline for PM2.5 since 2021: 5 micrograms per cubic metre, annual average.
The common mistake

Thinking air pollution is mainly a lung problem, or mainly about traffic. Most deaths are from heart disease and stroke, and across the world, smoke from cooking fires is as big a killer as exhaust fumes.
