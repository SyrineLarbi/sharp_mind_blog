Sharp Mind · Biology · Topic 07 of 20

Human physiology. A body that spends most of its effort staying the same.

Anatomy is the map of the body. Physiology is how it runs. And the surprising answer to "what is the body doing all day?" is that it is fighting change. Outside, the temperature swings, meals come and go, you stand up, run for a bus, or climb a mountain. Inside, your temperature, blood sugar, blood acidity and salt levels barely move. Keeping them steady takes sensors, control centres and responses working constantly. Understanding that one idea, feedback, explains a large part of both health and disease.

Question 1 · What is it?

The science of how living bodies function

Physiology asks how organs and systems work and how they cooperate. Its central idea is that a body keeps its inner conditions steady while the outside world changes.

Chapter 01

The sea inside you

In the mid-nineteenth century the French physiologist Claude Bernard noticed something about the fluid that bathes the body's cells. Its temperature, its saltiness and its sugar level stayed remarkably constant, whatever the animal ate or wherever it was. He called this fluid the milieu intérieur, the "inner environment", and argued that keeping it constant is the condition for a free and independent life. A lizard slows down on a cold morning. A human does not, because the human keeps its inner sea warm.

In the 1920s the American physiologist Walter Cannon gave the idea its modern name: homeostasis, from Greek words for "staying similar". He explained it in a popular book, The Wisdom of the Body (1932). Homeostasis does not mean nothing changes. It means the body changes constantly in order to keep a few key things the same, as a tightrope walker moves all the time to stay upright.

Look at the blood acidity row. The healthy range is a tenth of a pH unit wide. Every one of these variables is held within narrow limits by its own control system, and the systems are connected. Laboratory "normal ranges" vary a little between labs and countries.

Chapter 03

Two messaging systems: wires and post

To keep things steady, the body needs to send messages from sensors to control centres and on to the organs that respond. It has two ways to do it.

The nervous system: the wires

Electrical signals along nerves, reaching a precise target in thousandths of a second. Used for fast adjustments: pulling a hand from a flame, adjusting blood pressure as you stand, changing your breathing rate. Much of it is automatic, run by the autonomic nervous system, which works without conscious thought.

Hormones: the post

Chemical messengers released into the blood, which reaches every cell. Only cells with the matching receptor respond. Slower, from seconds to days, but long-lasting and widespread. Used for blood sugar, water balance, growth, stress and reproduction. See Hormones.

Many of the most important control centres sit in a small region at the base of the brain, the hypothalamus. It monitors temperature, water and energy, and it is the bridge between the two systems: it sends nerve signals and also controls the pituitary gland, which releases hormones that control other glands.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Sensor, control centre, response: the same loop, again and again

Every homeostatic system has the same three parts. Once you can see the loop, you can understand temperature, sugar, pressure, breathing and water in the same way.

Chapter 04

Negative feedback, and why fever is not a broken thermostat

A home heating system has a sensor (the thermometer), a control centre with a target value or set point (the thermostat), and an effector that acts (the boiler). When the room gets too cold, the boiler turns on; when it is warm enough, it turns off. The response pushes the variable back towards the target. That is called negative feedback: "negative" because the response opposes the change.

Temperature control as a negative feedback loop
   body too warm                              body too cold
        |                                           |
   sensors in skin and brain --> HYPOTHALAMUS <-- sensors
        |               (set point ~37 C)           |
        v                                           v
   - sweat (cooling as it evaporates)          - shiver (muscle heat)
   - widen skin vessels (red face)             - narrow skin vessels (pale)
   - behaviour: shade, remove clothes          - behaviour: shelter, eat, huddle
        |                                           |
        +-------->  temperature returns to range  <-+

Fever shows that the set point itself can move. During an infection, immune signals tell the hypothalamus to raise its target, say to 39 °C. At that moment the body at 37 °C "feels" too cold, so you shiver and feel chilly while your temperature climbs. When the infection fades and the set point drops, you suddenly feel hot and sweat. Fever is a controlled change of target, not a loss of control. A moderate fever probably helps fight some infections. Heat stroke, by contrast, is true failure: the body's cooling cannot keep up, and temperature rises past the set point.

Chapter 05

Blood sugar: two hormones pulling in opposite directions

Your brain runs mainly on glucose and cannot store much of it, so blood sugar must never fall far. After a meal, sugar rises. Cells in the pancreas sense it and release insulin, which tells muscle, fat and liver cells to take glucose in and store it. Between meals, sugar falls. Other pancreatic cells release glucagon, which tells the liver to release stored sugar. The two hormones work like an accelerator and a brake.

Before insulin could be given as a drug, type 1 diabetes, in which the body destroys its own insulin-making cells, was a death sentence, usually within months or a few years. In 1921–22 researchers in Toronto, including Frederick Banting and Charles Best, extracted insulin and first used it in a 14-year-old boy, Leonard Thompson, in January 1922. It was one of the most dramatic results in medical history. Type 2 diabetes is different: cells stop responding well to insulin, which is explained in Volume 45.

Chapter 06

Blood pressure: why you feel dizzy when you stand up fast

The heart pumps roughly five litres of blood a minute at rest, about the total blood volume of an adult. That output is simply heart rate times the volume pumped per beat, and during hard exercise it can rise several times over. Blood pressure depends on that output and on how wide or narrow the small arteries are.

When you stand up, gravity pulls blood into your legs, and pressure in the head drops for a moment. Pressure sensors called baroreceptors, in the walls of the main arteries in the neck and chest, detect the fall. Within a heartbeat or two, nerve signals speed up the heart and narrow blood vessels, and pressure recovers. If you stand very fast, or are dehydrated, or the reflex is slow, as it often is in older people, there is a moment of dizziness or even fainting. Over the longer term, the kidneys and hormones adjust pressure by changing the amount of salt and water in the body.

Chapter 07

Breathing is driven by carbon dioxide, not oxygen

Most people assume you breathe because you need oxygen. You do need it. But the main signal that makes you breathe is a rise in carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide dissolved in blood makes it more acidic, and sensors in the brainstem respond strongly to that change. The urge to breathe when you hold your breath is mostly carbon dioxide building up, not oxygen running out.

That has a dangerous consequence. If a swimmer breathes very fast and deep before diving to swim far underwater, they blow off carbon dioxide. The urge to breathe arrives late. Oxygen can fall far enough for them to black out before they feel the need to surface. This "shallow water blackout" kills strong swimmers every year, which is why deliberate over-breathing before underwater swimming is strongly discouraged.

Chapter 08

Water: the kidneys and the thirst hormone

Your kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid from the blood every day and return all but about one and a half litres. The final amount of urine is controlled mainly by antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin). When your blood gets too concentrated, because you sweated or did not drink, the hypothalamus senses it, releases more ADH, and the kidneys hold on to water. You also feel thirsty. When you drink a lot, ADH falls, and you pass more, paler urine.

Alcohol suppresses ADH. That is why a night of drinking makes you pass more urine than you drank and wake up dehydrated. Urine colour is a rough everyday gauge: pale straw usually means well hydrated, dark amber usually means you need water, although some foods, vitamins and medicines change the colour.

Chapter 09

The exceptions: when the body amplifies on purpose

A few systems use positive feedback, where the response strengthens the change instead of reversing it. Positive feedback drives a process to a fast finish. In childbirth, pressure of the baby's head on the cervix triggers release of the hormone oxytocin, which strengthens contractions, which push the head harder, which releases more oxytocin, until the baby is born. Blood clotting works the same way: each step activates more of the next, so a clot forms in minutes.

Positive feedback is useful only when it has a natural end. When it runs out of control, it is dangerous. In severe shock, falling blood pressure starves the heart of blood, which weakens it, which lowers pressure further. Much of intensive care is about breaking such vicious circles.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Disease is often a control loop gone wrong

Many common chronic diseases are failures of homeostasis. And physiology explains what happens at the edges of human endurance.

Chapter 10

Four ways a control loop can fail

FailureWhat breaksExample
No signalThe messenger is missingType 1 diabetes: no insulin
Signal ignoredThe target stops respondingType 2 diabetes: insulin resistance
Wrong set pointThe target value creepsHigh blood pressure, where the body comes to defend a higher level
OverwhelmedThe challenge exceeds the responseHeat stroke, severe blood loss, hypothermia

This way of thinking is how doctors approach many problems. Find the loop, then find which part of it has failed. Treatment then replaces the missing signal (insulin), makes the target respond better (some diabetes drugs), lowers the set point (blood pressure drugs), or supports the body until it recovers (intensive care).

Chapter 11

At the edges: altitude, heat and the deep

At the top of Mount Everest the air pressure is about a third of that at sea level, so each breath carries about a third as much oxygen. For decades many experts doubted anyone could survive there without bottled oxygen. In 1978 Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler did it. They could do so because of acclimatisation: over weeks at altitude, the body breathes faster, the kidneys release a hormone called EPO that tells bone marrow to make more red blood cells, and the blood becomes thicker with oxygen carriers. Athletes train at altitude for the same reason, and some have cheated by injecting EPO.

Some populations have adapted over many generations. Several highland peoples, including Tibetans, carry genetic variants that help them live at altitude, and their adaptations differ from those of people living in the Andes. Heat shows the opposite limit. The body cools mainly by sweating, and sweat cools only when it evaporates. In very humid heat, it cannot, which is why hot, humid heatwaves are much more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature.

Chapter 12

Exercise: the loops working together

A hard run is a test of every system at once. Muscles burn energy many times faster than at rest. Carbon dioxide rises, so you breathe harder. The heart beats faster and pushes more per beat. Blood vessels to the muscles widen and those to the gut narrow. Temperature rises, so you sweat. Blood sugar is held up by stored sugar released from the liver. When demand outruns oxygen supply, muscles switch partly to a less efficient energy route that does not need oxygen, and the burning feeling and breathlessness tell you it is time to slow down.

With regular training, the loops adapt. The heart pumps more per beat, so a fit person's resting heart rate is often lower. Muscles grow more mitochondria and capillaries. Fitness is, in large part, physiology retuned by repeated challenge. The details are in Exercise physiology, and why exercise is the most reliable health intervention is in Volume 33.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Stability is powerful, and expensive

Homeostasis gives humans enormous freedom. It also costs energy, and its alarms can cause harm.

Chapter 13

The benefits and costs, weighed

FeatureBenefitCost
Warm-bloodednessActive in cold weather, day and night; fast nerves and muscles.Huge energy bill. A mammal needs far more food than a reptile of the same size.
Narrow rangesEnzymes and nerves work at their best.Little margin: small drifts in sodium, sugar or temperature become emergencies.
Strong defence responsesFever, inflammation and clotting protect against infection and bleeding.When they overreact, as in sepsis or unwanted clots, they become the danger.
Adjustable set pointsThe body can adapt to altitude, training and fasting.Set points can drift in a harmful direction, as in high blood pressure.
RedundancySeveral systems back each other up, such as nerves and hormones for blood pressure.Hard to treat precisely; a drug blocking one route may be offset by another.

Physiology as a science has its own limits. Many "normal" ranges were built from limited groups of people. The famous 37 °C comes from measurements by the German physician Carl Wunderlich in the nineteenth century, and several modern studies suggest average body temperature today is slightly lower. Many classic experiments were done mostly on young men, and how physiology differs by sex, age and ancestry is still being filled in.

Chapter 15

The body as a set of conversations

Physiology is moving from organs studied one at a time to systems studied together. The gut talks to the brain, fat tissue releases hormones that affect appetite, muscles release signals during exercise that affect other organs, and the microbes in the gut influence several of these conversations (see The microbiome). Some of these links are well established; others are early and oversold.

The idea to keep is the loop. Sensor, set point, response, and back again. When you understand which loop a symptom belongs to, and which part of it has failed, the body stops looking like a list of organs and starts looking like what it is: a system that works very hard to stay the same.

The core idea

The body keeps key variables steady through homeostasis. Each control system has a sensor, a control centre with a set point, and a response, usually by negative feedback. Nerves give fast control; hormones give slow, wide control. Many diseases are a failure in one part of a loop.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Core temperature about 37 °C; blood pH 7.35–7.45.
  • The heart pumps about 5 litres a minute at rest.
  • Kidneys filter about 180 litres a day and keep all but about 1.5.
The common mistake

Believing you breathe mainly because oxygen runs low. The main trigger is rising carbon dioxide, which is why over-breathing before underwater swimming is dangerous.

Test yourself

Can you explain why you shiver at the start of a fever and sweat at the end? If so, you understand set points.

The words, in plain words
Physiology
The study of how the body and its parts function.
Homeostasis
Keeping inner conditions steady while the outside changes.
Set point
The target value a control system defends.
Negative feedback
A response that reverses a change, bringing a value back to target.
Positive feedback
A response that amplifies a change, driving a process to completion.
Hypothalamus
A small brain region that controls temperature, water and hormones.
Autonomic nervous system
The automatic part of the nervous system that runs the organs.
Insulin
The hormone that lowers blood sugar by moving glucose into cells.
Glucagon
The hormone that raises blood sugar by releasing stores from the liver.
Baroreceptor
A pressure sensor in a major artery.
ADH
Antidiuretic hormone: tells the kidneys to keep water.
Acclimatisation
The body's adjustment over days or weeks to a new environment, such as altitude.
