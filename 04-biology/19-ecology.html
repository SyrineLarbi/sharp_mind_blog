Ecology. Nothing in nature lives alone, and pulling one thread moves the whole web.

Remove one species of starfish from a rocky shore, and within a few years most of the other species there are gone. Bring wolves back to a national park, and people argue for decades about whether the rivers changed shape. Ecology is the science of these connections: who eats whom, who helps whom, how energy and nutrients move, and why populations boom and crash. It is not the same as environmentalism, though it gives environmental debates most of their evidence. This file explains how living systems work and why their behaviour so often surprises us.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of living things in their household

Ecology studies how organisms interact with each other and with their physical surroundings, at every scale from a single pond to the whole planet.

Chapter 01

A word about houses

The German zoologist Ernst Haeckel coined the word in 1866, from the Greek oikos, meaning house or household. The same root gives us "economics". The idea was that living things share a household and must be understood through their relationships within it: what they eat, what eats them, where they live and what they compete for.

People often use "ecology" to mean caring for the environment. Strictly, that is environmentalism, a set of values and a political movement. Ecology is the science; environmentalism is one thing people decide to do with what the science finds. An ecologist might study how a pest population grows in order to help farmers kill it more efficiently.

Ecologists work at several levels, each including the one before.

Chapter 02

Habitat, niche and ecosystem

Three terms carry much of the field. A habitat is where an organism lives: a coral reef, a forest floor, the inside of a cow's gut. A niche is how it makes a living there: what it eats, when it is active, what conditions it can tolerate. A useful shorthand is that the habitat is the address and the niche is the job.

The word ecosystem was introduced in 1935 by the British botanist Arthur Tansley. His point was that living things and their physical surroundings form one system and cannot be understood separately. A forest is not just trees. It is trees, fungi, insects, birds, soil, rain and sunlight, all exchanging energy and materials. Ecosystems have no fixed size. A rotting log is one; so is the Amazon basin. An ecosystem is defined by its connections, not by its borders.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Energy flows through, matter goes round, and species push and pull

Three rules explain most of ecology. Energy enters and is lost, so it must keep arriving. Materials are recycled. And every species is shaped by others.

Chapter 03

Energy: why big fierce animals are rare

Almost every ecosystem runs on sunlight. Plants, algae and some bacteria capture it by photosynthesis and store it in sugars. They are the producers. Animals that eat them are primary consumers; animals that eat those are secondary consumers, and so on. Each level is called a trophic level, from a Greek word for food. The chain of who eats whom is a food chain, and in reality chains branch and cross into a food web.

At each step, most of the energy is lost. Animals burn food to move, keep warm and stay alive, and much of what they eat is not digested. As a rough rule of thumb, only about a tenth of the energy at one level ends up in the bodies of the next, though the real figure varies widely.

This one fact explains a lot. It is why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels: there is not enough energy left at the top. It is why lions are far rarer than antelope, and why a large predator needs a huge territory. And it is why feeding people on plants takes much less land than feeding the same people on meat. Energy is not recycled; it flows through and leaks away as heat.

Chapter 04

Matter: the planet's recycling system

Unlike energy, the atoms that make up living things are used again and again. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and water move in loops between living things, soil, water and air. These loops are called biogeochemical cycles.

The heroes are the decomposers: bacteria, fungi, worms and other small creatures that break down dead matter and return its nutrients to the soil. Without them, the world would be buried in dead leaves and bodies, and plants would run out of nutrients. Most land plants also depend on fungi that wrap around or enter their roots, called mycorrhizae, which trade water and minerals for sugar.

Nitrogen shows how tightly the system is balanced. The air is about 78% nitrogen, but plants cannot use it in that form. Only certain bacteria, some living in nodules on the roots of beans and peas, can convert it into a usable form. Since the early twentieth century, humans have made huge quantities of fertiliser from air nitrogen by an industrial process, which feeds billions of people. Excess fertiliser washing into rivers and seas then feeds algae, which die and decompose, using up oxygen and creating "dead zones". Adding too much of a good thing to a cycle is as disruptive as taking it away. See Carbon cycle and Water pollution.

Chapter 05

Populations: booms, limits and cycles

Given plenty of food and no enemies, any population grows faster and faster, because more individuals produce more offspring. This is exponential growth. It never lasts. As numbers rise, food runs short, disease spreads more easily, and predators find more prey. Growth slows and the population levels off near the number the environment can support, called its carrying capacity.

Real populations often overshoot and swing. The most famous record comes from an unlikely source: the fur trade. For about a century, the Hudson's Bay Company in Canada kept records of how many lynx and snowshoe hare pelts trappers brought in. The numbers rise and fall in a cycle of roughly ten years, with lynx peaks following hare peaks. When hares are plentiful, lynx thrive and multiply; then hares are eaten down and run short of food, and the lynx starve. Later research showed the hares' own food supply and other predators matter too. Predator and prey numbers are linked, but the link is rarely as simple as the textbook graph.

Chapter 06

The four ways species affect each other

Competition

Two species need the same limited resource. In the 1930s the Russian biologist Georgy Gause grew two species of single-celled Paramecium together on the same food; one always drove the other out. The idea that two species cannot share exactly the same niche forever is called competitive exclusion. In nature, competitors usually split the niche, feeding at different times, places or heights.

Predation and grazing

One species eats another. Predators shape prey, which evolve speed, camouflage, shells or poisons, and prey shape predators in turn. Parasites, which feed on a host without usually killing it quickly, are a close relative and are extremely common.

Mutualism

Both species gain. Bees get nectar and flowers get pollinated. Fungi on roots trade minerals for sugar. Cleaner fish remove parasites from larger fish. Your gut bacteria digest fibre you could not, as The passengers describes.

Commensalism

One species gains and the other is barely affected, such as birds nesting in a tree. In practice it is hard to prove the other side gains or loses nothing, so the category is fuzzy.

Chapter 07

Keystones and cascades

In the 1960s the American ecologist Robert Paine began an experiment on the rocky shore of Washington State. In one area he repeatedly removed a purple starfish, a predator of mussels, and left a nearby area alone. Without the starfish, mussels spread across the rocks and crowded out almost everything else. The number of species in the experimental area fell sharply. Paine called the starfish a keystone species, after the central stone at the top of an arch: small relative to the whole, but remove it and the structure collapses.

Effects that ripple down through a food web are called trophic cascades. On the Pacific coast of North America, sea otters eat sea urchins, and urchins eat kelp, the giant seaweed that forms underwater forests. Where otters were hunted out for fur, urchins multiplied and stripped the kelp. Where otters returned, kelp forests recovered. The predator at the top indirectly protects the plant at the bottom.

The best-known claimed cascade is more debated. Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995. A popular story says they scared elk away from rivers, letting willows and aspens recover, which in turn changed the rivers themselves. Some studies support part of this. Others argue the effects were smaller and patchier, and that bears, cougars, human hunting and drought also changed elk numbers. The honest summary is that wolves mattered, but the tidy viral version is overstated.

Chapter 08

Disturbance and recovery

Ecosystems are not frozen. They are repeatedly disturbed by fire, flood, storms, landslides and volcanoes, and then rebuilt. The predictable sequence of communities that colonise a disturbed area is called succession. On bare rock, lichens and mosses arrive first, building a thin soil; then grasses, then shrubs, then trees.

When Mount St Helens in the United States erupted in 1980, it flattened forest over hundreds of square kilometres. Ecologists expected a slow restart from the edges. Instead, life returned faster than predicted, from survivors: gophers underground, plants under late snow, and seeds and roots in the soil. Some ecosystems even depend on disturbance. Many pine forests and grasslands are adapted to regular fire, and some pine cones only release their seeds after heat. Suppressing all fires can let fuel build up until a rare, catastrophic fire destroys what small fires would have spared. Stability in nature often comes from regular small disruptions, not from their absence.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Services we do not pay for, and mistakes we keep making

Ecology matters because human life depends on working ecosystems, and because ignoring how they work has repeatedly caused expensive disasters.

Chapter 09

What nature does for free

Ecologists use the term ecosystem services for the benefits people get from nature. The list is long and mostly invisible until it fails.

  • Pollination. Roughly three-quarters of the leading types of food crops benefit at least partly from animal pollinators, mainly insects. Many staple grains, such as wheat, rice and maize, are pollinated by wind, so the effect is largest on fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds.
  • Clean water. Forests and wetlands filter water and slow floods. New York City famously chose to protect the forested watershed that supplies it, rather than build a far costlier filtration plant.
  • Coastal protection. Mangroves, reefs and salt marshes absorb the force of waves and storm surges.
  • Soil fertility and pest control. Soil organisms recycle nutrients, and birds, bats and insects eat crop pests.

In 1997 a team led by the economist Robert Costanza estimated the global value of such services at around 33 trillion dollars a year, then more than the world's total economic output. The number was rough and debated, but it made a point. Much of what keeps economies running is supplied by ecosystems that appear in no accounts.

Chapter 10

When people ignore the web

History is full of interventions that went wrong because of connections nobody considered. In 1935 cane toads from the Americas were released in Queensland, Australia, to eat beetles damaging sugar cane. They did little to the beetles. They spread across a vast area of northern Australia, and because they are poisonous, native predators that tried to eat them, such as some quolls, monitor lizards and snakes, died in large numbers.

In the 1950s and 1960s, the Nile perch, a large predatory fish, was introduced into Lake Victoria in East Africa to boost fishing. It created a valuable export fishery. It also contributed to the disappearance of a large share of the lake's hundreds of species of small cichlid fish, one of the most remarkable bursts of evolution known, alongside pollution and other pressures.

Pesticides offer another lesson. Some chemicals that break down slowly become more concentrated at each step up a food chain, a process called biomagnification. That is how DDT sprayed on crops ended up thinning the eggshells of eagles and falcons, as Rachel Carson documented. An ecosystem returns your actions to you by routes you did not plan.

Chapter 11

The scale of current change

The main pressures on ecosystems today are well known: changes in how land and sea are used, especially clearing forests and grasslands for farming; direct exploitation through fishing, hunting and logging; climate change; pollution; and invasive species. In 2019 the international science panel on biodiversity, IPBES, concluded that around one million species of animals and plants were threatened with extinction, many within decades.

The WWF's Living Planet Report of 2024 found that monitored populations of wild vertebrates had declined by an average of 73% since 1970. That figure is often misread. It does not mean that 73% of all animals have disappeared. It is an average of the percentage changes across thousands of tracked populations, some falling steeply and some rising. It is still a strong signal of widespread decline, especially in freshwater and in Latin America. The fuller picture is in Biodiversity and Deforestation.

That last point deserves a sentence. The old idea of a balance of nature, in which undisturbed ecosystems settle into a fixed perfect state, has been largely replaced by a picture of constant change, disturbance and recovery, as Chapter 08 showed. Ecosystems can have several stable states, and a push can tip them from one into another, such as a clear lake into a murky one dominated by algae. Getting them back may be much harder than tipping them over.

Chapter 13

The policy debates ecology feeds

Should nature be valued in money? Supporters of putting a price on ecosystem services argue that what is not counted is ignored in decisions, so valuation protects nature. Critics argue that pricing makes nature seem replaceable and that some things have value beyond any price. Many governments now use both kinds of argument.

How much land should be protected, and for whom? In 2022, nearly every country signed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, including a target to conserve 30% of land and sea by 2030, often called "30 by 30". Supporters see it as the minimum to stop losses. Critics, including some Indigenous groups, warn that protected areas have sometimes displaced local people, and argue that rights for those communities, who manage large areas of well-conserved land, matter as much as lines on maps. Most current approaches try to combine protection with local rights, with mixed results so far.

Chapter 15

Listening to ecosystems: the new toolkit

The biggest change in ecology may be in how it is measured. Every organism sheds traces of DNA into water and soil. By sampling a litre of river water and reading the DNA fragments in it, researchers can detect which fish, amphibians and even mammals live upstream. This is environmental DNA, or eDNA, and it can find rare species that are almost never seen.

Automatic recorders capture birdsong, bat calls and even reef sounds, and AI software identifies the species. Satellites track forest loss almost in real time. Camera traps photograph shy animals day and night. Citizen scientists log millions of sightings through phone apps. Ecology is shifting from scarce data to abundant data, which should make its predictions better. The harder part, as Chapter 13 showed, will remain deciding what to do with what it finds. The core idea

Ecology studies how living things interact with each other and their surroundings. Energy flows through ecosystems and is lost at each step; matter is recycled by decomposers and cycles. Species compete, eat, help and depend on each other, so changing one can cascade through the whole web.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Roughly 10% of energy passes from one food level to the next.
  • About three-quarters of leading food crop types benefit from animal pollination.
  • About 1 million species threatened with extinction (IPBES, 2019).
The common mistake

Believing nature settles into a fixed balance if left alone. Ecosystems are always changing, and some need regular disturbance; the question is which changes they can recover from.

Test yourself

Can you explain why removing sea otters could destroy a kelp forest? If you can, you understand food webs, keystone species and trophic cascades together.

Keystone species

Trophic cascade

Succession

Biomagnification
The build-up of a persistent chemical at each step up a food chain.
Ecosystem services
The benefits people get from nature, such as pollination and clean water.
