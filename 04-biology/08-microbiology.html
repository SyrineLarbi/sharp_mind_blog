Microbiology. The invisible majority that made the planet and still runs it.

Most life on Earth is too small to see. Microbes were here for billions of years before any animal, they put the oxygen in the air, they recycle every dead thing, and a spoonful of garden soil holds billions of them. A small minority cause disease, and fighting those has saved more lives than almost anything else humans have done. Microbiology is the science of all of them: how to see them, grow them, name them and use them, and why most are not our enemies.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of living things too small to see

"Microbe" is not a family of living things. It is a size category, and it includes some of the most different organisms on Earth.

Chapter 01

A world nobody suspected

When Antonie van Leeuwenhoek sent letters to the Royal Society in London in the 1670s describing tiny swimming creatures in rainwater, many readers doubted him. Nobody else had lenses good enough to check. Eventually others confirmed what he saw. For the next two centuries, though, those "animalcules" were treated as a curiosity. Few people imagined they could cause disease, ferment wine, or shape the chemistry of the whole planet.

That is the central surprise of microbiology. The visible living world, everything from mosses to whales, is a thin layer on top of a far older, larger and more varied microbial world. Life on Earth was microbial for most of its history, roughly the first three billion years, before large multicellular organisms appeared. Microbes live in boiling hot springs, in Antarctic ice, in rocks kilometres underground, and in clouds.

Chapter 03

The tree of life was redrawn by a microbe

Until the 1970s, biologists split life into two great groups: cells with a nucleus and cells without. Then the American microbiologist Carl Woese compared one molecule present in all cells, the RNA of the ribosome, across many species. In 1977 he and George Fox reported that some microbes that looked like bacteria were as different from bacteria, at the molecular level, as bacteria are from humans. They became a separate group, the archaea.

Life is now usually drawn as three domains: bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes, the cells with a nucleus that include all animals, plants and fungi. On that tree, everything you can see with the naked eye sits on one branch. Many researchers now think eukaryotes themselves arose from a merger between an archaeon and a bacterium, which would make the tree look more like a web at its base. The exact shape is still debated.

Question 2 · How does it work?

How you study something you cannot see

Microbiology became a science when researchers learned to prove what microbes do, to grow them pure, and later to read their DNA without growing them at all.

Chapter 04

The flask with the swan neck

For centuries many people believed that life could arise on its own from non-living matter, an idea called spontaneous generation. Leave broth out, and it soon swarms with microbes. Where did they come from, if not from the broth itself?

Around 1860 the French chemist Louis Pasteur settled the question with an elegant design. He boiled broth in flasks whose necks he had bent into a long S-shape, like a swan's neck. Air could reach the broth, but dust and microbes settled in the bend and never got there. The broth stayed clear for as long as he watched. Break off the neck, or tip the broth into the bend, and it quickly clouded with life. The microbes came from the air, not from the broth. Pasteur also showed that specific microbes caused wine and beer to ferment or spoil, and that gentle heating killed the spoilers. That process, still used for milk, is called pasteurisation.

Chapter 05

Koch's rules: proving one germ causes one disease

The German doctor Robert Koch turned the suspicion that germs cause disease into a method. In 1876 he showed that a specific bacterium caused anthrax, and in 1882 he identified the bacterium that causes tuberculosis, then one of the leading causes of death in Europe. His approach became known as Koch's postulates: find the microbe in every case of the disease, grow it pure, give it to a healthy animal and see the disease appear, then recover the same microbe again.

To grow microbes pure, his laboratory needed a firm surface. Potato slices and gelatine did not work well, since gelatine melts at body temperature. The answer was agar, a jelly made from seaweed that stays solid when warm. It was suggested by Fanny Hesse, the wife of one of Koch's colleagues, who used it in her kitchen. Koch's assistant Julius Petri designed the shallow covered glass dish still used today. On agar in a Petri dish, a single cell grows into a visible spot, a colony, made of millions of identical copies. Pure cultures made it possible to study one species at a time.

The postulates have limits. Some microbes cannot be grown in the lab, some people carry a germ without getting ill, and some diseases need more than one microbe. Modern versions use DNA evidence instead.

Chapter 06

Staining and sorting

Most bacteria are colourless and hard to see under a microscope. In 1884 the Danish scientist Hans Christian Gram developed a staining method that still opens almost every bacterial investigation. Bacteria are dyed purple, washed with alcohol, then dyed pink. Bacteria with a thick wall keep the purple and are called Gram-positive. Those with a thin wall and an extra outer membrane lose it and turn pink: Gram-negative.

The colour is more than a label. That outer membrane blocks many antibiotics, so Gram-negative infections are often harder to treat, and several of the most worrying drug-resistant bacteria are Gram-negative. A Gram stain in a hospital laboratory, with the bacterium's shape (round, rod or spiral) and its growth on different media, can point doctors towards a first treatment within hours, before more precise tests come back.

Chapter 07

Most microbes refuse to grow, so we read their DNA instead

For a century, a microbe that could not be grown in a dish was almost invisible to science. Then researchers noticed a puzzle. Under the microscope, a sample of soil or seawater held far more cells than ever grew into colonies on agar, often a hundred times more or more. This gap became known as the "great plate count anomaly". Most microbial species have never been grown in a lab, because they need partners, conditions or food we have not worked out.

The answer was to skip growing them. Using Woese's ribosomal RNA gene as a barcode, researchers could extract DNA from a sample of soil, water or gut and sequence it to see who was there. The next step, metagenomics, sequences all the DNA in a sample at once and reconstructs genomes from it. These methods revealed huge numbers of previously unknown groups. Estimates of how many microbial species exist on Earth range widely, from millions to, in one well-known estimate, a trillion. The true number is genuinely unknown.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Microbes made the air, feed the soil, and kill or save us

Remove animals from Earth and microbes would carry on. Remove microbes, and nearly everything else would soon collapse.

Chapter 08

The oxygen you breathe was made by bacteria

Early Earth's atmosphere had almost no free oxygen. Then a group of bacteria, the cyanobacteria, evolved a way to use sunlight to split water, releasing oxygen as waste. Over a long period this transformed the planet. Around 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen began to build up in the air, an event known as the Great Oxidation Event. It poisoned many microbes that could not cope with oxygen and made possible the energy-rich, oxygen-using life that came later.

Cyanobacteria are still at work. Microscopic ocean organisms, including cyanobacteria and algae, carry out roughly half of all photosynthesis on Earth. And the chloroplasts in every plant cell are descended from cyanobacteria that were swallowed and kept, just as mitochondria descend from swallowed bacteria (see Cell biology). Every green leaf is, in a sense, a home for captured bacteria.

Chapter 09

The planet's recycling crew

When a tree falls or an animal dies, fungi and bacteria break it down and return carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus to the soil and air. Without decomposers, dead material would pile up and the nutrients locked in it would be lost to new life.

Nitrogen shows how essential microbes are. Proteins and DNA need nitrogen, and the air is nearly 80% nitrogen gas. But almost no organism can use that gas directly, because its two atoms are bound very tightly. Certain bacteria can break the bond and turn nitrogen into forms plants can use, a process called nitrogen fixation. Some live in nodules on the roots of beans, peas and clover, which is why farmers have long rotated those crops to enrich the soil. Since the early twentieth century, industry has also fixed nitrogen with the energy-hungry Haber–Bosch process to make fertiliser. Between them, microbes and factories supply the nitrogen in the food that feeds humanity.

Chapter 10

The payoff of germ theory

Once people accepted that specific microbes cause specific diseases, a chain of practical changes followed. The British surgeon Joseph Lister began using carbolic acid to kill germs in wounds and on instruments in the 1860s, and deaths after surgery fell. Cities built sewers and treated water, which cut cholera and typhoid (Sewers, not surgeons). Vaccines were developed for one disease after another. Antibiotics arrived in the 1940s.

Together with better nutrition, these changes help explain why life expectancy in rich countries rose by decades during the twentieth century, much of it from far fewer children dying of infections. Germ theory is probably the most life-saving idea in the history of medicine. Infections still kill millions of people a year, mostly in poorer countries, and drug resistance is eroding some of the gains (Antibiotic resistance).

Chapter 11

Microbes at work

Humans were using microbes long before they knew microbes existed. Bread, beer, wine, cheese, yoghurt, soy sauce, kimchi and vinegar are all made by yeasts or bacteria. Since the twentieth century, microbes have also become factories. Penicillin is made by a mould. Many enzymes in washing powders come from microbes. In 1982 human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria became one of the first medicines of its kind to be approved, replacing insulin taken from the pancreases of pigs and cattle.

Microbes also clean up after us. Sewage treatment relies on bacteria that eat organic waste. After oil spills, naturally occurring microbes break down part of the oil, a process sometimes encouraged by adding nutrients. This use of living things to remove pollution is called bioremediation, and it works better for some pollutants than others.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Friends far outnumber foes, and our tools have blind spots

The microbial world is mostly helpful to humans. The science that studies it has real strengths and some built-in limits.

Chapter 13

The field's strengths and blind spots

Strengths

Microbiology turned infectious disease from fate into a problem to be solved. Its tools, from culture to sequencing, now identify a pathogen in hours. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the virus's genome was published within weeks of the first reports, and sequencing tracked new variants around the world in close to real time.

Blind spots

Most species still cannot be grown, so we know many only from DNA. Lab strains grown for decades can behave differently from wild ones. Knowing which microbes are present does not prove what they are doing, a gap that has led to many overstated claims about the microbiome. And funding has long favoured the few microbes that cause human disease over the many that run ecosystems.

There is also a cultural cost. The success of germ theory taught people to see microbes mainly as enemies. The modern picture, in which most microbes are neutral or helpful and a few are dangerous, has not fully reached everyday habits, such as heavy use of antibacterial products at home, which public health agencies generally say adds little for healthy households compared with plain soap.

Chapter 15

From fighting microbes to managing them

For about a century, the main question of medical microbiology was how to kill germs. The question is changing to how to manage microbial communities: in the gut, in hospitals, in soil and in farm animals. That means using antibiotics more carefully, protecting helpful species, and sometimes adding microbes rather than removing them.

The useful attitude for a non-specialist is respect, not fear. Microbes are the oldest, most numerous and most important living things on Earth. A few deserve our vigilance. Most deserve our thanks. The next two files look closely at the two groups that matter most for health: viruses and bacteria.

The core idea

Microbiology studies bacteria, archaea, microscopic fungi, protists and viruses. Pasteur and Koch proved that specific microbes cause specific effects. Microbes made Earth's oxygen, recycle its nutrients, and mostly help us. Most species cannot be grown in a lab and are known only from their DNA.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Life was microbial for roughly the first three billion years.
  • Oxygen began building up about 2.4 billion years ago, thanks to cyanobacteria.
  • Ocean microbes and algae do roughly half of the planet's photosynthesis.
The common mistake

Thinking of microbes as germs. Disease-causing species are a small minority; most microbes are harmless or essential.
