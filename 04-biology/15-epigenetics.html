Epigenetics. The same score, played differently in every cell.

A neuron in your brain and a cell in your liver carry exactly the same DNA. Yet one sends electrical signals and the other breaks down alcohol. The difference is not in the genes themselves but in which genes each cell has switched on and which it has silenced. Epigenetics is the study of those switches. It explains how one fertilised egg builds a body, why identical twins drift apart, and how some cancers start. It has also become one of the most over-sold ideas in popular science. Both sides are worth understanding.

Question 1 · What is it?

Changes in what genes do, without changes in what genes say

The prefix "epi" means "on top of". Epigenetics is the layer of chemical control sitting on top of the DNA sequence, deciding which parts of it are read.

Chapter 01

Two hundred jobs from one instruction book

Your DNA contains about 20,000 genes that code for proteins. A typical cell uses only a fraction of them. A red blood cell precursor switches on the genes for haemoglobin. A skin cell switches on the genes for keratin, the tough protein in skin and hair. Each cell type has its own pattern of on and off, and it keeps that pattern for life. When it divides, its daughters inherit the pattern too. A dividing skin cell makes skin cells, not liver cells.

That memory cannot be stored in the DNA sequence, because the sequence is the same in both. It is stored in chemical marks attached to the DNA and to the proteins it is wrapped around. Epigenetics is the study of changes in gene activity that can be passed on when cells divide, without any change to the DNA sequence itself.

An analogy helps. Think of the genome as a huge cookbook, identical in every kitchen. Each kitchen has put sticky notes on some recipes ("use often") and taped other pages shut ("never"). The book is the same; what gets cooked is not. Epigenetic marks are the notes and the tape.

Chapter 02

The landscape and the cat

The word was coined in the early 1940s by the British biologist Conrad Waddington. He pictured development as a ball rolling down a hilly landscape. At the top, the ball can go anywhere. As it rolls it enters valleys, and the valleys branch, until the ball settles in one, which is the cell's final type. Climbing back out is very hard. That image, the epigenetic landscape, is still used today. Reprogramming a skin cell into a stem cell is like pushing the ball back up the hill.

The most visible example walks around many homes. A tortoiseshell or calico cat has patches of orange and black fur. The gene for those coat colours sits on the X chromosome. A female cat has two X chromosomes, one with the orange version and one with the black. Early in development, each cell in a female embryo shuts down one of its two X chromosomes at random, and all of that cell's descendants keep the same one shut. The British geneticist Mary Lyon proposed this X-inactivation in 1961. The patches on the cat are the descendants of different early cells.

The proof is striking. In 2001 researchers in Texas cloned a calico cat. The clone, named CC, had exactly the same DNA as her donor, but a different coat pattern. Identical genes, different epigenetic choices, visibly different animals. Human females do X-inactivation too; it just does not show on the skin.

Chapter 03

What epigenetics is not

Because the word sounds like a loophole in genetics, it is often misused. Three corrections help.

  • It does not mean genes do not matter. Epigenetic marks can only switch on or off what the DNA already contains. They cannot write a new gene.
  • It is not the same as "environment". Most epigenetic marks are set by the body's own developmental programme. Only some respond to diet, stress or toxins, and usually modestly.
  • It is not usually inherited by children. Most marks are wiped clean when eggs and sperm are made, as Chapter 06 explains. Inheritance across generations is real in some plants and animals but is uncertain and contested in humans.
Question 2 · How does it work?

Tags on the DNA, and the way it is packed

There are two main mechanisms. Small chemical tags on the DNA itself, and changes to the proteins it is wound around. Both control whether the cell's reading machinery can reach a gene.

Chapter 04

DNA methylation: the "do not read" tag

DNA is written in four chemical letters: A, C, G and T. The best-studied epigenetic mark is a tiny chemical group, called a methyl group (one carbon atom with three hydrogens), attached to the letter C. It usually happens where a C is followed by a G, a spot written "CpG".

When many Cs near the start of a gene carry methyl tags, the gene is usually silenced. The tags attract proteins that close the region up and block the machinery that reads genes. This is called DNA methylation. Special enzymes add the tags, others maintain them during cell division, and others can remove them.

Methylation does real work. It keeps the second X chromosome silent in female cells. It keeps many "jumping genes", stretches of DNA left by ancient viruses that could otherwise copy themselves around the genome, locked away. And it fixes cell identity, so that a liver cell's non-liver genes stay off. Methylation is one of the cell's main ways of saying "this page stays shut".

Chapter 05

Packaging: spools, loose and tight

Each of your cells holds about two metres of DNA, packed into a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre across. It fits because it is wound around protein spools called histones. DNA plus its proteins is called chromatin.

The histones have tails that stick out, and cells attach many kinds of chemical tags to those tails. Some, such as acetyl groups, tend to loosen the packing, so genes become readable. Others help tighten it. Loosely packed regions are called open chromatin; tightly packed regions are closed. Whether a gene can be read depends partly on how tightly its stretch of DNA is wound.

Scientists sometimes talk of a "histone code", a language of tag combinations. That phrase is still debated, because the meaning of each tag often depends on context. A third layer involves RNA molecules that do not make proteins but help guide silencing. The general idea is in RNA.

Chapter 06

Copying the marks, and wiping them clean

When a cell divides, it copies its DNA. Enzymes follow behind and copy the methylation pattern onto the new strand, so the daughter cells keep the same switches. That is how a cell type stays itself through many divisions.

Between generations, the system is mostly reset. Two large waves of erasure happen: one while the cells that will become eggs or sperm are forming, and another in the early embryo just after fertilisation. Then new patterns are written as the embryo develops. This reset is why most of the epigenetic changes you gather during life are not passed to your children.

A small set of genes escapes the reset on purpose. In genomic imprinting, a gene is switched on or off depending on whether it came from the mother or the father. The clearest human example is a region of chromosome 15. If a child lacks the working father's copy of this region, the result is Prader-Willi syndrome, with constant hunger and other problems. If the child lacks the working mother's copy, it is Angelman syndrome, a different condition with severe developmental delay. Same region, two diseases, decided by which parent's copy is missing.

Chapter 07

When the environment writes the marks

Some marks respond to conditions. The best-known experiment used a strain of mice called "agouti". These mice carry a gene variant that, when active, makes them yellow, obese and prone to diabetes. How active it is depends on its methylation. In 2003 Robert Waterland and Randy Jirtle in the United States fed pregnant agouti mice a diet rich in folic acid and other nutrients that supply methyl groups. More of their pups were brown and lean. The DNA sequence was the same; the methylation, and the animals, were different.

Honeybees offer another example. Queens and workers are genetically alike, yet a queen is larger, lives far longer and lays eggs. The difference comes from what larvae are fed, especially royal jelly. Research has shown that changes in DNA methylation are part of the story, though the full mechanism involves other factors too.

These cases are real and important. But they are cases where a strong, specific effect was found in a carefully controlled animal. Most claims about everyday human life are far weaker, as Question 4 explains.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Famine, cancer, and the age of your cells

Epigenetics matters because it links early life to later health, because it is broken in cancer, and because it has given us a new way to measure ageing.

Chapter 08

The Dutch Hunger Winter

In the winter of 1944 to 1945, a German blockade cut food supplies to the western Netherlands. Official rations fell at times to well below a thousand calories a day. About twenty thousand people died. Babies were still being conceived and born, and the Netherlands kept good records.

Decades later, researchers studied those babies as adults. People exposed to the famine in the womb, especially early in pregnancy, had higher rates of obesity, raised cholesterol and heart disease than siblings born before or after. In 2008 a team including Bas Heijmans and L. H. Lumey reported that about six decades later, people conceived during the famine had different methylation at a gene called IGF2, which is involved in growth, than their unexposed siblings.

This is one of the strongest human examples that conditions before birth can leave lasting epigenetic marks. Two cautions apply. The differences were small, and it is not proven that those marks caused the later diseases rather than simply travelling with them. The broader idea that early life shapes adult health is well supported; the exact epigenetic chain is still being worked out.

Chapter 09

Cancer: when the switches fail

Cancer begins with changes that let a cell divide out of control. Many are mutations in the DNA sequence. But cancer cells also show widespread epigenetic disorder. Genes that normally stop tumours, called tumour suppressor genes, are often silenced by too much methylation, even when their sequence is fine. At the same time, much of the rest of the genome loses methylation, which makes it unstable. The general story of cancer is in What cancer is.

Unlike a mutation, an epigenetic mark can in principle be reversed with drugs. That idea has produced real medicines. Azacitidine and decitabine, which block DNA methylation, were approved in the United States in the mid-2000s for myelodysplastic syndromes, a group of bone-marrow cancers, and are used in some forms of leukaemia. Drugs that block histone deacetylases, the enzymes that strip acetyl tags, are approved for some lymphomas. Epigenetic drugs work best so far in blood cancers; results in solid tumours have been more limited.

Chapter 10

Epigenetic clocks: how old are your cells?

As we age, methylation at certain spots in the genome changes in a regular way. In 2013 the German-American researcher Steve Horvath published an "epigenetic clock": a formula that uses methylation at a few hundred sites to estimate a person's age, often within a few years, from many tissues. Other clocks followed.

The interesting part is the gap. If your clock reads older than your birth certificate, studies find you are, on average, at higher risk of disease and earlier death. Smoking, obesity and some illnesses tend to push the clock forward. That makes clocks valuable research tools for testing whether a treatment slows ageing, without waiting decades.

Commercial "biological age" tests are now sold directly to the public. Treat their numbers with caution. Results can vary between companies and even between repeated samples, and no one yet knows whether turning the clock back actually adds healthy years, or just changes the reading. See Ageing, longevity and the honest state of it.

Chapter 11

Can experiences be inherited? The contested question

In plants, the answer is clearly yes for some marks. In 1999 the British plant scientist Enrico Coen and colleagues found that a strange form of the toadflax flower, first described by Linnaeus in the 1700s, is caused not by a mutation but by heavy methylation of one gene, passed down through seeds. In tiny worms used in the lab, small RNA signals can carry effects for several generations.

In mammals the evidence is weaker. Some mouse studies have reported that a father's diet or stress changes his offspring, perhaps through small RNAs in sperm. A 2014 study reported that mice trained to fear a smell had offspring more sensitive to it; that result has been widely discussed and questioned.

In humans, the best-known evidence comes from records in Överkalix, a remote parish in northern Sweden. Researchers found that how much food a grandparent had in childhood was associated with the death rates of grandchildren. Such studies are fascinating but small, and families share culture, wealth and habits as well as cells, which makes it very hard to separate biological inheritance from everything else. The honest position: transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans is possible but unproven, and genuinely contested.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A real revolution, and a magnet for hype

For the cell, epigenetics is a powerful system with failure points. For science and culture, it is a powerful idea that is easy to stretch too far.

Chapter 13

How to read an epigenetics headline

Popular books and wellness websites often claim that you can "rewrite your genes" with positive thinking, special diets or supplements, or that trauma is reliably handed down to grandchildren through DNA. These claims usually stretch real but limited findings. Four questions help.

  • Which tissue? Most human studies measure blood or saliva, because they are easy to collect. Marks in blood may say little about the brain.
  • Cause or consequence? A disease can change methylation, just as methylation might contribute to disease. A correlation does not show which came first.
  • How big? Many reported differences are a few percent at a few sites. Statistically real and biologically uncertain.
  • Mouse or human? The famous dramatic results are mostly from animals bred for the experiment.

None of this makes epigenetics unimportant. It has transformed biology's understanding of development and cancer. The mistake is to treat a mechanism for fine control as a promise of easy transformation. The habits that improve health, such as not smoking, staying active and sleeping well, are worth doing whether or not they change your methylation.

Chapter 15

Who gets to read your marks?

Epigenetic data raises questions that genetic data did not. Your DNA sequence is fixed. Your methylation changes, and it carries traces of age, smoking and possibly other habits and exposures. In principle, a sample could estimate how old someone is, whether they smoke, and something about their health risks.

Researchers have explored using age estimates from methylation in forensic work, to help identify an unknown person from a sample. Insurers and employers could one day be tempted by "biological age". Most countries' genetic privacy laws were written for DNA sequences and may not clearly cover these newer markers. The likely direction is that epigenetic testing becomes cheaper faster than the rules for using it are written. That makes the questions in Chapter 13 worth asking of anyone who wants your sample.

The core idea

Every cell has the same DNA, but each switches on a different set of genes. Epigenetic marks, mainly DNA methylation and histone tags, control that choice, are copied when cells divide, and are mostly reset between generations. Some marks respond to the environment, and their failure contributes to cancer.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 20,000 protein-coding genes, the same in every cell.
  • About two metres of DNA wound inside each cell nucleus.
  • Hunger Winter marks still detectable about six decades later (2008 study).
The common mistake

Thinking epigenetics means you can rewrite your genes or that your life experiences pass to your grandchildren. Marks switch existing genes; they do not write new ones, and human inheritance of experience is unproven.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a cloned calico cat looks different from its donor? If you can, you understand the difference between the genome and the epigenome.
