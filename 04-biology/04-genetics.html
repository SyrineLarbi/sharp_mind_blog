Genetics. Why you look like your family, and why you are not a copy of anyone.

Genetics is the science of inheritance: how traits pass from parents to children, and why each child is a new combination. It began with a monk counting peas and now reads entire genomes for a few hundred dollars. Its central lesson is more subtle than most people expect. A few traits are decided by one gene, cleanly and predictably. Most, from height to the risk of heart disease, are nudged by thousands of small genetic differences and shaped by the life you live. Understanding that difference protects you from both hype and fear.

Question 1 · What is it?

The rules by which differences are passed on

Before genetics, people thought a child's traits were a blend of the parents', like mixing paint. That idea is wrong, and seeing why is the start of the whole subject.

Chapter 01

A monk, a garden and ten thousand pea plants

Gregor Mendel was a friar in the city of Brno, now in the Czech Republic. Over about eight years in the 1850s and 1860s he bred thousands of pea plants in the monastery garden and counted their traits: tall or short, round seeds or wrinkled, purple flowers or white. He published his results in 1866. Almost nobody noticed. In 1900 three other botanists independently found the same patterns and then found his paper.

Here is what he saw. Cross a pure purple-flowered plant with a pure white one, and every child has purple flowers. White seems to have vanished. But let those children breed with each other, and white comes back in about a quarter of the next generation. If inheritance were blending, the white could never return. Mendel concluded that traits are carried by separate units that do not mix. They can be hidden for a generation and reappear unchanged. We now call those units genes.

Blending would also have been a problem for Darwin. If every new useful variation were diluted by half in each generation, it would soon disappear. Mendel's units solved that, but the two ideas were not joined until the 1930s and 1940s.

Chapter 02

Genes, alleles and chromosomes

A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for one product, usually a protein. Humans have about 20,000 genes that code for proteins. A gene can come in slightly different versions, called alleles. The gene for the ABO blood group, for example, comes in A, B and O versions.

Genes sit on chromosomes, very long DNA molecules packed tightly with proteins. Most human cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs. One member of each pair comes from your mother, the other from your father. So you carry two copies of almost every gene, which may be the same allele or two different ones. The 23rd pair decides biological sex in most cases: usually two X chromosomes in females, and one X and one smaller Y in males.

Any two people share about 99.9% of their DNA letters. All visible human variety, and much inherited disease risk, lives in the remaining tenth of a percent, which is still millions of letters.

Chapter 03

Genotype, phenotype, dominant and recessive

Your genotype is the set of alleles you carry. Your phenotype is what can actually be observed: eye colour, blood group, height, a disease. The two are not the same thing, for two reasons. First, one allele can hide another. Second, the environment affects how genes show.

An allele is dominant if one copy is enough to show its effect, and recessive if it shows only when both copies are that allele. In Mendel's peas, purple was dominant and white recessive. A plant with one purple and one white allele looked purple but could pass on white. People with one copy of a recessive disease allele are called carriers: usually healthy, but able to pass it on. Dominant does not mean common or stronger in any wider sense. It only describes what happens when the two copies differ.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Shuffle, deal, mutate, and add up thousands of small effects

Inheritance runs on a few mechanisms: how eggs and sperm are made, how single genes pass on, where new variation comes from, and how many genes combine.

Chapter 04

The shuffle: why siblings differ

Eggs and sperm are made by a special division called meiosis. It halves the chromosome number from 46 to 23, so that egg plus sperm makes 46 again. It also shuffles in two ways. First, for each of the 23 pairs, which copy goes into a given egg or sperm is chance, like flipping a coin 23 times. That alone gives over 8 million combinations. Second, before they separate, the paired chromosomes swap matching pieces with each other, a process called crossing over. So each chromosome you pass on is a new patchwork of your mother's and father's versions.

The result is that no two eggs or sperm are alike, and siblings, apart from identical twins, share on average about half their genetic variants. Identical twins come from one fertilised egg that split, so they share nearly all of their DNA. They are one of the most useful natural experiments in genetics, because any difference between them is mostly down to environment and chance.

Colour blindness shows the X-linked pattern clearly. Red-green colour blindness affects about 1 in 12 men of Northern European ancestry but only about 1 in 200 women, because a woman needs the variant on both of her X chromosomes. Queen Victoria carried haemophilia, and it spread through several royal families of Europe through her daughters.

Chapter 06

Mutation: where new variation comes from

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Most happen through copying errors or damage, from chemicals, radiation or simple chemical wear. Each person is born with a few dozen new mutations that neither parent had, most in DNA that does not matter much. More new mutations come through the father as he ages, because sperm-making cells keep dividing throughout life.

Mutations come in sizes. A single letter can change. A few letters can be lost or added, which can shift the reading of the three-letter code and scramble a protein. Or whole chromosomes can be affected. Down syndrome is usually caused by a third copy of chromosome 21, an error in sorting chromosomes when the egg or sperm is made. Its likelihood rises with the mother's age.

The word "mutation" sounds bad, but most mutations are neutral, some are harmful and a few are useful. Without them there would be no variation for evolution to work with. The ability of many adults to digest milk, which spread in some populations that kept cattle, comes from mutations that keep the milk-digesting enzyme switched on after childhood.

Chapter 07

Most traits: thousands of genes, each with a tiny effect

Height is strongly inherited. Tall parents tend to have tall children. But there is no "height gene". A very large study published in 2022, using the DNA of more than five million people, found over 12,000 common genetic variants linked to height. Each adds or removes a millimetre or less. Traits like this are called polygenic, "many genes", and most human traits and common diseases are polygenic: weight, blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and many aspects of behaviour.

Researchers find these variants with genome-wide association studies (GWAS). They compare the DNA of many people with and without a trait, at hundreds of thousands of points, and look for variants that are more common in one group. Adding up a person's variants gives a polygenic score, an estimate of genetic tendency.

The most misunderstood number

Heritability is the share of the variation in a trait, within a particular group in a particular environment, that is linked to genetic differences. Height has a heritability of about 80% in well-fed populations. That does not mean 80% of your height comes from genes, which makes no sense for one person. And heritability can change when the environment changes. If everyone ate the same diet, the share of height differences due to genes would go up, because environment would explain less.

Chapter 08

Genes and environment work together

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a recessive condition in which the body cannot break down phenylalanine, an amino acid found in most protein foods. Untreated, it causes severe brain damage. Treated with a special diet from birth, children develop normally. The genes are the same in both cases. The outcome is completely different. That is why most rich countries test newborns for PKU with a heel-prick blood test, a practice that began in the 1960s.

Whole populations show the same principle. Average adult height rose by several centimetres in many countries during the twentieth century, much too fast for genes to have changed. Better food and less childhood illness did it. Genes set a range of possible outcomes. The environment decides where in the range a person ends up. The study of how the environment can change which genes are active, without changing the DNA itself, is Epigenetics.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicine, family decisions, crime, and a dark history

Genetic knowledge changes what doctors can predict, what families can choose, and what police can find. It has also been badly misused.

Chapter 09

Sickle cell: a harmful gene with a hidden benefit

Sickle cell disease is caused by a change of a single DNA letter in the gene for haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells. With two copies, red cells can bend into a stiff sickle shape, block small blood vessels, and cause severe pain, organ damage and shorter life. So why is the allele common in parts of Africa, the Middle East and India?

Because people with one copy are partly protected against severe malaria, which has killed huge numbers of people in those regions for thousands of years. Where malaria is common, carriers survive and have children more often. The same allele is a burden in two copies and a shield in one, and natural selection keeps it in the population. It is the textbook example of how "good" and "bad" genes depend on the environment. In 2023 regulators in the UK and the US approved the first gene-editing treatment for sickle cell disease, though it is very expensive and demanding to receive.

Chapter 10

Testing, counselling and hard choices

Genetic testing now enters life at many points. Carrier screening tells couples whether both carry the same recessive allele. In some Ashkenazi Jewish communities, voluntary screening programmes since the 1970s have made Tay-Sachs disease, a fatal childhood illness, far rarer. Prenatal testing can detect conditions such as Down syndrome during pregnancy. Predictive testing tells adults about future risks. Women with harmful variants in the BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes have a much higher lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer, and some choose surgery to reduce it.

Each of these raises personal and ethical questions with no single right answer. Should someone at risk of Huntington's, which has no cure, find out? Many choose not to. How should prenatal results be used? Views differ sharply between people, religions and countries. This is why genetic counsellors exist: professionals who explain what a result means and what it does not, and support decisions rather than making them.

Health note

This file is general education about genetics, not medical advice. A genetic result should be interpreted with a doctor or genetic counsellor.

Chapter 11

Families, ancestry, crime, and the shadow of eugenics

Consumer DNA tests have been taken by tens of millions of people. They reunite relatives, reveal unknown parents, and estimate ancestry, although "percent ancestry" figures are statistical estimates that depend on the reference groups a company uses. In 2018 investigators in California identified a long-sought serial killer, the "Golden State Killer", by uploading crime-scene DNA to a public genealogy database and tracing distant relatives. The case opened a new method of policing and a lasting argument about privacy, because your DNA also says a lot about relatives who never agreed to share it.

The darkest chapter must be named plainly. In the early twentieth century, the eugenics movement claimed that society could be improved by stopping "unfit" people from having children. Many countries passed laws allowing forced sterilisation, including the United States, where the Supreme Court approved it in 1927, and Sweden. Nazi Germany took it furthest, from forced sterilisation to the murder of disabled people. The science behind eugenics was poor, and its values were worse. That history is why today's debates about embryo selection and genetic data are taken so seriously.

Chapter 13

The two errors to avoid

Genetic determinism

The belief that genes fix your fate. For a few conditions, such as Huntington's, they nearly do. For most traits, a genetic tendency shifts the odds modestly. People with a high polygenic risk for heart disease can lower it substantially through lifestyle and medicine. A tendency is not a destiny.

Genetic denial

The opposite belief, that genes do not matter for anything people care about. Twin and family studies consistently show that genetic differences contribute to almost every measured human trait, including some aspects of personality and behaviour. Ignoring that can lead to blaming people, or parents, for things they did not choose.

The honest middle is uncomfortable for both sides. Genetic differences between individuals are real and matter. But for most traits the effect of any single variant is tiny, the environment matters a great deal, and differences in averages between social groups cannot be read off from heritability within a group. Debates in this area, especially about intelligence and behaviour, remain contested and politically charged, and deserve careful reading rather than headlines.

Chapter 15

The real future question: who decides?

Reading DNA is now cheap. Interpreting it is getting better. Changing it is possible in some cases (Genetic engineering, and Gene editing in Volume 50). The scientific path is fairly clear. The open questions are social. Who owns genetic data? Can insurers or employers use it? Some countries ban that for health insurance and employment, but rules vary and often do not cover life insurance. Should parents choose traits for their children? Most countries ban editing human embryos for pregnancy, after a Chinese researcher's 2018 announcement that he had done exactly that was met with international condemnation.

The useful position for a cultured non-specialist is clear. Genes matter, environments matter, and for most traits neither one decides alone. When you hear "scientists find the gene for" anything complex, assume it is one small contributor among thousands, and ask how large the effect really is.

The core idea

Traits pass on through genes that come in versions called alleles, two copies each, one from each parent. Meiosis shuffles them so every child is new. A few traits follow one gene; most are polygenic and shaped by the environment.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs; about 20,000 protein-coding genes.
  • Any two people share about 99.9% of their DNA.
  • Two carrier parents: a 1 in 4 chance for each child of a recessive condition.
The common mistake

Reading "80% heritable" as "80% caused by genes in me". Heritability describes variation in a group, in one environment, not the make-up of one person.
