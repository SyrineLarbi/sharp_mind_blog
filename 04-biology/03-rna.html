RNA. The working copy that turned out to run the whole workshop.

For decades RNA was treated as a humble go-between: DNA holds the plans, proteins do the work, and RNA carries messages from one to the other. That picture was right but far too small. RNA builds proteins, switches genes off, edits its own messages, and forms the working heart of the machine that makes every protein in every cell. It may even be the molecule life started with. In the 2020s it also became a medicine given to billions of people.

Question 1 · What is it?

DNA's close cousin, built for action instead of storage

RNA is a chain of chemical letters, like DNA. Small differences in its chemistry make it short-lived, flexible and able to fold into working shapes. Those differences explain almost everything it does.

Chapter 01

The photocopy you are allowed to take out of the library

Imagine a reference library that holds one precious master copy of every recipe and never lends it out. If you want to cook, you photocopy the page you need, take the copy to the kitchen, use it, and throw it away. DNA is the master copy, kept safely inside the cell's nucleus. RNA is the photocopy: a temporary working copy of one gene, made when it is needed and destroyed soon after.

The analogy explains why the cell bothers. Copies can be made in large numbers, so a gene that is needed a lot can be read thousands of times at once. Copies can be thrown away quickly, so the cell can stop making a protein within minutes or hours by stopping the copying. And the master copy stays protected from wear and damage. The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein was called the "central dogma" by Francis Crick in 1958. It remains the main route, though RNA turned out to do much more than carry messages.

The key difference is the last one in the shape row. A single strand of RNA can fold back and pair with itself, making hairpins, loops and knots. Shape is what lets a molecule do work. DNA's neat double helix is excellent for storing information and poor at doing anything else. RNA can do both, a little less well than each specialist.

Only messenger RNA carries a recipe for a protein. The others are non-coding: they are the finished product themselves. That surprised biologists. Only about 1–2% of human DNA directly codes for proteins, yet a much larger share is copied into RNA at some point. How much of that RNA has a real job, and how much is background noise, is one of the open arguments in the field.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Copy, edit, read, and then destroy

The life of a messenger RNA has four stages. Each one is a point where the cell can control how much protein it makes.

Chapter 04

Transcription: making the copy

Copying DNA into RNA is called transcription, like transcribing a text into another script. An enzyme called RNA polymerase sits on the DNA at the start of a gene, unzips a short stretch, and builds a matching RNA strand letter by letter, using the pairing rules: opposite a G it places a C, opposite an A it places a U. Behind it, the DNA zips back up.

The cell decides which genes to copy using proteins called transcription factors, which bind to control regions near genes and either invite or block the polymerase. This is why a liver cell and a nerve cell can carry identical DNA and look completely different: they copy different genes. The speed is real but not instant. The largest human gene, for the muscle protein dystrophin, is so long that copying it once takes many hours.

Chapter 05

Editing: cutting out the pieces nobody reads

In the late 1970s researchers found something nobody expected. In animals and plants, most genes are broken up. The useful parts, called exons, are separated by long stretches called introns that are copied into the first RNA and then cut out. A large molecular machine called the spliceosome removes the introns and joins the exons together, a process called splicing. Phillip Sharp and Richard Roberts shared the 1993 Nobel Prize for discovering split genes.

Splicing is not just tidying. The same gene can be spliced in different ways, keeping some exons in one cell and others in another, a process called alternative splicing. Most human genes with several exons are spliced in more than one way. That is part of how about 20,000 human genes can produce a much larger variety of proteins.

Before the message leaves the nucleus, the cell adds a protective chemical cap to its front end and a long tail of A letters to its back end. Both help the message survive and be read. Then it is exported through pores in the nuclear membrane.

Chapter 06

Translation: reading three letters at a time

Turning the RNA message into a protein is called translation, because it changes languages, from nucleotide letters to amino acids. The ribosome clamps onto the message and moves along it three letters at a time. Each three-letter word is called a codon. With four letters there are 64 possible codons. Sixty-one name one of the 20 amino acids, and three mean "stop". The codon AUG means "start here" as well as naming the amino acid methionine.

From gene to protein, one short stretch
  DNA (template)   T A C | A A A | C C G | A T T
                     |       |       |       |      transcription
  mRNA             A U G | U U U | G G C | U A A
                     |       |       |       |      translation (each tRNA brings one amino acid)
  protein         Met  -  Phe  -  Gly  -  STOP

  AUG = start + methionine     UUU = phenylalanine     GGC = glycine     UAA = stop

Who reads the words? Transfer RNAs. Each tRNA has, on one end, three letters that pair with one codon, and on the other end the matching amino acid. It is a physical dictionary entry. The ribosome lines up the tRNAs one after another and joins their amino acids into a chain, which then folds into a working protein.

The first word of the code was cracked in 1961. Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei made an artificial RNA containing only U letters and added it to an extract of bacterial cells. The extract made a protein containing only phenylalanine. So UUU meant phenylalanine. Within about five years, the whole table was worked out.

Chapter 07

The surprise at the centre: the ribosome is mostly RNA

Biologists long assumed that only proteins could act as enzymes, molecules that speed up chemical reactions. In the early 1980s Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman each found RNA molecules that do exactly that, cutting and joining RNA without any protein. Such RNAs are called ribozymes, and the two shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The bigger shock came in 2000, when the first detailed atomic structures of the ribosome were published. The spot where amino acids are actually joined into a chain is made of RNA, not protein. The proteins sit mostly around the outside like scaffolding. Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, Thomas Steitz and Ada Yonath shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work. The most important machine in every living cell is, at its working heart, an RNA enzyme. That fact is a strong clue about life's history, as Chapter 09 explains.

Chapter 08

Turning messages down: RNA interference and microRNAs

In 1998 Andrew Fire and Craig Mello injected double-stranded RNA into worms and found that it silenced the matching gene far more powerfully than expected. Cells, it turned out, have machinery that chops double-stranded RNA into short pieces and uses them as search guides to find and destroy any message with the matching sequence. This is RNA interference. It probably began as a defence against viruses, many of which produce double-stranded RNA. Fire and Mello received the 2006 Nobel Prize.

Cells use the same machinery for their own purposes. In 1993 Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun, working on the same kind of worm, found a tiny RNA that controlled the timing of development by binding to another gene's message and turning it down. These microRNAs were at first thought to be a worm curiosity. They turned out to exist across animals and plants, and humans have hundreds of them. Each can dampen many target messages. Ambros and Ruvkun shared the 2024 Nobel Prize. Gene control does not stop when a gene is copied; a whole layer of regulation acts on the RNA afterwards.

Finally, every message is destroyed. Enzymes chew it from its ends once the cap or tail wear away. That is not a flaw. A message that lasts minutes lets the cell react quickly to changes.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The origin of life, the fastest viruses, and a new class of medicine

RNA matters in three very different places: in the deep past, in the diseases that change fastest, and in the pharmacy.

Chapter 09

The RNA world: which came first?

Life today has a chicken-and-egg problem. DNA holds the instructions for proteins, but proteins are needed to copy DNA. Neither can work without the other, so how did either start? RNA offers a way out, because it can both carry information and act as an enzyme. The RNA world hypothesis, named by the biologist Walter Gilbert in 1986, proposes that early life used RNA for both jobs, and that DNA (a more stable store) and proteins (better workers) took over later.

The evidence is circumstantial but consistent. The ribosome's working core is RNA. Several of the small helper molecules that enzymes need, including the energy molecule ATP, are built from RNA letters. And chemists have made RNA molecules in the lab that can copy short pieces of RNA. The hypothesis is widely favoured but not proven, and it has real difficulties. RNA is fragile, and how its letters could have formed and joined on the early Earth is still argued over. Other researchers favour scenarios in which metabolism or simpler molecules came first. Treat it as the leading idea, not settled history.

Chapter 10

RNA viruses: why the flu changes every year

Many of the viruses that trouble humans most keep their genes in RNA instead of DNA: influenza, measles, hepatitis C, HIV and the coronaviruses, including the one that causes COVID-19. When these viruses copy their RNA, their copying enzymes usually lack the careful proofreading that cells use for DNA. So they make many more mistakes per copy, and that mutation rate is why they change so fast. It is why a new flu vaccine is needed most years. Coronaviruses are an interesting partial exception: they have a proofreading enzyme, which helps them keep their unusually long RNA genome intact. They still changed quickly during the pandemic because there were so many infections.

HIV adds a twist. It is a retrovirus: it carries an enzyme, reverse transcriptase, that copies its RNA backwards into DNA, which then inserts into the infected cell's own DNA. That is why HIV infection cannot simply be cleared, and why many HIV drugs target that one enzyme. The same enzyme, borrowed from viruses, is a basic laboratory tool. The "PCR test" for COVID-19 first used reverse transcriptase to turn the virus's RNA into DNA, which could then be copied and detected. More on these viruses is in Viruses.

Chapter 11

RNA as medicine, beyond vaccines

Volume 13 explains how mRNA vaccines work. They are the best known RNA medicine, but not the only kind. RNA drugs share one big idea. If you know the letters of the gene behind a disease, you can design an RNA drug for it on a computer, by writing the matching sequence. Traditional drug discovery screens huge numbers of chemicals hoping one fits a protein. RNA drugs work by sequence, and that is much faster to design.

Silencing a harmful gene

Short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) use the cell's RNA interference machinery to destroy a chosen message. The first such drug, patisiran, was approved in the US in 2018 for a rare inherited nerve disease caused by a faulty liver protein. Several more have followed, including one that lowers "bad" LDL cholesterol with an injection twice a year.

Fixing how a message is spliced

Antisense drugs are short, chemically modified strands that stick to a chosen RNA. Nusinersen, approved in 2016 for spinal muscular atrophy, a severe inherited disease of the nerves that control muscles, changes how a backup gene is spliced so that it makes more of the missing protein. It was a landmark for a disease that often killed infants.

Health note

This is general education about how RNA medicines work, not medical advice. Whether a treatment suits any person is a question for a doctor.

Chapter 13

The cost and trust problem

The technical limits are being worked on. Two other problems are harder. The first is cost. Many RNA drugs for rare diseases are extremely expensive, because they serve few patients and took years to develop. A drug that works but that health systems cannot afford helps far fewer people than its science deserves.

The second is trust. mRNA vaccines arrived at speed during a frightening pandemic, and they became a political symbol in several countries. The scientific consensus is that their benefits greatly outweighed their risks for most groups, while rare side effects, such as heart inflammation mostly in young men, were real. Public confidence fell in some places anyway, and some governments have since reduced funding for mRNA vaccine research while others increased it. How that plays out will shape the technology as much as any laboratory result.

Chapter 15

From messenger to platform

The deeper change is in how people think about RNA. For most of the twentieth century it was a middle step in a diagram. Now it is a platform: a way to deliver an instruction to the body by writing a sequence. A vaccine teaches the immune system a shape. A silencing drug removes a harmful protein. A splicing drug repairs a broken message. Some of the newest gene-editing tools also arrive in the cell as RNA, because RNA does its job and then disappears. That links this topic to Genetic engineering and to CRISPR.

The honest summary is that RNA medicine is real, proven and still early. The design side moves fast. Delivery, cost, manufacturing and public trust move slowly. Expect steady progress rather than a revolution every year, and treat any headline claiming RNA will cure "all cancers" or "all genetic diseases" with care.

The core idea

RNA is a single-stranded, short-lived cousin of DNA. Cells transcribe genes into RNA, splice it, translate it into protein at the ribosome, and then destroy it. Because RNA can fold into working shapes, it also catalyses reactions and regulates genes, which is why it may have come before DNA.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 64 codons: 61 for the 20 amino acids, 3 for "stop".
  • Only about 1–2% of human DNA codes for proteins.
  • About 20,000 human genes, made into far more protein variants through splicing.
The common mistake

Thinking RNA is only a messenger. The ribosome's working core is RNA, and many RNAs never become protein at all: they are the finished, working product.
