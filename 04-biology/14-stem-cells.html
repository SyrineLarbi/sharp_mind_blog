Stem cells. The cells that have not yet decided what to be.

You began as one cell. That cell divided into the roughly two hundred kinds of cell in your body: nerve, muscle, bone, skin, blood. Most of your cells have long since chosen a job and kept it. A small number have not. They are stem cells, and they quietly rebuild your blood, gut and skin every day. Medicine has spent sixty years learning to use them. A few treatments save lives right now. Many more are promised, and a worrying number of clinics sell treatments that do not work. This file separates the three.

Question 1 · What is it?

Cells defined by two abilities

A stem cell can copy itself, and it can produce cells more specialised than itself. Any cell that does both counts. How far it can specialise decides what it is useful for.

Chapter 01

Copy yourself, and make something else

Most cells in your body are differentiated. That means they have specialised. A red blood cell carries oxygen and has even thrown out its nucleus to make room. A neuron sends signals. Neither can turn into anything else, and many cannot divide at all.

A stem cell is different in two ways. First, it can self-renew: when it divides, at least one of the daughter cells stays a stem cell. Second, it can differentiate: its other daughter can begin a path toward a specialised job. Think of a stem cell as a bank account that pays out money without ever going to zero. The account keeps its balance while the payouts build the body.

Stem cells differ in how many kinds of cell they can make. Biologists call this potency.

LevelCan becomeExample
TotipotentEvery cell of the body plus the placentaThe fertilised egg and its first few divisions

MultipotentSeveral related types within one familyBlood stem cells in bone marrow make all blood and immune cells
UnipotentOne type only, but can keep renewingCertain skin and muscle stem cells

As an embryo develops, cells move down this ladder. Each step closes some doors. For most of history, biologists believed those doors could never reopen. Chapter 06 is about the discovery that they can.

Chapter 02

Lumps on a mouse spleen

The modern study of stem cells began with a side effect of the atomic age. In the early 1960s, at the Ontario Cancer Institute in Toronto, the physicist James Till and the doctor Ernest McCulloch were studying how radiation kills. A heavy dose destroys the bone marrow, where blood is made. They found that injecting bone-marrow cells into such mice saved them. Small lumps then appeared on the mice's spleens.

Each lump, they showed, had grown from a single injected cell. And each contained several kinds of blood cell. Some of the lumps could even produce new lumps when transplanted again. In other words, one cell could both renew itself and give rise to many types. It was the first clear proof that adult stem cells exist, published in papers from 1961 onward.

Chapter 03

The stem-cell treatment that already works: bone marrow

Long before the phrase "stem cell therapy" became fashionable, doctors were already performing one. A bone marrow transplant works because it delivers blood stem cells. The American doctor E. Donnall Thomas and his team developed the method from the late 1950s, and by the 1970s it was curing some patients with leukaemia, a cancer of blood cells. Thomas shared the Nobel Prize in 1990.

The logic is simple. High-dose chemotherapy or radiation destroys the cancer, and with it the patient's own blood-forming system. Then donor stem cells are infused into a vein. They find their way to the bone marrow and rebuild every kind of blood and immune cell. Today the cells are often collected from the donor's bloodstream rather than from the bone, after a drug coaxes them out of the marrow, and cord blood from newborns is another source. Worldwide, these transplants are now performed tens of thousands of times a year. When you hear that stem-cell treatment is "still experimental", remember that this one has been standard for decades.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Niches in the body, recipes in the dish

Inside the body, stem cells live in special neighbourhoods that tell them when to divide. Outside it, scientists have learned to make them and to steer what they become.

Chapter 04

Where adult stem cells live, and what they do

Adult stem cells, sometimes called tissue stem cells, sit in particular places called niches. A niche is a microscopic neighbourhood of supporting cells and signals that keeps the stem cells calm most of the time and tells them when to divide.

Some tissues depend on them heavily. The lining of your small intestine is replaced every few days, from stem cells at the bottom of tiny pits in the gut wall. The outer layer of your skin is renewed roughly every month. Your bone marrow produces billions of new blood cells every day. Hair follicles have their own stem cells that drive each cycle of growth.

Other tissues have very little capacity. Heart muscle in adults renews only very slowly, so a heart attack leaves a scar rather than new muscle. The brain makes few new neurons in adulthood, and how many it makes in humans is still debated. This uneven ability is the main reason some injuries heal and others last for life. Some animals do far better. Flatworms called planarians can regrow an entire body from a small fragment, and the salamander called the axolotl can regrow a lost leg. Both rely on stem-cell-like cells we are still trying to understand.

Chapter 05

Embryonic stem cells

About five days after fertilisation, an embryo is a hollow ball of a hundred or so cells called a blastocyst. Inside it is a small cluster, the inner cell mass, that would go on to form the entire body. Those cells are pluripotent.

In 1998 the American biologist James Thomson and his team at the University of Wisconsin grew these cells in the lab from human embryos donated by couples who had finished fertility treatment and no longer needed them. The cells could be kept dividing indefinitely, and they could be turned into heart, nerve and gut cells in a dish. These are human embryonic stem cells.

Their power made them famous, and the way they are obtained made them controversial. Taking the inner cell mass destroys the embryo. People who regard an early embryo as having the moral status of a person saw this as unacceptable. Others saw a surplus embryo that would be discarded anyway as a fair source of research that could relieve great suffering. That debate shaped laws and funding for a decade, as Chapter 11 explains.

Chapter 06

Turning back the clock: reprogramming

Does a specialised cell still hold all the genetic instructions for a whole body? In 1962 the British biologist John Gurdon showed that it does. He took the nucleus from a frog's intestinal cell and put it into an egg whose own nucleus had been removed. Some of these eggs grew into tadpoles. The same principle, called nuclear transfer, produced Dolly the sheep in Scotland in 1996, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell.

Nuclear transfer needs eggs and is inefficient. The breakthrough came from Japan. In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka and Kazutoshi Takahashi at Kyoto University showed that switching on just four genes in mouse skin cells turned them back into pluripotent cells. In 2007 Yamanaka's team, and separately Thomson's team, did the same with human cells. The results are called induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPS cells. Gurdon and Yamanaka shared the Nobel Prize in 2012.

The four genes do not change the DNA code. They change which genes are switched on, wiping out the cell's "memory" of being skin. That switching system is the subject of the next file, Epigenetics. Specialisation turned out to be a setting, not a permanent loss.

Chapter 07

Steering a cell's fate, and growing mini-organs

Having pluripotent cells is only the start. To make, say, dopamine-producing neurons for a Parkinson's patient, scientists must guide the cells through the same steps an embryo uses. They do this by adding signalling molecules in a set order over days or weeks, copying the instructions an embryo would have received.

The check step matters. Pluripotent cells that slip through can form a teratoma, a tumour containing mixed tissues. Making cells safe and pure is a large part of the work.

Stem cells can also organise themselves. Given the right gel and signals, they form organoids: tiny three-dimensional structures, a few millimetres across, that copy part of an organ's structure. The Dutch researcher Hans Clevers grew mini-guts from single intestinal stem cells in 2009. "Brain organoids" followed a few years later. They are not real organs, have no blood supply and stay small, but they let scientists watch human development and disease in a way that used to be impossible.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Repairing the body, and understanding disease

Stem cells matter for two reasons: as treatments that replace lost cells, and as tools that let researchers study human disease in a dish.

Chapter 08

Proven treatments, beyond the bone marrow

The list of truly established stem-cell treatments is short, and that is worth stating plainly.

  • Blood stem-cell transplants for leukaemias, lymphomas, some inherited immune and blood disorders, and certain other conditions.
  • Edited blood stem cells. The approved CRISPR therapy for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia takes the patient's own stem cells, edits them, and returns them (Volume 13). Several older gene therapies work the same way.
  • Skin and cornea. Burn units have long grown sheets of a patient's skin cells, which rely on the stem cells within them. In 2015 the European Union approved a product made from a patient's own eye stem cells to repair corneas damaged by chemical burns.

One case shows what combining these ideas can do. In 2015 a seven-year-old boy in Germany was dying of a genetic disease that made his skin blister and fall away over most of his body. A team led by the Italian scientist Michele De Luca took a small patch of his skin, corrected the faulty gene in its stem cells, and grew new skin in the lab. Grafts covered about 80% of his body. The results, published in 2017, showed the new skin renewing itself normally, because the stem cells, not the sheets, were doing the work.

Chapter 09

In the pipeline: eyes, brains and pancreases

Beyond the proven list, a wave of clinical trials is testing cells made from pluripotent stem cells. These are real trials, mostly early stage, which means they test safety in small groups first.

In 2014, in Japan, a team led by Masayo Takahashi transplanted a sheet of retinal cells made from a patient's own iPS cells into her eye. She had age-related macular degeneration, a common cause of sight loss. It was the first transplant of cells made from iPS cells into a person, and it was intended to test safety.

For Parkinson's disease, which destroys dopamine-producing neurons, teams in Japan, the United States and Europe have transplanted lab-made dopamine neurons into patients' brains. Early results published in 2025 reported that the cells survived and the procedures appeared safe, with signs of benefit in some patients that larger trials must confirm. For type 1 diabetes, trials of lab-made insulin-producing cells have allowed some participants to reduce or stop insulin injections, though most also need drugs that suppress the immune system. Early success in a few dozen patients is encouraging and is not yet a treatment. The diabetes side is in Hormones.

Chapter 10

Disease in a dish

The quietest contribution may turn out to be the largest. With iPS cells, scientists can take a skin or blood sample from a person with an inherited disease, such as a heart rhythm disorder or a form of motor neurone disease, and grow that person's heart or nerve cells in a dish. Those cells carry the patient's exact genetic make-up.

That makes three things possible. Researchers can watch how the disease begins in human cells instead of guessing from mice. Drug companies can screen thousands of compounds against real human cells. And cells can be tested for toxic side effects before a drug reaches people. In the United States, a 2022 law removed the old requirement that new drugs be tested in animals before human trials, partly because such lab-grown human models now exist. Animal testing is not disappearing soon, but its monopoly has ended.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Great promise, real risks, and a market that runs ahead of the science

Each source of stem cells has its own strengths and problems, and the field as a whole carries ethical and commercial risks.

Chapter 11

The embryo debate, fairly stated

The ethical argument over embryonic stem cells is about one question: what moral status does an embryo a few days old have? Many religious traditions, including Catholic teaching, hold that human life deserving protection begins at conception, so destroying an embryo is wrong whatever the purpose. Others hold that moral status grows gradually with development, and that an embryo of about a hundred cells, with no nervous system, can be used for serious research, especially if it would otherwise be discarded.

Governments have chosen different lines. In the United States, federal funding was restricted in 2001 to cell lines that already existed, then expanded in 2009. Some European countries, such as Germany, have strict limits, while the United Kingdom allows embryo research under licence. Many countries set a 14-day limit on growing human embryos in the lab, a rule that goes back to a 1984 UK report chaired by the philosopher Mary Warnock. iPS cells eased the debate, because they need no embryo, but they did not end it, because embryonic cells remain the reference standard for research.

Embryonic stem cellsCan make any cell type. Well-studied reference standard.Requires destroying an embryo. Donor cells may be rejected. Tumour risk if impure.

Rejection deserves a note. Cells from another person are attacked by the immune system unless the patient takes suppressing drugs for life, which carry their own risks. Using a patient's own iPS cells avoids this but costs a lot of time and money. One response is to build banks of donor cell lines that match large parts of the population, and another is to edit cells so the immune system ignores them.

Chapter 13

The dark side: clinics that sell hope

Hundreds of private clinics around the world have advertised stem-cell injections for arthritis, autism, spinal injury, ageing and much else. Most use cells taken from the patient's own fat or blood, processed briefly and injected back. For almost all of these uses, there is no good evidence that they work.

The harm can be severe. A 2017 report in a leading medical journal described three women in the United States who had cells from their own fat injected into both eyes at a clinic, for macular degeneration. All three suffered severe, lasting loss of vision. Other patients have developed infections and even tumours. A treatment sold for cash, for many unrelated conditions, without a registered trial, is a warning sign, not a breakthrough. Regulators in several countries have closed clinics, but the market persists.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Anyone considering a stem-cell treatment should ask whether it is approved for their condition or offered within a registered clinical trial, and discuss it with their own doctor.

Chapter 15

Models of embryos, and the rules they test

Stem cells can now be coaxed into structures that resemble early embryos, called embryo models. Some copy features of human development around the second week, a stage that has been almost impossible to study because it happens just after an embryo implants in the womb. Many miscarriages and some birth defects arise then, so the scientific interest is real.

The models also challenge the old rules. Are they embryos? Most cannot develop further, but as they improve the line may blur. In 2021 the International Society for Stem Cell Research relaxed its own guidance on the 14-day limit, saying research beyond it might be considered case by case, while many national laws stayed as they were. Expect the science of stem cells to keep forcing new decisions about where life, and protection, begins. The genetic side of these questions continues in Genetic engineering.

The core idea

Stem cells can renew themselves and produce specialised cells. Adult stem cells maintain tissues such as blood, gut and skin. Embryonic and reprogrammed (iPS) cells can make any cell type. Blood stem-cell transplants are long established; most other uses are still in trials.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Adult stem cells first proven in 1961 (Till and McCulloch).
  • Human embryonic stem cells first grown in 1998; iPS cells made from human skin in 2007.
  • The gut lining renews every few days; adult heart muscle barely renews.
The common mistake

Believing that a clinic offering "stem cells" for many conditions is ahead of medicine. Outside blood disorders and a few other uses, proven treatments are still rare, and real progress happens in registered trials.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a bone marrow transplant can rebuild a whole immune system while a heart attack leaves a permanent scar? If so, you understand niches, potency and the uneven repair of the body.
