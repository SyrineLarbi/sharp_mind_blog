Human reproduction. A surprisingly inefficient process that every person alive has passed through.

Each of us began when one sperm out of hundreds of millions reached one egg released that month. Even then, many fertilised eggs never become a pregnancy. Human reproduction is slow, costly and uncertain compared with that of most animals, and that is part of why it is so closely tied to hormones, medicine, culture and politics. This file explains the biology from egg and sperm to birth, then turns to the questions most people meet in real life: falling birth rates, infertility, IVF, and the new technologies arriving at the edges.

Question 1 · What is it?

Two half-sets of instructions, combined into a new person

Humans reproduce sexually. Each parent contributes one cell carrying half of a full set of genes, and the two halves combine into a new and unique combination.

Chapter 01

Why halves, and why mix?

Most cells in your body carry 46 chromosomes, the packages of DNA, arranged in 23 pairs. One of each pair came from your mother and one from your father. Eggs and sperm are different. They are made by a special kind of cell division called meiosis, which halves the number to 23. When an egg and a sperm join, the total goes back to 46.

Meiosis also shuffles. Before the split, each chromosome pair swaps segments, and the pairs are divided at random. Each egg or sperm therefore carries a new mixture of the parent's genes. That is why two siblings with the same parents can look so different, and why, apart from identical twins, no two people have ever had the same genome.

Why do it this way? Many organisms simply copy themselves, which is faster. A leading explanation is that mixing genes every generation creates variety, which helps a population keep ahead of fast-evolving parasites and diseases. This is often called the Red Queen hypothesis, after the character in Through the Looking-Glass who has to keep running to stay in the same place. Sex is expensive, and the best-supported idea is that it pays by making each generation a moving target. The genetic rules are in Genetics.

Chapter 02

Eggs and sperm: opposite strategies

The two sex cells, called gametes, could hardly be more different.

A woman's eggs are mostly formed before she is born, and they age with her. Sperm are made fresh, taking roughly two to three months from start to finish. That difference matters for fertility and age, as Chapter 10 explains.

Chapter 03

How long it took to see what happens

For most of history, conception was a mystery filled with wrong theories. Some thinkers believed the man supplied the whole future person and the woman merely the soil. In 1677 the Dutch microscope maker Antonie van Leeuwenhoek became one of the first people to see sperm. The mammalian egg was not identified until 1827, by the Baltic German biologist Karl Ernst von Baer. In 1876 the German zoologist Oscar Hertwig watched a sperm enter a sea urchin egg and saw the two nuclei fuse. Only then was it clear that both parents contribute equally to the inherited material. Human fertilisation itself was not observed outside the body until the work that led to IVF, in the 1960s and 1970s.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A monthly cycle, a short window, and nine months of building

Reproduction is coordinated by hormones from the brain and ovaries. The timing is precise, the chances in any one cycle are modest, and development after conception follows a strict schedule.

Chapter 04

The menstrual cycle

From puberty to menopause, the female body prepares for a possible pregnancy roughly once a month. The average cycle is often quoted as 28 days, but anything from about 21 to 35 days is normal in adults, and cycles vary from month to month.

One 28-day cycle, simplified (day 1 = first day of bleeding)

       | lining |  oestrogen rises  | LH surge|  progesterone   | hormones fall,
       |  shed  |  lining rebuilds  | egg out |  from corpus    | lining sheds,
       |        |  (FSH from brain) |         |  luteum         | cycle restarts
       <---- follicular phase ----->         <--- luteal phase --->

Here is the logic. The pituitary gland at the base of the brain releases FSH, which makes a few egg-containing sacs in the ovary, called follicles, start to grow. Usually one takes the lead. It releases oestrogen, which rebuilds the lining of the womb (the uterus). When oestrogen is high enough, it triggers a surge of LH from the pituitary, and about a day later the follicle bursts and releases its egg. That is ovulation.

The empty follicle becomes a temporary gland that releases progesterone, which makes the lining ready to receive an embryo. If no pregnancy starts, this gland breaks down after about two weeks, hormone levels fall, the lining is shed as a period, and the cycle begins again. The feedback loops behind this are explained in Hormones. The second half of the cycle is more consistent in length than the first, which is why ovulation is not always on day 14.

Chapter 05

Fertilisation: a race with very few finishers

An ejaculation releases tens to hundreds of millions of sperm. Most never get far. The acidic vagina, the narrow cervix, and the immune system remove most of them, and half of those remaining head toward the wrong fallopian tube, the tube through which the egg travels from the ovary. Only a few hundred typically reach the egg.

Fertilisation usually happens in the fallopian tube. Several sperm push through the egg's outer layers, but as soon as one fuses with the egg, the egg's surface changes and blocks the rest. Because sperm can survive up to about five days and the egg about one day, the fertile window is roughly six days each cycle, ending on the day of ovulation. Even for a young, healthy couple timing things well, the chance of pregnancy in any one cycle is well under one in three.

The sperm also decides the baby's chromosomal sex. An egg always carries an X chromosome. A sperm carries either an X or a Y. XX usually develops as female, XY as male, because a gene on the Y chromosome, called SRY, switches on the development of testes. A small proportion of people have variations in chromosomes or in sex development, which medicine calls differences of sex development.

Chapter 06

Implantation, and the hormone a test detects

The fertilised egg, now a single cell called a zygote, divides as it drifts down the tube. After about five days it is a blastocyst, a hollow ball of cells described in Stem cells. Around six to ten days after ovulation, it burrows into the lining of the uterus. This is implantation, and it is when a pregnancy, medically speaking, begins.

The outer cells of the embryo, which will become part of the placenta, start releasing a hormone called hCG. It signals the ovary to keep producing progesterone, so the lining is not shed. hCG passes into the blood and urine, and it is what every home pregnancy test detects, using antibodies that stick to it and produce a coloured line.

Many early embryos do not survive. Some fail to implant; others are lost so early that a period simply arrives a little late. A large share of these losses are caused by chance errors in the chromosomes. Of pregnancies that are recognised, roughly 10 to 20% end in miscarriage, most in the first twelve weeks. Early loss is common and usually reflects the embryo's chromosomes, not anything the parents did.

Chapter 07

Nine months of construction

Doctors count pregnancy from the first day of the last period, so a full-term pregnancy is about 40 weeks, even though conception happens about two weeks into that count. Pregnancy is often divided into three trimesters of roughly three months.

The most delicate work happens first. In the weeks after implantation, the embryo forms its heart, brain, spine, limbs and other organs. By about ten weeks of pregnancy the basic body plan is complete, and the embryo is called a fetus. The rest of pregnancy is mostly growth and maturing, especially of the lungs and brain.

Because organs form early, harmful exposures early in pregnancy can do the most damage. The clearest warning came from thalidomide, a sedative also used for morning sickness in the late 1950s and early 1960s. It caused severe limb and other defects in more than ten thousand babies, mainly in Europe, and led directly to stricter drug testing laws around the world. A positive lesson came later: taking folic acid before conception and in early pregnancy substantially reduces the risk of defects of the brain and spine, called neural tube defects, which is why many countries add it to flour.

Chapter 08

Birth, and why it is so hard for humans

Labour is driven partly by the hormone oxytocin, in one of the body's rare positive feedback loops: contractions trigger oxytocin, which strengthens contractions, until the baby is born. What starts labour in the first place is still not fully understood.

Human birth is unusually difficult compared with that of other primates. The baby's head is large, and the mother's pelvis is shaped for walking upright, so the baby must rotate to pass through the birth canal. A long-standing explanation, the "obstetric dilemma", says evolution compromised between a big brain and an efficient walking pelvis. Some researchers argue the mother's energy limits matter more. The debate continues. What is not in doubt is that skilled help at birth saves lives, as the maternal health figures in Chapter 12 show.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Birth rates, infertility, and the medicine of making families

Reproduction shapes the size and age of whole populations, it is a source of private difficulty for many people, and it has produced some of the most influential medical technologies of the last century.

Chapter 09

The great fall in birth rates

The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have over her life at current rates. A rate of about 2.1 keeps a population stable in the long run without migration; that level is called replacement fertility. In the 1960s the global rate was around five. By the mid-2020s, according to United Nations estimates, it had fallen to about 2.2 to 2.3.

The fall is uneven. Much of sub-Saharan Africa still has rates above four. Most of Europe, East Asia and the Americas are below replacement. South Korea has the lowest rate recorded for any country, around 0.7 in 2023. The causes include falling child death rates, more education and paid work for women, the costs of housing and child-rearing, later marriage, and access to contraception. Birth rates usually fall when life gets safer and choices widen, and governments have found it hard to push them back up. The social and health-system consequences are in Aging populations.

Chapter 10

Infertility: common, and shared between the sexes

Infertility is usually defined as not becoming pregnant after a year of regular unprotected sex. In 2023 the World Health Organization estimated that about one in six people worldwide experience it at some point in their lives. It is often thought of as a women's issue, but problems on the male side, such as low sperm count or poor sperm movement, contribute to a large share of cases. Often both partners have a factor, and sometimes no cause is found.

Age is the biggest single factor for women. Because eggs are formed before birth and age with the body, both their number and their chromosomal quality decline, especially from the mid-thirties onward, and the chance of miscarriage rises. Male fertility declines with age as well, more gradually. Other causes include blocked fallopian tubes, often after infection; conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome and endometriosis; and smoking and obesity in either partner.

Chapter 11

IVF: conception in a dish

On 25 July 1978, in Oldham, England, Louise Brown was born. She was the first baby conceived by in vitro fertilisation (IVF), "in vitro" meaning "in glass". The physiologist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe had worked on the method for about a decade, against heavy criticism. Edwards received the Nobel Prize in 2010. By recent estimates, more than 12 million babies have since been born through IVF and related methods.

The basic steps are straightforward. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once. The eggs are collected with a fine needle and mixed with sperm in a dish, or, since the early 1990s, a single sperm can be injected directly into each egg, a method called ICSI that helped many men with very low sperm counts. After a few days, one embryo is placed in the uterus, and spare embryos can be frozen.

IVF is powerful but not magic. Success rates per attempt depend strongly on the woman's age, falling sharply after about 40 when her own eggs are used. Treatment is expensive, physically demanding and emotionally hard, and public funding varies widely between countries. IVF can help overcome many problems, but it cannot undo the effect of age on eggs. That is why egg freezing, which stores a younger woman's eggs for later use, has grown rapidly, though frozen eggs give no guarantee of a later baby.

Chapter 12

Contraception and the safety of childbirth

The ability to prevent pregnancy reliably transformed lives. The combined pill, approved in the United States in 1960, works through the hormone feedback loops described above, stopping ovulation. Long-acting methods, such as implants and intrauterine devices (IUDs), are now among the most effective. Contraception is closely linked with women's education, work and health, and with falling family size.

Pregnancy itself still carries risk. The World Health Organization estimates that roughly a quarter of a million women die each year from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth in the early 2020s, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and most from causes that are preventable with good care, such as heavy bleeding after birth, infections, and high blood pressure in pregnancy. In rich countries these deaths are rare. The gap is not biology but access to skilled care, which is covered in Maternal health.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A costly biology, powerful technologies, and real ethical disagreement

Human reproductive biology has trade-offs built in, and the technologies developed to help it have their own benefits and costs.

The ethical debates here are real and should be stated fairly. Some religious traditions object to IVF because it separates conception from sex or because spare embryos may be destroyed; others accept it. Testing embryos for serious disease is widely accepted, but some disability advocates argue it sends a message about whose lives are valued. Laws vary sharply. Some countries permit paid surrogacy, many restrict or ban it, and rules on egg and sperm donation, donor anonymity and access for single people and same-sex couples differ widely. The same technology can be permitted, regulated or banned depending on the country.

Chapter 14

A live dispute: are sperm counts falling?

In 2017, and again in an updated analysis in 2022, a team led by the Israeli epidemiologist Hagai Levine combined many earlier studies and reported that average sperm counts in men had fallen by about half since the 1970s, possibly faster in recent years. The finding received huge attention.

Other researchers have challenged it. Counting methods have changed over the decades, the older studies differ in who was sampled, and data from many parts of the world are thin. Even if counts have fallen, most men remain within the range considered fertile, and the causes, whether obesity, smoking, chemicals or something else, are not established. This is a genuinely contested question: a real concern, worth studying, not yet a settled fact. It links to the debate about endocrine disruptors described in Hormones.

Chapter 16

The questions the next technologies will raise

If eggs could one day be made from any adult's skin cells, the age limit on female fertility might disappear, and two men could in principle have a child genetically related to both. Mice with two fathers have already been produced in the lab, though with major difficulties. Each step would revive questions societies have argued over since IVF: what counts as a parent, what may be done to embryos, and how far selection should go.

The likely pattern is the one seen since 1978. A technique first appears as alarming, is used to help people with a clear medical need, becomes routine in some countries, and stays controversial or banned in others. Reproductive biology will keep advancing faster than agreement about how to use it. That ends the Biology category. The core idea

Each parent contributes a gamete with half the chromosomes, shuffled by meiosis. A hormone-driven monthly cycle releases an egg; fertilisation is possible in a window of about six days; the embryo implants and builds its organs in the first weeks. Medicine can now prevent, assist and sometimes replace parts of this process.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Replacement fertility is about 2.1 children per woman; the world was about 2.2 to 2.3 in the mid-2020s.
  • About 1 in 6 people experience infertility at some point (WHO, 2023).
  • First IVF baby in 1978; more than 12 million since.
The common mistake

Thinking IVF can make up for age. IVF bypasses many problems, but egg quality still declines with age, so success rates with a woman's own eggs fall steeply after about 40.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a pregnancy test works about two weeks after ovulation but not two days after? If so, you understand implantation and hCG.
