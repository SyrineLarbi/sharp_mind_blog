Genetic engineering. Fifty years of rewriting life, one letter and one argument at a time.

For ten thousand years people changed the genes of plants and animals the slow way, by choosing which ones to breed. In 1973 two scientists in California moved a gene from one organism into another directly, and a new era began. Since then, genetic engineering has given diabetics human insulin made by bacteria, put insect-resistant cotton in fields across the world, and, very recently, cured some inherited diseases. It has also produced some of the most heated public arguments in science. This file covers the tools, the uses, the evidence and the disputes.

Question 1 · What is it?

Changing an organism's DNA directly, in a laboratory

Genetic engineering means adding, removing or changing specific genes on purpose, rather than waiting for a lucky mix through breeding.

Chapter 01

We have always changed genes; we just did it blind

The maize in a modern field looks nothing like its wild ancestor, a grass from Mexico called teosinte, which has a few hard kernels on a tiny ear. Over thousands of years, farmers saved seed from the best plants and slowly reshaped its genes. Dogs, wheat, cows and cabbages all went through similar changes. Broccoli, cauliflower and kale are all one wild cabbage species, bred in different directions.

Breeding works, but it is slow and imprecise. You can only combine genes from closely related organisms, and each cross reshuffles thousands of genes at once. In the twentieth century, breeders even used radiation and chemicals to cause random mutations and then picked useful ones. Several common crop varieties were made this way.

Genetic engineering changes the method, not the fact, of altering genes. It picks a specific gene, changes it or moves it, and can take it from any organism, even across kingdoms of life. That precision is what makes it powerful, and moving genes between unrelated species is a large part of what makes some people uneasy.

Chapter 02

1973, and the scientists who paused themselves

In 1973 Stanley Cohen at Stanford and Herbert Boyer at the University of California, San Francisco, cut pieces of DNA from different sources and joined them inside a small ring of bacterial DNA. When they put the ring into bacteria, the bacteria copied the new gene every time they divided. Joined DNA from different sources is called recombinant DNA, and this was its first working demonstration.

The researchers themselves worried. What if a bacterium carrying a cancer gene escaped from the lab? In 1975 about 140 scientists, lawyers and journalists met at Asilomar in California, at a meeting organised by the biochemist Paul Berg and others. They agreed on safety rules graded by risk, including physical containment and weakened lab strains of bacteria that could not survive outside. It was one of the first times a scientific field paused to set its own limits before any harm occurred. The feared disasters did not happen, and many of the rules were later relaxed, but Asilomar is still cited whenever a powerful new technology appears.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Cut, paste, deliver, check

Every genetic engineering project, from bacteria to people, has the same four steps: get the gene, put it into a carrier, deliver it into cells, and check that it works.

Chapter 04

A human gene in a bacterium: the insulin story

For sixty years after its discovery, insulin for diabetes was extracted from the pancreases of cows and pigs from slaughterhouses. It worked, but supply depended on the meat industry, and some patients reacted to animal proteins.

Making human insulin in bacteria, simplified
  1. write or copy the human insulin gene
  2. cut open a plasmid with a restriction enzyme
  3. insert the gene; seal it with ligase      plasmid: (====[INSULIN]====)
  4. put plasmids into E. coli bacteria
  5. grow bacteria in huge steel tanks; they read the gene and make insulin
  6. break the cells, purify the insulin, check it is identical to human insulin

The young company Genentech, co-founded by Herbert Boyer, made human insulin this way in 1978, and in 1982 it went on sale as the first medicine made by genetic engineering. The bacteria did not need to understand the gene. They simply read it like their own. That is the deep reason genetic engineering works at all: the genetic code is the same in almost every living thing, as Volume 27 explains. Today much insulin is made in engineered bacteria or yeast, and the protein is sometimes altered slightly to act faster or last longer.

Chapter 05

Getting genes into plants

Plant engineers had a natural helper. A soil bacterium called Agrobacterium tumefaciens causes tumours on plants by inserting a piece of its own DNA into the plant's cells. Scientists removed the tumour-causing genes from that piece and replaced them with genes they wanted. The bacterium then delivered the new genes for them. For plants it does not infect well, such as some cereals, researchers used a "gene gun" that fires microscopic gold or tungsten particles coated with DNA into the cells.

Either way, the changed cells are grown into whole new plants using the fact that many plant cells can regenerate a complete plant. The seeds then carry the new gene. A plant or animal that carries a gene from another species is called transgenic. In everyday language, these are "GMOs", genetically modified organisms.

Chapter 06

Engineered animals: mostly for research

The most important engineered animal is the lab mouse. From the late 1980s, scientists learned to switch off a single chosen gene in mice to see what it does. These knockout mice revealed the roles of thousands of genes, and the researchers who developed the method, Mario Capecchi, Martin Evans and Oliver Smithies, shared the Nobel Prize in 2007. Much of modern medical research rests on them.

Engineered animals for food are rare. In 2015 the US regulator approved a salmon carrying a growth-hormone gene from another salmon species, which lets it reach market size faster. It was the first genetically engineered animal approved for food in the United States, after about two decades of review. Other examples include pigs edited to resist a serious viral disease and, in medicine, pigs edited so their organs might be transplanted into people (Transplants, and pig organs).

Chapter 07

Gene therapy: a hard road to real cures

Gene therapy means giving a patient a working copy of a gene, usually to treat an inherited disease caused by a faulty one. The idea is old; making it safe took decades.

The field nearly collapsed in 1999, when an 18-year-old American volunteer, Jesse Gelsinger, died from a massive immune reaction to the viral vector in a trial. A few years later, a French trial for a severe immune disorder cured several children but caused leukaemia in some, because the vector inserted itself next to a cancer gene. Research slowed and safety standards rose.

Then the approvals came. In 2017 the United States approved a gene therapy for an inherited form of blindness, delivered by injection under the retina, and the first CAR-T therapies, in which a patient's own immune cells are engineered to hunt their cancer. In 2019 came a gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy, a disease that killed most babies with its severe form before age two. It is given once, as an infusion. Its list price at launch, about 2.1 million dollars, was then the highest of any medicine. The science works for a growing list of diseases; the price is now the main problem.

Chapter 08

From adding genes to editing them

The older methods add a gene somewhere in the genome. They cannot easily fix a typo in place. Since 2012, CRISPR has made it possible to go to an exact address and cut, change or replace the letters there. The method, and its newer, gentler forms called base editing and prime editing, are explained in Volume 13. The first CRISPR medicine, for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia, was approved in late 2023.

Editing also changed agriculture and research, because it can make small changes, such as switching off one gene, that look identical to a natural mutation and leave no foreign DNA behind. That fact sits at the centre of a regulatory argument in Chapter 12. And in 2018 a Chinese scientist, He Jiankui, announced that he had edited human embryos that were then born as twin girls. The work was condemned worldwide, and he was imprisoned. That episode, and the ethics of changing embryos, are covered in Volume 50.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Medicines, harvests and the whole of modern biology

Genetic engineering is less visible than it is important. Much of it happens inside tanks, fields and laboratories rather than in headlines.

Chapter 09

The medicines you do not know are engineered

Insulin was the first. Since then, engineered cells have become the factories for a large share of modern medicines. The clotting factors that people with haemophilia need used to come from donated blood, and in the 1980s contaminated supplies infected many patients with HIV and hepatitis C. Recombinant versions, made by engineered cells, removed that risk. The hepatitis B vaccine has been made in engineered yeast since the 1980s. Most monoclonal antibodies, used for cancer, arthritis and many other diseases, are made in engineered animal cells grown in huge tanks (Monoclonal antibodies).

Growth hormone is a telling case. Children who needed it were once treated with hormone extracted from the pituitary glands of dead people. Some developed a fatal brain disease, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, from contaminated material. Engineered growth hormone, approved in 1985, ended that danger. Engineering made many medicines not only more plentiful but safer.

Chapter 10

Crops: what has actually been grown

Genetically engineered crops were first planted commercially in the mid-1990s. By around 2019 they covered roughly 190 million hectares, more than ten percent of the world's cropland, mostly in the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and India. A few crops dominate: soybeans, maize, cotton and rapeseed (canola). Two traits dominate too.

Insect resistance (Bt crops)

Plants carry a gene from a soil bacterium, Bacillus thuringiensis, that makes a protein toxic to certain caterpillars and beetles but harmless to people. Organic farmers have sprayed the same bacterium for decades. Bt cotton is now most of the cotton grown in India and China, and has reduced insecticide spraying in many areas.

Herbicide tolerance

Plants survive a weedkiller, most often glyphosate, so farmers can spray the whole field and kill only the weeds. This made weed control simpler and helped reduce ploughing, which protects soil. It also greatly increased glyphosate use, with consequences discussed in Chapter 12.

Smaller examples matter locally. In the 1990s a virus was destroying Hawaii's papaya trees. An engineered virus-resistant papaya, released in 1998, is widely credited with saving the industry. Golden Rice, engineered to make beta-carotene, which the body turns into vitamin A, was designed to fight a deficiency that causes blindness and death in poor children. After two decades of development and dispute, the Philippines approved it for planting in 2021, but a court there halted commercial planting in 2024 pending further review. The wider farming picture is in Sustainable agriculture.

Chapter 11

The engine of biological research

The least visible use may be the most far-reaching. Nearly every biology laboratory uses genetic engineering every day. Scientists attach a gene for a glowing jellyfish protein to a gene they study, so they can watch where it is made in a living cell. They switch genes off in mice, fish, flies and yeast to learn what those genes do. They grow engineered cells to make the proteins they need.

Much of what we now know about cancer, the immune system and the brain came from these methods. The optogenetics described in Neuroscience depends on putting an algae gene into mouse neurons. Genetic engineering is less a product than a way of asking questions, and most of its value has come from the answers.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Safe to eat, harder to judge as a system

The scientific evidence on food safety is clearer than the public debate suggests. The questions about farming systems, ownership and ethics are harder and more open.

Chapter 12

What the evidence shows, and what is still argued

In 2016 the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine reviewed hundreds of studies and twenty years of data. It found no substantiated evidence that the engineered crops on the market were less safe to eat than conventional ones. Many scientific bodies, including the World Health Organization for the foods it has assessed, have reached similar conclusions. On the narrow question of eating approved GM food, the scientific consensus is strong. The open questions are elsewhere.

IssueThe case forThe concern
Yields and pesticidesBt crops cut insecticide spraying and losses to pests in many regions.Pests evolve. Some insects have become resistant to Bt proteins.
Weed controlSimpler control, less ploughing, less soil erosion.Heavy reliance on one herbicide bred glyphosate-resistant weeds across large areas.

EnvironmentPotential crops that need less water, fertiliser or spray.Genes can spread to wild relatives; effects on ecosystems need monitoring.
MedicineSafer, more plentiful drugs and new cures.Very high prices for gene therapies; unequal access worldwide.

Rules differ sharply by region. The United States regulates mainly by what a product is. The European Union has regulated by how it was made, with strict approval, labelling and few GM crops grown. In 2018 the EU's top court ruled that gene-edited crops count as GMOs under existing law, even when the change matches a natural mutation. The EU has since been negotiating lighter rules for some edited plants, while countries such as Japan, Argentina and England have already eased theirs.

Chapter 13

The deeper objections, fairly stated

Some objections are not about measurable risk at all. Some people feel that moving genes between species crosses a natural line, or that it treats living things as mere material. Some religious thinkers frame it as overstepping a human role; others in the same traditions see healing and feeding people as a duty that justifies it. Defenders reply that nature itself moves genes between species, as Agrobacterium shows, and that breeding by radiation, which has caused little public alarm, is far less precise.

The sharpest disagreement concerns human embryos. Correcting a fatal disease and selecting desirable traits use the same technique, and changes in an embryo pass to all future generations, who cannot consent. Most countries prohibit editing embryos for pregnancy. Reasonable people disagree about where lines should be drawn, and Volume 40 shows why different ethical frameworks answer differently (What to do with three frameworks).

Chapter 15

The real bottleneck: who can afford it

The biology is advancing faster than the economics. One-time gene therapies are priced in the millions of dollars because development is costly, patients are few, and companies argue that a lifelong cure saves decades of care. Sickle cell disease is most common in sub-Saharan Africa, India and among people of African descent, yet the first cure is mostly available in rich countries. Health systems are testing new payment methods, such as paying in instalments only while the treatment keeps working.

The direction that would change most lives is not the most dramatic. It is making existing therapies cheaper, simpler and deliverable outside elite hospitals, for example by editing cells inside the body instead of in specialised laboratories. The industry built around these tools is the subject of the next file, Biotechnology.

The core idea

Genetic engineering changes specific genes directly, using tools borrowed from bacteria and viruses. It works because the genetic code is shared by almost all life. It makes medicines, crops and research possible, and its hardest questions are about systems, prices and ethics rather than food safety.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First recombinant DNA in 1973; first engineered medicine (insulin) sold in 1982.
  • GM crops covered roughly 190 million hectares around 2019.
  • First CRISPR medicine approved in late 2023.
The common mistake

Treating "GMO" as one thing that is either good or bad. Each product should be judged on its own trait, its own risks and its own farming system, not on the method alone.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a bacterium can make human insulin without any human cells involved? If so, you understand why genetic engineering works at all.

The words, in plain words
Genetic engineering
Directly changing an organism's DNA in a laboratory.
Recombinant DNA
DNA made by joining pieces from different sources.
Restriction enzyme
A bacterial protein that cuts DNA at a specific sequence.
Plasmid
A small ring of DNA in bacteria, used to carry genes into cells.
Vector
Anything used to deliver a gene into a cell, such as a plasmid or an emptied virus.
PCR
Polymerase chain reaction: a method for copying a stretch of DNA billions of times.
Transgenic
Carrying a gene from another species.
GMO
Genetically modified organism; in law, usually one changed by genetic engineering.
Knockout
An organism, usually a mouse, with one chosen gene switched off.
Gene therapy
Treating disease by adding, fixing or switching off genes in a patient's cells.
CAR-T
A cancer treatment using a patient's immune cells engineered to recognise the cancer.
Bt crop
A crop carrying a bacterial gene that makes it toxic to certain insect pests.
Gene drive
A genetic change designed to spread through a wild population faster than normal inheritance.
