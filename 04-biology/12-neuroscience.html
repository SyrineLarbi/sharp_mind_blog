Neuroscience. The organ that studies itself, and is still mostly a mystery to itself.

Every thought you have ever had, every face you have recognised and every decision you regret was produced by a wet, soft organ that weighs about as much as a bag of sugar. Neuroscience is the attempt to explain how. It has made real progress: we know how a single nerve cell sends a signal, which drugs act on which chemical, and roughly which regions do what. But the step from cells to a mind is still the largest open question in biology. This file is the gateway. It gives you the whole map in one place, and it points into the Neuroscience folder for every region of that map.

Question 1 · What is it?

The science of the nervous system, from molecules to minds

Neuroscience is not one subject but a stack of them. Chemists, doctors, psychologists, engineers and computer scientists all work on the same organ at different levels.

Chapter 01

A small organ with a large bill

The adult human brain weighs about 1.3 to 1.4 kilograms, roughly 2% of a typical body. Yet at rest it uses about a fifth of the body's energy. It is the most expensive tissue you own, and it never switches off, not even in deep sleep. That cost tells you something. Evolution does not keep expensive equipment unless it pays for itself.

The working parts are cells. The best-known are neurons, nerve cells that carry signals. A careful count published in 2009 by the Brazilian researcher Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues put the human total at about 86 billion. Alongside them are glial cells, the support staff. For decades textbooks said glia outnumber neurons ten to one. The same counting work suggested the real ratio is closer to one to one. That is a useful early lesson: even basic "facts" about the brain have been revised in living memory.

Neuroscience studies all of this, plus the nerves that run through the spinal cord and out to every muscle and organ. Together they form the nervous system. The brain and spinal cord are the central nervous system. Everything else, the cables to your fingertips and gut, is the peripheral nervous system.

Chapter 03

How the field was born, in four arguments

Modern neuroscience grew out of a handful of disputes that were settled by evidence.

Is the brain one tissue or many cells? In the late 1800s the Italian Camillo Golgi invented a silver stain that turned a few nerve cells black, so they could be seen whole under a microscope. The Spaniard Santiago Ramón y Cajal used Golgi's own stain to argue the opposite of Golgi's view. Golgi thought the nervous system was one continuous net. Cajal showed it is made of separate cells that nearly touch. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1906 and disagreed in their acceptance speeches. Cajal was right. His idea is called the neuron doctrine.

Do different parts do different jobs? In 1861 the French doctor Paul Broca examined a patient who could understand speech but say almost nothing except one syllable. After the patient died, Broca found damage in a specific patch of the left frontal lobe. That region still carries his name. It was strong early evidence that functions are localised, at least partly.

Is the signal electrical? In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, working on the unusually thick nerve fibre of a squid, worked out the exact sequence of electrical changes that make up a nerve impulse. Their equations are still used today. Is it chemical? Work through the early twentieth century showed that, at most junctions, neurons talk by releasing chemicals. Both answers turned out to be true, at different points along the same journey.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Electricity inside the cell, chemistry between cells

The basic trick is simple and repeated billions of times. What makes a brain is the pattern of connections, and the way that pattern changes with use.

Chapter 04

The spike: an all-or-nothing message

A neuron has three main parts. The dendrites are branches that receive input. The cell body adds the input up. The axon is a long cable that carries the output, sometimes over a metre, from your spine to your toes.

The cell keeps a small voltage across its outer skin, the membrane, by pumping charged atoms (ions) in and out. When enough input arrives, tiny gates in the membrane open. Sodium ions rush in, the voltage flips for about a millisecond, and then potassium ions flow out and reset it. This flip is the action potential, or "spike". It travels down the axon like a falling row of dominoes.

Two features matter. First, a spike is all or nothing. It does not come in big and small sizes. A neuron signals "more" by firing more often, not more loudly. Second, speed depends on insulation. Many axons are wrapped in a fatty sheath called myelin. The spike jumps between gaps in the sheath and can travel at over 100 metres per second. Unwrapped fibres manage about one metre per second. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which the immune system damages myelin, and signals slow or fail.

Chapter 05

The synapse: where almost everything happens

When a spike reaches the end of the axon, it usually cannot jump to the next cell. There is a gap, about 20 millionths of a millimetre wide. The junction is a synapse. The spike makes the cell release a chemical messenger, a neurotransmitter, which crosses the gap and fits into receptor molecules on the next neuron, like a key in a lock.

Some messengers push the next cell toward firing. Glutamate is the main "go" signal in the brain. Others hold it back. GABA is the main "stop" signal. A neuron may receive thousands of these inputs and fires only when the "go" votes win. A third group, including dopamine and serotonin, act more like volume knobs. They change how whole networks respond rather than carrying a single message.

This is why almost every drug that changes the mind works at the synapse. Caffeine blocks receptors for adenosine, a chemical that builds up while you are awake and makes you sleepy. Common antidepressants called SSRIs slow the clearing away of serotonin. Alcohol boosts the effect of GABA, which is part of why it sedates. Anaesthetics, painkillers and many poisons act at synapses too. The details are in Neurotransmitters.

Chapter 06

The map: the main parts and what they are for

The brain was built by evolution adding layers on top of older layers, so the oldest functions sit deepest.

Two surprises are worth knowing. The cerebellum, the small cauliflower-shaped part at the back, holds most of the brain's neurons, about 69 billion of the 86 billion in Herculano-Houzel's count, because it is packed with tiny cells. And the left and right hemispheres are not "logic" and "creativity". Some functions do lean to one side, such as language to the left in most people, but almost every real task uses both. The popular "left-brain, right-brain" personality types have no support. The full tour is in Structure of the brain.

Chapter 07

Plasticity: a brain that rewires itself

A brain is not wired once and then fixed. Synapses get stronger with use and weaker without it, new connections sprout, and unused ones are pruned. This ability is called plasticity. In 1973 Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo showed that a burst of activity could make a synapse in a rabbit's hippocampus respond more strongly for hours afterward. This long-term potentiation is still the leading model of how learning is stored in cells.

Plasticity shows at larger scales too. In a well-known 2000 study, Eleanor Maguire and colleagues scanned London taxi drivers, who must learn the city's maze of streets by heart. On average, a rear part of their hippocampus was larger than in comparison volunteers, and the difference grew with years in the job. Experience physically reshapes the organ that has it. Plasticity is strongest in childhood but continues for life. It is also why rehabilitation after a stroke can work: undamaged regions can take over some lost jobs with practice. See Brain plasticity and, for the cellular story of memory, Memory formation.

Chapter 08

How anyone knows any of this: the toolkit

You cannot simply open a living human brain and watch. Every method is a compromise between seeing where something happens and seeing when, and between precision and safety.

MethodWhat it measuresStrengthWeakness
Brain damage studiesWhat is lost when a region is injuredShows what a region is needed forInjuries are messy and rarely limited to one region
EEGElectrical waves through the scalpMillisecond timing, cheap, safeBlurry about location
fMRIChanges in blood oxygen near active areasGood location, whole brain, harmlessIndirect and slow, lagging activity by seconds
Electrodes in the brainSpikes from single neuronsThe real signal, exactly timedNeeds surgery, so in humans mainly done in patients who need it anyway
OptogeneticsEffect of switching chosen cells on or off with lightProves cause, not just correlationRequires genetic changes, so used in animals

EEG goes back to the German psychiatrist Hans Berger in the 1920s. Functional MRI arrived in the early 1990s and transformed the field. Optogenetics, developed in the mid-2000s, lets researchers insert a light-sensitive protein taken from algae into specific neurons in mice and then control those cells with a flash of light. A colourful brain scan is a statistical map, not a photograph of thought. Keep that in mind whenever a headline says a region "lights up".

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Disease, medicine, and the question of who we are

Neuroscience matters for three reasons: brain disorders are among the largest causes of suffering on Earth, they are unusually hard to treat, and the field keeps raising questions about responsibility and self.

Chapter 09

The largest health burden most people never think about

A 2024 analysis from the Global Burden of Disease project, released with the World Health Organization, estimated that more than one in three people worldwide lived with a condition affecting the nervous system in 2021. Counted together, these conditions were the leading cause of illness and disability in the world. The list is long: stroke, migraine, dementia, epilepsy, nerve damage from diabetes, meningitis, and conditions in newborns caused by complications at birth.

Dementia alone affects well over 55 million people, and the number rises as populations age. Parkinson's disease, in which cells that make dopamine die, is one of the fastest-growing neurological conditions. Mental disorders such as depression, anxiety and schizophrenia are counted separately, and they add a further enormous burden. All of these are, in the end, problems of the nervous system. See Neurodegenerative diseases and Alzheimer's disease.

Chapter 10

Why brain medicines are so hard to make

Drugs for brain disorders fail in clinical trials more often than drugs for most other organs. There are three main reasons.

The first is a wall. Blood vessels in the brain are lined with cells packed unusually tightly, forming the blood-brain barrier. It keeps out germs and toxins, and it also keeps out most medicines. The second is that the brain is hard to sample. A doctor can biopsy a liver; nobody routinely takes a piece of a living brain. The third is that animal models fit poorly. A mouse does not get Alzheimer's disease or depression the way people do, so a drug that helps a mouse often does nothing for a person.

There are real successes, and they show what works. L-DOPA, introduced in the 1960s, replaces missing dopamine in Parkinson's disease and remains a standard treatment. Deep brain stimulation, in which thin wires deliver steady pulses to a deep structure, can reduce Parkinson's tremor dramatically. Better understanding of the synapse led to many of the psychiatric drugs covered in Medication, without the mythology.

Chapter 11

Beyond the clinic: classrooms, courts and myths

Findings from neuroscience now reach places far from the laboratory. Sleep research has changed advice about school start times. Brain development research is regularly cited in arguments about how young offenders should be sentenced, because the frontal regions involved in self-control keep maturing into the mid-twenties. Courts in several countries have seen brain scans offered as evidence, although judges are generally cautious about them.

The flip side is neuromyths: false beliefs dressed in brain language. Three are very common. "We use only 10% of our brain" is false; scans show activity across the whole brain, and damage to almost any area causes problems. "People learn best in their preferred learning style" has failed when tested properly. "Left-brained and right-brained people" is, as Chapter 06 explained, not a thing. A brain picture or a brain word makes a claim feel more scientific without making it more true. Studies have found that adding irrelevant neuroscience to a weak explanation makes non-experts rate it as better.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful science with honest limits

Neuroscience is very good at some questions and weak at others. Knowing which is which protects you from both cynicism and hype.

Chapter 12

What the field does well, and where it struggles

AreaStrengthWeakness
Single cells and synapsesUnderstood in fine detail, with precise physics and chemistry.Knowing the parts does not tell you what the whole network computes.
DrugsClear mechanisms for many existing medicines.High failure rate for new ones, especially for dementia.
Brain imagingSafe, non-invasive views of the whole working brain.Indirect, slow, and easy to over-interpret.
Animal researchAllows precise experiments that prove cause and effect.Results often fail to transfer to humans.
Big questionsClear progress on perception, movement, memory and reward.No agreed explanation of consciousness.

One famous warning came from a joke. In 2009 the researcher Craig Bennett and colleagues put a dead Atlantic salmon in an fMRI scanner, showed it photos of people, and analysed the data with standard methods of the time. A few spots appeared to "respond". The point was serious: if you test tens of thousands of tiny brain locations, some will look active by pure chance unless you correct for it. The poster helped push the field toward stricter statistics.

A second problem is size. Many brain studies have used small groups of volunteers. A 2013 analysis led by Katherine Button argued that much of neuroscience was "underpowered", meaning studies were too small to detect real effects reliably, so the positive results they did report were often exaggerated. Larger, shared datasets have since become more common.

Chapter 13

The gap that may or may not close

Suppose you knew every neuron firing in someone's brain while they tasted coffee. Would you know what the taste is like for them? Philosophers call this the problem of subjective experience, and the Australian philosopher David Chalmers named it the "hard problem" in the 1990s.

Scientists take different positions. Some think it is a real gap that brain data will never fully close. Others think it will dissolve as we explain more of what the brain does, the way "what is life?" dissolved once biology explained metabolism and reproduction. This is genuinely contested, and you will hear confident people on both sides. A fair summary is that neuroscience has found many brain signatures that go with being conscious, but no theory that most researchers accept. The live contenders are in Consciousness.

Chapter 15

Who owns the data in your head?

Consumer headbands that read brain waves are already sold for meditation and sleep tracking. They record far less than a medical device, but the data is personal in a new way. It may reveal attention, fatigue or mood. In 2021 Chile amended its constitution to protect "mental integrity" and brain activity, one of the first countries to write such "neurorights" into law. Other governments and bodies have begun to discuss neural data as a special category deserving extra protection.

The practical outlook combines both earlier themes. The tools will get better faster than our understanding of the mind, so societies will have to make rules about reading and changing brain activity before science can say exactly what that activity means. The wider landscape is in Neurotechnology and The future of neuroscience.

The core idea

The nervous system is built from neurons that send electrical spikes along their length and pass chemical messages across synapses. Behaviour and thought come from the pattern of those connections, which changes with experience. Neuroscience understands the parts well and the whole much less well.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 86 billion neurons, most of them in the cerebellum.
  • The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of resting energy.
  • More than 1 in 3 people worldwide lived with a neurological condition in 2021.
The common mistake

Reading "this region lights up" as "this region is where the feeling lives". Brain functions come from networks, and a scan shows a statistical estimate of blood flow, not a thought.

Test yourself

Can you explain why caffeine, antidepressants and alcohol all change your mind, using the word "synapse"? If so, you have the central idea of the whole field.

The words, in plain words
Neuron
A nerve cell that receives signals, adds them up and sends its own signal on.
Glial cells
Support cells that feed, insulate and protect neurons, roughly as numerous as neurons.
Axon
The long output cable of a neuron.
Dendrites
The branching input side of a neuron.
Action potential
The brief all-or-nothing electrical pulse a neuron sends down its axon; a "spike".
Myelin
A fatty insulating sheath around many axons that makes signals much faster.
Synapse
The junction where one neuron passes a message to the next, usually by chemicals.
Neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger released at a synapse, such as glutamate, GABA or dopamine.
Plasticity
The brain's ability to change its connections with use and experience.
Long-term potentiation
A lasting strengthening of a synapse after strong activity, a leading model of learning.
fMRI
A scan that tracks changes in blood oxygen to estimate which areas are working harder.
Blood-brain barrier
The tightly sealed lining of brain blood vessels that keeps most substances, and most drugs, out.
Optogenetics
A lab method that makes chosen neurons respond to light so they can be switched on or off.
