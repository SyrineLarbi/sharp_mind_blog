Sharp Mind · Biology · Topic 09 of 20

Viruses. A set of instructions that only works inside someone else's factory.

A virus is the simplest thing in biology that can evolve. It is a few genes wrapped in protein, with no machinery of its own. Outside a cell it does nothing at all. Inside one, it takes over and turns the cell into a factory for new viruses. That simple strategy has produced smallpox, HIV, influenza and COVID-19. It also shaped human evolution: pieces of ancient viruses make up a noticeable share of your own DNA, and one of them helps build the placenta.

Question 1 · What is it?

Genes in a box, waiting for a cell

Viruses were discovered as something that passed through a filter fine enough to stop every known germ. Their nature turned out to be stranger than anyone guessed.

Chapter 01

The germ that passed through the filter

In the 1880s and 1890s, tobacco farmers in Europe were losing crops to a disease that mottled the leaves, called tobacco mosaic disease. In 1892 the Russian scientist Dmitri Ivanovsky pressed the sap of sick plants through a porcelain filter with holes small enough to stop any known bacterium. The filtered sap still made healthy plants sick. In 1898 the Dutch scientist Martinus Beijerinck repeated the work and argued that this was a new kind of infectious agent, which could multiply only in living tissue. The word virus, Latin for "poison" or "slimy liquid", stuck.

Nobody could see one until the electron microscope arrived. The first images of tobacco mosaic virus, in 1939, showed tiny rods. For about forty years, viruses were known only by what they did, not by what they looked like.

Chapter 02

The parts of a virus

Every virus has two parts. The first is its genome, the genetic instructions, made of DNA or RNA, never both. The second is a protein shell called the capsid that protects the genome. Many viruses, including flu, HIV and coronaviruses, also wrap themselves in an envelope, a fatty membrane stolen from the last cell they left, studded with their own proteins. The single complete virus particle is called a virion.

The envelope matters in daily life. Fat dissolves in soap and alcohol, so enveloped viruses are destroyed by handwashing and alcohol gel fairly easily. Viruses without an envelope, such as norovirus, are much tougher. That is why norovirus spreads so well and why thorough washing with soap and water, plus cleaning with bleach-based products, is advised during outbreaks.

Chapter 03

Alive or not?

A virus does not eat, grow, produce energy or respond to its surroundings. Outside a cell it is chemically inert, and some viruses can even be crystallised like salt. Yet viruses carry genes, reproduce (with help) and evolve by natural selection. So are they alive?

Biologists disagree, and the answer depends on the definition you choose. In 2003 researchers described the mimivirus, a virus that infects amoebae and is so large it was first mistaken for a bacterium. It has around a thousand genes, more than some bacteria, including some for making proteins. Giant viruses like this blur the line further. A useful way to think about it: a virus is not alive on its own, but it is part of life, the way a recipe is part of cooking.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Break in, take over, copy, escape

Every virus follows the same basic cycle. The details of each step explain which cells it infects, how fast it changes, and how it can be stopped.

Chapter 04

The life cycle of a virus

The virus life cycle, in five steps
  1. ATTACH    a virus protein fits a receptor on the cell surface (key in lock)
       |
  2. ENTER     fuses with the membrane, or is swallowed by the cell; releases its genome
       |
  3. COPY      the cell's ribosomes and enzymes now make viral proteins and genomes
       |       (the cell's own work is often shut down)
  4. ASSEMBLE  new genomes are packed into new capsids
       |
  5. RELEASE   bud out through the membrane (taking an envelope) or burst the cell
               -> hundreds to thousands of new virions from one cell

The first step decides almost everything. A virus can only infect a cell that carries the right receptor, a protein on the surface that the virus has evolved to grab. HIV binds a protein called CD4, found mostly on certain immune cells, which is why HIV destroys the immune system. SARS-CoV-2 binds a protein called ACE2, found on cells of the airways and several other organs. The receptor is the reason a virus attacks one tissue and ignores another, and it is also the main target of many vaccines: block the virus's key, and it cannot open the lock.

Chapter 05

Spillover: how animal viruses become human ones

Most new human viruses come from animals. When a virus jumps from an animal to a person, it is called spillover, and a disease that passes from animals to humans is a zoonosis. HIV began as a virus of chimpanzees in Central Africa and crossed into humans in the early twentieth century, probably through hunting and butchering. Bats carry many coronaviruses and are thought to be the original source of the SARS virus of 2002–03 and of several others.

Most spillovers go nowhere. The virus infects a person but cannot spread well between people. The dangerous cases are the ones where the virus can also transmit from person to person. Risk rises when people and animals mix more: forest clearing, wildlife trade, and large farms of crowded animals. In 2024 a bird flu virus, H5N1, was found spreading in dairy cattle in the United States, with a small number of human infections, which virologists watched closely as a possible step towards spread in mammals.

Chapter 06

Changing shape: drift, shift and the 1918 flu

Many RNA viruses copy themselves carelessly, without the proofreading that cells use, so they mutate fast (see RNA). Flu shows two kinds of change. Antigenic drift is the slow build-up of small mutations in the virus's surface proteins, so last year's immunity fits a little less well. That is why the flu vaccine is updated every year.

Antigenic shift is rare and dangerous. Because the flu genome comes in eight separate pieces, two different flu viruses infecting the same cell, say one from birds and one from humans, can swap pieces. The result can be a virus with surface proteins that almost nobody's immune system has seen. That is how flu pandemics start. The 1918 pandemic killed an estimated 50 million people worldwide, though estimates range widely. A shift-like reassortment also produced the 2009 "swine flu" pandemic, which spread worldwide but was far less deadly.

Chapter 07

Hiding: viruses that stay for life

Some viruses do not just come and go. They hide. After chickenpox clears, the virus that caused it retreats into nerve cells near the spine and goes silent, a state called latency. Decades later, often when immunity weakens with age or stress, it can wake up and travel down the nerve to the skin, causing a painful rash in a band: shingles. Cold sore viruses do the same. HIV hides by inserting a DNA copy of its genome into the DNA of the cells it infects, which is why it cannot yet be cured, only controlled.

Some viruses cause cancer. The German virologist Harald zur Hausen showed that certain types of human papillomavirus cause cervical cancer, work that earned him a share of the 2008 Nobel Prize. HPV vaccines, introduced from 2006, prevent infection with the most dangerous types. Countries with high vaccination rates are already seeing large falls in cervical precancer and cancer in vaccinated age groups. Hepatitis B and C viruses can cause liver cancer, and hepatitis B also has a vaccine.

Chapter 08

Fighting back: why antivirals are hard, and when they work

A virus uses the cell's own machinery, so most ways of stopping it would also harm the cell. Antiviral drugs work by targeting the few things that belong only to the virus: its copying enzyme, the enzyme that cuts its proteins into working pieces, or the protein it uses to enter. That is why antivirals are usually specific to one virus or family.

When it works, it can be spectacular. In the mid-1990s, combinations of three HIV drugs given together turned HIV from a near-certain death sentence into a manageable long-term condition. People treated early now have a life expectancy close to normal in many countries, and those whose virus is suppressed to undetectable levels do not pass it on sexually. For hepatitis C, drugs introduced from around 2014 cure the large majority of patients in a few months. The lesson is that combination therapy beats a single drug, because the virus would need several lucky mutations at once to escape.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The greatest killers, a great victory, and a hidden benefit

Viruses have shaped human history through disease. They have also shaped human biology in ways that were discovered only recently.

Chapter 09

Smallpox: the only human disease ever eradicated

Smallpox killed perhaps three in ten of the people it infected and left many survivors scarred or blind. One widely cited estimate is that it killed around 300 million people in the twentieth century alone. In 1796 the English doctor Edward Jenner showed that infecting someone with cowpox, a mild related disease, protected them against smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.

In 1967 the World Health Organization launched an intensified global campaign. Instead of vaccinating everyone, teams found each new case, then vaccinated everyone around it, a method called ring vaccination. It worked because smallpox has no animal host and causes an obvious rash, so cases could not hide. The last natural case was in Somalia in 1977, and in 1980 the WHO declared smallpox eradicated. It remains the only human disease ever wiped out, and it shows what coordinated public health can achieve. Polio is close: as of the mid-2020s, wild polio still circulates only in Pakistan and Afghanistan.

Chapter 11

The viruses inside your DNA

Retroviruses, the family that includes HIV, insert their genes into the DNA of the cells they infect. If that happens in an egg or sperm cell, the viral DNA can be passed on to every descendant. Over tens of millions of years, this happened many times to our ancestors. Today about 8% of the human genome consists of the remains of such ancient viruses, called endogenous retroviruses. Most are broken and silent.

A few have been put to work. A protein called syncytin, which helps form a key layer of the placenta, comes from an old viral gene that once helped a virus fuse with cells. Mammal pregnancy, in part, runs on borrowed viral machinery. Viruses also matter well beyond humans. In the oceans, viruses that infect bacteria, called bacteriophages or phages, kill a large share of marine bacteria every day, releasing nutrients and influencing global carbon cycles. They are the most numerous biological entities on Earth.

Harms and uses of viruses, and the tools we use against them

Viruses are not only enemies. Researchers have turned their abilities into tools. And each of our defences has its own trade-offs.

Chapter 13

Our defences, weighed

Vaccines

Strengths: prevention, not cure; protect people around you; ended smallpox and nearly ended polio. Weaknesses: some viruses, such as HIV, mutate or hide too well for an effective vaccine so far; flu needs yearly updates; rare side effects are real; uptake depends on trust.

Antivirals and public measures

Strengths: antivirals can control or cure some infections, such as HIV and hepatitis C. Testing, isolation and ventilation slow spread of almost any virus. Weaknesses: antivirals exist for few viruses and resistance can develop. Public measures carry large social and economic costs, and how far to use them was fiercely debated during COVID-19.

Chapter 15

Living with viruses

Viruses will never be defeated as a group. There are too many, they change too fast, and most of them infect bacteria, plants and animals rather than us. The realistic goal is early detection, fast response, and strong routine vaccination, so that each new threat meets a prepared world. Wastewater testing, genome sequencing, and platform vaccines are the main new tools.

The other lesson is about science and trust. The tools that ended smallpox depended on people accepting vaccines and on countries cooperating, even during the Cold War. The biology of viruses is predictable; the human response is not. That is why the future of virology is as much about communication, fairness and cooperation as about laboratories.

The core idea

A virus is a genome in a protein coat, sometimes inside a fatty envelope. It attaches to a receptor, enters, hijacks the cell to copy itself, and escapes. Viruses mutate, jump species, and sometimes hide for life. Vaccines prevent them; antivirals target the few parts that belong only to the virus.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980, the only human disease ever eradicated.
  • The 1918 flu killed around 50 million people (estimates vary widely).
  • About 8% of human DNA comes from ancient viruses.
The common mistake

Asking for antibiotics for a cold or flu. Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses, and using them for viral infections only breeds resistance.
