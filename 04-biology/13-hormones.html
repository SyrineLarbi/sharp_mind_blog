Sharp Mind · Biology · Topic 13 of 20

Hormones. Messages posted to the whole body, read only by the cells holding the right key.

Your body has two ways of passing on instructions. Nerves are like telephone lines: fast, private and pointed at one place. Hormones are like a radio broadcast: slower, sent through the blood to every corner, and heard only by cells tuned to that station. Hormones decide when you grow, when you feel hungry, how you respond to danger, how much sugar is in your blood and when puberty starts. Tiny amounts do enormous work, which is why small faults in the system cause large diseases, and why hormone medicines are some of the most powerful drugs ever made.

Question 1 · What is it?

Chemical messages carried by the blood

A hormone is a substance made in one place, released into the blood, and acting somewhere else. That simple definition covers about fifty known human hormones and more being found.

Chapter 01

The experiment that named them

In 1902 two physiologists in London, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling, were puzzling over a small mystery. When partly digested, acidic food leaves the stomach and enters the small intestine, the pancreas releases digestive juice. Everyone assumed nerves carried that signal. Bayliss and Starling cut the nerves to a dog's intestine, and the pancreas still responded. Then they scraped cells from the intestine lining, mixed them with acid, and injected the extract into the blood. The pancreas started pouring out juice.

The signal was a chemical travelling in the blood. They called it secretin. A few years later Starling suggested the general name hormone, from a Greek word meaning "to set in motion". The point of the experiment was bigger than digestion. The body has a second communication network that does not need nerves at all. The study of it is called endocrinology, and the glands that release hormones are the endocrine glands.

Chapter 02

Two ways to send a message

The nervous system and the hormone system both carry instructions. They are built for different jobs, and most important functions use both.

NervesHormones
RouteA wire to one exact targetThe bloodstream, reaching every tissue
SpeedThousandths of a secondSeconds to hours, sometimes days
How long the effect lastsUsually stops when the signal stopsCan last minutes to years (puberty)
Who listensOnly the cell at the end of the wireAny cell with a matching receptor
Typical jobsMovement, sensation, quick reflexesGrowth, metabolism, reproduction, long-term balance

A good everyday example shows both working together. If a car swerves at you, your nerves make you jump within a fraction of a second. Your racing heart and shaky hands a minute later come mostly from adrenaline, a hormone, still circulating after the danger has passed. The nerves acted first; the hormone kept the whole body on alert. The neural side is in Neuroscience.

Chapter 03

The glands and what they send

Endocrine glands are small and scattered. Together they weigh far less than a kilogram, and yet they regulate almost everything.

Many organs that are not called glands also make hormones. The gut releases GLP-1 and the hunger hormone ghrelin. Fat tissue releases leptin, which reports how much energy is stored. The kidneys release erythropoietin (EPO), which tells bone marrow to make red blood cells. Almost every organ is partly an endocrine organ.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Keys, locks, and thermostats

Every hormone follows the same few rules. It needs a receptor to act, it acts differently on different cells, and its level is held steady by feedback.

Chapter 04

A message only the right cells can read

A hormone in the blood washes past every cell in the body. It affects only cells that carry a receptor for it: a protein shaped to grab that specific molecule. Without the receptor, the cell ignores the hormone completely. With it, the cell responds. This is how one broadcast message can have a precise effect.

The same hormone can also mean different things to different cells, because each cell type reacts to the signal in its own way. Adrenaline speeds up heart muscle, relaxes the airways in the lungs and narrows blood vessels in the skin. It is one key, but behind each lock is a different machine.

Hormones come in two broad chemical families, and they reach their receptors differently.

Water-soluble hormones

Most are small proteins, such as insulin and growth hormone, or modified amino acids, such as adrenaline. They cannot pass through the oily cell membrane. They bind to receptors on the outside of the cell, which set off a chain of chemical relays inside. Effects can start within seconds. Insulin is a protein, which is why it must be injected: taken by mouth, it would be digested like food.

Fat-soluble hormones

These include the steroids, made from cholesterol (cortisol, oestrogen, testosterone), and thyroid hormone. They slip through the membrane and bind to receptors inside the cell, often in the nucleus. There they switch genes on or off. Effects take hours to days but can last a long time. This is why many steroid medicines work as pills.

Chapter 05

Feedback: the thermostat principle

Most hormones are held within a narrow range by negative feedback. A heating thermostat works the same way. When the room is cold, the heating comes on. When it reaches the set temperature, the heating turns off. The result rises, and the rising result switches off its own cause.

The thyroid shows the pattern clearly. It is controlled by a three-step chain.

The thyroid feedback loop
   HYPOTHALAMUS  --- releases TRH --->  PITUITARY
        ^                                   |
        |                              releases TSH
        |                                   v
        |                                THYROID
        |                                   |
        +----- high T4/T3 says "enough" ----+-- releases T4 and T3 --> every cell
              (both brain glands slow down)        (metabolic speed up)

When thyroid hormone is low, the brain sends more TSH, the "thyroid-stimulating hormone". When it is high, the brain sends less. This is why doctors test TSH to check the thyroid. A high TSH usually means the thyroid is underactive, because the brain is shouting at a gland that is not listening. The answer feels backwards until you see the loop.

The same structure, hypothalamus to pituitary to target gland, controls the adrenal glands and the reproductive organs. These chains are called axes, as in the "HPA axis" for stress. A few systems use positive feedback instead, where the result drives more of itself. The clearest example is childbirth: contractions trigger the hormone oxytocin, which strengthens contractions, until the baby is born and the loop ends.

Chapter 06

Hormones keep time

Hormone levels are not flat. Many rise and fall on a daily cycle set by the brain's body clock. Cortisol normally peaks shortly after you wake up, helping to mobilise energy for the day, and falls to its lowest around midnight. Melatonin does the opposite. It rises in the evening when light fades and signals "night" to the rest of the body. Bright screens late at night can delay that rise.

Other hormones work on longer rhythms. Growth hormone is released mainly in bursts during deep sleep, which is one reason children need so much of it. The menstrual cycle is a roughly monthly rhythm run by the pituitary hormones LH and FSH together with oestrogen and progesterone from the ovaries. Puberty is a rhythm of years. Many hormones are released in pulses rather than steadily, and the timing itself carries information. When a hormone is released can matter as much as how much. That is one reason a single blood test sometimes misleads, and why doctors often ask for a morning sample.

Chapter 07

The stress response, in two speeds

Stress is the best-known example of hormones at work, and it runs at two speeds.

The fast lane takes seconds. Nerves tell the inner part of the adrenal glands to release adrenaline (also called epinephrine) and its relative noradrenaline. The heart speeds up, blood pressure rises, the airways widen, sugar is released into the blood, and digestion pauses. This is the "fight or flight" response that the American physiologist Walter Cannon described in the early twentieth century.

The slow lane takes minutes. The hypothalamus signals the pituitary, which releases ACTH, which tells the outer part of the adrenal glands to release cortisol. Cortisol keeps sugar available, shapes the immune response and helps the body recover. Then, through feedback, it switches its own production down.

The system is excellent for short emergencies. Problems arise when it stays switched on for weeks or months. Long-running high cortisol is associated with raised blood sugar, weight gain around the waist, poor sleep, and weaker immune defence. The difference between helpful and harmful stress is mostly a matter of how long it lasts and whether it switches off. The psychological side is in Stress, burnout, and what is different about them.

Chapter 08

Blood sugar: two hormones in a tug of war

The pancreas holds blood sugar (glucose) steady with a pair of opposing hormones. After a meal, as glucose rises, it releases insulin, which tells muscle, fat and liver cells to take glucose in and store it. Between meals, as glucose falls, it releases glucagon, which tells the liver to release stored glucose back into the blood. Two hormones pulling in opposite directions give finer control than one could alone.

Before insulin was available, type 1 diabetes was a death sentence, usually within months or a few years of diagnosis. In 1921 and 1922, a team at the University of Toronto including Frederick Banting, Charles Best, John Macleod and James Collip extracted insulin from animal pancreases. In January 1922 a 14-year-old boy, Leonard Thompson, became the first person to receive it, and after a purer batch his blood sugar fell. Banting and Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923. Few discoveries have turned a fatal disease into a manageable one so quickly. The two types of diabetes are explained in Volume 45.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Small amounts, large consequences

Hormones work at tiny concentrations. A little too much or too little, held for long enough, reshapes a life. That makes them central to disease, to medicine and to public health.

Chapter 09

When the system goes wrong

Endocrine disorders fall into two simple groups: too much of a hormone, or too little. The symptoms usually follow logically from what the hormone does.

Health note

This is general education, not medical advice. Symptoms like these have many causes. A doctor can arrange the right tests.

Chapter 10

A public health triumph made of salt

The thyroid needs iodine to build its hormones. In regions with little iodine in the soil, such as many mountain areas and places far from the sea, people used to develop goitre, a swollen thyroid visible in the neck. Worse, iodine shortage during pregnancy harms the developing brain of the baby. The World Health Organization describes iodine deficiency as the world's most common preventable cause of brain damage.

The fix is almost absurdly cheap: add a little iodine to table salt. Switzerland and the United States began doing this in the 1920s, and many countries followed, especially after international campaigns from the 1990s onward. Goitre, once common in parts of Europe, became rare. Understanding one hormone led to one of the most cost-effective health measures ever adopted. Iodine deficiency has not disappeared everywhere, and it is still monitored in many countries.

Chapter 11

Hormones as medicines

Because hormones are so powerful, copying or blocking them has produced some of medicine's biggest tools.

  • Replacement. Insulin for type 1 diabetes, thyroxine tablets for an underactive thyroid, and cortisol replacement for Addison's disease give back what the body cannot make.
  • Contraception. The combined pill, first approved in the United States in 1960, uses versions of oestrogen and progesterone to stop ovulation by feeding back on the brain. It changed family life and women's working lives across much of the world.
  • Anti-inflammatory steroids. Drugs based on cortisol, such as prednisolone, calm the immune system. They help in asthma, arthritis and many other conditions. Taken for long periods, they also cause the effects of too much cortisol, which is why doctors try to use the lowest dose for the shortest time.
  • Blocking hormones in cancer. Some breast cancers grow in response to oestrogen and many prostate cancers to testosterone. Drugs that block these hormones slow the cancer.
  • Appetite and weight. The GLP-1 medicines described in Volume 13 are long-lasting copies of a gut hormone.

One rule runs through all of them. Because a hormone acts wherever its receptor is, a hormone medicine acts there too, wanted or not. Side effects are part of the design, not an accident.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A brilliant broadcast system with blurry edges

The strengths of hormonal control and its weaknesses are the same features seen from two sides.

Chapter 12

What the design buys, and what it costs

FeatureAdvantageDisadvantage
Broadcast through the bloodOne signal coordinates many organs at once, as in stress or puberty.Hard to target. Drugs and disorders affect every tissue with the receptor.
Slow and long-lastingSuited to processes that take months or years, such as growth.Too slow for emergencies, and slow to correct when levels go wrong.
Feedback loopsKeep levels steady automatically.One faulty step can throw the whole chain out, and taking hormones from outside can switch off the body's own supply.

The feedback point has a practical sting. Someone who takes high-dose steroid tablets for months switches off their own cortisol production, because the brain sees plenty and stops asking for more. If they then stop suddenly, the adrenal glands may not restart in time. That is why such courses are reduced gradually under medical advice. Anabolic steroids used for muscle building do something similar to the testes, which can shrink and stop producing testosterone.

Chapter 13

A case study in weighing risk: menopause hormone therapy

At menopause the ovaries stop releasing eggs and oestrogen levels fall, which can cause hot flushes, poor sleep and, over years, thinner bones. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) tops oestrogen back up. In 2002 an early report from a large American trial, the Women's Health Initiative, found raised risks of breast cancer, stroke and blood clots in the combined-hormone group. Use fell sharply around the world almost overnight.

Later analysis gave a more nuanced picture. The women in the trial were on average in their sixties, many years past menopause. For women who start treatment under 60 or within about ten years of menopause, major medical societies now generally conclude that the benefits for symptoms often outweigh the risks. Risks still depend on the type of hormone, the dose, the route (patches carry a lower clot risk than tablets) and personal history. The same treatment can be wise for one person and unwise for another, which is the honest lesson of almost every hormone medicine. Chapter 15

The live debate: endocrine disruptors

Some manufactured chemicals can bind to hormone receptors or interfere with hormone production. They are called endocrine disruptors. Known examples include the pesticide DDT and certain industrial chemicals. Others, such as bisphenol A (BPA), once common in hard plastics and the linings of food cans, and some phthalates, which make plastics soft, are under active study.

The debate is real and it is about dose. Laboratory and animal studies show effects, and hormones work at very low concentrations, so small exposures could matter. On the other hand, proving harm from everyday human exposure is hard, and regulators have reached different conclusions. The European Union has taken a stricter line than some others; in 2024 it moved to ban BPA in materials that touch food. A fair summary is that concern is scientifically grounded, but the size of the risk at ordinary exposure is still contested. Expect more regulation, more testing, and continued disagreement about where to draw the line. The chemistry side is in Toxicology.

The core idea

Hormones are chemical messages carried in the blood. They act only on cells with the right receptor, they are held steady by feedback loops run largely from the hypothalamus and pituitary, and they control slow, whole-body processes such as growth, metabolism, stress and reproduction.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Hormones were discovered in 1902 (secretin, Bayliss and Starling).
  • Insulin was first given to a patient in January 1922.
  • Cortisol peaks soon after waking and is lowest around midnight.
The common mistake

Thinking more of a hormone is always more of its effect. Taking a hormone from outside can switch off your own production through feedback, which is why stopping some treatments suddenly is dangerous.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a high TSH result usually means an underactive thyroid? If you can, you have understood feedback, and with it most of endocrinology.
