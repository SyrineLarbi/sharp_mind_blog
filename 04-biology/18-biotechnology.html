Biotechnology. Putting living things to work, from the brewery to the vaccine plant.

A yeast cell is a factory smaller than a speck of dust. Feed it sugar and it makes alcohol and carbon dioxide, which is why bread rises and beer exists. Give it a new gene and it can make a medicine. Biotechnology is the use of living cells, or the molecules they make, to produce something useful. It is the oldest technology in the kitchen and one of the newest in medicine. It produced most of the world's best-selling drugs, the vaccines of the pandemic and the enzymes in your washing powder. This file explains how the factories work, what they have changed, and what they cost.

Question 1 · What is it?

Living cells as tools and factories

Biotechnology is any technology that uses living organisms, cells or their molecules to make products or solve problems. Its oldest forms are thousands of years old; its modern form began when scientists learned to change the cells' instructions.

Chapter 01

Older than writing

People were brewing beer, leavening bread and turning milk into cheese and yoghurt thousands of years ago, in Mesopotamia, Egypt and China. They did not know why it worked. They only knew that a bit of the last batch, kept warm, made the next one. That "bit" was a population of living microbes.

The explanation came in the 1850s and 1860s from the French chemist Louis Pasteur. Many scientists then thought fermentation was a purely chemical process. Pasteur showed that it is done by living yeast and bacteria, and that different microbes produce different results: one kind makes wine, another sours it into vinegar. Once fermentation was understood as the work of living cells, it could be controlled, and eventually redesigned.

The word "biotechnology" itself was coined in 1919 by the Hungarian engineer Károly Ereky, who was writing about large-scale pig farming and the industrial use of living things. The idea is that broad: any time living matter is used as a production tool.

Chapter 03

The leap from a dish to a tank: penicillin

Discovering a useful molecule and producing it in quantity are separate problems, and the second is often harder. Penicillin proved it. Alexander Fleming noticed the mould's effect in 1928, but for more than a decade almost nobody could make enough to treat a patient. In 1941, at Oxford, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain treated a policeman with an infected wound. He improved, then died when the supply ran out.

The problem was solved by industrial biology during the Second World War. British and American scientists worked with drug companies in the United States. A government laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, found that growing the mould in a by-product of corn processing raised yields sharply, and a strain from a mouldy cantaloupe melon bought in a local market produced far more penicillin than Fleming's. Companies learned to grow the mould in huge stirred and aerated tanks, called deep-tank fermentation. By the D-Day landings in 1944, penicillin was being produced in large quantities for Allied troops.

That leap created the template for modern biotechnology. The organism is the factory, the tank is the factory building, and most of the engineering is about keeping the organism productive at scale.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Choose the cell, give it instructions, grow it, purify the product

Every biotech product goes through the same chain. The scientific part often gets the headlines; the engineering and quality control usually decide the cost.

Chapter 04

Inside the bioreactor

A bioreactor is a steel tank, from a few litres in a lab to tens of thousands of litres in a plant, in which cells are grown under tightly controlled conditions. Temperature, acidity, oxygen and nutrients must stay in a narrow range. A small contamination by the wrong microbe can ruin a batch worth millions.

The biotech production chain
  CHOOSE A HOST   bacteria (fast, cheap) / yeast / animal cells (complex proteins)
       |
  ENGINEER IT     insert the gene for the product; pick the best-producing cells
       |
  UPSTREAM        grow cells: small flask -> seed tank -> production bioreactor
       |          (control heat, oxygen, acidity, food; days to weeks)
  DOWNSTREAM      separate cells, purify the product, remove every contaminant
       |          (often the most expensive step)
  FORMULATE       make a stable liquid, powder or tablet; test every batch

The choice of host matters. Bacteria such as E. coli grow fast and cheaply but cannot finish many complex human proteins properly. Yeast is a middle ground. For the most complex medicines, companies use mammalian cells, most often a line originally taken from Chinese hamster ovaries in the 1950s, which can add the sugar chains that many human proteins need. Living factories are less predictable than chemical ones, which is why biotech manufacturing is regulated batch by batch.

Chapter 05

Why a biologic is not an ordinary pill

Most traditional medicines are small molecules made by chemistry. Aspirin has 21 atoms. A medicine made by living cells, called a biologic, is usually a large protein. An antibody has tens of thousands of atoms folded into a precise shape. You could not make it by mixing chemicals in a flask, and it would be digested if swallowed, so most biologics are injected.

Size and complexity change the business too. When a chemical drug's patent expires, any competent manufacturer can make an identical copy, a generic, and prices often collapse. A copy of a biologic, made in different cells by a different process, cannot be guaranteed identical, only highly similar. These copies are called biosimilars. They must pass more tests, cost more to develop, and usually bring smaller price falls. The phrase "the process is the product" sums up biotech manufacturing: change the process and you may change the medicine.

Chapter 06

Platforms: technologies you can point at any target

The most valuable biotech advances are often platforms, methods that can be re-aimed at new diseases with modest changes.

  • Monoclonal antibodies, first made in 1975 by César Milstein and Georges Köhler, can be designed to bind almost any target molecule (Volume 13).
  • mRNA delivers a recipe that the body's own cells follow (Volume 13).
  • Viral vectors deliver genes for gene therapies and some vaccines.
  • Engineered immune cells, such as CAR-T, turn a patient's cells into a living drug.

Platforms explain the speed of the COVID-19 vaccines. Chinese scientists published the virus's genetic sequence in January 2020. Because mRNA and viral-vector platforms already existed, vaccines were designed within days or weeks, and the United Kingdom authorised the first mRNA vaccine in early December 2020. Decades of slow work on the platforms made one fast year possible.

Chapter 07

Enzymes: the quiet workhorses of industry

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a specific chemical reaction without being used up. Life depends on thousands of them. Industry uses them because they work at mild temperatures, in water, and on exactly one kind of molecule.

Look at a packet of laundry powder. Many contain enzymes that break down protein stains such as blood and egg, fat stains, and starch. Because they work well in cool water, they let washing machines run at lower temperatures, which saves energy. Enzymes turn corn starch into sweet syrups, make fruit juice clearer, soften denim for "stone-washed" jeans, and help make bread stay soft. Most hard cheese is now made with enzymes from engineered microbes rather than from calves' stomachs, as Volume 15 explains.

Microbes also make bulk chemicals. Citric acid, used in soft drinks and many foods, is made by a mould. Ethanol fuel is made by yeast fermenting sugar cane or corn. White biotechnology replaces heat, pressure and harsh chemicals with living catalysts, and that is often both cheaper and cleaner. The chemistry of catalysts is in Catalysts.

Chapter 08

Reading life: diagnostics and sequencing

Biotechnology does not only make things. It also measures them. Most modern medical tests rely on biological molecules. A home pregnancy test and a rapid COVID test both use antibodies that grab a target molecule and trigger a coloured line. A PCR test copies a tiny amount of a virus's genetic material millions of times so it can be detected.

Reading DNA has become dramatically cheaper. The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, cost about 2.7 billion dollars over thirteen years. By the early 2020s a whole human genome could be read for a few hundred dollars in a day or two. That fall, faster than the famous improvement in computer chips, has made genetic diagnosis of rare diseases, tracking of new virus variants and much of modern biology possible. The method is explained in DNA sequencing.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Health, food, and the economy

Biotechnology now shapes what medicines exist, how food is made, and which countries lead in a strategic industry.

Chapter 09

The medicines that changed chronic disease

Biologics transformed the treatment of several chronic diseases. Rheumatoid arthritis once commonly led to crippled joints. Antibody drugs that block a single inflammatory signal now keep many patients working and mobile. The best-known of these, adalimumab (sold as Humira), was for years the world's best-selling medicine. Antibodies are now standard in several cancers, in severe asthma, in psoriasis and in some forms of migraine.

Biologics now make up a large and growing share of spending on medicines in rich countries. That reflects real benefit and also high prices. A year of treatment can cost tens of thousands of dollars or more. The central health-policy question about biotechnology is less "does it work?" than "who can afford it?" The arrival of biosimilars has started to bring prices down in Europe and, more slowly, in the United States.

Chapter 10

Food from tanks instead of animals

Biotechnology may also change what we eat. Precision fermentation uses engineered microbes to make specific animal proteins, such as whey from milk or egg-white protein, without the animal. Several such proteins are already sold in ice creams and protein drinks.

Cultivated meat goes further: animal cells grown in bioreactors into tissue. Singapore became the first country to allow sales, of a cultivated chicken product, in 2020. The United States approved two companies' cultivated chicken in 2023. Other countries, including Italy, have banned it, and several US states have passed bans too. The obstacles are technical and economic. Growing animal cells needs sterile conditions and expensive nutrients, and producing meat at the price and scale of a chicken farm has not been achieved. Cultivated meat exists; cheap cultivated meat does not yet. The environmental case is in Sustainable agriculture.

Chapter 11

A strategic industry

The pandemic showed governments that the ability to make vaccines and medicines at home is a matter of national security. Countries with production plants got vaccines first. Many lower-income countries waited months longer, and the World Health Organization repeatedly criticised the imbalance. In response, new vaccine plants have been planned in Africa and elsewhere, including an mRNA technology-transfer hub in South Africa supported by the WHO.

Biotech research clusters around a few places: Boston and Cambridge in Massachusetts, the San Francisco Bay Area, Cambridge and Oxford in England, Basel in Switzerland, and a fast-growing sector in China, whose companies now license a substantial share of new drug candidates to Western firms. Biotechnology has become part of the competition between great powers, alongside computer chips. The supply-chain side of that competition is in Computer chips, which follows a similar logic.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Extraordinary products, expensive failures, and dual use

Biotechnology's strengths are real and visible. Its weaknesses are cost, a very high failure rate, and the fact that the same knowledge can be misused.

Chapter 12

The failure rate deserves emphasis. Analyses of clinical trials generally find that only around one in ten medicines that enter human testing eventually reach approval, and the figure is lower in some fields, such as brain diseases. Each success has to pay for the failures. That is one reason prices are high, though how much of the price is justified by it is a matter of fierce debate between companies, insurers and governments.

Chapter 13

Dual use: the problem that comes with power

Almost every biological technique that helps can, in principle, also harm. Knowing how a virus spreads between cells helps design vaccines and could help make it worse. This is called the dual-use problem. It has been argued over since the Asilomar meeting in 1975, described in Genetic engineering.

Two debates are especially active. The first is over "gain-of-function" research, experiments that make a pathogen more transmissible or dangerous in order to study it. Supporters say it helps prepare for natural threats; critics say the risk of an accident outweighs the knowledge. The debate grew sharper during the COVID-19 pandemic, alongside a separate dispute over the pandemic's origin that remains unresolved. The second concerns DNA synthesis. Companies that make DNA to order now commonly screen requests for dangerous sequences, and governments are making some screening a requirement. Most researchers see these risks as real but manageable through oversight, while others argue some research should not be done at all. Both positions are held by serious scientists.

Chapter 15

From borrowing to engineering

For most of its history, biotechnology borrowed what nature had already made: a mould, a yeast, an enzyme. The field now called synthetic biology aims to design biological parts like electronic components and assemble them into microbes that do new things, such as sensing a pollutant, making a drug precursor, or fixing nitrogen from the air for crops so farmers need less fertiliser.

The honest picture is mixed. Some early synthetic-biology companies raised large sums and then struggled, because biology turned out to be less predictable than the engineering metaphor suggested. Others built lasting businesses in fragrances, enzymes and research tools. The likely future is steady, uneven progress: big wins in medicines and diagnostics, slower ones in bulk chemicals and food, and a continuing need for rules about safety and access. The core idea

Biotechnology uses living cells and their molecules as tools and factories. The method is the same from brewing to vaccines: choose a cell, give it instructions, grow it under control, purify the product. It has produced medicines chemistry could not, at high cost and with real dual-use risks.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Word coined in 1919; first engineered medicine sold in 1982.
  • Roughly 1 in 10 drugs entering human trials is eventually approved.
  • Reading a human genome fell from about 2.7 billion dollars (finished 2003) to a few hundred.
The common mistake

Thinking that discovering a molecule is the hard part. Making it reliably, purely and cheaply at scale is often harder, as penicillin and cultivated meat both show.

The words, in plain words
Biotechnology
Using living things, cells or their molecules to make products or solve problems.
Fermentation
Growing microbes so they convert food such as sugar into a useful product.
Bioreactor
A controlled tank in which cells are grown to make a product.
Upstream and downstream
Growing the cells, and then separating and purifying what they make.
Biologic
A medicine, usually a large protein, made by living cells.
Biosimilar
A highly similar, approved copy of a biologic made by another company.
Generic
An identical copy of a chemical drug after its patent expires.
Enzyme
A protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction.
Platform technology
A method that can be re-aimed at new targets with small changes.
Precision fermentation
Engineered microbes making one specific protein, such as a milk protein.
Cultivated meat
Meat grown from animal cells in bioreactors rather than from a slaughtered animal.
Dual use
Knowledge or tools that can be used both to help and to harm.
Synthetic biology
Designing and building new biological parts and systems.
