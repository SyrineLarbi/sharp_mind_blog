Bacteria. One cell, no nucleus, and three and a half billion years of practice.

Bacteria are the oldest and most successful living things on Earth. Each is a single cell, a few thousandths of a millimetre long, with no nucleus. They can double in number in under an hour, swap genes with strangers, talk to each other with chemicals, and survive boiling as dormant spores. A handful cause plague, cholera and tuberculosis. The rest digest our food, fix nitrogen for our crops, and gave us some of the most powerful tools in modern biology.

Question 1 · What is it?

A complete living cell, stripped down to essentials

A bacterium does everything a living thing must do, inside one tiny cell with no inner compartments. Simple does not mean primitive. It means optimised.

Chapter 01

The oldest residents

Some of the oldest likely signs of life on Earth are layered rocks called stromatolites, built up by mats of microbes trapping sediment. The oldest examples are about 3.5 billion years old, although some of the oldest claims are debated. Living stromatolites still grow today, for instance in Shark Bay in Western Australia. For more than half of Earth's history, bacteria and archaea were the only life there was.

Bacteria live everywhere life can exist: in soil, in oceans, in hot springs, deep underground, on and inside every animal. Their total mass on Earth is huge, far larger than that of all animals combined, and most of it lives underground and in the oceans. By almost any measure, numbers, variety, age or range, bacteria are the dominant form of life on the planet. Humans are latecomers living in a bacterial world.

The bacterial flagellum is worth a moment. At its base is a true rotary motor, built from proteins and powered by a flow of charged particles across the membrane. It can spin very fast and reverse direction. It is one of the very few wheel-like rotating parts known in living things.

Chapter 03

Shapes and names

Bacteria come in a few basic shapes, and the shape is often part of the name. Spheres are cocci (from Greek for "berry"); rods are bacilli ("little sticks"); spirals are spirilla or, when flexible, spirochaetes. Cocci in grape-like clusters are Staphylococcus; cocci in chains are Streptococcus.

Every species has a two-part scientific name, genus and species, written in italics. Escherichia coli, the most studied organism in biology, is named after Theodor Escherich, the German-Austrian doctor who described it in 1885. Most strains of E. coli live harmlessly in the gut. A few have picked up toxin genes and cause serious food poisoning. The same species name can cover both a harmless resident and a dangerous strain, which is why laboratories identify the exact strain in outbreaks.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Grow fast, share genes, talk, and wait out bad times

Four abilities explain most of what bacteria do: exponential growth, sideways gene sharing, chemical communication, and dormancy.

Chapter 04

Splitting in two, again and again

A bacterium reproduces by copying its chromosome, growing, and splitting into two identical cells. This is binary fission. In ideal laboratory conditions, E. coli can divide about every 20 minutes. Others are far slower: the tuberculosis bacterium takes most of a day, which is one reason TB treatment lasts months.

Doubling is deceptive. One cell becomes two, then four, then eight. After ten doublings there are about a thousand; after twenty, about a million. With a 20-minute doubling time, that is under seven hours. This is why cooked food left at room temperature can go from safe to dangerous in a few hours, and why the food safety advice in The dangerous middle is about time as well as temperature. In reality growth slows as food runs out and waste builds up.

Chapter 05

Sharing genes with strangers, and how that revealed DNA

Bacteria do not only pass genes to their offspring. They also exchange genes sideways, a process called horizontal gene transfer. There are three routes. In transformation, a bacterium picks up loose DNA from its surroundings, often from dead cells. In conjugation, two bacteria connect through a thin tube and one passes a plasmid to the other. In transduction, a virus that infects bacteria accidentally carries a piece of one host's DNA into the next.

Transformation made history. In 1928 the British microbiologist Frederick Griffith found that a harmless strain of pneumonia bacteria became deadly when mixed with heat-killed deadly bacteria. Something from the dead cells had changed the living ones. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty in New York showed that the "something" was DNA. It was among the first strong evidence that genes are made of DNA, nearly a decade before its structure was solved. The same sideways sharing is what lets resistance spread between species, as Volume 13 explains.

Chapter 06

Bacteria talk, and build cities

A glowing marine bacterium, Vibrio fischeri, lives inside the light organ of the Hawaiian bobtail squid. The bacteria glow only when they are crowded together in large numbers, not when they are scattered in seawater. Each cell releases a small signalling molecule. When enough cells are present, the signal passes a threshold and they all switch on their light genes together. This head-counting by chemistry is called quorum sensing, after the minimum number of members needed for a meeting to make decisions. The squid uses the light to hide its shadow from predators below on moonlit nights.

Many disease-causing bacteria use quorum sensing too, waiting until they are numerous before releasing toxins. Crowds of bacteria also build biofilms: dense communities stuck to a surface and wrapped in a slimy matrix they produce. Dental plaque is a biofilm. So are the slippery coats on river stones and the infections that grow on catheters and artificial joints. Bacteria in a biofilm can be far more tolerant of antibiotics than the same bacteria swimming freely, which is why infected medical implants often must be removed.

Chapter 07

Spores: waiting out the bad times

Some bacteria, including the families that cause anthrax, tetanus and botulism, can turn into spores when conditions get bad. A spore is a dried-out, armoured, dormant form with almost no activity. Spores can survive boiling, drying, radiation and many disinfectants, and remain viable for years or longer. When conditions improve, they wake up and grow again.

Spores explain several practical rules. Hospitals sterilise surgical instruments in autoclaves, which use steam under pressure at about 121 °C, because ordinary boiling at 100 °C does not reliably kill spores. Home canning must be done properly for low-acid foods, because the spores of Clostridium botulinum can survive and, in an airless jar, produce botulinum toxin. And honey is not given to babies under one year old, because it can contain these spores, which an infant's immature gut cannot keep in check.

Chapter 08

How a few bacteria cause disease

Bacteria that cause disease are called pathogens, and they harm us in two main ways. Some invade, multiplying in tissues and triggering inflammation that damages the body: tuberculosis in the lungs, or meningitis around the brain. Others mostly release toxins, poisonous proteins. The cholera bacterium stays in the gut but releases a toxin that makes gut cells pour out water, causing severe diarrhoea that can kill through dehydration within a day. Treatment with clean water containing the right amounts of salt and sugar, called oral rehydration solution, saves most patients.

Botulinum toxin, from Clostridium botulinum, is among the most poisonous substances known. It blocks the signal from nerves to muscles, causing paralysis. In tiny, controlled doses, the same toxin is the medicine sold as Botox, used to relax muscles for conditions such as some migraines and muscle spasms, and in cosmetics. Tuberculosis remains the deadliest bacterial disease. The World Health Organization estimated about 1.25 million deaths from it in 2023, making it again the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious agent.

Health note

This file is general education about bacteria, not medical advice. If you think you have a bacterial infection, see a doctor, and take antibiotics only as prescribed.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Plague, cholera, and the tools of modern biology

Bacteria have changed the course of history, and studying them has given science some of its most important methods.

Chapter 09

How antibiotics hit bacteria and spare you

An antibiotic is useful only if it harms bacteria much more than human cells. That is possible because bacteria are built differently. Each main class of antibiotic attacks one of those differences.

TargetWhy it is different in bacteriaExample drugs
Cell wall buildingHuman cells have no peptidoglycan wallPenicillins, cephalosporins
RibosomesBacterial ribosomes are a different size and shapeTetracyclines, macrolides, aminoglycosides
DNA copying enzymesBacterial versions differ from oursFluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin
Making a vitamin (folate)Bacteria make it; humans get it from foodSulfonamides, trimethoprim

One complication is that mitochondria, descended from bacteria, have bacteria-like ribosomes. That is part of why some antibiotics have side effects. And because antibiotics cannot tell harmful bacteria from helpful ones, a course of them can disturb the gut's community. In some people, especially older patients in hospital, that lets a dangerous species, Clostridioides difficile, take over and cause severe diarrhoea.

Chapter 10

Plague and cholera: bacteria that shaped history

The Black Death swept through Europe, the Middle East and North Africa from 1347 to 1351. It killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe's population. The bacterium responsible, Yersinia pestis, was identified in 1894 in Hong Kong by the Swiss-French doctor Alexandre Yersin, and it spreads mainly through fleas carried by rats and other rodents. DNA extracted from the teeth of medieval plague victims has since confirmed it. The labour shortages that followed are thought to have raised wages and helped weaken serfdom in parts of Europe, although historians debate how much.

Cholera gave epidemiology its founding story. In 1854, during an outbreak in London's Soho, the doctor John Snow mapped deaths and found them clustered around one public water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded local officials to remove the pump handle. The outbreak was already declining, but his map became the classic demonstration that cholera spreads through dirty water, not "bad air". That idea led to the sewers and clean water described in Sewers, not surgeons. Cholera still causes large outbreaks where clean water and sanitation are lacking.

Chapter 11

The gifts: PCR and CRISPR came from bacteria

In the 1960s the American microbiologist Thomas Brock found a bacterium living in the hot springs of Yellowstone National Park and named it Thermus aquaticus. Its enzymes work at temperatures that destroy most proteins. Two decades later, its DNA-copying enzyme, Taq polymerase, made the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) practical. PCR copies a chosen piece of DNA billions of times by cycling the temperature up and down, and a heat-proof enzyme survives every cycle. PCR is now basic to genetic testing, forensics and the diagnosis of infections.

The gene-editing tool CRISPR is also a bacterial invention. Bacteria keep short records of viruses that have attacked them, stored in their DNA, and use them to guide an enzyme that cuts matching viral DNA if the virus returns. It is an immune system with a memory. Researchers turned it into a programmable editor, described in CRISPR. Some of the most powerful tools in modern biology were found by studying obscure bacteria for no practical reason.

Chapter 13

Antibiotics: the miracle and its price

What antibiotics gave us

Pneumonia, infected wounds, meningitis and childbirth infections went from frequent killers to treatable. They also made modern surgery, chemotherapy, transplants and intensive care safe enough to do routinely, as Volume 13 explains.

What they cost

Every use selects for resistant bacteria. They disturb helpful gut bacteria. Heavy use in farming spreads resistance. And very few genuinely new classes have reached patients in recent decades, while resistance keeps rising.

The honest balance is clear. Antibiotics are among the greatest medical advances ever, and they are a shared resource that every unnecessary use wears down. That is why doctors now prescribe them more carefully, why shorter courses are often recommended where evidence supports it, and why many countries have restricted their routine use in healthy farm animals.

Chapter 15

Partners first, enemies second

The twentieth century treated bacteria mostly as an enemy to be wiped out. The twenty-first is learning a more balanced view. Bacteria are partners in digestion, farming and industry, and a source of tools nobody would have invented alone. The harmful minority is still dangerous, and resistance means the fight against it is getting harder, not easier.

The practical habits are simple and well supported. Wash hands with soap, handle and store food safely, keep vaccinations up to date, and use antibiotics only when they are really needed. Each of those protects you, and each also protects the shared resource that antibiotics have become. The core idea

A bacterium is a single cell with no nucleus, a protective wall, one main chromosome and often plasmids. Bacteria grow by doubling, swap genes sideways, coordinate by quorum sensing, and some survive as spores. A minority cause disease through invasion or toxins; antibiotics work by hitting parts that human cells lack.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bacterial life goes back about 3.5 billion years.
  • E. coli can double every 20 minutes in ideal conditions: a million cells in under seven hours.
  • Tuberculosis killed about 1.25 million people in 2023 (WHO estimate).
The common mistake

Asking for antibiotics for a cold, or keeping leftovers to take "just in case". Unneeded antibiotics breed resistance and harm your helpful bacteria. Take them only as your doctor advises.

Can you explain why penicillin kills bacteria but does not harm your own cells? If so, you understand the bacterial cell wall.
