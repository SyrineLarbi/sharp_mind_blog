Human anatomy. The map of the body, and the history written into its shape.

Anatomy is the study of the body's structure: what is where, what it is made of, and how the parts connect. It sounds like memorising names, and medical students do memorise thousands. But underneath the names is a set of patterns that make sense. Bones are levers, the gut is a long tube with the body wrapped around it, and many awkward details only make sense as leftovers of an evolutionary past. Knowing the map makes every conversation about health, sport and injury clearer.

Question 1 · What is it?

A science of looking carefully, which took centuries to get right

Anatomy is one of the oldest sciences, and for a long time one of the most wrong, because people were not allowed to look.

Chapter 01

The book that corrected thirteen centuries

In the third century BCE, physicians in Alexandria, Egypt, such as Herophilus, dissected human bodies and described nerves, the brain and the liver with real accuracy. Then human dissection became forbidden or rare for a very long time. In the second century CE, the Greek physician Galen, working in Rome, wrote about anatomy so thoroughly that his books were treated as the final word for over a thousand years. The problem was that he had mostly dissected animals, such as pigs and apes, and assumed humans were the same.

In 1543 a 28-year-old professor at the University of Padua, Andreas Vesalius, published De humani corporis fabrica ("On the Fabric of the Human Body"). He had dissected human corpses himself, instead of reading Galen aloud while an assistant cut, which was the usual method. His book, with superb large woodcut illustrations, corrected many of Galen's errors. For example, Galen described a network of blood vessels at the base of the brain that exists in some animals but not in humans. Vesalius's real contribution was a method: trust what the body shows over what the authorities say. The same year, Copernicus published his book putting the Sun at the centre, and 1543 is often called the start of the Scientific Revolution.

Chapter 02

A precise language for a body that moves

"Above" or "in front" is useless if the patient is lying down, bending over or upside down. So anatomists describe everything from one standard pose, the anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms by the sides, palms facing forward. Every direction is defined from that pose, whatever the body is actually doing.

The body is also sliced, in imagination and in scans, along three planes. A sagittal plane divides left from right, a coronal plane divides front from back, and a transverse plane divides top from bottom. When you see a CT or MRI image, it is almost always one of these slices. Most anatomical names are Latin or Greek, which once made them shared across languages and still does.

Chapter 03

From cells to systems

The body is organised in levels. Cells (see Cell biology) form tissues, groups of similar cells doing one job. There are only four basic kinds: epithelial tissue covers surfaces and lines tubes; connective tissue, such as bone, cartilage, fat and blood, supports and connects; muscle tissue contracts; and nervous tissue carries signals. Tissues combine into organs, and organs that work together form organ systems.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A frame, a set of engines, a tube, and a wiring system

Here is the body as a tour of its structure, focused on the patterns that explain the details.

Chapter 04

The skeleton: living levers

An adult skeleton usually has 206 bones. A newborn has many more, roughly 270 to 300, because many bones start as separate pieces and fuse during childhood. The gaps between the plates of a baby's skull, the soft spots you can feel, let the head squeeze through the birth canal and the brain grow. The longest bone is the thigh bone, the femur. The smallest is the stapes, a stirrup-shaped bone in the middle ear about the size of a grain of rice.

Bone is not dead material like the skeleton in a classroom. It is living tissue with blood vessels and nerves, and it is constantly broken down and rebuilt. Bone responds to load: it thickens where it is stressed, which is why astronauts lose bone in weightlessness and why weight-bearing exercise protects against osteoporosis, the thinning of bone with age. Inside many large bones is marrow, where most blood cells are made.

Where bones meet are joints. Some, like the joints in the adult skull, barely move. Others move freely: the shoulder is a shallow ball-and-socket joint with a huge range of movement and a high risk of dislocation; the hip is a deep ball-and-socket, more stable and less flexible. Mobility and stability trade against each other, joint by joint. Bones are held together at joints by ligaments; muscles attach to bones by tendons.

Chapter 05

Muscles: three kinds, and they only pull

There are three kinds of muscle tissue. Skeletal muscle moves bones and is under your conscious control; humans have more than 600 named skeletal muscles. Cardiac muscle is found only in the heart and contracts rhythmically for a lifetime. Smooth muscle lines hollow organs such as the gut, blood vessels and bladder, and works without conscious control.

The key fact about skeletal muscles is simple. Muscles can only pull. They never push. So they work in opposing pairs. The biceps at the front of the upper arm pulls to bend the elbow; the triceps at the back pulls to straighten it. Every movement you make is a negotiation between muscles on opposite sides of a joint. Most limb muscles attach close to the joint they move. That gives a poor lever for force but a fast, wide movement at the hand or foot, a trade-off that favours speed.

Chapter 06

The trunk: two cavities and a dome between them

The trunk holds two main spaces. The thoracic cavity, or chest, is enclosed by the ribs and contains the heart and lungs. The abdominal cavity below contains the stomach, liver, intestines, kidneys and other organs, with the pelvis beneath. Between the two is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle, the diaphragm. When it contracts and flattens, the chest gets bigger and air rushes in. It is the main muscle of breathing.

A useful way to picture the digestive system is that the body is wrapped around a tube. From mouth to anus, the gut is open to the outside world at both ends. Food inside it is not yet truly "inside" you until it is absorbed through the wall. The small intestine is several metres long and lined with tiny finger-like folds that give it an enormous surface. The liver, the largest internal organ at around 1.5 kilograms, sits mostly on the right under the ribs.

Chapter 07

The heart and the vessels: a double pump and a road network

The heart is about the size of a fist and sits in the middle of the chest, tilted to the left. It has four chambers. The two upper ones, the atria, receive blood. The two lower ones, the ventricles, pump it out. The right side sends blood to the lungs; the left side sends it round the whole body, which is why the left ventricle's wall is much thicker. Four valves act as one-way doors. The "lub-dub" of a heartbeat is the sound of valves closing.

Blood leaves the heart in arteries, which have thick muscular walls to take the pressure. Arteries branch into ever smaller vessels down to capillaries, so narrow that red blood cells pass in single file, where oxygen and food leave the blood. Blood returns in veins, which have thinner walls and, in the limbs, small valves that stop it falling back. Alongside runs a second network, the lymphatic system, which collects fluid that leaks out of capillaries and returns it to the blood, passing through lymph nodes on the way. Swollen "glands" in your neck during a cold are lymph nodes busy with an immune response.

Chapter 08

The wiring, and the wrapper

The nervous system has two parts. The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord. The brain weighs about 1.3 to 1.4 kilograms in an adult, and the spinal cord, about as thick as a finger, runs down a canal inside the stacked bones of the spine. The peripheral nervous system is the network of nerves that branch out to every part of the body. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves leave the cord between the vertebrae, and twelve pairs of cranial nerves come directly from the brain, serving the eyes, face, tongue and more. The brain's own anatomy has its own file (Structure of the brain).

Wrapping everything is the skin, usually counted as the body's largest organ, with an area of around two square metres in an adult. It is a barrier against germs and water loss, a temperature regulator through sweat and blood flow, a sense organ for touch, pain and heat, and the place where the body makes vitamin D in sunlight.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Every operation depends on it, and its history has a dark side

Anatomy is the foundation of surgery and diagnosis. It also explains why some parts of you are badly designed.

Chapter 09

Nobody is the textbook

Anatomy books show a typical body. Real bodies vary, and surgeons must expect it. Some people lack a small forearm muscle, the palmaris longus, on one or both sides; the share varies a lot between populations. Arteries branch in different patterns. Organs can even be mirror-reversed: in a rare condition called situs inversus, the heart sits on the right and the liver on the left, often with no symptoms at all.

That is why surgery depends on knowing both the standard map and its common variations. A surgeon removing a gallbladder must identify the ducts and arteries correctly before cutting, because a misidentified variation can cause serious injury. Modern imaging lets doctors see a patient's own anatomy before an operation, which has made many procedures safer and more precise.

Chapter 10

Where the bodies came from

For centuries anatomy schools needed corpses, and the legal supply was tiny. In Britain the main legal source was the bodies of executed criminals. Demand far exceeded supply, so "resurrection men" dug up freshly buried bodies and sold them to anatomy teachers. Families guarded graves and built iron cages over them.

In Edinburgh in 1828, William Burke and William Hare went further. They murdered around sixteen people and sold the bodies to a well-known anatomy lecturer. The scandal helped push Britain to pass the Anatomy Act of 1832, which allowed unclaimed bodies, often of the poor who died in workhouses, to be used for dissection. That ended the grave-robbing trade but placed the burden on the poorest. Today, in most countries, anatomy depends on people who choose to donate their bodies to science. Every doctor's knowledge of the body rests, quite literally, on the generosity of the dead. Many medical schools hold services of thanks for their donors.

Chapter 11

A body built by history, not by a designer

Some features of human anatomy make little sense as engineering and a lot of sense as history. Evolution can only modify what already exists. It cannot start again from a clean sheet.

The nerve that takes a detour

The recurrent laryngeal nerve controls the voice box in the neck. But it runs from the brain down into the chest, loops under the main artery near the heart, and climbs back up. In fish ancestors, the route was short and direct. As necks lengthened and the heart moved down, the nerve was dragged along. In a giraffe the detour is several metres long.

The eye wired backwards

In the human eye, the nerve fibres run across the front of the light-sensing layer, then exit through a hole in it. That hole creates a blind spot in each eye, which the brain fills in so you never notice. The octopus evolved a camera eye separately, with the wiring behind, and has no such blind spot.

Walking upright adds more. The spine evolved as a horizontal beam in four-legged ancestors and was turned into a vertical column with curves to balance the body over the hips. It works, but lower back pain is among the most common health complaints in the world. The human pelvis must allow both upright walking and the birth of a baby with a large head, and childbirth is hard and risky for humans compared with most other primates. How much of that is a strict trade-off between walking and birth is still debated. The shape of the body is a record of its past, and some of its weaknesses come straight from that record.

Chapter 13

What anatomy as a science got wrong

For centuries the "standard" body in textbooks was a male one, with female anatomy treated as a variation, often only in the reproductive chapter. Some female anatomy was described poorly for a surprisingly long time. The full structure of the clitoris, much larger than its visible part, was described in detail by the Australian urologist Helen O'Connell only in 1998, and it was missing or inaccurate in many textbooks after that. Textbook illustrations have also mostly shown light skin, which can make conditions harder to recognise on darker skin.

Strengths of today's anatomy

Extremely detailed and well tested. Dissection, microscopy and imaging agree. Scans can show an individual's anatomy in three dimensions, in a living person, without cutting.

Weaknesses that remain

Teaching often still centres one "typical" body. Variation by sex, age and ancestry is less well documented. Some medical schools have reduced hands-on dissection to save time and cost, and surgeons disagree about whether that harms training.

Chapter 15

A map that is finally being drawn for everyone

The large-scale map of the human body is essentially complete. The future of anatomy lies in three places: the very small, where cell atlases are describing tissues cell by cell; the individual, where imaging gives each patient their own map; and the overlooked, where variation by sex, age and ancestry is finally being documented properly.

For everyday life, a little anatomy goes a long way. Knowing roughly where your organs are, which muscles oppose which, and how joints trade stability for movement helps you describe symptoms to a doctor, understand an injury, and judge exercise advice. The next file turns the map into a working machine.

The core idea

Anatomy describes the body's structure from a standard anatomical position, at levels from tissues to organ systems. Bones are living levers, muscles only pull, the body is wrapped around a tube, and many details are evolutionary compromises, not designs.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 206 bones in an adult; roughly 270–300 at birth.
  • More than 600 skeletal muscles.
  • Brain about 1.3–1.4 kg; skin about 2 square metres.
The common mistake

Thinking everyone's insides match the textbook picture. Variation is normal, and surgeons plan for it.

Test yourself

Can you explain why the shoulder dislocates more often than the hip? If so, you understand the trade-off between mobility and stability.
