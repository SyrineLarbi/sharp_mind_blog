Sharp Mind · Biology · Topic 01 of 20

Cell biology. A city smaller than a speck of dust, running every second of your life.

Every living thing you have ever seen is made of cells, or is one. A cell is the smallest unit that can take in fuel, build its own parts, respond to its surroundings and copy itself. Inside, it is not a bag of jelly. It is crowded, organised and busy, with compartments that do different jobs, a skeleton that moves, motors that walk along tracks, and a strict schedule for when to divide and when to die. Once you understand one cell, most of medicine starts to make sense, because nearly every disease is, in the end, cells doing the wrong thing.

Question 1 · What is it?

The study of the smallest thing that is alive

Cell biology asks what a cell is made of, how its parts work together, and how cells become tissues, organs and whole bodies. It started with a microscope and a piece of cork.

Chapter 01

A slice of cork and a rule with no exceptions

In 1665 the English scientist Robert Hooke published a book of drawings made through an early microscope. One showed a thin slice of cork. It was full of tiny empty boxes, and they reminded him of the small rooms monks lived in, so he called them cells. What he saw were only the dead walls of plant cells. A few years later the Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, grinding his own superb lenses, saw living single-celled creatures swimming in pond water and scraped from his own teeth. He called them "little animals".

It took nearly two centuries to turn those observations into a theory. In the late 1830s the German scientists Matthias Schleiden (working on plants) and Theodor Schwann (working on animals) argued that all living things are built from cells. In 1855 the physician Rudolf Virchow added the piece that made it powerful: every cell comes from an existing cell, by division. Together these ideas are called cell theory, and they still hold. No cell has ever been seen to arise from non-living matter today. Every cell in your body descends, by an unbroken chain of divisions, from a single fertilised egg, and that egg descends from cells in your parents, and so on back about four billion years.

That is the first thing to understand about cell biology. It is not just the study of small things. It is the study of the one unit that all life shares, and the only route by which life continues.

Chapter 02

Two basic designs

All cells share some basics: a membrane around the outside, DNA holding instructions, and ribosomes, the small machines that build proteins. Beyond that, life comes in two very different designs.

The word prokaryote comes from Greek for "before the kernel", meaning before the nucleus. Eukaryote means "true kernel". A micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre. So a typical human cell is ten to a hundred times wider than a typical bacterium, and its volume can be thousands of times larger. Bacteria get their own file in this category (Bacteria). This one is mostly about the eukaryotic cell, the kind you are made of.

Chapter 03

How small, and how many

A careful estimate published in 2016 put the number of cells in an average adult human body at about 30 trillion. Most of them, by count, are red blood cells, which are small and extremely numerous. By mass, muscle and fat cells dominate. Biologists traditionally say there are a few hundred named cell types: nerve cells over a metre long, fat cells that are mostly one big droplet, sperm cells with a tail, and red blood cells that throw out their own nucleus to make room for more oxygen-carrying protein.

The scale of things, roughly (each step is about ten times smaller)
  1 mm      a grain of salt; a human egg cell is ~0.1 mm, just visible

The scale explains why it took so long to understand cells. An ordinary light microscope cannot show two objects as separate if they are closer than about 200 nanometres, because of the way light waves spread. Most of the cell's machinery is smaller than the smallest thing light can show. Seeing it needed electron microscopes, from the 1930s onward, and later new tricks, covered in Chapter 13.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A walled city with a power plant, a factory and a post office

The usual comparison is a city, and it is a good one, as long as you remember that nobody is in charge. Every part works by chemistry, and the order emerges from millions of molecules each doing one simple thing.

Chapter 04

The membrane: a wall with guarded gates

The cell membrane is a double layer of fatty molecules called phospholipids. Volume 27 explains how it assembles itself. What matters here is what it lets through. Small molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide slip straight across. Water crosses slowly on its own and quickly through special channel proteins. But charged particles, such as sodium and potassium, and large molecules, such as sugar, cannot cross the fatty layer at all without help.

That help comes from proteins set into the membrane, and they come in two main kinds. Channels are tunnels that open and let one kind of particle flow downhill, from where it is concentrated to where it is scarce. Pumps push particles uphill, against that flow, and they cost energy. The most famous is the sodium-potassium pump. It pushes sodium out of the cell and potassium in, all the time, and it uses a large share of the energy your body burns at rest. The result is a cell that is slightly charged, like a small battery. Nerve cells use that charge to send signals, which is why a thought is physically an electrical event.

The membrane also carries receptors, proteins that stick out of the surface and change shape when a particular molecule, such as a hormone, binds to them. That shape change sends a message inside. Most medicines work by fitting into one of these receptors or channels, like a key into a lock, which is why Volume 44 calls side effects unavoidable (Lock and key).

Chapter 05

The compartments and what each one does

A eukaryotic cell is divided into rooms called organelles, "little organs", each wrapped in its own membrane. Here is the main cast and what they do.

Follow one protein to see the system work. Insulin is made in certain cells of the pancreas. The gene is copied in the nucleus. The copy travels out to a ribosome on the rough ER, which builds the protein chain straight into the ER, where it folds. Small bubbles of membrane carry it to the Golgi, where it is trimmed into its active form and packed into storage bubbles. When blood sugar rises, those bubbles merge with the outer membrane and release insulin into the blood. The whole route, from gene to release, is a production line with quality control at each step. Misfolded proteins are caught and sent for destruction.

Breaking down is as important as building. In 2016 the Japanese biologist Yoshinori Ohsumi won the Nobel Prize for showing how cells digest their own worn parts, a process called autophagy, "self-eating". The cell wraps old material in a membrane and delivers it to a lysosome to be recycled. When autophagy fails, damaged parts pile up, and that has been linked to several diseases of the nervous system.

Chapter 06

Energy: one currency for everything

Every job in the cell, pumping, building, moving, is paid for in the same currency. It is a small molecule called ATP (adenosine triphosphate). ATP carries three phosphate groups. Snapping off the last one releases a useful burst of energy, and the leftover molecule is then recharged. Each ATP is used and recharged many times a day. A common textbook estimate is that you turn over roughly your own body weight in ATP every day, while holding only a few tens of grams at any moment. ATP is not stored energy; it is spending money, constantly earned and spent.

Most of it is earned in the mitochondria. Food is broken into small pieces, and their electrons are passed along a chain of proteins in the mitochondrion's folded inner membrane. As the electrons pass, the chain pumps protons (hydrogen ions) to one side of the membrane, building up pressure, like water behind a dam. The protons flow back through a rotating protein machine, ATP synthase, which turns as they pass and makes ATP. Oxygen waits at the end of the chain to collect the spent electrons, forming water. That is why you breathe: oxygen is the final collector at the end of your cells' power line. Cyanide kills by blocking that chain.

The idea that mitochondria were once free-living bacteria, the endosymbiotic theory, was championed by the American biologist Lynn Margulis from 1967, against strong resistance. The evidence is now overwhelming. Mitochondria have their own small circle of DNA (in humans 37 genes), a double membrane, ribosomes that look like bacterial ones, and they divide by splitting, as bacteria do. You inherit them almost entirely from your mother, because the egg supplies them and the sperm's are destroyed.

Chapter 07

A skeleton that moves, and motors that walk

A cell is not a soft balloon. It has an inner scaffold called the cytoskeleton, made of protein fibres. Some are cables that give shape and let the cell crawl. Others are hollow tubes, microtubules, that run out from the centre like the spokes of a wheel.

Microtubules are roads. Motor proteins such as kinesin carry cargo along them, taking one step at a time, each step paid with one ATP. Seen in animations, kinesin looks startlingly like a tiny two-legged walker carrying a sack. In a nerve cell that is a metre long, running from your spine to your foot, these motors are the only way to deliver new parts to the far end. When transport fails there, the far end of the nerve suffers first.

The cytoskeleton is also how cells move. White blood cells crawl through tissue toward an infection by building cables at their front edge and pulling at the back. Sperm swim with a whip driven by microtubules. And when a cell divides, microtubules form the machine that pulls the two sets of chromosomes apart.

Chapter 08

Division: copy everything, then split, with checks at every stage

To become two, a cell must copy its whole genome, about three billion letters per set in a human, and share the copies exactly. This happens in a fixed order called the cell cycle: grow, copy the DNA, grow again, then divide. The division of the nucleus is called mitosis.

   chromosome attached?  <-----  CHECKPOINT: was copying complete?

The checkpoints are the important part. Proteins act as inspectors and stop the cycle if something is wrong. The best known is a protein called p53. When it detects damaged DNA, it pauses the cycle for repair, and if repair is impossible it tells the cell to kill itself. p53 is so important that it is often called "the guardian of the genome", and its gene is damaged in roughly half of all human cancers. Cancer is, at the level of the cell, a failure of these brakes and inspectors: the cell keeps dividing when it should stop.

There is a second kind of division, meiosis, used only to make eggs and sperm. It halves the number of chromosomes and shuffles the parents' versions, so each egg or sperm is unique. The details belong to Genetics.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Every disease, every drug and every body starts here

Doctors treat organs. But the problem is almost always inside cells, and so is the solution.

Chapter 09

One broken gate: cystic fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis is an inherited disease that fills the lungs with thick, sticky mucus and causes repeated infections. For most of history, children with it died young. In 1989 researchers found the cause: a faulty gene for one membrane protein, a channel called CFTR that lets chloride move out of cells lining the lungs and gut. When the channel is missing or stuck shut, salt and water stop moving properly, and the mucus becomes thick.

That is cell biology turned into medicine. In the most common mutation, the protein is made but misfolds, and the cell's quality control destroys it before it reaches the membrane. Drugs called CFTR modulators help the faulty protein fold and reach the surface, and then help it open. A three-drug combination approved in the United States in 2019 works for most patients, and it has greatly improved lung function and life expectancy for many of them. Knowing exactly which part of which cell has failed is what made a targeted drug possible.

Chapter 10

The woman whose cells never died

In 1951 Henrietta Lacks, a 31-year-old Black woman in Baltimore, was treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins Hospital. A sample of her tumour was taken without her knowledge, which was common practice then. Most human cells die after a few weeks in a dish. Hers did not. They kept dividing, and they have been dividing in laboratories around the world ever since. They are called HeLa cells, from the first letters of her names.

HeLa cells became the workhorse of cell biology. They were used to test the polio vaccine in the 1950s and in countless studies of cancer, viruses and drugs since. Henrietta Lacks died the same year, and her family did not learn for decades that her cells were being used. The case became a landmark in debates about consent and who benefits from tissue taken from patients. Rules have tightened since, and in 2023 her family reached a settlement with one company that had sold the cells. Today most research using human tissue requires informed consent, although rules differ by country.

Why did her cells not stop? Ordinary human cells can divide only a limited number of times, often around 40 to 60, before they stop for good. This is the Hayflick limit, named after Leonard Hayflick, who described it in the early 1960s. One reason is that the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes, telomeres, get shorter with each division. Her cancer cells had switched on an enzyme that rebuilds those caps, along with other changes, so they escaped the limit.

Chapter 11

Death on purpose

Your fingers were once joined by webbing, like a duck's foot. In the womb, the cells between them killed themselves on schedule, and the fingers separated. This tidy, planned suicide is called apoptosis. The cell shrinks, chops up its own DNA, and breaks into neat packets that neighbours eat. Nothing spills, so there is no inflammation.

Much of what we know came from a tiny worm, C. elegans, in which researchers could follow every single cell as the animal developed. Exactly the same cells die in every worm. Sydney Brenner, Robert Horvitz and John Sulston shared the 2002 Nobel Prize for this work. Planned cell death is not a failure. It is how bodies are sculpted and how damaged cells are removed before they become dangerous. Too little of it helps cancers survive. Too much contributes to some degenerative diseases.

Put Chapters 08 to 11 together and a simple picture appears. A healthy body is a balance between cells that divide, cells that repair, and cells that die when told. Most of medicine is about restoring that balance when it tips.

Chapter 13

How we look, and what each method misses

Strengths of the toolkit

Electron microscopes show the cell's parts down to a few nanometres. Since the 2010s, cryo-electron microscopy, which images flash-frozen samples, has shown individual protein machines in near-atomic detail (Nobel Prize 2017). Super-resolution light microscopes beat the 200-nanometre limit using glowing tags that switch on and off (Nobel Prize 2014). Glowing proteins borrowed from jellyfish let researchers watch chosen proteins move inside living cells.

Weaknesses

Electron microscopes need dead, fixed cells, so they show a snapshot, not a movie. Cells in a plastic dish, often cancer lines like HeLa, behave differently from cells in a body: flat instead of three-dimensional, without neighbours, blood supply or immune cells. Many drugs that work beautifully in dishes and mice fail in people. Studies of "average" cells also hide the fact that two neighbouring cells can behave quite differently.

Researchers are attacking these weaknesses. Organoids are small three-dimensional clumps grown from stem cells that partly copy a real organ, such as a mini-gut or a mini-kidney. Single-cell sequencing reads which genes are active in thousands of individual cells at once, instead of a blended average. Neither is a full substitute for a living body, and both are still young.

Chapter 15

The deeper shift: from parts list to behaviour

For most of the twentieth century, cell biology was about naming the parts. Today the parts list is largely known. The hard questions now are about behaviour. How does a cell decide to divide, to move or to die? How do thousands of signals combine into one decision? Why do two genetically identical cells next to each other behave differently?

Answering those questions needs data from millions of single cells over time, and that is where automated microscopes, sequencing and machine learning are converging. The likely future of cell biology is less about discovering new organelles and more about predicting what a cell will do. If that works, drugs could be tested on detailed models of a patient's own cells before anyone takes them. That goal is real and widely shared. How soon it arrives is not known.

For a curious non-specialist, the useful takeaway is simpler. When you hear about a new treatment, ask two questions. Which cells does it act on, and what inside them does it change? Those two questions will make most health news far easier to judge.

The core idea

A cell is the smallest living unit. It is a membrane-wrapped city of compartments, powered by ATP made mostly in mitochondria, that builds proteins from instructions in its DNA, and that divides, repairs or kills itself under strict checkpoints. Most disease is a failure of one of those jobs.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 30 trillion cells in an adult human (2016 estimate), most of them red blood cells by count.
  • A typical human cell is about 10–100 micrometres across; a bacterium about 1–5.
  • Ordinary human cells divide roughly 40–60 times before stopping (the Hayflick limit).
The common mistake

Picturing a cell as a simple blob of jelly with a nucleus in the middle. It is packed, structured and constantly moving, with motors, tracks, factories and waste systems.
