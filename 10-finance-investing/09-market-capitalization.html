Market capitalization. The price tag the stock market puts on a whole company, every second of the day.

A share price on its own tells you almost nothing about how big a company is. Multiply it by the number of shares, though, and you get the market's current price for the entire business. That number, market capitalization, decides which companies count as giants, how most stock indexes are built, and therefore where trillions of dollars of savings flow. It is the most quoted figure in finance, and one of the most misunderstood.

Question 1 · What is it?

Price times shares: the market's price for the whole business

Market capitalization, usually shortened to "market cap", is what it would cost, at today's share price, to buy every share a company has issued.

Chapter 01

The expensive share that belongs to a smaller company

Company A's shares cost 500 dollars each. Company B's cost 50. Which company is bigger? Most people instinctively say A. The honest answer is: you cannot tell yet. If A has 100 million shares, the whole company is valued at 50 billion dollars. If B has 10 billion shares, it is valued at 500 billion, ten times more.

A real example makes the point. Berkshire Hathaway, Warren Buffett's company, has never split its original "class A" shares, so a single one has cost hundreds of thousands of dollars in recent years. Yet several companies whose shares cost a few hundred dollars are worth far more in total. A share price is the price of a slice. Market cap is the price of the whole cake.

The formula is simple:

The one formula
  market capitalization  =  share price  x  number of shares outstanding

  Company A:   $500  x     100 million  =   $50 billion
  Company B:    $50  x  10,000 million  =  $500 billion

Shares outstanding means all the shares currently held by investors, including company insiders, but not shares the company has bought back and holds itself. The figure changes every time the share price moves, so a large company's market cap can rise or fall by tens of billions of dollars in a single trading day.

Chapter 02

The size labels, and why they are fuzzy

Investors sort companies into size bands. There is no official rulebook, and each index provider draws the lines slightly differently, but a common rough guide in US dollars looks like this.

LabelRough rangeTypical character
Mega-capAbove about $200 billionHousehold names, global reach, heavily researched
Large-capAbout $10 billion to $200 billionEstablished leaders in their industries
Mid-capAbout $2 billion to $10 billionGrowing firms, often expanding into new markets
Small-capAbout $300 million to $2 billionYounger or niche companies; more volatile
Micro-capBelow about $300 millionThinly traded, little research, higher risk

The bands drift upward over time as markets grow, so a "large" company in 1990 might be a mid-cap by today's standards. The labels describe a company's position in the market, not its quality. A small-cap can be an excellent business and a mega-cap a fading one.

Question 2 · How does it work?

What moves the number, and what the number moves

Market cap changes with the share price and the share count. In turn it decides how much weight each company gets in most indexes, and so how much money flows into it.

Chapter 03

What changes market cap, and what only looks as if it does

The main driver is the share price, which reflects what investors collectively expect the company to earn in the future and how risky they think those earnings are. Good results, a new product or falling interest rates can raise it. A scandal, a lost contract or rising rates can cut it.

Some corporate actions change the share count. Here people often get confused.

EventWhat happens to sharesEffect on market cap
Stock split (say 4-for-1)Four times as many shares, each at a quarter of the priceNone by itself. The cake is cut into more slices.
Share buybackThe company uses cash to buy and cancel its own sharesFewer shares, but the company also has less cash. Each remaining share owns a bigger slice.
New share issueThe company sells new shares for cashMore shares and more cash. Existing owners are "diluted": each owns a smaller slice.
Dividend paidCash leaves the company for shareholdersThe price typically drops by about the dividend on the day the share stops carrying it.

None of these mechanical events creates value on its own. They rearrange who owns what. Value is created or destroyed by what the business actually earns. Buybacks and dividends are covered in more depth in Dividends.

Chapter 04

Market cap versus enterprise value

Market cap is the price of the shares, not the price of the business. Imagine buying a house worth 300,000 that comes with a 200,000 mortgage you must take over. The seller asks only 100,000 for the keys, but you are really paying 300,000. Companies work the same way. If you bought every share, you would also inherit the company's debts, and its cash.

So analysts use enterprise value (EV): market cap plus debt, minus cash. A company with a market cap of 100 billion, debts of 30 billion and cash of 10 billion has an enterprise value of 120 billion. Two companies with the same market cap can have very different enterprise values, and the one with more debt is the more expensive to own outright. Enterprise value is the starting point for many of the ratios in Company valuation.

Chapter 05

Cap-weighted indexes: the bigger you are, the more you count

A stock index is a list of companies whose combined price is tracked as one number. The best-known ones, such as the S&P 500 in the US or the MSCI World index of developed markets, are capitalization-weighted. Each company's influence on the index is proportional to its market cap. If a company makes up 7% of the total value of all companies in the index, it gets 7% of the weight. Index funds that track the index buy shares in those same proportions.

Most major indexes also adjust for free float: they count only shares that are actually available to trade, leaving out large blocks held by founders, governments or parent companies. A company whose government owns 80% of the shares counts for much less than its full market cap suggests.

Cap weighting has a quiet elegance. When prices move, the weights move with them automatically, so a cap-weighted fund never needs to trade just because prices changed. It trades only when companies enter or leave the index or issue and cancel shares. That keeps costs very low. It also means that a cap-weighted index fund holds exactly the market, which is the portfolio that, on average, investors as a whole must hold. That is the logic behind Index funds.

Chapter 06

Joining the club: what index inclusion does

Because so much money tracks the big indexes, being added to one matters. When a company joins the S&P 500, every fund that tracks the index has to buy its shares, usually all on the same day. In December 2020 Tesla joined the S&P 500 as one of the largest companies ever added at that point. Its shares rose sharply between the announcement and the inclusion date, as investors anticipated the forced buying.

Researchers have long studied this "index effect". Earlier decades showed noticeable price jumps on inclusion. More recent studies suggest the effect has shrunk as traders learned to anticipate it. Either way, inclusion brings lasting benefits: more attention from analysts, easier trading, and a broader base of owners. Note that the S&P 500 is not a purely mechanical list of the 500 largest US firms. A committee chooses members, using rules that include size, trading activity and a record of profits.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where the world's savings go

Because indexes follow market cap, so does a large share of global investment. That makes the distribution of market cap a question for every saver, not just for traders.

Chapter 07

The age of the giants

In August 2018 Apple became the first US company to be valued at one trillion dollars. Within a few years several American technology companies passed that mark, and in 2025 the chipmaker Nvidia became the first company to pass four trillion. A small group of US technology firms, nicknamed the "Magnificent Seven" (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia and Tesla), came to dominate the market.

The consequence for ordinary savers is concentration. By 2024 and 2025 the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 made up well over a third of the whole index, a level not seen in decades. Someone who believes they own "the 500 biggest US companies" through an index fund actually has a large part of their money riding on a handful of related technology businesses. A cap-weighted index is diversified by count but can be concentrated by value.

The same pattern shows up between countries. In the mid-2020s US companies made up roughly seven-tenths of the MSCI World developed-markets index. A "global" index fund is therefore, in large part, a bet on American companies, and on the US dollar.

Chapter 08

Japan 1989: when the biggest was not the safest

Concentration has happened before, and history offers a warning. At the end of 1989 Japanese companies made up a larger share of the world's stock-market value than American ones. Japanese banks and industrial groups filled the list of the world's largest firms. A global investor who weighted by market cap had a huge bet on Japan.

Then the Japanese bubble burst. The Nikkei 225 index fell by more than half over the following years and did not regain its 1989 peak until early 2024, more than three decades later. The lesson is not that today's giants will repeat this. Their profits are real and very large. It is that market cap records what investors currently believe, and investors can collectively be wrong for a very long time. Volume 38 describes how such beliefs build up (Bubbles, and why they keep happening), and the wider story of crashes is in Financial crises.

Chapter 09

The size effect: do small companies earn more?

In the early 1990s the economists Eugene Fama and Kenneth French published influential research on US shares. Among other findings, they showed that, historically, small companies had earned higher average returns than large ones. They proposed "size" as one of a few factors that explain differences in returns, alongside how cheap a share is relative to its accounts (value).

The size effect is now one of the most debated results in finance. In many periods since it was published, small-caps have not outperformed, and some researchers argue the effect was weaker than it first looked, or is mostly found among very small, hard-to-trade companies. Others think it survives in some forms, for example among small companies that are also profitable. The honest summary is that small companies are riskier, and whether investors are reliably paid extra for that risk is contested. The general link between risk and reward is in Risk and return.

Chapter 10

A company is not a country

Headlines often say a company is "worth more than" some country's economy, comparing a market cap with a gross domestic product (GDP). This compares two different kinds of number. GDP is a flow: the value of goods and services a country produces in one year. Market cap is a stock: the price today of all the company's future profits, for as long as it exists. It is like comparing a person's yearly salary with the price of a house. The comparison makes a vivid headline and means very little. GDP itself is explained in GDP.

A related idea is more useful: the total market cap of a country's listed companies divided by its GDP. Warren Buffett once described this ratio as perhaps the best single measure of where valuations stand. When it is very high by historical standards, as it was in the US in the mid-2020s, it suggests shares are expensive relative to the economy. It is a rough gauge, though, because many large companies now earn much of their profit abroad.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A useful measure with real blind spots

Market cap is fast, objective and universal. It is also only an opinion, and weighting by it has costs.

Chapter 11

Weighting by size, and the alternatives

MethodStrengthsWeaknesses
Cap weightingMirrors the market; very little trading; lowest cost; can hold huge sums easilyPuts most money in whatever has already risen most; can become concentrated in a few firms or one country
Equal weightingEvery company counts the same; less concentration; more exposure to smaller firmsNeeds regular rebalancing, so higher costs; harder to run with very large sums
Fundamental weightingWeights by sales, profits or dividends, not price, so it buys less of whatever looks expensiveMore trading and higher fees; long periods of lagging when expensive shares keep rising

Beyond index design, market cap as a measure has its own strengths and weaknesses.

What it does well

It is objective and updated constantly. It gathers the views of millions of investors into one number. It makes companies in different countries and industries comparable. And it is the honest answer to "what would it cost to buy this business in the market today?"

Where it misleads

It ignores debt, so it can make indebted companies look cheap. It can swing on mood rather than results. It says nothing about whether a company is a good investment at its price. And for thinly traded small firms, the last trade may not reflect what a large buyer would actually pay.

Chapter 13

How giants shrink

In March 2000, at the peak of the dot-com bubble, the network-equipment maker Cisco was briefly the most valuable company in the world. Its business kept growing for years afterwards, but its share price fell steeply when the bubble burst and stayed far below that peak for decades. General Electric, once the world's most valuable company, shrank and eventually split itself into separate firms in the 2020s.

These stories show the two ways a giant shrinks. Sometimes the business weakens. Just as often the business stays sound, but the price investors paid assumed growth that could never be delivered. A high market cap is a forecast built into a price; when the forecast is too optimistic, the cap falls even if the company keeps doing well. That is why the next two topics, Financial statements and Company valuation, ask what a company actually earns and what that should be worth.

The core idea

Market cap is share price times shares outstanding: the market's current price for all of a company's shares. Most indexes are cap-weighted, so the biggest companies attract the most money. It is a measure of belief, not of quality, and it ignores debt, which enterprise value adds back.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Apple became the first US company worth $1 trillion in 2018; Nvidia passed $4 trillion in 2025.
  • The ten largest S&P 500 companies made up well over a third of the index by the mid-2020s.
  • Japan's Nikkei took until 2024 to regain its 1989 peak.
The common mistake

Thinking a high share price means a big or expensive company. Price per share tells you nothing on its own; you need the share count, and to judge "expensive" you need profits.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a 4-for-1 stock split leaves market cap unchanged, and why two firms with the same market cap can cost very different amounts to buy outright? Then you understand shares, slices and enterprise value.

The words, in plain words
Market capitalization
Share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding.
Shares outstanding
All the shares held by investors, not counting shares the company holds itself.
Free float
The shares actually available to trade, excluding locked-up blocks held by founders, states or parent firms.
Mega-, large-, mid-, small-, micro-cap
Rough size bands, drawn differently by different index providers.
Enterprise value
Market cap plus debt minus cash: the cost of buying the whole business, debts included.
Stock split
Dividing each share into several cheaper ones, without changing the total value.
Share buyback
A company buying its own shares from the market, usually to cancel them.
Dilution
The shrinking of each owner's slice when a company issues new shares.
Cap-weighted index
An index where each company counts in proportion to its market cap.
Equal-weighted index
An index where every company counts the same, whatever its size.
Index effect
The price movement around a company being added to or removed from a major index.
Size premium
The contested idea that small companies earn higher returns over time than large ones.
