Dividends. The moment a company stops promising and actually hands its owners some cash.

A share is a claim on a company's profits. Most of the time that claim is abstract: the profits stay inside the business and the owner hopes the share price will reflect them. A dividend is the exception, a payment in cash, straight into the shareholder's account. Dividends have funded retirements for generations, and reinvested dividends explain a surprisingly large part of what long-term investors have earned. They are also widely misunderstood, because a dividend is not free money. It is your own money, moved from the company's pocket to yours.

Question 1 · What is it?

A share of the profits, paid in cash

A dividend is a payment a company makes to its shareholders, usually in cash and usually out of profits, decided by its board of directors.

Chapter 01

The baker's choice

Imagine you own a share of a successful bakery. At the end of a good year it has 100,000 of profit. The owners face a choice. They can keep the money in the business, perhaps to open a second shop. Or they can pay it out to themselves. Or a bit of each. If they pay out 40,000, that is a dividend, and each owner receives a slice in proportion to how much of the bakery they own.

Listed companies make exactly the same choice, just with millions of owners. Their board of directors decides how much to pay, and the payment is quoted per share: "a dividend of 1.20 per share". If you own 500 shares, you receive 600, possibly minus tax.

The key idea is that a dividend does not create wealth; it moves wealth. Before the payment, the cash belonged to the company, which you partly own. After it, the cash is in your account and the company is worth correspondingly less. Whether that move is a good idea depends on whether the company or the shareholder can put the money to better use.

Chapter 02

Who pays, and who does not

Dividends are typical of mature companies: utilities, banks, oil producers, food and tobacco firms, telecoms. They earn steady profits and have fewer opportunities to reinvest all of them at high returns. Young, fast-growing companies usually pay nothing, because every unit of profit is more valuable inside the business, funding growth.

Some famous companies have refused to pay for decades. Berkshire Hathaway, run by Warren Buffett, has argued that it can reinvest the money better than its shareholders could. Apple paid no dividend for many years before restarting payments in 2012. Meta (Facebook's parent) and Alphabet (Google's parent) announced their first-ever dividends in 2024, a sign that they now saw themselves as mature cash machines rather than start-ups. A first dividend is often a quiet announcement that a company's fastest-growing years are behind it.

Habits also differ by country. In the UK and parts of Europe, dividends have traditionally been a larger part of shareholder returns than in the US, where companies use buybacks more heavily. Those are discussed in Chapter 05.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Dates, yields, ratios, and the buyback alternative

Dividends follow a fixed calendar, are measured with two simple ratios, and compete with a second way of returning cash: buying back shares.

Chapter 03

The four dates, and the price drop

Every dividend has a timetable. The names vary slightly between countries, but the sequence is the same.

DateWhat happens
Declaration dateThe board announces the amount and the other dates.
Ex-dividend dateFrom this day, buyers of the share no longer receive the coming dividend. To get it, you must own the share before this date.
Record dateThe company checks its register to see who the shareholders are. It is usually close to the ex-dividend date.
Payment dateThe cash arrives in shareholders' accounts, often a few weeks later.

On the ex-dividend date the share price usually drops by roughly the amount of the dividend, other news aside. This puzzles beginners, but it is logical. Someone buying the share the day before gets the coming dividend; someone buying the day after does not, so they pay less. This is why buying a share just to "catch" a dividend does not produce free money: what you gain in cash, you lose in share price, and you may owe tax on the cash.

Payment frequency varies. US companies typically pay quarterly. Many European and UK companies pay twice a year, often a smaller "interim" dividend and a larger "final" one. Some pay once a year.

Chapter 04

Two ratios: yield and payout

The two numbers everyone quotes
  dividend yield  =  dividends per share over a year  /  share price

  payout ratio    =  dividends per share  /  earnings per share

  Example: share price 50, yearly dividend 2, earnings per share 4
     yield  = 2 / 50 = 4%
     payout = 2 / 4  = 50%   (half the profit paid out, half kept)

The dividend yield tells you how much cash income you get for each unit invested, at today's price. The payout ratio tells you how much of the company's profit the dividend uses up. A payout of 50% leaves room to keep paying in a bad year. A payout of 110% means the company is paying out more than it earns, using savings or borrowing, which cannot last.

A very high yield is often a warning rather than a bargain. Because yield is dividend divided by price, a collapsing share price makes the yield jump. The market may be signalling that it expects the dividend to be cut. This is the yield trap: buying a share for its 10% yield just before the dividend is halved and the price falls further. A yield far above that of similar companies is the market asking a question; find out what it is before buying.

Chapter 05

Buybacks: the other way to hand back cash

A company that wants to return cash has a second option. It can use the money to buy its own shares on the market and cancel them. This is a share buyback (or repurchase). No cash lands in your account, but you now own a larger slice of a company with fewer shares, so each share is entitled to more of the future profit.

In 1961 the economists Merton Miller and Franco Modigliani showed that, in an idealised world with no taxes or trading costs, how a company pays out cash should not change what it is worth. A shareholder who wants income from a company that pays no dividend can sell a few shares and create a "home-made dividend". A shareholder who receives an unwanted dividend can reinvest it. Either way the total wealth is the same.

In the real world taxes and habits break this symmetry. In many countries dividends are taxed when received, while buybacks create gains that are taxed only when the shareholder sells, and that may be years later or at a lower rate. Buybacks are also more flexible, since a company can stop them without the shock of a dividend cut. In the US, buybacks by large companies have exceeded dividends in many recent years. They are also politically controversial: critics argue they can flatter earnings per share and serve executives paid in shares. Since 2023 the US has charged a 1% tax on buybacks by listed companies.

Chapter 06

Special dividends, scrip and reinvestment plans

Not every dividend is a regular cash payment. A special dividend is a one-off payment, often after selling a business or a year of exceptional profits. Companies label it "special" precisely so that shareholders do not expect it again.

A scrip dividend offers new shares instead of cash. The company keeps its cash; the shareholder gets more shares, but so does everyone else who takes the option, so the share count grows. A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) automatically uses your cash dividends to buy more shares of the same company, often at low or no cost. Most accumulation-type index funds do the same thing internally: instead of paying out dividends, they reinvest them, which is why the fund's price rises a little faster than the index it tracks.

Chapter 07

Smoothing: why boards hate cutting

In 1956 the economist John Lintner interviewed managers of US companies about how they set dividends. He found that they did not simply pay a fixed share of each year's profit. They had a long-term target payout and moved towards it slowly. They raised dividends only when they were confident the higher level could be maintained, and they were very reluctant to cut. Later research has broadly supported this picture.

The result is that dividends are much smoother than profits. A company may see earnings swing by 30% in a year while its dividend rises a few per cent, steadily. Some companies make a long record of increases part of their identity. In the US, members of the S&P 500 that have raised their dividend for at least 25 consecutive years are grouped as the "Dividend Aristocrats". Because boards smooth dividends, a change in the dividend tells investors what management really thinks about the future.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Long-run returns, honest signals, and real incomes

Dividends matter because of compounding, because of what they reveal, and because millions of people live on them.

Chapter 08

The quiet engine of long-run returns

Stock indexes are usually quoted as price indexes: they track prices only, and ignore dividends. A total return index assumes every dividend is reinvested. Over a few months the two barely differ. Over decades, the gap becomes enormous.

Consider a dividend yield of 3% a year, reinvested and compounding. Over 30 years that alone multiplies your holding by about 2.4 (1.03 multiplied by itself thirty times), before any rise in the share price at all. Historical studies of the US and UK markets have found that reinvested dividends account for a large share, in many long periods the majority, of the total real return of shares. The exact proportion depends on the country and the years chosen.

An investor who spends every dividend and one who reinvests them can own the same shares and end up with wildly different wealth. This is compounding at work, as Volume 38 explains (Interest, and the astonishing effect of compounding). It is also why comparing a fund's performance with a price index, rather than a total return index, makes the fund look better than it is.

Chapter 09

What a dividend says

Accounts can be shaped by judgement, as Financial statements explains. Cash paid out is harder to fake. Paying a dividend year after year requires real cash. So investors read dividend decisions as signals. A rise suggests management is confident. A cut is often read as an admission that things are worse than they look, and share prices frequently fall sharply on the news.

Signals matter most in a crisis. During the 2008 financial crisis many banks cut or suspended their dividends. In March 2020, at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, the Bank of England's regulators asked UK banks to suspend dividends, and the European Central Bank made a similar recommendation to eurozone banks, so that banks would keep their capital to absorb losses and keep lending. Those cases show the other side of dividends: cash paid to shareholders is cash no longer available as a cushion. The role of bank capital is covered in Financial crises.

Chapter 10

Income for real lives, and the tax question

For many retirees, charities and pension funds, dividends are the income that pays the bills. A portfolio of mature companies paying steady dividends can provide cash without selling any shares, which many people find psychologically easier than selling a little each year, even when the economics are similar. University endowments and foundations often spend part of their dividend income each year.

Tax shapes the picture strongly, and it varies by country. Some countries tax dividends at lower rates than wages; some give credits for tax the company already paid on its profits; many tax-sheltered retirement accounts ignore dividend tax altogether. Dividends from foreign companies often suffer a withholding tax deducted in the company's home country, sometimes reduced by a tax treaty. The details change often, so this is general education, not tax advice. The broader logic of how pension savings work is in Pensions.

Discipline and income, against flexibility and tax

Chapter 12

The dividend illusion

Many investors treat dividends as a kind of interest that comes on top of the share's value, and choose shares mainly for their yield. That habit has two costs. First, it confuses income with return: a share yielding 6% whose price falls 10% has lost money. Second, it concentrates portfolios. High-yield shares cluster in a few industries, such as banks, energy, utilities and tobacco, so a yield-hunting portfolio can end up badly undiversified, as Diversification explains.

The healthier view is simple. What matters is total return: dividends plus price change, after costs and taxes. Dividends are one way of receiving that return, valuable for income and as a signal, but not a gift. A company that pays nothing and reinvests well can make its owners just as rich.

Chapter 14

What will not change

Whatever the mix of dividends and buybacks, the underlying question stays the same: who can earn more with this cash, the company or its owners? When a company has good projects, keeping the cash is right. When it does not, handing it back is right. The worst outcome is a company that keeps cash it cannot use well, or one that pays out cash it does not really have.

For the individual investor the lessons are durable too. Reinvest dividends while you are building wealth, judge every investment by total return, distrust yields that look too good, and remember that the dividend that matters is the one that can be paid for decades, not the biggest one this year. The core idea

A dividend is a cash payment from profits to shareholders. It moves wealth rather than creating it: the share price drops by about the dividend. Boards smooth dividends, so changes act as signals, and reinvested dividends are a large part of long-run total return.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Yield = yearly dividend / price; payout ratio = dividend / earnings.
  • A 3% yield reinvested for 30 years multiplies a holding by about 2.4 on its own.
  • "Dividend Aristocrats": S&P 500 companies with 25+ years of consecutive increases.
The common mistake

Buying the highest yield available. An unusually high yield often means the market expects a cut, and income is not the same as return.

Test yourself

Can you explain why buying a share the day before it goes ex-dividend does not make you richer, and why a dividend cut can hit a share price harder than a fall in profits? If so, you understand the mechanics and the signal.
