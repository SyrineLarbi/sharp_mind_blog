Pensions. A promise made across forty years, and the argument about who carries the risk.

A pension is a way of moving money from the years when you can work to the years when you cannot. That sounds simple. It is one of the hardest problems in finance, because nobody knows how long they will live, what investments will return, or what prices will be in four decades. Every pension system is an answer to one question: who bears those unknowns, the worker, the employer, or the state? Over the past half-century the answer has quietly shifted, and most people have not noticed that the risk moved onto them.

Question 1 · What is it?

Income for the years after work

A pension is a regular income in old age, paid for by contributions or taxes during working life. Most countries combine several kinds.

Chapter 01

Bismarck's promise

For most of history there was no retirement. People worked until they could not, and then depended on their children, their savings or charity. Soldiers and some officials received pensions, but ordinary workers did not.

That changed in Germany in 1889, when the government of Otto von Bismarck created the first national old-age insurance for workers. Workers, employers and the state all contributed, and the pension was paid from age 70. Bismarck's motives were partly political: he hoped social insurance would take support away from the socialist movement. The idea spread. Britain introduced a tax-funded old-age pension in 1908, again from age 70, and the United States created Social Security in 1935, during the Great Depression.

Those early ages tell an important story. In 1889 few workers reached 70, and those who did usually did not live long after. Today, in rich countries, a typical 65-year-old can expect to live another twenty years or so. Pensions were designed for a short old age, and they now pay for a long one. Almost every pension debate since follows from that change.

Chapter 02

The three pillars

Pension experts, following a framework popularised by the World Bank in the 1990s, often describe retirement income as resting on several "pillars".

PillarWhat it isExamplesWho runs it
State pensionA basic income for all who qualify, usually paid from current taxes or social contributionsUS Social Security, the UK State Pension, France's general schemeGovernment
Workplace pensionA scheme run by or through an employer, often compulsory or automaticCompany schemes, public-sector schemes, US 401(k) plans, Australia's superannuationEmployers, industry funds, insurers
Personal savingsVoluntary saving, often with tax advantagesIndividual retirement accounts, personal pensions, ordinary investments, a paid-off homeIndividuals, through banks and fund firms

Countries weigh these very differently. In France and Italy, the state pension provides most of a typical retiree's income. In the Netherlands, Denmark and Australia, large workplace funds play a much bigger role. In the United States, Social Security gives a base and many people rely on workplace accounts on top. There is no single "pension system". There are national mixtures, shaped by history and politics.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Three ways to pay for old age

Pensions are paid for in one of three ways: by today's workers, by an employer's promise backed by a fund, or by your own savings pot. Each handles risk differently.

Chapter 03

Pay-as-you-go: today's workers pay today's pensioners

Most state pensions are pay-as-you-go. The contributions taken from today's workers' wages are not saved for them. They are paid out straight away to today's retirees. When today's workers retire, they will be paid by the workers of that time. It is less a savings account than a contract between generations.

The system works well when there are many workers for each pensioner. It strains when that ratio falls. Demographers measure this with the old-age dependency ratio, the number of people of pension age for every hundred people of working age. Across rich countries it has been rising for decades, because people live longer and families have fewer children. In Japan, Italy and Germany it is among the highest in the world. When the ratio rises, a pay-as-you-go system has only three choices: raise contributions, cut pensions, or raise the pension age. Every pension reform you read about is some mix of those three.

Chapter 04

Defined benefit: the employer's promise

For much of the twentieth century, a good job came with a defined benefit (DB) pension. The employer promises a pension based on a formula, typically a fraction of your salary for each year worked. Work forty years in a scheme paying one-sixtieth per year, and you retire on two-thirds of your final or average salary, for life, usually rising with inflation.

To keep that promise, the employer puts money into a fund, which invests it. If investments do badly, or members live longer than expected, the employer must add more. In a DB scheme, the employer carries the investment risk and the longevity risk. That is wonderful for members and expensive for employers. As life expectancy rose, interest rates fell and accounting rules made the cost visible, most private employers in the US and UK closed their DB schemes to new workers. Many public-sector workers, such as teachers, nurses and civil servants in many countries, still have them.

When a company with a DB scheme fails, the promise can fail with it. In 1963 the American carmaker Studebaker closed its main plant and thousands of workers lost most of their promised pensions. Episodes like that led to a 1974 US law, ERISA, that set standards for private pensions and created a government insurer for them. Britain set up a similar Pension Protection Fund in 2005.

Chapter 05

Defined contribution: your own pot

Today most new private-sector workers in the US, UK and many other countries are in defined contribution (DC) schemes. You and usually your employer pay a fixed share of your salary into an account in your name. The money is invested, mostly in funds of shares and bonds. At retirement you have whatever the pot has grown to. Nothing is promised.

The engine of a DC pot is compounding, explained in Volume 38. Because returns earn further returns, early contributions do far more work than late ones.

The same 100 a month, at 5% a year, depending on when you start (illustrative)
  start at 25, stop at 65   (40 years, 48,000 paid in)   ->  about 150,000
  start at 35, stop at 65   (30 years, 36,000 paid in)   ->  about  83,000
  start at 45, stop at 65   (20 years, 24,000 paid in)   ->  about  41,000

  the first ten years of saving produce more than the last twenty
  a 1% yearly fee over 40 years cuts the final pot by about a fifth

(Real returns vary from year to year, and these figures ignore inflation and taxes.) Two lessons follow. Starting early matters more than almost anything else, and fees matter far more than they look. A charge of one percent a year sounds tiny, but over a working life it can take a fifth or more of the pot. Low-cost index funds, described in Index funds, are one answer.

Chapter 06

Turning a pot into an income

A DC pot raises a problem DB schemes never had: how to spend it. You do not know how long you will live. Spend too fast and you run out at 85. Spend too slowly and you live poorer than you needed to. This is longevity risk.

One answer is an annuity: you hand the pot, or part of it, to an insurer, which pays you a fixed income for life. The insurer can do this because it pools thousands of people; those who die early effectively pay for those who live long. Annuities remove the risk of running out, but the income they offer depends heavily on interest rates when you buy, and many people dislike giving up control of their savings.

The other answer is drawdown: keep the pot invested and take money out as you go. A rule of thumb from American research in the 1990s, often called the "4 percent rule", suggests that withdrawing about 4 percent of the starting pot in the first year, then raising it with inflation, historically lasted around thirty years in most US market periods. It is a guide, not a guarantee. Drawdown carries sequence risk: a market crash in the first years of retirement does far more damage than the same crash later, because you are selling investments at low prices to live on. Many retirees combine the two approaches.

Chapter 07

The giant funds behind the promises

Pension funds are among the largest investors on Earth. Japan's Government Pension Investment Fund and Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, which despite its name is a national oil-wealth fund, each manage well over a trillion dollars. Large funds in the Netherlands, Canada, the United States and Australia own significant slices of the world's shares, bonds, property and infrastructure.

Their decisions move markets and shape companies, because they are often among the largest shareholders and can vote on how firms are run. A DB fund's main job is to match its investments to its future payments, which are long-term and often linked to inflation. Many therefore hold large amounts of long-dated government bonds. That choice was at the centre of a crisis in Britain in 2022, described in Chapter 09.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Old age, public budgets and financial stability

Pensions decide whether old people are poor. They are also one of the largest items in government spending, and pension funds are large enough to shake a bond market.

Chapter 08

The demographic squeeze

Before modern pensions, old age was strongly linked to poverty. In many rich countries today, people over 65 are no more likely to be poor than working-age adults, and in some they are less likely. Pensions are one of the great social achievements of the twentieth century.

The cost is large. Public pension spending in OECD countries averages roughly 8 percent of GDP, and well over 10 percent in France and Italy. As populations age, that cost rises unless rules change. In the United States, the trust funds that support Social Security are projected by the system's own trustees to run out in the mid-2030s. After that, incoming payroll taxes would still cover most, but not all, of promised benefits, roughly four-fifths, unless Congress acts. The question is not whether the system collapses, but who pays to close the gap: workers, retirees or taxpayers in general.

Raising the pension age is the most common answer, and the most unpopular. In 2023 France raised its minimum retirement age from 62 to 64 despite months of strikes and protests involving millions of people. Britain's state pension age is rising from 66 to 67 between 2026 and 2028. Several countries, such as Denmark, now link the pension age to life expectancy, so it rises automatically as people live longer.

Chapter 09

When the safe strategy nearly broke the bond market

Many British DB schemes used a strategy called liability-driven investment (LDI). Its aim was sensible: make the fund's assets rise and fall in step with its promised payments. To do it cheaply, many funds used borrowed money and derivatives linked to long-term government bonds, called gilts. As long as gilt prices moved slowly, it worked.

On 23 September 2022 the British government announced large unfunded tax cuts in a "mini-budget". Investors sold gilts and long-term yields rose faster than almost ever before. LDI funds faced sudden demands for collateral, and to raise cash they had to sell more gilts, which pushed prices down further. It was a spiral. On 28 September the Bank of England stepped in to buy long-dated gilts to stop it. A strategy meant to reduce risk had created a new one, because the borrowing hidden inside it turned a bond-market move into a fire sale. Regulators have since required LDI funds to hold much larger cash buffers.

Pensions have also been looted. In 1991, after the death of the British publisher Robert Maxwell, it emerged that hundreds of millions of pounds had been taken from his companies' pension funds. Thousands of retired workers faced losing their pensions. The scandal led to tighter laws on who controls pension money and how schemes are supervised.

Chapter 10

The nudge that worked

For years, millions of workers with access to a workplace pension did not join. They meant to, someday. Behavioural economists, notably Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi, argued that the problem was not reason but inertia: people stick with whatever happens if they do nothing, the default.

So change the default. From 2012 Britain required employers to enrol workers into a pension automatically, with the right to opt out. Participation among eligible private-sector employees rose from under half to well over four-fifths within a few years. Minimum contributions were raised in steps to 8 percent of qualifying earnings, shared between worker and employer. New Zealand, the United States (in many company plans) and others use similar designs. It is one of the clearest cases in public policy where changing what happens "by default" changed millions of lives. The ideas behind it are in Psychology · Behavioral economics. The remaining problem is that 8 percent is widely seen as too low for a comfortable retirement on its own.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every design moves risk to someone

No pension system removes the uncertainties of old age. Each one decides who carries them.

Chapter 12

The great risk transfer

Read the table by columns and a pattern appears. The move from DB to DC over the past forty years moved two large risks, poor investment returns and a long life, from employers onto individual workers. Some of this was fair: DB pensions mostly rewarded people who stayed with one employer for decades, and penalised those who moved, which modern careers rarely allow. DC is more portable, more transparent and less likely to leave a company crippled by old promises.

But individuals are badly placed to carry those risks. A company with ten thousand pensioners can average out who lives long and who does not. A single person cannot. And individuals make choices that economists consider poor: saving too little, picking funds badly, panicking in crashes. The shift put decisions that once belonged to actuaries into the hands of people who have never met one. Some countries now try to share risk again through "collective" DC schemes, which pool members' money and longevity. The Netherlands is moving its large system in this direction during the 2020s.

Chapter 14

The end of the cliff-edge retirement

The twentieth-century model of retirement was a cliff: full-time work one day, full-time leisure the next, at a fixed age. That model is already eroding. More people in their sixties and seventies work part time, change roles or start businesses, partly from choice and partly from need. Some countries make it easier to draw part of a pension while still working.

The deeper change may be in how people think about pensions. For the generation now retiring with DB pensions, a pension was something an employer gave them. For younger workers, it is something they build themselves, through a pot they can see. That makes knowledge itself part of the pension: knowing to start early, to keep fees low, to avoid selling in panics, and to plan how to turn a pot into income. The arithmetic has not changed since Bismarck. What has changed is who has to do it.

The core idea

Pensions move income from working years to old age. Pay-as-you-go state pensions depend on today's workers; defined benefit schemes are employer promises; defined contribution pots depend on your savings and markets. The central question is always who carries investment and longevity risk.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First national old-age insurance: Germany, 1889, paid from age 70.
  • A typical 65-year-old in a rich country can expect about twenty more years.
  • UK auto-enrolment minimum: 8 percent of qualifying earnings.
The common mistake

Waiting to "start a pension later". The first decade of saving does more than the last two, because of compounding. And ignoring a "small" one percent fee.
