Portfolio management. The art of owning many things on purpose, and then mostly leaving them alone.

Most people think investing is about picking winners. Professionals spend surprisingly little time on that. Their real work is deciding how much of the money goes into each broad kind of asset, keeping those proportions steady as markets move, keeping costs and taxes low, and stopping their clients (and themselves) from panicking at the worst moment. That work is called portfolio management. It is less exciting than stock picking, and it explains far more of what an investor actually ends up with.

Question 1 · What is it?

A set of decisions, not a list of holdings

A portfolio is everything an investor owns, seen as one whole. Managing it means making that whole fit a purpose, rather than judging each piece on its own.

Chapter 01

Two people, the same shares, different portfolios

Imagine two people who each own shares in the same large oil company. One is a 30-year-old nurse who owns nothing else except a pension fund full of bonds. The other is a 55-year-old oil engineer whose salary, bonus and pension all depend on the oil industry. The share is identical. Its role is not. For the nurse it adds a little variety. For the engineer it doubles a bet she is already making with her whole career.

This is the founding idea of portfolio management. An investment cannot be judged in isolation; it must be judged by what it does to everything else you own. A risky asset can make a portfolio safer if it tends to rise when the rest falls. A "safe" asset can make a portfolio fragile if it fails at the same moment as everything else.

In finance, a portfolio means the full collection of an investor's assets: shares (stocks), bonds, cash, property, funds, and sometimes less common things such as gold or private businesses. A portfolio manager is anyone who decides what goes into that collection and in what proportions. That can be a pension-fund professional, a robo-adviser app, or you with a spreadsheet.

Chapter 02

The four questions every portfolio answers

Before any asset is chosen, a good manager writes down the answers to four questions. Professionals call the document an investment policy statement: a promise made in calm weather about how to behave in a storm.

QuestionWhat it meansExample answer
GoalWhat the money is for"Retirement income from age 65", or "a house deposit in four years"
HorizonWhen the money will be neededThirty years away, or next spring
RiskHow large a fall the investor can survive, financially and emotionally"A 30% drop would not change my plans, and I would not sell"
ConstraintsTaxes, laws, ethics, liquidity needs"No tobacco", "must be able to withdraw 5% a year"

The risk question has two halves that people mix up. Risk capacity is financial: could you afford a large loss without changing your life? A young person with a stable job and decades ahead has a lot. Risk tolerance is psychological: could you watch the loss without selling? These do not always match. Some young investors have huge capacity and almost no tolerance. The portfolio has to respect the lower of the two, because a plan you abandon in a crash is worse than a modest plan you keep.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Allocate, maintain, keep costs down, and repeat

The engine of portfolio management has four parts: choosing the mix, keeping the mix, deciding whether to try to beat the market, and protecting returns from fees and taxes.

Chapter 03

Asset allocation: the decision that matters most

Asset allocation means splitting money between broad asset classes, mainly shares, bonds and cash, and sometimes property or commodities. Each class behaves differently. Shares have historically offered the highest long-run returns and the deepest falls. High-quality government bonds have offered lower returns and much smaller falls. Cash barely moves in price but is slowly eroded by inflation.

The classic default is the 60/40 portfolio: 60% in shares and 40% in bonds. It is not magic. It is simply a middle point that many pension funds and advisers have used for decades, because it captures much of the growth of shares while the bonds soften the falls.

In 1986 three researchers, Gary Brinson, Randolph Hood and Gilbert Beebower, studied large US pension funds. They found that the funds' allocation policy explained the great majority (over 90%) of the ups and downs of their returns over time. That finding is often misquoted as "allocation explains 90% of your returns". It does not say that. It says that the swings in a portfolio come mainly from its mix of asset classes, not from which particular shares or bonds it picked. For an ordinary investor that is the practical point: get the mix right and the details matter far less.

Chapter 04

The theory: Markowitz and the free lunch

In 1952 a young economist called Harry Markowitz published a short paper called "Portfolio Selection". It later earned him a share of the 1990 Nobel memorial prize in economics. His insight was that investors should care about two things together: expected return and risk, where risk is measured by how much returns jump around (their volatility).

The key ingredient is correlation, a measure of how closely two assets move together. If two assets rise and fall at exactly the same time, combining them reduces nothing. If they move somewhat independently, a mix of the two can be less volatile than either alone, without giving up much return. This is why diversification is often called "the only free lunch in finance". You can lower risk without lowering expected return, as long as the things you combine do not all fail together.

Markowitz drew the best possible mixes as a curve, the efficient frontier. Any portfolio below the curve is wasteful: some other mix offers more return for the same risk. In practice the theory is fragile, because it needs estimates of future returns and correlations, and small errors in those estimates produce wild recommendations. Most professionals therefore use the idea rather than the formula. The full story of why mixing works is in Diversification, and the price of risk itself is in Risk and return.

Chapter 05

Rebalancing: selling what did well, on purpose

A portfolio does not stay at its chosen mix. Suppose you start with 60 in shares and 40 in bonds. Shares rise 30% in a good year, and bonds stay flat. You now hold 78 in shares and 40 in bonds, so shares are about 66% of the total. Without doing anything, you have become a riskier investor than you decided to be.

How a 60/40 drifts, and how rebalancing pulls it back
            start        after a strong year     after rebalancing
 shares    60  (60%)  ->   78  (66%)          ->   70.8  (60%)
 bonds     40  (40%)  ->   40  (34%)          ->   47.2  (40%)
 total    100             118                     118

 rebalancing = sell 7.2 of shares, buy 7.2 of bonds

Rebalancing means trading back to the target mix. It forces you to sell some of what has risen and buy some of what has fallen. That feels wrong, which is part of why it works: it is a built-in rule for buying low and selling high. Its main job, though, is not to raise returns. It is to keep risk where you chose it. Common methods are calendar rebalancing (once a year, say) and threshold rebalancing (whenever a class drifts more than, for example, five percentage points from target). New contributions can do much of the work, by being directed to whichever class is underweight, which avoids selling and the taxes that can follow.

Chapter 06

Active or passive: try to beat the market, or own it

Inside each asset class, a manager chooses between two philosophies. Active management tries to beat a benchmark, such as a stock index, by choosing particular shares or by timing when to be in or out. Passive management simply buys the whole index, through an index fund or an ETF, and accepts the market's return minus a small fee.

The argument for passive is partly arithmetic. The economist William Sharpe pointed out in 1991 that, before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market's return, because all active investors together are the market. After costs, which are higher for active funds, the average active dollar must therefore trail the average passive dollar. Some active managers will beat the market, but they are hard to identify in advance, and past winners often fail to repeat.

The evidence agrees. S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes regular scorecards (called SPIVA) comparing funds with their benchmarks. Year after year they show that, over periods of fifteen years or more, a large majority of actively managed US large-company share funds, typically around nine in ten, have trailed the S&P 500 index. The case for passive is not that active managers are foolish. It is that they compete against each other, and the fees are the one thing guaranteed.

Chapter 07

Costs and taxes: the leaks you can actually plug

Returns are uncertain. Costs are certain. That makes cost the most controllable part of investing. Consider 100,000 invested for 30 years at a 6% yearly return.

Yearly feeReturn after feeValue after 30 yearsLost to fees, compounded
0.1% (cheap index fund)5.9%about 558,000about 16,000
1.0% (typical active fund or adviser)5.0%about 432,000about 142,000

A fee of 1% sounds small because it is quoted per year. Over three decades it removes about a quarter of the final pot, because a fee compounds against you exactly as returns compound for you. Fund costs are usually shown as an expense ratio or "ongoing charge", the percentage of your money taken each year. Trading costs and advice fees come on top.

Taxes leak the same way. Professionals use asset location (keeping the most heavily taxed holdings inside tax-sheltered accounts) and tax-loss harvesting (selling a fallen investment to record a loss that offsets taxable gains). The rules differ by country and change often, so this is general education, not tax advice.

Chapter 08

The glide path: changing the mix as life moves

A sensible allocation at 25 is not sensible at 64. Young investors have decades to recover from a crash and most of their wealth is still in their future earnings, which act a little like a bond. Older investors have less time and more money at stake. So the standard advice is to hold more shares when young and shift gradually towards bonds and cash as the need for the money approaches.

The planned path of that shift is called a glide path. Target-date funds automate it: you choose a fund labelled with your expected retirement year, and it gradually becomes more conservative on its own. In the United States these funds became a common default option in workplace retirement plans. Their weakness is that they treat everyone born in the same year alike, whatever their job, health, other savings or pension.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The gap between what investments earn and what investors keep

The difference between a good and a bad outcome is rarely the choice of shares. It is usually the mix, the costs, the timing of withdrawals, and above all behaviour.

Chapter 09

The behaviour gap

Here is a puzzle. A fund can earn 8% a year for a decade while the average person who invested in it earns much less. How? Because people pour money in after the fund has done well, when prices are high, and pull money out after it has done badly, when prices are low. The fund's return is measured on money that stayed put. The investors' return is measured on money that moved at the wrong times.

The research firm Morningstar publishes a regular study of this, called "Mind the Gap". Its estimates for US funds have generally found that the average investor's return trails the funds' own returns by somewhere around one percentage point a year, with wider gaps in the most volatile kinds of fund. Over thirty years, a one-point gap compounds into a large difference.

This is why good portfolio management is partly psychology. Volume 35 explains why losses hurt more than equal gains please (Losses hurt more than gains please), and Volume 38 lists the ways money decisions go wrong (Why money decisions go wrong). A written plan, automatic contributions and fixed rebalancing rules exist mainly to protect investors from their own reactions.

Chapter 10

How the giants do it

Norway's oil fund

Norway invests its oil revenues in the Government Pension Fund Global, one of the largest funds in the world, worth well over a trillion dollars in the mid-2020s. It owns small slices of thousands of companies across dozens of countries, mostly close to index weights, with low costs and a fixed target for shares versus bonds. It publishes its holdings and excludes some companies on ethical grounds.

The endowment model

From the mid-1980s, David Swensen ran Yale University's endowment and moved much of it out of public shares and bonds into private equity, hedge funds, property and timber. For years Yale's results were outstanding, and many universities copied the approach. It depends on a very long horizon, access to top private funds, and expert staff. Swensen himself argued that ordinary investors should not try to imitate it and should use low-cost index funds instead.

The right portfolio depends on the investor's horizon, skills, access and costs, so what suits a permanent fund with specialist staff can be a disaster for a family.

Chapter 11

Sequence risk: why the order of returns matters at the end

While you are saving, the order of good and bad years matters surprisingly little. Over thirty years of steady contributions, a crash early on even lets you buy cheaply. But once you start withdrawing, order matters a great deal. A crash in the first years of retirement forces you to sell assets at low prices to pay the bills. Those assets are gone and cannot recover when the market does.

Two retirees can experience exactly the same average return over twenty years and end up in very different places, depending only on whether the bad years came first or last. This is called sequence-of-returns risk. It is the main reason portfolios become more cautious near retirement, and why many planners suggest holding a few years of spending in cash or short-term bonds, so that shares never have to be sold in a slump. The wider retirement picture is in Pensions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every approach trades something away

There is no portfolio without a cost. The question is which cost you can live with.

Chapter 13

When diversification lets you down

The 60/40 portfolio relies on bonds rising, or at least holding steady, when shares fall. For most of the period from about 2000 to 2021 that is what happened: in a crisis, investors fled to government bonds, and their prices rose. Then came 2022. Inflation surged, central banks raised interest rates quickly, and both shares and bonds fell sharply in the same year. For a standard US 60/40 portfolio, 2022 was one of the worst years in about a century.

The lesson is that correlations are not laws of nature; they change with the economic regime. When the main danger is a recession, bonds tend to protect. When the main danger is inflation, bonds and shares can both suffer, because higher interest rates hurt both. Why rates move bond prices is the subject of Interest rates and markets. A good manager assumes the cushion will sometimes fail, and sizes the risk so that the failure is survivable.

Chapter 15

The question that will shape the next decade

For a generation of investors, bonds were the dependable shock absorber. Whether they will play that role again depends on inflation. If inflation stays low and stable, the old relationship, in which bonds rise in a recession, will probably return. If inflation becomes more volatile, managers will look for other cushions: inflation-linked bonds, cash, commodities, or simply fewer shares.

Nobody knows which world is coming. That uncertainty is itself the best argument for the basics. A portfolio built to survive several possible futures beats one optimised for the future you happen to expect. Diversify across assets and countries, keep costs low, rebalance by rule, and write the plan down before the next storm arrives.

The core idea

Portfolio management starts from goals, horizon and risk, then sets an asset allocation that drives most of the portfolio's behaviour, keeps it in place by rebalancing, and protects returns from costs, taxes and panic. Each holding is judged by what it does to the whole.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Allocation policy explained over 90% of the swings in pension fund returns (Brinson, Hood and Beebower, 1986).
  • Over 15 years or more, roughly 9 in 10 active US large-company share funds trailed the S&P 500 (SPIVA scorecards).
  • A 1% yearly fee removes about a quarter of a 30-year pot.
The common mistake

Spending all the effort on choosing individual investments and none on the mix, the fees and the plan for a crash. The mix and the behaviour decide most of the outcome.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a 60/40 portfolio that is never rebalanced slowly becomes riskier, and why the same crash hurts a retiree more than a saver? If so, you understand allocation, drift and sequence risk.
