Index funds. Stop trying to find the needle. Buy the haystack.

For most of the twentieth century, investing meant paying an expert to choose which shares to buy. The index fund proposed something that sounded lazy, even absurd: choose nothing, own everything, and charge almost nothing for it. It was mocked when it launched. Fifty years later, in the United States, more money sits in index funds than in funds run by stock-pickers. The reason is not fashion. It is a piece of arithmetic so simple that it is easy to miss.

Volume 38 states the conclusion in two sentences in What a price actually is: most stock-pickers, professionals included, do worse than a broad slice of the market after fees. This file shows why that is true, where the idea came from, and what changes when index funds become the biggest owners in the market. The wrapper most index funds now use is explained in ETFs.

Question 1 · What is it?

A fund that copies a list instead of making choices

An index fund holds the shares or bonds in a published index, in the same proportions, and changes them only when the index changes. It does not try to beat the market. It tries to be the market, as cheaply as possible.

Chapter 01

"Bogle's folly"

In 1975 John Bogle, who had just been pushed out of his job at a traditional fund company, founded a new firm called Vanguard. The next year it launched a fund for ordinary investors that simply bought the shares in the S&P 500 index, then about the 500 largest American companies. It employed no analysts and made no predictions. Much of the industry laughed at it. One investment research firm circulated a poster declaring index funds "un-American". Its first offering raised only about 11 million dollars, a small fraction of its target, and critics called it "Bogle's folly".

Bogle was not the first. In 1971 a team at the bank Wells Fargo had built an index fund for the pension fund of the luggage maker Samsonite. In 1974 the economist Paul Samuelson had written an essay calling for someone to start a low-cost fund that simply tracked the market. But Bogle's fund was the first that anyone could buy. It took two decades to catch on, and then it rebuilt the investment industry. In the United States, assets in index funds overtook those in actively managed funds around the end of 2023.

Chapter 02

How the copying is done

An index is a rule for building a list of investments. The index fund follows the rule. Most major share indexes use market-capitalisation weighting: each company is held in proportion to its total market value. If one company is worth twice as much as another, the fund holds twice as much of it.

Weighting methodHow it worksEffect
Market-capHold each firm by its market valueVery low trading needed, since weights move with prices by themselves. Big firms dominate.
EqualHold the same amount of every firmMore weight on smaller firms. Needs regular trading to reset.
PriceWeight by share price (the Dow Jones Industrial Average)A historical oddity. A high share price, not a big company, means more weight.
Factor or "smart beta"Tilt toward cheap, small or stable firms by a fixed ruleA middle ground between indexing and active choice. Slightly higher fees.

Market-cap weighting has a lovely property. When prices change, the fund's holdings change in exactly the same proportions automatically. The fund only has to trade when companies enter or leave the index, or when investors add or withdraw money. Low trading means low cost, which is the whole point.

Chapter 03

The ideas underneath

Two ideas from the 1960s and 1970s made index funds intellectually respectable. The first is the efficient-market hypothesis, associated above all with the Chicago economist Eugene Fama, who later shared the 2013 Nobel prize in economics. In its everyday form it says that share prices already reflect the information that is publicly available. If good news is known, it is already in the price, so you cannot easily profit from it.

The second was popularised by the Princeton economist Burton Malkiel in his 1973 book A Random Walk Down Wall Street. Short-term price movements, he argued, are close to unpredictable, like the steps of someone wandering at random. If so, paying a professional to predict them is paying for something nobody can reliably supply.

Economists still argue over how efficient markets really are. Bubbles and crashes suggest prices can be badly wrong for long periods. But the case for index funds does not require markets to be perfect. It only requires that mistakes are hard to spot in advance and expensive to exploit. Chapter 04 shows why even that is more than you need.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Arithmetic, fees and time

The case for index funds rests on a simple subtraction. Fees and time then multiply that small difference into a large one.

Chapter 04

The arithmetic that cannot be escaped

In 1991 the Stanford economist William Sharpe, another Nobel winner, published a two-page article called "The Arithmetic of Active Management". Its argument needs no statistics.

Sharpe's arithmetic, in four lines
   1. every share in the market is owned by someone
   2. so  (index investors) + (active investors)  =  the whole market
   3. index investors earn the market return, by definition
   -> so active investors, as a group, must ALSO earn the market return, before costs

   active investors pay more in fees and trading  ->  after costs, the average active
   dollar must do WORSE than the average index dollar. Always. In every market.

Notice what this does and does not say. It does not say no active investor can win. Some will beat the market, sometimes by a lot. It says that the average active investor must lose to the index by roughly the extra amount they pay, and that every winner's gain is matched by someone else's loss. Investing in shares is not a zero-sum game overall, because companies grow. But beating the market is. The question for any one investor is whether they can reliably be on the winning side, and know it in advance.

Chapter 05

Fees, compounded

The difference between an index fund and an active fund is often about 1 per cent a year. That sounds tiny. Here is what it does over a working life.

10,000 invested for 30 yearsYearly return after feesFinal value (approx.)
Market return 6%, index fund fee 0.05%5.95%about 56,600
Market return 6%, active fund fee 1.5%4.5%about 37,500

The illustrative numbers assume the active fund simply matches the market before its fee. Under that assumption, the higher fee takes about a third of the final result, and it is charged whether the fund does well or badly. Volume 38's chapter on compounding explains why small yearly differences grow so large. Fees are compounding working in reverse.

There are hidden costs too. Active funds trade more, and each trade pays a spread and, in some countries, taxes. Index funds trade little. That is why Bogle liked to say, in various forms, that in investing you get what you don't pay for.

Chapter 06

Keeping up with the index

An index fund never matches its index exactly. Its fee is subtracted, it holds a little cash to pay departing investors, and it pays costs when the index changes. The gap between the fund's return and the index's return is called tracking difference, and how much that gap wobbles over time is tracking error. For large funds on major indexes, both are very small, usually close to the fee itself. Some funds even beat their index slightly by lending out shares for a fee.

When a company is added to a major index, every fund tracking that index must buy it at roughly the same moment. When Tesla joined the S&P 500 in December 2020, index funds had to buy tens of billions of dollars of its shares, and traders bought ahead of them. This "index effect" is a small, known cost of following public rules. Some fund managers reduce it by trading a little before or after the official date.

Chapter 07

The choice that still matters: which index

Indexing removes the choice of shares, but not the choice of index. A fund on the S&P 500 holds only American large companies. A fund on a global index such as the MSCI World or MSCI All Country World holds thousands of companies across many countries. A fund on the FTSE 100 holds a hundred large British companies, heavy in oil, mining and banks.

Many investors hold mostly their home country's shares, a habit called home bias. It feels safe because the names are familiar. But a small home market can be dominated by a few industries, and your job already depends on your home economy. A global index is the most complete version of "buy the haystack". In the mid-2020s, American companies made up around 60 per cent or more of a global index by value, so even a global fund is heavily American. How much to hold in shares versus bonds is a separate question, explored in Portfolio management.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The evidence, the savings, and a new kind of owner

Index funds matter because the evidence for them is strong, because they have saved ordinary savers enormous sums, and because they are turning a few firms into owners of almost every large company.

Chapter 08

The scoreboard

Since 2002, S&P Dow Jones Indices has published a regular scorecard called SPIVA, comparing active funds with their benchmark indexes. Its results are consistent across years and countries. Over one year, a fair number of active funds beat their index. Over ten or fifteen years, the share that do falls sharply. In US large-company shares, a large majority of active funds, typically well over 80 per cent, have trailed the S&P 500 over fifteen-year periods. Results in Europe, Japan and other markets are similar in direction.

Worse, past winners rarely keep winning. Studies of fund performance find that funds in the top group over one period are not much more likely than chance to be in the top group over the next. Picking a good active fund turns out to be almost as hard as picking good shares.

The most famous demonstration was a bet. In 2007 Warren Buffett wagered a million dollars, for charity, that a plain S&P 500 index fund would beat a selection of hedge funds chosen by a professional investment firm over the ten years from 2008 to 2017. The index fund returned roughly 7 per cent a year. The hedge fund selection averaged around 2 per cent. Buffett also wrote that his own instructions for money left to his wife put 90 per cent in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund.

Chapter 09

Who gained

The clearest winners are ordinary savers. As money moved to index funds, fees across the whole fund industry fell, because active managers had to compete. Pension funds that once paid managers to pick shares now often hold index funds for most of their assets. Estimates of the total saved by investors run into hundreds of billions of dollars, though such figures depend heavily on assumptions, so treat any single number with caution.

Index funds also made a good investment strategy simple enough to explain in one sentence. That matters. A strategy you understand is a strategy you can stick with when markets fall, and sticking with it is where much of the return comes from. The behavioural side of that, including why people sell in panics, is in Behavioral economics.

Chapter 10

The new owners of everything

Because index funds hold every company in the index, the biggest fund managers now own a slice of nearly every large listed firm. Three American firms, BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street, often called the "Big Three", together hold a large share of the votes at most large US companies. Academic work around 2017 found that, combined, they were the largest single shareholder in the large majority of S&P 500 companies.

That creates real questions. Who should decide how those votes are cast: the fund manager, or the millions of savers whose money it is? Some funds now let investors choose voting policies themselves. A further debate concerns common ownership. If the same few investors own all the airlines, do the airlines compete less hard? Some studies found evidence of softer competition. Others disputed the methods. This is contested, and regulators in the US and Europe are still watching it.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The best default, with honest limits

Index funds are not magic. They guarantee the market's return, minus a little, and that includes the market's crashes.

Chapter 12

The two serious critiques

The free-rider problem

Index funds accept market prices without doing any research. They rely on active investors to set those prices correctly. If almost everyone indexed, who would notice that a company was overpriced? Economists Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz showed in 1980 that a perfectly efficient market cannot exist, because no one would then pay to gather information. The likely answer is a balance: as indexing grows, mispricings become more common, rewarding the active investors who remain, until the share settles.

Concentration

By the mid-2020s the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 made up roughly a third or more of the index, an unusually high share by historical standards, led by a few technology giants. An index fund buyer is therefore making a large bet on a handful of firms. Critics such as the investor Michael Burry warned in 2019 that passive investing resembled a bubble. Defenders reply that cap weighting simply mirrors what the market says those firms are worth.

Both critiques have force. Neither has yet changed the basic arithmetic of Chapter 04. For an individual saver, the index fund remains the default that is hardest to beat, while the questions it raises are about the market as a whole.

Chapter 14

Why the strategy probably survives its own success

The Grossman-Stiglitz argument suggests a self-correcting system. If index funds grow so large that prices become sloppy, it becomes easier for careful active investors to win, money flows back to them, and prices sharpen again. Most economists expect the market to settle at some mix of the two rather than tip into chaos, though no one can say where that mix is.

What will likely change is the meaning of "the market". As companies stay private longer and indexes become more concentrated, owning the haystack may mean owning a slightly different haystack from the one Bogle bought in 1976. The core lesson, though, comes from subtraction rather than from any market condition: before costs, the average investor earns the market return, so whoever pays least keeps the most.

The core idea

An index fund copies an index instead of choosing investments. Because active investors as a group must earn the market return before costs, the average active dollar trails the index by its extra fees. Low fees compound into a large advantage over decades.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First retail index fund: Vanguard, 1976, on the S&P 500.
  • Over fifteen years, typically well over 80 per cent of US large-cap active funds trail the S&P 500 (SPIVA).
  • A 1.5 per cent yearly fee can take about a third of the final value over 30 years.
The common mistake

Thinking an index fund is safe. It is cheap and diversified, not low-risk. It falls exactly as far as the market does.
