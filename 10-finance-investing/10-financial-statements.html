Financial statements. Three documents that tell you what a company earned, what it owns, and where the cash really went.

Every listed company publishes its accounts, and almost nobody outside finance reads them. That is a pity, because the core of them is not hard. There are three main statements. Each answers one simple question, and together they reveal things no press release will tell you: whether profits are turning into cash, whether debts are piling up, and whether the story management is telling matches the numbers. You do not need to be an accountant to read them. You need to know what each one is for.

Question 1 · What is it?

Three questions any owner would ask

Financial statements are standardised reports that describe a company's performance over a period and its position at a point in time.

Chapter 01

The lemonade stand test

Suppose you lend money to a teenager who runs a lemonade stand all summer. In September you would want to know three things. First: did the stand make money? Second: what does the stand own now, and what does it owe? Third: where did the actual cash go? These sound like the same question. They are not. The stand might have made a profit on paper while all its cash is tied up in a new juicer and in unpaid bills from neighbours who bought on credit.

Every company in the world answers those three questions with three documents:

StatementThe question it answersCovers
Income statement (profit and loss, or P&L)Did we make money?A period, such as a year or a quarter
Balance sheetWhat do we own and owe?A single date, like a photograph
Cash flow statementWhere did the cash come from and go?A period

Profit, position and cash are three different things, and a company can look healthy on one while failing on another. Learning to read all three together is the whole skill.

Chapter 02

Who writes the rules, and who checks the work

Listed companies must publish accounts regularly: at least yearly, and in many markets every half year or quarter. In the United States the annual report filed with the regulator is called the 10-K. Elsewhere it is usually simply the annual report.

The accounts follow a rulebook called an accounting standard. There are two big families. IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards), set by a body based in London, is required or permitted for listed companies in well over a hundred jurisdictions, including the European Union. US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) applies to US companies. The two are broadly similar but differ in many details, so the same company could report somewhat different profits under each.

Finally, an independent firm of auditors checks the accounts and gives an opinion on whether they present a "true and fair view" (in the IFRS world) or are "presented fairly" (in the US). An audit is not a guarantee. Auditors test samples and controls; they do not check every transaction. But a company whose auditor refuses to sign, or resigns suddenly, is sending a loud warning.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Reading each statement, and seeing how they lock together

Each statement has a standard shape. Once you know the shape, you can read any company's accounts in any industry.

Chapter 03

The income statement: from sales to profit, layer by layer

The income statement starts with the money earned from customers and subtracts costs in a fixed order. Each line removes one more kind of cost, which is why analysts talk about the "top line" (sales) and the "bottom line" (final profit).

A simple income statement (any currency, in thousands)
  Revenue (sales)                         1,000
  - Cost of goods sold                     -400   lemons, sugar, cups
  = Gross profit                            600   60% gross margin
  - Operating expenses                     -350   wages, rent, marketing
  = Operating profit                        250   25% operating margin
  - Interest on debt                        -50
  = Profit before tax                       200
  - Tax                                     -50
  = Net income (net profit)                 150   15% net margin

  Net income / number of shares  =  earnings per share (EPS)

Each layer tells you something different. Gross profit shows whether the product itself is worth making. Operating profit shows whether the whole business runs at a profit before the effects of debt and tax. Net income is what is left for the owners. Dividing net income by the number of shares gives earnings per share, the number most quoted in the financial press.

Chapter 04

Accrual accounting: profit is not cash

The income statement uses accrual accounting. Revenue is recorded when it is earned, not when the cash arrives. Costs are recorded when they are incurred, not when they are paid. If the lemonade stand sells 100 cups to an office on a 60-day invoice, the sale counts as revenue now, even though the money arrives in two months.

Accrual also spreads big purchases over time. If the stand buys a juicer for 300 that will last three years, it does not record a cost of 300 in year one and nothing afterwards. It records about 100 a year. That yearly charge is called depreciation for physical things and amortisation for intangible ones such as software licences.

Accrual accounting exists for a good reason: it matches costs to the revenue they help produce, so profit reflects the real economics of the period. Its price is that profit involves judgement. How long will the juicer last? Will that office actually pay? Every such judgement is a place where honest error, or deliberate optimism, can enter.

Chapter 05

The balance sheet: a photograph of everything owned and owed

The balance sheet rests on one equation that always holds:

The accounting equation
  ASSETS  =  LIABILITIES  +  EQUITY

  what the company      what it owes       what is left over
  owns or controls      to others          for the owners

  Lemonade stand on 30 September:
    cash 120 + juicer 200 + money owed by customers 80  =  400 of assets
    bank loan 150 + unpaid supplier bills 50            =  200 of liabilities
    equity                                               =  200

Assets are things the company owns or controls that should bring future benefit: cash, stock waiting to be sold (inventory), money customers owe (receivables), buildings, machines. Liabilities are what it owes: loans, unpaid bills (payables), taxes due. Equity is the difference, the owners' share on paper. Both sides are split into "current" items, due or usable within a year, and "non-current" items, which last longer.

One crucial point: equity on the balance sheet is not the same as what the company is worth in the market. Book value records mostly historical costs. Market value, as Market capitalization explains, records investors' guesses about the future. For many modern companies the market value is many times the book value.

Chapter 06

The cash flow statement: following the money

Because profit involves estimates, many investors trust the cash flow statement most. Cash either arrived in the bank account or it did not. The statement sorts every movement of cash into three groups.

SectionWhat it showsHealthy pattern for a mature company
Operating activitiesCash from running the business: customers paying, suppliers and staff being paidPositive, and roughly in line with profit over time
Investing activitiesCash spent on or received from long-term assets: machines, buildings, acquisitionsUsually negative, as the company reinvests
Financing activitiesCash from or to lenders and owners: borrowing, repaying, issuing shares, dividends, buybacksDepends on the strategy

A widely used summary is free cash flow: operating cash flow minus the spending needed to maintain and grow the business's physical assets (called capital expenditure, or capex). Definitions vary a little, but the idea is the cash genuinely available to repay debt or reward owners. Over several years, a company whose profits never turn into free cash flow deserves hard questions.

Chapter 07

How the three statements lock together

The statements are not three separate stories. They are three views of the same set of records, and they link at exact points.

The links between the statements
  INCOME STATEMENT                 CASH FLOW STATEMENT              BALANCE SHEET
  ----------------                 -------------------              -------------
  net income  --------------->    starts from net income
                                   + add back depreciation
                                   - cash tied up in receivables
                                     and inventory
                                   = operating cash flow
                                   + investing + financing
                                   = change in cash  ---------->   cash (end of year)

  net income - dividends  ---------------------------------->   retained earnings
                                                                  (part of equity)

This interlocking is why professional analysts build all three together. If a company's profit rises but its receivables rise even faster, the cash flow statement will show it: customers are being given longer to pay, perhaps to make sales look better. Numbers that fit together across all three statements are much harder to fake than a single impressive figure.

Chapter 08

The notes, and the "adjusted" numbers

The statements are followed by many pages of notes. They explain the accounting choices, break down the totals, and disclose things that do not appear in the main tables: pending lawsuits, leases, pension obligations, details of debt and when it falls due. Experienced readers often say the notes are where the real information lives.

Companies also publish their own preferred measures, often called "adjusted" or "non-GAAP" figures. The best known is EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It can be useful for comparing businesses with different debts or tax positions. It can also flatter a company by ignoring real costs, because machines do wear out and must be replaced. Regulators require companies to show how adjusted figures reconcile with the official ones. The safe habit is to read the official figures first and treat every adjusted figure as a claim that needs explaining.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Accounts reveal health, risk, and sometimes fraud

Lenders, investors, suppliers, employees and governments all rely on these documents. When they are read well, they warn early. When they are false, the damage can be enormous.

Chapter 09

Profitable and broke at the same time

One of the oldest sayings in business is that companies rarely die from losses; they die from running out of cash. A fast-growing firm can be profitable on every sale and still go bankrupt. It must buy materials and pay staff before customers pay their invoices. The faster it grows, the more cash gets stuck in stock and unpaid invoices. This money tied up in day-to-day operations is called working capital.

The income statement alone would never show the problem. The cash flow statement would show it at once, as operating cash flow falling below profit and then turning negative. This is why lenders often look at cash flow before profit: banks are repaid in cash, not in accounting earnings.

Chapter 10

When the accounts lie: Enron and Wirecard

In 2001 Enron, a US energy-trading company once ranked among the largest in America, collapsed into bankruptcy. It had used complicated "special purpose entities" to keep debts off its balance sheet and to record profits that did not really exist. Its auditor, Arthur Andersen, one of the five biggest accounting firms in the world, was convicted in 2002 of obstructing justice for destroying documents (the conviction was later overturned on appeal, but the firm had already collapsed). In response, the US passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, which made senior executives personally certify their accounts and tightened rules on auditors.

Nearly two decades later, the German payments company Wirecard admitted in June 2020 that about 1.9 billion euros of cash it had reported, supposedly held in accounts in Asia, probably did not exist. It filed for insolvency within days. Journalists, notably at the Financial Times, and short sellers had raised questions for years.

Both cases show the same thing. The most dangerous lies are often in the balance sheet, about what a company owns and owes, rather than in the headline profit. And in both, the warning signs, profits that did not turn into cash and structures nobody could explain, were visible to readers willing to ask.

Chapter 11

A handful of ratios anyone can calculate

Raw numbers mean little until you compare them with something: last year, a competitor, or another line of the same accounts. That comparison is what ratios do.

RatioHow to calculate itWhat it tells you
Gross marginGross profit / revenueHow much each sale earns before overheads; signals pricing power
Operating marginOperating profit / revenueHow efficiently the whole business runs
Return on equityNet income / equityHow much profit the owners' money produces each year
Debt to equityTotal debt / equityHow much the company relies on borrowing
Current ratioCurrent assets / current liabilitiesWhether it can pay bills due within a year
Cash conversionOperating cash flow / net incomeWhether profits are actually becoming cash

Ratios differ hugely between industries. A supermarket may run on a thin operating margin and still be an excellent business, because it sells enormous volumes. A software firm may have very high gross margins. Always compare a ratio with the same company's history or with close competitors, never with an abstract "good" number. The ratios that combine accounts with the share price, such as price-to-earnings, belong to Company valuation.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A shared language with blind spots

Standardised accounts are one of the quiet foundations of modern capitalism. They are also backward-looking, built on judgement, and weak at measuring what modern firms are made of.

Chapter 12

What accounts do well, and where they fall short

FeatureStrengthWeakness
Standard rulesComparable across companies and years; a common language for investors worldwideTwo main rulebooks, many local options, and room for choices that change the numbers
Accrual accountingMatches costs to revenue, so profit reflects the period's real activityDepends on estimates that can be stretched; profit and cash can drift apart
Historical costObjective and verifiable: what was actually paidOld assets may be shown far below or above their true current value
Independent auditAn outside check that deters many errors and fraudsSampling, not total checking; auditors are paid by the company they audit
Regular publicationInvestors get fresh, legally required informationBackward-looking, and quarterly pressure can push managers towards short-term choices
Chapter 13

What the balance sheet cannot see

Accounting rules were designed in an age of factories. A machine bought for a million appears on the balance sheet as an asset. A million spent on research, software developed in-house, or building a brand is mostly recorded as an expense in the year it is spent, and never appears as an asset at all (unless the company buys another firm that owns such things).

For a modern technology or pharmaceutical company, the most valuable things it owns, its software, patents, data, brand and the skills of its people, may barely show up. That is a large part of why market values are often many times book values. For many of today's most valuable companies, the balance sheet describes the least important part of the business. Accountants and academics have debated this for decades, and there is no agreed fix, because measuring intangibles reliably is genuinely hard.

One more trend deserves mention. Even as reporting expands, the core skill stays the same. Whether the reader is a person or a program, the questions are the lemonade stand's: did it make money, what does it own and owe, and where did the cash go? A reader who can answer those three, and check that the answers agree, is better armed than most headlines.

The core idea

The income statement shows profit over a period, the balance sheet shows what is owned and owed on one date, and the cash flow statement shows where cash actually moved. They link together, and reading them together reveals whether profits are real and sustainable.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The accounting equation always holds: assets = liabilities + equity.
  • Wirecard admitted that about 1.9 billion euros of reported cash probably did not exist (2020).
  • The Enron collapse (2001) led to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002).
The common mistake

Treating profit as cash. A company can report profits every year and still run out of money, because profit includes sales not yet paid for and estimates not yet tested.

Test yourself

Can you explain how a fast-growing, profitable company could go bankrupt, and which of the three statements would show it first? If so, you understand accruals, working capital and cash flow.

The words, in plain words
Income statement
The report of revenue, costs and profit over a period. Also called the profit and loss account.
Balance sheet
A snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity on one date.
Cash flow statement
The record of cash coming in and going out, sorted into operating, investing and financing.
Revenue
Money earned from customers, before any costs. The "top line".
Net income
Profit after every cost, interest and tax. The "bottom line".
Earnings per share
Net income divided by the number of shares.
Accrual accounting
Recording revenue and costs when they are earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
Depreciation
Spreading the cost of a long-lasting physical asset over the years it is used.
Equity (book value)
Assets minus liabilities: the owners' share on paper.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations, mainly stock and unpaid customer invoices, minus unpaid supplier bills.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus spending on long-term assets; cash truly available to owners and lenders.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; a popular but flattering adjusted measure.
IFRS and US GAAP
The two main families of accounting rules.
