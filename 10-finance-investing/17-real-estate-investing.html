Sharp Mind · Finance & Investing · Topic 17 of 20

Real estate investing. The world's biggest asset, bought mostly with borrowed money by people who live inside it.

For most families, a home is the largest thing they will ever buy and the largest debt they will ever owe. Added together, the world's buildings and land are worth more than all its shares and bonds combined. Property has made many ordinary people wealthy, mainly because they were allowed to borrow four or five times their savings to buy it. The same borrowing has caused some of the worst financial crises in history. Understanding real estate as an investment means separating the three things it gives you, a place to live or let, a stream of rent, and a bet on a price, and seeing how debt multiplies all three.

Question 1 · What is it?

Buying land and buildings for the income and the price

Real estate investing means owning property, directly or through a fund, to earn rent, to profit from a rise in value, or both. Most people do it without calling it investing.

Chapter 01

The asset most people already own

Ask people whether they invest and many will say no. Ask whether they own their home and many will say yes. In most rich countries more than half of households own their home, and for the middle of the wealth distribution, the home is usually most of what they have. For ordinary families, housing, not shares, is the main investment.

The global numbers are huge. Estimates by the property consultancy Savills put the value of all the world's real estate at several hundred trillion dollars in the early 2020s, with homes making up the great majority. That is more than the value of all listed shares and all bonds put together. Most of that value is not in the bricks. It is in the land under them, and above all in land near jobs, schools and transport. A house in a busy capital and an identical house in a declining town can differ in price by a factor of ten. The building is the same; the location is not.

Each route offers the same underlying exposure to land and buildings, wrapped differently. The wrapping decides how much you can borrow, how much work you do, how quickly you can sell and how you are taxed. Those differences often matter more than the property itself.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rent, price, and the multiplier of debt

A property earns money in two ways. A mortgage magnifies both, in both directions. And a long list of costs quietly takes a share.

Chapter 03

Two sources of return

The first source is income: the rent tenants pay, or for a home owner the rent they no longer have to pay. The second is capital growth: the change in the property's price over time. Total return is the two added together.

Investors measure the income with the yield. If a flat costs 200,000 and earns 10,000 a year in rent, its gross yield is 5 percent. After taking out repairs, insurance, management, property taxes and empty months between tenants, the net yield might be 3 or 3.5 percent. For commercial buildings, professionals use a similar measure called the capitalisation rate, or cap rate: the yearly net operating income divided by the price. The lower the cap rate, the more investors are paying for each unit of income.

Yields and prices move in opposite directions, just as with bonds. If rents stay the same and buyers become willing to accept a lower yield, prices rise. Much of the price growth in property over the past forty years came not from rents rising, but from investors accepting lower yields as interest rates fell. When rates rose sharply in 2022 and 2023, that process went into reverse, and commercial property values fell in many countries.

Chapter 04

Leverage: why a mortgage changes everything

Almost nobody buys property with cash. A buyer puts down a deposit, often 10 to 25 percent of the price, and borrows the rest with a mortgage, a loan secured on the property. The part you own outright, the price minus the debt, is your equity. Borrowing to invest is called leverage, and it changes the arithmetic dramatically.

The same 10 percent price move, with and without a mortgage

  price paid                 300,000                300,000

  price falls 10%  -> 270,000
     your loss               -30,000 = -10%         -30,000 = -50% on your money

  price falls 20%  -> 240,000                        equity = 0  (loan = value)
  price falls 25%  -> 225,000                        "negative equity": you owe more than it's worth

(The sheet ignores interest and costs to keep the point clear.) With a 20 percent deposit, every move in the price is multiplied five times on your own money. In a rising market this feels like genius. In a falling one it can trap people. A household in negative equity cannot sell without finding cash to repay the bank, so it cannot move for a job, and if it loses income it may lose the house. That happened to millions of American households after 2007. Leverage is the main reason property has made ordinary people rich, and the main reason property crashes become banking crises.

Chapter 05

What actually sets property prices

Four forces do most of the work. The first is interest rates. Most buyers are limited by how much monthly payment they can afford, not by the price itself. When mortgage rates fall, the same payment supports a bigger loan, so buyers bid more. When rates rise, the reverse happens. In the United States, average 30-year mortgage rates rose from about 3 percent in 2021 to around 7 percent in 2023, which roughly doubled the interest cost of a typical new loan.

The second is incomes and jobs. Places where good jobs cluster attract people, who compete for homes. The third is supply. Where planning rules make it hard to build, as in many successful cities, rising demand turns into rising prices instead of more homes. Where building is easy, prices rise more slowly. The fourth is credit: how easily banks lend, and how large a loan they allow against a given income or deposit. Regulators in many countries now cap loan sizes for exactly this reason, as described in Banking · Mortgages.

Over long periods, the picture is more modest than most people believe. The economist Robert Shiller, who built a long-run index of American house prices, found that after removing inflation, US home prices rose surprisingly little for most of the twentieth century, before the boom that began in the late 1990s. A large 2019 study of sixteen rich countries since 1870, by Òscar Jordà, Moritz Schularick, Alan Taylor and colleagues, found that housing's total return, rent included, was similar to that of shares, with smaller swings. Rent, not price, did most of the work.

Chapter 06

The costs nobody puts in the advert

Shares can be bought for a tiny fee. Property cannot. Buying and selling involves transfer taxes (stamp duty in Britain), lawyers, surveys, agents and mortgage fees. Together these can easily reach 5 to 10 percent of the price for a round trip, more in some countries. A property bought and sold within a few years can lose money even if its price rises.

Then come running costs: maintenance, which is often estimated at around one percent of a home's value per year on average, insurance, property taxes, service charges for flats, and for landlords, management fees, legal duties and voids, the months a property stands empty. Rental property is also a part-time job. Tenants call when the boiler breaks. Laws on tenants' rights, eviction and energy standards change, and they vary greatly between countries and even cities.

Finally, property is illiquid: it takes weeks or months to sell, and you cannot sell one bedroom when you need some cash. In a downturn, sales can dry up entirely. People who have to sell then often take a large discount.

Chapter 07

REITs: property you can buy in a second

In 1960 the United States created a new kind of company, the real estate investment trust, or REIT. A REIT owns and runs income-producing property, such as offices, flats, warehouses, data centres or phone masts, and its shares trade on the stock market. In return for paying little or no corporate tax, it must pay out most of its taxable income to shareholders, at least 90 percent in the US. Many other countries, including Britain, France, Japan and Singapore, have since created similar structures.

REITs solve several problems at once. You can invest a small amount, spread across hundreds of buildings, and sell in seconds. The cost is that their prices move every day with the stock market, so they feel more volatile than a house whose price you only see when you sell. The house is not really steadier; you are simply not shown its price every day. Over long periods REIT returns broadly reflect the property underneath. Some open-ended property funds, which promise daily withdrawals while owning buildings that take months to sell, have had to freeze withdrawals in crises, as several British funds did after the Brexit vote in 2016 and again in 2020.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Where wealth lives, and where crises start

Because property is so large and so heavily borrowed against, its price shapes inequality between generations, the health of banks and the fortunes of whole economies.

Chapter 08

Housing and the wealth gap

In many rich countries house prices have risen much faster than wages since the 1990s. In parts of Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, a typical home now costs many times a typical yearly income, far more than a generation ago. That has created a sharp divide. People who bought early, often older, have gained large wealth without doing anything. People who did not, often younger, face high rents and need larger deposits, which many can only gather with help from their parents.

Economists disagree about the main cause. Some stress low interest rates, which raised the price of all long-lived assets. Others stress planning rules that block new building in the places people want to live. Others point to tax advantages for home owners and landlords, or to foreign and investor buyers in certain cities. The honest answer is that the mix differs from city to city. What is not in doubt is that housing is now one of the main channels through which wealth passes from one generation to the next. The wider picture is in Economics · Inequality.

Chapter 09

When property breaks

Three episodes show how large the damage can be. In Japan, land and share prices soared in the late 1980s. At the peak, people joked that the grounds of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo were worth more than all the land in California. After 1991 the bubble deflated. Commercial land prices in the big cities fell by well over half and stayed down for decades, and banks carried bad property loans for years, contributing to what was called the "lost decade".

In the United States, prices rose steeply from the late 1990s to 2006, helped by easy lending, including loans to borrowers who could not afford them. National house prices then fell by roughly a quarter to a third over the following years. Millions of homes were repossessed, and losses on mortgage-backed securities set off the global financial crisis of 2008, described in Financial crises.

In China, property and related industries grew to be a very large part of the economy, by some estimates around a quarter of all activity. Developers borrowed heavily and sold flats before they were built. From 2021 the government tightened rules on developer debt, and many developers defaulted, the largest being Evergrande, which a Hong Kong court ordered to be wound up in 2024. Prices and sales have fallen, and many families have paid for flats that were not finished. How deep and long China's adjustment will be is still being argued.

Chapter 10

Offices after the pandemic

Commercial property had its own shock. When Covid-19 sent office workers home in 2020, many expected them to return within a year. Many did not, at least not every day. Hybrid working, with two or three days in the office, became normal in many industries. Companies that once leased whole floors began to need less space.

In the United States, office vacancy rates rose to around one in five buildings' space by 2024 in national averages, and higher in some cities. Rising interest rates hit at the same time. Values of older office buildings fell sharply, some selling for a fraction of their pre-pandemic prices. The pain is uneven. New, well-located, energy-efficient offices have held up far better than old ones, a pattern people describe as a "flight to quality". Meanwhile, warehouses for online shopping and data centres for computing have boomed. Commercial property is not one market but many, and they can move in opposite directions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Solid, useful and slow, with a hidden lever

Property's strengths are real. Most of its dangers come from the borrowing and from the concentration of a family's wealth in one building.

Chapter 12

Renting versus buying is maths, not morals

In many cultures, renting is seen as "throwing money away". It is not that simple. An owner also pays money they will never see again: mortgage interest, maintenance, insurance, property taxes and the costs of buying and selling. A renter who invests the deposit and the difference in monthly costs can, in some markets and periods, end up as wealthy as the owner.

Which choice wins depends on the ratio of prices to rents, interest rates, how long you stay, and whether the renter actually invests the difference, which many do not. Buying works partly as a forced savings plan, and that behavioural benefit is real. But a person who moves often, or lives where prices are very high compared with rents, may be better off renting. The point is to run the numbers for your situation rather than follow a slogan. Volume 38's short list that actually matters sets out the broader rules.

Chapter 14

Climate change and the value of a place

Property prices have always depended on location. Climate change is adding a new layer to that old rule. A house in a flood zone or a wildfire area may be lovely to live in and still become hard to insure. Without insurance, banks will not lend against it, and without a mortgage few buyers can purchase it. A home that cannot be insured or financed can lose value quickly, even if nothing has happened to it yet.

So far, prices in many risky areas have adjusted less than the risk might suggest, partly because buyers underestimate it and partly because public insurance schemes and disaster aid soften the cost. Many researchers expect that gap to close over time, but when and how fast is uncertain. For an investor, the lesson is the same one property has always taught, with a new variable added: buy the location, and ask what that location will be like in thirty years, not just today.

The core idea

Property returns come from rent and price change, and most value sits in the land and its location. Prices are driven by interest rates, incomes, supply and credit. A mortgage multiplies gains and losses, which is why property makes families rich and makes crises deep.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World real estate is worth more than all shares and bonds combined.
  • A 20 percent deposit multiplies every price move by about five.
  • Buying and selling can cost 5–10 percent of the price for a round trip.
The common mistake

Believing houses are safer than shares because their price rarely changes. You simply don't see a house's price every day. With a mortgage, a house is often the riskier of the two.

Test yourself

Can you explain why falling interest rates raised property prices for forty years, and what happened when rates jumped in 2022? If so, you understand yields, leverage and prices together.
