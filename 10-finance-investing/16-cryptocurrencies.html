Cryptocurrencies. An asset with no earnings, no issuer and no floor, worth whatever enough people agree it is.

In 2010 a programmer paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas. Fifteen years later those coins would have been worth more than a billion dollars. In between, the same asset fell by more than 70 percent at least four times. Cryptocurrencies are digital tokens recorded on a shared public ledger, and as an investment they are unlike anything else: no company behind them, no profits, no interest, only a fixed or programmed supply and a crowd's belief. This file looks at them as an asset, which is how most people actually meet them.

Question 1 · What is it?

A token on a ledger that nobody controls

A cryptocurrency is an entry in a public database kept by thousands of computers. Owning one means holding the secret key that lets you move that entry.

Chapter 01

Ten thousand coins for two pizzas

On 31 October 2008, weeks after the collapse of Lehman Brothers, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper describing "a peer-to-peer electronic cash system". In January 2009 the Bitcoin network went live. Nobody knows for certain who Nakamoto was, and the coins believed to belong to that person or group have never been moved.

For its first years bitcoin had almost no price. On 22 May 2010 a programmer in Florida, Laszlo Hanyecz, paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas, and fans still celebrate the date as "Bitcoin Pizza Day". The trade shows two things. First, a cryptocurrency starts with no value at all and gets one only when people decide to accept it. Second, the value can change by a factor of millions in a decade, in a way no ordinary asset ever has.

Chapter 02

What you actually own

A bitcoin is not a file on your phone. It is a balance recorded on the blockchain, a public ledger copied across thousands of computers around the world. What you hold is a private key, a very long secret number. Anyone with the key can move the coins. Anyone without it cannot, ever. There is no bank to call and no password reset.

Software that stores keys is called a wallet. You can keep the key yourself, on your phone or on a small offline device called a hardware wallet. This is self-custody: fully under your control, and fully your responsibility. Estimates suggest that several million bitcoins, a large share of all that exist, are lost forever because owners forgot passwords, threw away drives or died without passing on their keys.

Most people instead buy through an exchange, a company that holds the keys for them. That is far easier, and it brings back the thing crypto was designed to remove: a trusted middleman who can fail, freeze your account or steal. Crypto users have a saying for this: "not your keys, not your coins". Chapter 06 shows why it matters.

Chapter 03

Not one asset but several

"Crypto" covers thousands of tokens. They fall into a few families, and they carry very different risks.

Bitcoin alone has usually made up around half of the total value of all crypto assets, and the top handful make up most of the rest. Talking about "crypto" as one investment is like talking about "shares" as if Apple and a bankrupt start-up were the same thing.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A fixed supply, a moving crowd, and a lot of borrowed money

Crypto prices are set by supply and demand like any other price. What is unusual is that the supply is written in code and the demand has almost nothing to anchor it.

Chapter 04

Where the price comes from

Start with supply. New bitcoins are created as a reward for miners, who run powerful computers to add new blocks of transactions to the ledger. The reward is cut in half roughly every four years, in an event called the halving. The most recent was in April 2024. Because of these halvings, the total can never exceed 21 million, and more than nine tenths of those coins already exist. Ether works differently: it has no hard cap, and since 2022 it is secured by staking, where holders lock up coins as a guarantee instead of using mining machines.

Now demand. A share's price is anchored, however loosely, by the profits the company is expected to make. A bond's price is anchored by its interest payments. A bitcoin produces nothing. Its price depends only on how much others will pay for it later. That makes it closer to gold, or to a collectable, than to a share. Without cash flows to anchor it, nothing stops the price from going very high or very low. Supporters say the anchor is scarcity plus a growing network of users. Critics say it is a story that can stop being believed.

A useful comparison is a language. English has no value in itself, but it is very useful because so many people already speak it, and each new speaker makes it more useful to everyone else. This is a network effect, and bitcoin's supporters argue it works the same way: the more people hold, accept and build on it, the harder it is for a rival to replace it. The weakness of the comparison is that nobody sells their English in a panic. A network held together by expected price gains can shrink as quickly as it grew.

Chapter 05

Why it swings so hard

Bitcoin's history is a series of booms and crashes, each larger in dollars than the last.

Bitcoin's big cycles, approximately
  2013   rises above 1,000 dollars       2014-15  falls about 80%  (Mt. Gox collapses)
  2017   nears 20,000 dollars            2018     falls over 80%
  2021   peaks near 69,000 dollars       2022     falls over 75%  (Terra, Celsius, FTX)
  2024   passes 100,000 dollars          2025     new record highs

  pattern: long rise -> euphoria -> crash of 70-85% -> slow rebuild -> higher high
  (past cycles say nothing certain about the next one)

Three things drive the size of the swings. First, there is no anchor, as Chapter 04 explained. Second, much of the trading uses leverage, borrowed money. Some exchanges have offered leverage of 100 to 1, so a small fall forces automatic selling, called liquidation, which pushes the price down further and triggers more liquidations. Third, crypto trades every hour of every day, often in thin markets, so a big seller at three in the morning on a Sunday can move the price sharply.

Volume 38's chapter on bubbles (Bubbles, and why they keep happening) describes the pattern: a real innovation, prices rising for good reasons, then buying because prices are rising. Whether crypto as a whole is a bubble is fiercely argued. What is not argued is that it has gone through several bubble-shaped cycles.

Chapter 06

The year the middlemen failed

Crypto promised a financial system without trusted middlemen. In 2022 its middlemen collapsed in a chain reaction.

In May, a stablecoin called TerraUSD, which kept its dollar value not through reserves but through an algorithm linked to a sister token, Luna, lost its peg. Holders rushed out, the algorithm printed ever more Luna, and both tokens fell to almost nothing within days, wiping out tens of billions of dollars. Lenders and funds that had bet on Terra were hit next. The crypto lender Celsius froze withdrawals in June and later went bankrupt. A large crypto hedge fund, Three Arrows Capital, collapsed soon after.

In November came the biggest failure. FTX, one of the world's largest exchanges, run by Sam Bankman-Fried, turned out to have used customers' deposits to fund bets by a trading firm he also controlled. When customers asked for their money back, it was not there. FTX went bankrupt within days. Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison.

None of these failures was a failure of the blockchain itself. Bitcoin's ledger kept working perfectly throughout. They were failures of old-fashioned things: lending money you did not have, mixing customer funds with your own, and promising stability you could not deliver. The same mistakes have sunk banks for centuries, which is why Financial crises reads so similarly.

Chapter 07

Stablecoins, the quiet giant

The most used crypto tokens are not the exciting ones. They are stablecoins, which aim to stay worth exactly one dollar. The issuer receives a dollar, issues a token, and holds the dollar in reserves, mostly cash and short-term US government bonds. By 2025 stablecoins in circulation were worth well over 200 billion dollars, most of it in Tether and USD Coin.

People use them to move money between exchanges, to hold dollars in countries where the local currency is unstable, and to send payments across borders. The issuers earn interest on the reserves, which has made the business very profitable when interest rates are high. The risk is the old one: if holders doubt the reserves, they all ask for their dollars at once, a classic bank run. Stablecoins as money get their own treatment in Banking · Digital currencies.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

From internet curiosity to Wall Street product

Crypto now sits in pension funds, company balance sheets and national laws. It also remains the payment method of choice for ransomware and many scams. Both facts matter.

Chapter 08

The arrival of the big money

For most of its life, buying bitcoin meant opening an account on a crypto exchange. In January 2024 the US securities regulator, the SEC, approved the first spot bitcoin exchange-traded funds, ordinary funds traded on the stock market that hold actual bitcoin. Some of the world's largest asset managers launched them, and they drew tens of billions of dollars within months. Funds holding ether followed in mid-2024.

This changed who owns crypto. A financial adviser can now put a small slice of bitcoin into a client's normal investment account. Some companies hold bitcoin as a reserve asset, and one software company, Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy), turned itself into a vehicle for buying it with borrowed and newly raised money. The more crypto is held by the mainstream, the more its price moves with the mainstream. Since about 2020 bitcoin has often risen and fallen together with technology shares, which weakens the old claim that it protects you when everything else falls.

The arrival of funds also changed the practical risks for ordinary buyers. A regulated fund removes the danger of losing a key or trusting a dubious exchange, at the cost of a yearly fee and of never holding the coins yourself. It does nothing about the price risk. A fund holding bitcoin falls exactly as far as bitcoin does. And tax authorities in most countries now treat crypto as property or an asset, so selling at a profit, or even swapping one token for another, can create a tax bill, a detail many early buyers discovered too late. The rules vary by country.

Chapter 09

Where people actually use it as money

In rich countries crypto is mostly an investment. In some others it is a way to escape a weak currency or slow, costly transfers. People in countries with high inflation or strict limits on buying dollars, such as Argentina, Nigeria or Turkey, have used stablecoins to hold digital dollars on their phones. Migrant workers sometimes use crypto to send money home more cheaply than through traditional transfer firms.

The most famous national experiment was El Salvador, which made bitcoin legal tender in September 2021, obliging businesses to accept it. Uptake for everyday payments stayed low, and in early 2025, as part of a loan deal with the International Monetary Fund, the country made acceptance voluntary. Making people use a volatile asset as money turned out to be much harder than letting them choose it.

Chapter 10

Fraud, theft and crime

Crypto payments are hard to reverse and can cross borders in seconds. That makes them attractive to criminals. Ransomware gangs demand payment in crypto. So-called "pig butchering" scams build a friendship or romance over weeks and then steer the victim to a fake trading platform that shows invented profits until the money is gone. Hackers have stolen billions of dollars from exchanges and from the software bridges that link blockchains.

Two balancing facts. First, most crypto activity is not criminal, and blockchain analysis firms report that illicit transactions are a small share of the total. Second, a public ledger is in some ways easier to trace than cash: investigators have recovered large sums by following coins across the chain. But for an ordinary person, the practical rule is simple. Anyone who asks you to pay a bill, a fine or a fee in crypto is almost certainly a fraudster, as Volume 47 explains.

Chapter 12

The argument about what it is worth

Under every price chart is one unresolved question: does bitcoin have a fundamental value at all? Serious people disagree.

The case: digital gold

Gold also pays nothing, and it has held value for thousands of years because people agree it is scarce and hard to fake. Bitcoin is scarcer, easier to move and impossible to counterfeit. If it captures even part of gold's role as a store of value, today's price could be justified. Its survival through repeated crashes, the argument goes, is evidence that the belief is durable.

The case against: nothing inside

A share is a claim on profits; a bond on interest; a house on rent. Bitcoin is a claim on nothing but the next buyer. Gold has thousands of years of history and industrial uses; bitcoin has about fifteen years. If belief fades, there is no floor. Critics such as Warren Buffett have long said they would not own it for that reason.

Both sides agree on the practical implication. If you hold crypto, size it so that losing almost all of it would hurt but not ruin you. That is the rule most mainstream advisers apply, and it fits the evidence of the past fifteen years better than either "it will replace the dollar" or "it is worthless".

Chapter 14

The irony of success

Bitcoin was born as a protest against banks in the wreckage of 2008. Its future now looks more and more like a product sold by banks. Regulated funds hold it, regulated firms custody it, and governments write rules for it. That brings safety for ordinary investors, who are less likely to lose everything to a fraudulent exchange. It also brings the asset closer to the system it was meant to escape.

The likeliest outcome is not that crypto replaces money or that it vanishes. It is that a few assets, above all bitcoin and the main stablecoins, settle into a permanent place in finance, while thousands of other tokens keep appearing and disappearing. The technology may end up mattering most where it is least visible: in the plumbing that moves dollars and securities, rather than in the coins that make headlines.

The core idea

A cryptocurrency is a token on a public ledger, owned by whoever holds its private key. Its supply is set by code, but its demand has no anchor in profits or interest, so prices swing hugely. Most disasters have come from the middlemen, not the ledgers.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million, halving roughly every four years.
  • It has fallen 70 percent or more at least four times.
  • US spot bitcoin funds were approved in January 2024.
The common mistake

Assuming "on a blockchain" means "safe". The ledger can be perfect while the exchange holding your coins is insolvent, as FTX's customers found out.

Test yourself

Can you explain why bitcoin and a share of a company are valued in completely different ways? If you can, you have understood the core of the crypto debate.
