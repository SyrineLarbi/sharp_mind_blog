Diversification. The rare case where you can cut risk without paying for it in return.

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" sounds like common sense, and it is. What is less obvious is that the advice has a precise mathematical form, and that it delivers something almost unique in finance. Spreading money across things that do not move together lowers the swings of the whole without lowering its expected reward. Understanding why it works also shows its limits: it protects you from one company's disaster, but not from the whole market's.

Question 1 · What is it?

Many baskets, chosen so they do not all drop at once

Diversification means holding a mix of investments whose fortunes are not tightly linked, so that a disaster in one is cushioned by the others. The key is not the number of baskets. It is how differently they behave.

Chapter 01

An old idea

The instinct is ancient. A passage in the Talmud advises dividing one's wealth into three parts: a third in land, a third in business, and a third kept in reserve. Merchants who shipped goods by sea spread their cargoes across several ships, so that one wreck would not ruin them. Shakespeare gives the idea to Antonio, the merchant in The Merchant of Venice, who explains to his friends why he is not worried about his fortune.

My ventures are not in one bottom trusted,
Nor to one place; nor is my whole estate
Upon the fortune of this present year.

The Merchant of Venice · Act I, scene 1

Antonio names three kinds of spread in three lines: different ships ("bottoms"), different places, and different times. Then, in the play, news arrives that all his ships have been lost at once, and he cannot repay the debt he owes. Four centuries early, Shakespeare captured both the strength of diversification and its weakness: it protects you only if the baskets really do fail independently.

Chapter 02

What it does, and what it does not do

The file on risk and return split investment risk into two kinds. Specific risk belongs to one company or one asset: a factory fire, a fraud, a failed product. Market risk hits nearly everything at once: a recession or a financial crisis. Diversification attacks the first kind. It does almost nothing against the second.

What it does

Reduces the chance that a single bad event wipes you out. Narrows the range of outcomes. Keeps you holding the rare big winners that drive returns. And it does this without lowering expected return, because the market does not pay you for specific risk in the first place.

What it does not do

It does not stop the whole portfolio falling in a crash. It does not raise your expected return. It does not guarantee a profit. And it cannot help if everything you hold depends on the same underlying thing, however many names are on the list.

That second point in the first card is why diversification is often called the only free lunch in investing, a phrase widely attributed to the economist Harry Markowitz. Most ways to reduce risk cost you return. This one, up to a point, does not.

Chapter 03

The cost of one basket

In 2001 the American energy company Enron collapsed in one of the largest corporate frauds of its time. Thousands of employees lost their jobs. Many also lost their retirement savings, because their workplace pension accounts were heavily invested in Enron's own shares. By most accounts, more than half of the assets in the company's main retirement plan were Enron stock. When the shares became nearly worthless, income and savings disappeared together.

The Enron workers made a common mistake in an extreme form. Their job and their savings depended on the same company, so one failure hit both at once. The same logic applies to anyone whose savings are mostly in their employer's shares, their home country's market, or the industry they work in. Your human capital, the value of your future earnings, is already a large, undiversified bet. Sensible investing spreads the rest of your wealth away from it.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Correlation is the engine

The benefit of combining assets depends on one number: how closely their returns move together. Get that idea, and the rest is arithmetic.

Chapter 04

Umbrellas and ice cream

Imagine a seaside town with two businesses. One sells umbrellas, which does well in rainy summers. The other sells ice cream, which does well in sunny ones. Each business alone has a risky year depending on the weather. Own both, and the weather barely matters: what you lose on one, you gain on the other. Your income becomes steady, though neither business changed.

The statistical name for how two things move together is correlation. It runs from plus 1 to minus 1.

The correlation scale
   +1  move in perfect lockstep            no diversification benefit at all
   +0.5  usually move the same way         some benefit
    0   unrelated                          strong benefit
   -0.5  usually move opposite ways        very strong benefit
   -1  perfect mirror images               risk can in theory cancel completely

   umbrellas and ice cream : close to -1
   two big oil companies   : strongly positive
   shares and gold         : low, and unstable over time

Real investments almost never have correlations of minus 1. Most shares are positively correlated with each other, because they all respond to the same economy. But any correlation below plus 1 reduces risk when assets are combined, and the lower it is, the bigger the reduction.

Chapter 05

Why the whole is calmer than its parts

Here is the surprising part. If you combine two assets that each swing by 20 per cent a year, and their correlation is below 1, the combination swings by less than 20 per cent. Their average return stays the same, because the average of two returns is just the average. But the swings partly cancel. Expected returns add up simply. Risks do not, because some of the ups and downs offset each other.

Add more and more shares, and the specific risk keeps shrinking, but with diminishing effect. The first few extra holdings help a lot. After a few dozen, each new one helps very little.

Portfolio risk as you add more shares, roughly
   risk
    |*
    | *
    |  *
    |    *
    |       *   *
    |              *    *     *     *     *     *    <-- specific risk mostly gone
    | - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -  <-- market risk: the floor
    +-------------------------------------------------- number of shares
      1    5    10        30            100         500

Older studies suggested that ten to twenty shares removed most specific risk. Later research found that individual companies have become more volatile relative to the market, so more holdings are needed for the same effect, and that a portfolio of a few dozen shares still has a real chance of missing the rare big winners. No number of shares gets you below the floor of market risk. To lower that, you need different kinds of assets, not just more of the same kind.

Chapter 06

Markowitz and the efficient frontier

In 1952 a young economist trained at the University of Chicago, Harry Markowitz, published a paper called "Portfolio Selection". Before him, investors judged each holding on its own merits. Markowitz showed that what matters is how each holding affects the risk and return of the whole portfolio, which depends on its correlations with everything else. A risky asset can make a portfolio safer, if it tends to rise when the rest falls.

He showed that for any level of risk there is a mix of assets that gives the highest expected return, and for any target return there is a mix with the lowest risk. The set of these best mixes is called the efficient frontier. Any portfolio below it is wasting risk: you could get the same return with less. This work, now called modern portfolio theory, earned Markowitz a share of the 1990 Nobel prize in economics, alongside William Sharpe and Merton Miller. Its weakness is practical: it needs estimates of future returns and correlations, and small errors in those inputs can produce wild, unstable recommendations.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Because the future is lopsided and unknowable

Diversification matters for three reasons: share returns are dominated by a few winners, whole countries can fail, and it gives you a simple discipline for acting against your own emotions.

Chapter 08

The winners you cannot pick in advance

The file on stocks described research by Hendrik Bessembinder showing that most individual US shares since 1926 did worse than short-term government bills, while a small minority produced all of the market's net gains. This shape, many losers and a few giant winners, is the strongest practical argument for diversification.

Picture it as a lottery where a few tickets win enormous prizes. If you buy five tickets, you will probably lose. If you buy every ticket, you are certain to hold the winners. A concentrated portfolio is a bet that you can identify the rare winners in advance. A diversified one admits that you cannot, and collects their gains anyway. The investor Warren Buffett has argued that wide diversification makes little sense for someone who truly knows what they are doing. He has also advised ordinary investors to buy a low-cost index fund, which implies that he thinks very few do.

Chapter 09

Countries can fail too

Diversifying across companies in one country leaves you exposed to that country. Investors in Russian shares in 1917 and in Chinese shares in 1949 lost essentially everything when markets were shut and property was taken. Less dramatically, a Japanese investor who put everything into Japan's stock market at its 1989 peak waited more than three decades for the price index to recover.

Yet most people hold far more of their home market than its share of the world. This home bias feels safe because the names are familiar, and sometimes there are good reasons, such as currency and tax. But familiarity is not safety. The country you happen to live in is a single basket. One complication in the mid-2020s is that a global index is itself heavily concentrated, with American companies making up around 60 per cent or more of its value.

Chapter 10

Rebalancing: diversification as a habit

A diversified portfolio does not stay diversified on its own. Suppose you choose 60 per cent shares and 40 per cent bonds. After a few strong years for shares, you might hold 75 per cent shares without having decided to. Your risk has quietly risen.

Rebalancing means periodically selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged, to return to your chosen mix. It forces you to do what feels wrong: sell a little of the winner, buy a little of the loser. In practice that means trimming after booms and adding after falls, the opposite of what fear and greed suggest. The main purpose of rebalancing is to keep your risk where you chose it, not to boost returns, though in choppy markets it can sometimes do both. Many investors rebalance once a year, or when the mix drifts by more than a set amount. The psychology behind why this is hard is in Why money decisions go wrong.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A free lunch with a fine print

Diversification is close to universal advice. It still has costs, limits, and one dangerous moment when it stops working.

Chapter 11

Weighing it up

FeatureThe advantageThe disadvantage
Removes specific riskNo single failure can ruin you.You also give up the chance of a single huge success transforming your wealth.
Same expected return, less riskThe closest thing to a free lunch in finance.Only up to the floor of market risk. Beyond it, lower risk costs return.
Holds the winnersGuarantees exposure to whichever companies turn out to be giants.Guarantees exposure to the losers too. You will always own something that is doing badly.
Smoother rideEasier to stay invested through bad periods.A diversified portfolio always lags the best single asset, which can feel like failure.
Simple to getOne global index fund delivers most of it cheaply.Adding exotic "diversifiers" often adds fees and complexity without much benefit.

The investor Peter Lynch coined the word "diworsification" in the late 1980s for companies that buy unrelated businesses and become worse at everything. The same thing happens to portfolios. Holding twelve funds that all own the same large companies is not diversification. It is duplication with extra fees.

Chapter 12

When everything falls together

Correlations are not fixed. In calm times, different markets and assets move fairly independently. In a panic, investors sell whatever they can, to raise cash or cover losses, and many correlations jump towards plus 1. In late 2008, shares in almost every country, corporate bonds, property and many commodities fell together. Diversification across risky assets tends to weaken exactly when you need it most.

In that crisis, the assets that held up were the safest ones: high-quality government bonds and cash. That is why they remain in most diversified portfolios even though they pay less. But 2022 showed that even this pairing can fail. When inflation was the problem, shares and government bonds fell together, as the file on bonds explains. The honest summary is that no combination of assets protects against every kind of crisis. Diversification reduces the damage from most shocks. It cannot remove the risk of the market as a whole.

Chapter 14

The humility at the centre

Under the mathematics, diversification is an admission. It says: I do not know which company, country or asset will do best, and I do not trust myself to find out in advance. That is uncomfortable, because it means accepting that you will never own only the winners.

The alternative is worse. The history of investing is full of people who were sure: the employees who held only their employer, the investors who bought only one country's boom, the funds with one big bet magnified by borrowing. Diversification is not a way to get rich quickly. It is a way to make sure that no single mistake, yours or the world's, can finish you, so that time and compounding get the chance to do their work.

The core idea

Combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated lowers risk without lowering expected return, because the swings partly cancel. Diversification removes specific risk but not market risk, and in panics correlations rise, so it weakens just when it is most needed.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Correlation runs from +1 (lockstep) to −1 (mirror image). Anything below +1 helps.
  • Markowitz's "Portfolio Selection" was published in 1952 and won a share of the 1990 Nobel prize.
  • Most specific risk fades after a few dozen holdings. Market risk never does.
The common mistake

Counting funds instead of exposures. Ten funds holding the same big companies is one basket, with ten sets of fees.

Test yourself

Why can adding a risky asset make a portfolio less risky? If you can explain it with the umbrella and ice cream shops, you understand correlation.
