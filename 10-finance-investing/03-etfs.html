ETFs. A whole basket of investments, wrapped so it trades like a single share.

An exchange-traded fund is a container. Inside it can be five hundred shares, a thousand bonds, a pile of gold bars or almost anything else. The container itself is listed on a stock exchange, so you can buy or sell it in seconds, like one share of one company. That simple packaging idea, launched in North America around 1990, became one of the most successful financial products ever made. Understanding how the wrapper works also explains its hidden weak points.

Question 1 · What is it?

A fund with a ticker symbol

An ETF is a pooled investment fund whose units are bought and sold on a stock exchange during the trading day. The word to remember is wrapper: the ETF is the package, not the contents.

Chapter 01

The Spider that changed investing

Before ETFs, an ordinary person who wanted to own a slice of the whole stock market had two poor choices. They could buy hundreds of shares one by one, paying a commission on each. Or they could buy a mutual fund, a pooled fund run by a manager, which was priced only once a day after the market closed.

In 1990 the Toronto Stock Exchange launched a product that let investors trade a basket of large Canadian companies as one unit. In January 1993 the American Stock Exchange and the asset manager State Street launched the SPDR S&P 500 Trust, a single listed security that held all the shares in the S&P 500 index. Traders nicknamed it the "Spider". It still trades under the ticker SPY and is one of the most traded securities in the world. For the first time, anyone could buy the whole American stock market in one click, at any moment of the trading day.

Growth was slow at first, then enormous. By the mid-2020s ETFs worldwide held well over 10 trillion dollars, and thousands of different ETFs were listed in the US and Europe alone.

Chapter 02

ETF, mutual fund, index fund: clearing up the words

These three words get mixed up constantly, because they describe different things. "ETF" and "mutual fund" describe the wrapper, how you buy and sell. "Index fund" describes the strategy, what the fund holds.

ETFTraditional mutual fund
Where you buyOn a stock exchange, through any brokerFrom the fund company or a platform
When the price is setContinuously, all dayOnce a day, after the close
Who you trade withAnother investor, usuallyThe fund itself
StrategyMostly index-tracking, but active ETFs are growingEither active or index-tracking
Typical minimumOne unit, or a fraction on some appsOften a set minimum amount

So an index fund can be an ETF or a mutual fund. And an ETF can track an index or be actively managed. Most ETFs track an index, which is why the two ideas are often merged, but the wrapper and the strategy are separate choices. In Europe, the most common ETFs follow EU fund rules known as UCITS, and in the US they fall under rules set by the Securities and Exchange Commission. The details differ by country.

That range is part of the story. The first ETFs were simple, cheap and broad. Many newer ones are narrow, more expensive, and designed to catch a fashionable theme. The wrapper is the same. The contents can be completely different in risk.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Two markets at once, joined by arbitrage

An ETF lives on two levels. Ordinary investors trade units with each other on the exchange. Behind them, a few large firms create and destroy units in bulk. That hidden second level is what keeps the price honest.

Chapter 04

Creation and redemption

A traditional mutual fund grows when investors send cash and shrinks when they ask for it back. The fund itself has to buy or sell shares each time. An ETF works differently. Units are created and destroyed only by a small number of big financial firms called authorised participants, usually large banks and trading firms.

The two levels of an ETF
   LEVEL 2  the exchange (where you trade)
   ---------------------------------------------------------
     you  <-- buy / sell units -->  other investors, market makers

                      ^ units in            | units out
                      |                     v
   LEVEL 1  the primary market (only authorised participants)
   ---------------------------------------------------------
     CREATE:  AP hands the fund a basket of the real shares
              -> fund hands AP a large block of new ETF units
     REDEEM:  AP hands back a block of units
              -> fund hands AP the real shares, units destroyed

Notice that in the classic version, no cash changes hands between the fund and the authorised participant. They swap shares for units and back. This is called an in-kind exchange, and it matters for costs and, in the US, for taxes (Chapter 07).

Chapter 05

Why the price stays close to the value

Every ETF has two numbers. The net asset value, or NAV, is what everything inside the fund is actually worth, divided by the number of units. The market price is what people are paying for a unit on the exchange right now. Nothing forces these two to be equal. Yet for big ETFs they almost always are, within a fraction of a per cent. The reason is arbitrage: buying something cheap in one place and selling it dear in another.

Suppose eager buyers push the ETF's price 1 per cent above its NAV. An authorised participant can buy the underlying shares cheaply, hand them to the fund for new units, and sell those units at the higher price, pocketing the difference. Those new units increase supply and push the price back down. If the price falls below NAV, the process runs in reverse: buy cheap units, redeem them for the more valuable shares, sell the shares. The profit motive of a few large firms is the invisible spring that pulls the price back to the value.

This works best when the underlying assets are easy to trade, as with large shares. When they are not, the spring weakens, and the price can drift away from the published NAV. Chapter 09 shows what happened when that was tested.

Chapter 06

Holding the real thing, or a promise of it

Most ETFs use physical replication: they actually own the shares or bonds in the index, either all of them or a large representative sample. Some, more common in Europe, use synthetic replication. The fund holds a different basket of assets and signs a contract called a swap with a bank, which promises to pay the index's return. That can track hard-to-reach markets more cheaply, but it adds a new risk: the bank on the other side of the contract. Rules limit that exposure and require collateral.

Many physical ETFs also lend some of their shares to other investors, usually short-sellers, in return for a fee and collateral. This securities lending earns a little extra income that can offset costs. It carries a small risk if a borrower fails and the collateral falls short. Fund documents state whether and how much a fund lends.

Chapter 07

The American tax advantage

In the United States, when a mutual fund sells shares at a profit to pay departing investors, the gain is often passed on to all remaining investors as a taxable distribution. They can owe tax even if they never sold anything. Because most US ETFs handle redemptions in kind, handing over shares instead of selling them, they can pass out their lowest-cost shares and rarely realise gains themselves. As a result, many US equity ETFs distribute little or no capital gains for years.

Varies by country

This particular advantage comes from US tax rules. In many other countries, including much of Europe, funds are taxed differently and the ETF wrapper may bring no special tax benefit. Some countries treat foreign-domiciled funds harshly. Local rules matter more than the wrapper. This file is general education, not tax or financial advice.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cheaper investing for everyone, and new questions for markets

ETFs lowered the cost of owning a diversified portfolio to almost nothing. They also became so large that their own behaviour now matters for the markets they hold.

Chapter 08

A price war that savers won

The yearly fee a fund charges is called its expense ratio or ongoing charge, shown as a percentage of what you hold. In the 1980s and 1990s, many actively managed funds charged around 1 to 2 per cent a year. Competition among ETF providers pushed the cost of the biggest broad index ETFs down to a few hundredths of a per cent by the 2020s. On a holding of 10,000, that is a few currency units a year instead of a hundred or more.

Small percentages compound. Over thirty years, a 1.5 per cent yearly fee can take a large share of the final value compared with a fee near zero, because the fee is charged every year on a growing balance. Volume 38 calls fees "the quiet killer". ETFs made that killer much quieter for anyone willing to buy plain, broad funds.

They also changed access. Because an ETF trades like a share, any brokerage account can hold one. Fractional shares and app-based monthly savings plans, popular in Germany and elsewhere in Europe in the 2020s, let people invest small sums regularly into a global portfolio. For a young saver, a cheap global equity ETF is now one of the simplest diversified investments available. How it fits into a whole plan is covered in Portfolio management.

Chapter 09

The March 2020 stress test

Critics had long warned that bond ETFs hid a mismatch. The ETF trades in seconds. Many corporate bonds trade rarely, sometimes not for days. What happens when everyone wants out at once?

In March 2020, as the Covid panic hit, it happened. Corporate bond markets nearly froze. Several large bond ETFs traded at prices well below their published NAV, at times by around 5 per cent or more. One view was that the ETFs had broken. The other, which many researchers later supported, was that the ETF price was the more honest number. The NAV was calculated from stale bond prices that no longer reflected what anyone would actually pay. On this view, the ETF was the only place where real prices were still being discovered. The discounts closed when the US Federal Reserve announced it would buy corporate bonds, including through ETFs. Both sides still argue over what would have happened without that rescue.

Chapter 10

When the wrapper moves the contents

As ETFs grew, a new question appeared. When money pours into an ETF, the fund must buy every share in its index, regardless of price or prospects. When a company joins a major index, ETFs and index funds must buy it. Some researchers argue this makes shares in the same index move together more and weakens the link between a share's price and its own business. Others find the effects small, and note that active investors still set most prices through their trading. This is a genuinely open research question, and it is taken up again in Index funds.

A great wrapper with sharp edges

The same features that make ETFs useful, instant trading and endless variety, also make them easy to misuse.

Chapter 12

The dangerous corners: leverage, decay and notes

A leveraged ETF aims to deliver two or three times an index's move each day. An inverse ETF aims to deliver the opposite of each day's move. The words "each day" hide a trap. Over longer periods these funds do not deliver two times, or minus one times, the index's overall return.

Two days, an index and its 2x leveraged ETF
                     index                     2x daily ETF
   start             100                       100
   day 1   +10 %     110                       +20 %  ->  120
   day 2   -10 %      99   (down 1 %)          -20 %  ->   96   (down 4 %)

The index lost 1 per cent. The leveraged fund lost 4 per cent, not 2. In choppy markets that go up and down without a clear direction, this drag grows every day, a pattern often called volatility decay. The fund providers say clearly that these products are for short-term trading. Many buyers hold them for months anyway.

A related product is the exchange-traded note, or ETN. It looks like an ETF on a screen, but it is not a fund that owns assets. It is an unsecured debt of the bank that issued it, promising to pay an index's return. If the bank fails, holders become ordinary creditors. In February 2018 a sudden spike in market volatility wiped out most of the value of some popular notes that had bet on calm markets, within a day. The lesson is that the letters "ET" say how a product trades, not what it holds or how risky it is.

Chapter 14

Too many choices?

There are now thousands of ETFs in the US alone, and the newest are rarely the broad, cheap funds that made ETFs popular. They track narrow themes, use leverage, or pay high monthly income through option strategies. Many small ETFs close within a few years because too little money arrives. Research on investor behaviour suggests people tend to buy thematic funds after a theme has already risen strongly, which often means buying near a peak.

The healthy reading of the ETF story is a wrapper that brought cheap diversification to almost everyone. The unhealthy reading is a wrapper that turned every passing fashion into a ticker symbol. Both are true. The useful habit is to ask two questions of any ETF: what exactly is inside, and what does it cost each year? If the answers are "the whole market" and "almost nothing", you are using the invention as it was meant to be used.

The core idea

An ETF is a fund wrapper that trades on an exchange. Authorised participants create and redeem units in bulk, and arbitrage keeps the market price close to the net asset value. The wrapper says how you trade, not what you own.

The numbers worth knowing
  • The first big US ETF, SPY, launched in January 1993.
  • Broad index ETFs can cost a few hundredths of a per cent a year.
  • Global ETF assets passed 10 trillion dollars by the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Assuming "ETF" means safe or diversified. A leveraged, thematic or bitcoin ETF is still an ETF, and its risk is the risk of what is inside.

Test yourself

An ETF trades 1 per cent above the value of its holdings. Who profits from closing that gap, and how? If you can explain it, you understand creation, redemption and arbitrage.

The words, in plain words
ETF
Exchange-traded fund: a pooled fund whose units trade on a stock exchange.
Mutual fund
A traditional pooled fund bought from and sold back to the fund, priced once a day.
Wrapper
The legal and trading structure of an investment, as opposed to what it holds.
Authorised participant
A large firm allowed to create and redeem ETF units directly with the fund.
In-kind
Paid in shares or bonds rather than cash.
Net asset value (NAV)
The value of everything a fund holds, per unit.
Arbitrage
Profiting from a price gap between two markets, which tends to close the gap.
Physical replication
Tracking an index by owning its actual holdings.
Synthetic replication
Tracking an index through a swap contract with a bank.
Expense ratio
The yearly fee, as a percentage of your holding.
Leveraged ETF
A fund aiming for a multiple of an index's daily move.
ETN
