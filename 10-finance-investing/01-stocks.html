Stocks. A small, legal slice of a business, priced every second by strangers.

A share looks like a number on a screen that goes up and down. Behind the number is something very concrete: a claim on part of a real company's future profits, plus a small vote in how it is run. Once you see a share as a piece of a business rather than a lottery ticket, most of the confusing behaviour of stock markets starts to make sense.

Question 1 · What is it?

Part-ownership of a business, cut into small pieces

A stock, also called a share or an equity, is a unit of ownership in a company. It is one of the oldest financial inventions still in daily use, and its basic design has barely changed in four hundred years.

Chapter 01

Ships, spices and the first shareholders

In the early 1600s a voyage from Europe to the spice islands of Asia could take years. Many ships never came back. A single merchant who paid for a whole voyage could be rich or ruined by one storm. The obvious fix was to share the risk: many people each pay for a small part, and each takes a small part of whatever comes home.

In 1602 the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials VOC, took that idea further. Its investors did not fund one voyage. They funded the company itself, for many years and many voyages. And because people would not want their money locked up for that long, their stakes could be sold to someone else in Amsterdam. That combination is usually described as the first modern share: a permanent stake in a business, which you can sell to another person without the business having to give the money back.

That last point is the clever part. The company keeps the capital and can plan for decades. The investor can still get out at any time, by finding a buyer. The trading of these stakes between investors is what we now call the stock market.

Chapter 02

What you actually own

If you own one share out of a company's 100 million, you own one hundred-millionth of it. That does not mean you can walk in and take a chair. It gives you a bundle of rights, and it is worth knowing exactly what they are.

RightWhat it meansThe catch
A claim on profitsYou own a slice of whatever the company earns, whether it pays it out or keeps itYou get paid last. Staff, suppliers, lenders and tax come first
DividendsCash the board chooses to pay out to owners, usually per share, often quarterly or twice a yearOptional. The board can cut or stop them
A voteOn the board of directors, big mergers, and some pay decisions, usually one vote per shareSome companies issue shares with weaker votes or none
Limited liabilityIf the company collapses owing billions, you lose what you paid for the share and nothing moreThat amount can be all of it
The right to sellYou can sell your share to anyone willing to buy itAt whatever price they will pay that day

Two words from this table matter most. The first is residual. Shareholders own what is left after everyone else is paid. In good years that leftover can be huge. In bad years it can be zero. The second is limited liability. Before it became normal in the nineteenth century, owners of a failed business could lose their houses. Limited liability is the reason ordinary people can own a piece of a company they will never visit.

Chapter 03

Why a company sells pieces of itself

A young company that needs money has two basic choices. It can borrow, which means paying interest whatever happens. Or it can sell ownership, which means giving up part of the future profits but owing nothing if things go badly. Borrowing is cheaper when you are sure of your income. Selling shares is safer when you are not. That is why risky, fast-growing firms lean on shares and steady utilities lean on loans.

The first time a company sells shares to the general public is called an initial public offering, or IPO. Banks help set the price, find the first buyers, and the shares start trading on an exchange. From then on the company is "listed" or "public". That sale, where the company receives the money, happens in the primary market. Almost all trading after that happens in the secondary market, where investors swap shares with each other and the company receives nothing at all. When you buy a share of a famous firm through an app today, your money goes to another investor, not to the business.

So why does a company care about its share price at all? Because a high price makes it cheaper to raise money later, and because staff and managers are often paid partly in shares.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A marketplace, a price, and three sources of return

The machinery of trading is mostly plumbing. The interesting part is what the price is trying to measure, and why your eventual return can be split into three clean pieces.

Chapter 04

The marketplace: buyers, sellers and the order book

New York's stock exchange traces its origin to 1792, when a group of brokers signed an agreement under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street. For most of the next two centuries trading meant people shouting on a floor. Today it is almost entirely electronic, and the core of every exchange is a list called the order book.

One share's order book, simplified
   people willing to SELL (asks)        people willing to BUY (bids)
   ----------------------------         ----------------------------
   500 shares at 100.04                 300 shares at 100.01
   200 shares at 100.03                 800 shares at 100.00
   100 shares at 100.02   <-- spread -->  400 shares at  99.99

   lowest ask 100.02  -  highest bid 100.01  =  spread of 0.01
   "the price" on your screen is simply the last trade that happened

The highest price any buyer is offering is the bid. The lowest price any seller will accept is the ask. The gap between them is the spread, a small hidden cost of trading. When you press "buy now" you take the cheapest ask. Big, popular companies have tiny spreads because so many people trade them. Small, obscure ones can have wide spreads.

Firms called market makers stand in the book on both sides all day, offering to buy slightly low and sell slightly high. They earn the spread, and in return there is almost always someone to trade with.

Chapter 05

What the price is trying to measure

Imagine a small bakery that will earn a profit of 10,000 a year for many years. What would you pay to own all of it? Not 10,000, because you would get that back in one year. Not a million, because you would wait a hundred years. Somewhere in between, depending on how sure you are of the profits, how fast they might grow, and what else you could do with your money. That is the whole logic of a share price. A share is worth the cash the business will hand its owners in the future, adjusted for how long you wait and how uncertain that cash is.

The adjustment for waiting is called discounting. Money in ten years is worth less than money today, because today's money could be earning interest in the meantime. So when interest rates rise, future profits are discounted more heavily and share prices tend to fall, especially for companies whose profits are mostly far in the future. This is one of the most important links in all of finance, and it is explored in Interest rates and markets.

A common shorthand is the price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E: the share price divided by the profit per share over a year. A P/E of 20 means investors are paying 20 years of current profit. A high P/E is not automatically "expensive". It may mean the crowd expects fast growth. A low P/E is not automatically a bargain. It may mean the crowd expects trouble. Methods for valuing companies more carefully live in Company valuation.

"In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run, it is a weighing machine."

Benjamin Graham · as retold by Warren Buffett

Graham's point is that daily prices reflect moods and fashions, a popularity vote. Over years, though, a share's value is pulled towards the real profits the company produces.

Chapter 06

Where the return comes from: three pieces

Over any long period, the total return from owning shares can be split into three parts. This split, popularised by the Vanguard founder John Bogle and others, is one of the most useful tools for thinking clearly about markets.

Total return, broken into its parts
   total return  =  dividend yield            (cash paid out each year)
                 +  growth in profits         (the business getting bigger)
                 +  change in valuation       (people paying more or less per unit of profit)

   the first two come from the business  -> slow, fairly reliable
   the third comes from the crowd's mood -> fast, and averages out to roughly nothing over very long spans

Here is why this matters. If shares rise 25 per cent in a year while company profits grew 5 per cent, most of that gain came from investors becoming more optimistic. That kind of gain cannot repeat forever, because people cannot become infinitely optimistic. Over decades, the return from shares tracks the return from the businesses. Over a single year, it tracks the mood.

Companies can also return cash by buying back their own shares and cancelling them, so each remaining share owns a slightly larger slice. See Dividends.

Chapter 07

"The market": how a crowd of prices becomes one number

When the news says "the market fell 2 per cent today", it means an index fell. An index is a basket of shares tracked as a single number. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, started in 1896, follows just 30 large American companies and is weighted, oddly, by share price. The S&P 500 follows about 500 large American companies weighted by their total market value, which is why a few giant technology firms now move it so much. The FTSE 100 in London, the DAX in Frankfurt, the CAC 40 in Paris and the Nikkei 225 in Tokyo do similar jobs for their countries. Global indexes, such as those from MSCI, cover thousands of companies in dozens of countries.

A company's size in this sense is its market capitalisation: share price times the number of shares (see Market capitalization). Buying a whole index cheaply is now one of the most common ways to invest at all, the subject of Index funds.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The engine of long-run wealth, and how unevenly it runs

Shares matter for three reasons: they have been the best-performing major asset over long periods, almost all of that return came from a small minority of companies, and stock markets decide who controls much of the world's business.

Chapter 08

The long record

Researchers such as Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton at the London Business School have reconstructed share returns back to 1900 for many countries. The broad finding is stable across their updates. Over the twentieth century and since, shares in most major markets beat bonds and cash by a wide margin, returning very roughly 3 to 7 per cent a year above inflation, depending on the country and the period chosen. The United States sits at the high end of that range.

Small differences in yearly return turn into large differences over a life, because returns compound. At 5 per cent above inflation, money roughly doubles in real terms every 14 years. Volume 38 explains the arithmetic in Interest, and the astonishing effect of compounding. The extra return that shares have paid over safer assets is called the equity risk premium. It is the reward for accepting that the ride is rough, and Question 4 shows how rough.

Be careful with averages

Long-run averages hide survivorship. The US and UK markets were winners, so they are studied most. Investors in Russia in 1917 and China in 1949 lost essentially everything when markets were closed and property seized. A historical average is a guide, not a promise. This file is general education, not financial advice.

Chapter 09

The few that carry the many

In 2018 the finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder published a study of every US-listed share from 1926 onward. The results surprised many professionals. Most individual shares, over their lifetimes, did worse than simply holding short-term US government bills. The overall market still did very well, because a small group of companies produced enormous gains. By his count, roughly 4 per cent of all listed companies accounted for all of the net wealth the US stock market created over that period.

This explains two puzzles at once. It explains why picking a handful of shares is so risky: the odds are that your handful does not include the rare giant winners. And it explains why owning the whole market works so well: you are guaranteed to hold the giants, whoever they turn out to be. Share returns form a long tail: many small losses and a few huge gains. That shape is the reason diversification is not just cautious but mathematically necessary.

Chapter 10

Ownership, power and the price signal

Stock markets are not only places to grow savings. They do three jobs for the economy.

They move capital

Companies with promising ideas can raise money from millions of strangers. Firms whose shares are valued highly can raise money cheaply. Firms the market doubts find it expensive.

They price information

A share price combines the views of everyone trading it. When news arrives, the price changes within seconds.

They assign control

Whoever owns the shares can, in principle, replace the board. That threat is meant to keep managers working for owners. Takeovers are the extreme version, covered in Mergers & acquisitions.

Who those owners are has changed. Much of the stock market is now held by pension funds, insurers and fund managers on behalf of ordinary savers, and a handful of giant index-fund managers vote on a large slice of big companies. Whether that concentration is healthy is a live political question. If you have a workplace pension, you very likely own shares already. See Pensions.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

High reward, real pain, and conflicts of interest

The case for shares is strong over long periods. The case against is about the journey: long falls, bad timing, and managers whose interests are not quite yours.

Chapter 11

Weighing it up

FeatureThe advantageThe disadvantage
Residual claimUnlimited upside. A successful company can grow many times over, and owners take the whole gain.Last in line. In a bankruptcy shareholders usually get nothing.
Real-asset backingCompanies can raise prices with inflation, so over long periods shares have tended to beat it.In bursts of high inflation and rising rates, shares often fall sharply first.
LiquidityYou can sell a large company's shares in seconds, almost any working day.Being able to sell instantly tempts people to sell in panic, at the worst moment.
Low cost of entryMany apps now let you buy a fraction of a share with a small sum.Easy trading encourages over-trading, which research links to lower returns for individuals.
Ownership and voiceOwners can vote out a board and share in the economy's growth.Managers may pursue their own pay, empire or short-term share price instead.
Constant pricingYou always know roughly what your holding is worth.You also see every fall, which makes holding on emotionally hard.

The conflict between owners and managers has a name: the principal-agent problem. The owners (principals) hire managers (agents) who know more and may want different things. Paying managers in shares was meant to fix it. Sometimes it instead encouraged short-term tricks to lift the price before share options paid out. No system has fully solved this, which is why reading financial statements remains a useful skill.

Chapter 12

What the bad decades actually looked like

It is easy to accept "shares are volatile" in the abstract. The real history is more sobering, and worth knowing in detail.

EpisodeWhat happenedHow long to recover
1929 crash, United StatesThe Dow fell close to 90 per cent from its 1929 peak to its 1932 lowAbout 25 years for the index to regain its price peak, though dividends and falling prices made the real wait shorter
Black Monday, October 1987The Dow fell about 22 per cent in a single day, still its largest one-day percentage dropAbout two years
Japan, 1989 onwardThe Nikkei peaked in December 1989 and then lost most of its valueThe price index did not pass its 1989 high until February 2024, more than 34 years later
Global financial crisisThe S&P 500 fell by more than half between late 2007 and March 2009About four to five years, including dividends

The lesson is not "avoid shares". It is that shares are for money you will not need for many years, and that a person who sold in March 2009 turned a temporary fall into a permanent loss. Japan's case adds a harder point: buying a whole market at a very high valuation can mean decades of poor returns, even when the economy does not collapse. Crises in general are covered in Financial crises.

Chapter 14

The shrinking public market

One trend is already well documented. The number of companies listed on US exchanges roughly halved between the late 1990s and the 2010s, through mergers, failures and fewer new listings. At the same time, money in private markets, meaning private equity and venture capital funds that buy companies which are not listed, grew enormously. Many young companies now stay private for a decade or more, raising large sums without ever doing an IPO.

That matters for ordinary savers. If companies do much of their fast growth before listing, the early gains go to the wealthy institutions that can invest privately, and the public gets the company only once it is mature. Regulators in several countries are debating whether to open private markets to ordinary investors. Supporters call it fairness. Critics point to high fees, little transparency and difficulty selling. Whichever way it goes, the basic logic of a share will not change: a residual claim on a business, worth what that business will eventually pay its owners.

The core idea

A stock is a residual claim on a company: a slice of what is left after everyone else is paid, plus a vote, with limited liability. Its price is the crowd's estimate of future cash to owners, discounted for time and risk. Long-run returns come from dividends and profit growth. Short-run returns come mostly from mood.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Shares in major markets returned very roughly 3–7 per cent a year above inflation since 1900, varying by country.
  • About 4 per cent of US-listed companies produced all the market's net wealth creation since 1926 (Bessembinder, 2018).
  • Japan's Nikkei took more than 34 years to regain its 1989 peak.
The common mistake

Thinking a good company is automatically a good investment. A great business bought at a price that already assumes greatness can still be a poor investment for years.

Can you explain why buying a share through an app sends no money to the company, and why the company still cares about its share price? If so, you understand primary and secondary markets.
