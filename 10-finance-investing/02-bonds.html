Bonds. A loan you can sell, and the quiet price behind every other price.

Shares get the headlines. Bonds are bigger, older in their modern form, and in many ways more important. A bond is simply a loan broken into pieces that investors can buy and sell. Its rules are fixed in advance, which makes it sound boring. But because its payments are fixed, its price must move whenever interest rates move, and that one mechanical fact reaches into mortgages, pensions, company valuations and government budgets everywhere.

Question 1 · What is it?

An IOU with a timetable

A bond is a promise to pay set amounts on set dates. Everything else about bonds follows from that promise and from how much people trust it.

Chapter 01

Three numbers define a bond

Suppose a government needs to borrow a billion to build a railway. It could go to one bank. Or it could split the loan into a million pieces of 1,000 each and sell them to anyone. Each piece is a bond. Each one says: we will pay you 40 every year for ten years, and then return your 1,000.

That sentence contains the three numbers that define every plain bond. The face value (or principal) is the 1,000 returned at the end. The coupon is the yearly interest, here 40, or 4 per cent of face value. The name comes from paper coupons that owners once literally cut off the bond and handed in for payment. The maturity is the date the loan ends, here ten years away.

The key difference from a share is this. A shareholder owns part of the business and gets whatever is left. A bondholder lends to the business and gets exactly what was promised, no more and no less, unless the borrower fails. The bondholder gives up the upside in exchange for being paid first.

The risk becomes inflation rather than default. A government that borrows in someone else's currency, often dollars, can truly run out of money. Many emerging-market crises started exactly there. The economics of government borrowing is covered in Public debt.

Chapter 03

A loan that outlived an empire

Governments have borrowed from their citizens for many centuries, but the British state gives the cleanest example of how long a bond can last. In 1751 Britain combined several debts into consols, short for consolidated annuities. They paid interest forever and had no maturity date at all. The government could repay them whenever it chose, and over the centuries it converted and refinanced them several times. The last of these perpetual bonds were finally repaid in 2015, more than 260 years after the first were created.

The lesson is that a bond is a promise that can be passed down. The original lenders died generations before the debt was repaid. Their bonds had been sold, inherited and sold again, thousands of times. That transferability is what turns a private loan into a market, and the market is what gives a bond a price that changes every day.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The seesaw, the clock and the credit score

Three forces move a bond's value: interest rates, time to maturity, and the chance the borrower will not pay. Understand those three and you understand the bond market.

Chapter 04

The seesaw: when rates rise, prices fall

This is the one idea about bonds that confuses almost everyone at first, so take it slowly. You buy the ten-year bond from Chapter 01 for 1,000. It pays 40 a year, 4 per cent. One year later, interest rates have risen, and new bonds of the same kind now pay 6 per cent, which is 60 a year. You want to sell yours. Who will pay 1,000 for a bond paying 40 when they can buy a new one paying 60?

Nobody. To sell, you must lower the price until your bond offers a buyer about the same overall return as a new one. The buyer then gets the 40 a year plus a gain at maturity, when they receive the full 1,000 for a bond they bought for less. In this example the price would fall to roughly 860. If rates had fallen instead, to 2 per cent, your 40-a-year bond would be attractive and its price would rise above 1,000.

The coupon never changes, so the price has to. That is the whole mechanism. It also means a bond is only "safe" in one specific sense: if the borrower pays and you hold to maturity, you get exactly what was promised. If you sell before that, you may get more or less than you paid.

Chapter 05

Yield: the one number that makes bonds comparable

Because prices move, the coupon alone does not tell you what return you would earn. For that, the market uses yield. The most common measure is yield to maturity: the single yearly rate of return you would earn if you bought the bond at today's price, received every coupon and held it until it repaid. In the example above, the bond bought for 860 has a coupon rate of 4 per cent but a yield to maturity of about 6 per cent.

This is why financial news talks about yields, not prices. "The ten-year Treasury yield rose to 4.5 per cent" means that bond prices fell until a buyer today would earn 4.5 per cent a year. Yield up means price down. They are two descriptions of the same event. Comparing yields across bonds of different maturities produces the yield curve, a chart closely watched as a signal about the economy. It belongs to Interest rates and markets, which treats it in detail.

Chapter 06

The clock: why long bonds swing harder

Not every bond reacts to rates by the same amount. A bond that repays next year barely moves. You will soon get your money back and can reinvest it at the new rate. A bond that repays in thirty years moves a great deal, because you are locked into the old coupon for a very long time.

The measure of this sensitivity is called duration, expressed in years. A useful rule of thumb is that a bond's price moves by roughly its duration, in per cent, for each one percentage point change in rates. A bond with a duration of 7 loses about 7 per cent if rates rise by one point. A thirty-year bond can have a duration near 20, so a two-point rise in rates could cut its price by more than a third. Long-dated bonds, often described as the safest assets in the world, can be very volatile in price.

Chapter 07

The credit score: will they pay?

The second risk is that the borrower does not pay, which is called default. Investors judge it partly with help from credit rating agencies, mainly S&P Global, Moody's and Fitch. They grade borrowers on a ladder from AAA, the strongest, downward.

Band (S&P / Fitch style)NameWhat it signals
AAA to AAHigh gradeVery strong capacity to pay
A to BBBInvestment gradeAdequate to strong; BBB- is the lowest rung
BB to BHigh yield ("junk")Payment depends on good conditions
CCC and belowDistressedDefault is a real possibility, or has happened

The line between BBB- and BB+ matters enormously. Many pension funds and insurers are allowed to hold only investment-grade bonds. A company downgraded below that line can face forced selling and a sudden jump in its borrowing costs. The extra yield a risky bond pays over a safe government bond of the same maturity is called the credit spread. When investors grow fearful, spreads widen fast. Ratings are opinions, not guarantees. Before the 2008 crisis the agencies gave top ratings to many mortgage securities that later collapsed, and their conflicts of interest, since issuers pay for ratings, were heavily criticised afterwards.

Chapter 08

The hidden thief: inflation

The third risk is the quietest. A bond's payments are fixed in money, not in what money buys. If inflation runs at 5 per cent and your bond pays 3 per cent, you are getting poorer every year while being paid in full. The return after inflation is called the real yield. Volume 38 explains why inflation is a transfer from savers to borrowers in Inflation, properly understood, and bondholders are the savers it hits hardest.

Some governments sell bonds that protect against this. The UK began issuing index-linked gilts in 1981. The US followed with Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, or TIPS, in 1997. Their payments rise with an official price index, so the investor locks in a real return. They are the cleanest way to see what markets expect inflation to be: compare the yield of a normal bond with an inflation-linked one of the same length, and the gap is roughly the market's inflation forecast.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

The foundation the rest of finance is built on

Bond yields set the cost of borrowing for almost everyone, act as the measuring stick for every other investment, and sit inside the pensions and banks most people rely on.

Chapter 09

The measuring stick for everything

The yield on a safe government bond is often called the risk-free rate. It is the return you can get without taking any credit risk. Every other investment is judged against it. A share, a property or a business project must offer more than the risk-free rate, or there is no reason to take the extra risk. When government bond yields rise, every other asset has to offer more to compete, so their prices tend to fall.

This is how bond markets reach into ordinary lives. In the United States, thirty-year fixed mortgage rates move closely with the ten-year Treasury yield. Company borrowing costs are set as a spread over government yields. And when a government's own bond yields jump, its budget suffers, because new borrowing becomes more expensive. Bond investors can discipline governments in a way voters cannot do quickly. That is why the political strategist James Carville said in the 1990s that if he were reincarnated, he would want to come back as the bond market, because "you can intimidate everybody".

Chapter 10

A market larger than the stock market

The global bond market is roughly the same size as the global stock market or larger, with well over 100 trillion dollars of debt outstanding in the mid-2020s. Governments are the largest borrowers. Most of it is not traded on exchanges the way shares are. It changes hands "over the counter", between banks and big investors by phone and electronic platforms.

The single most important part is the US Treasury market. Treasuries are used as collateral for loans, held by central banks around the world as reserves, and treated as the safe asset into which money flees in a crisis. That is why turmoil in the Treasury market, as in March 2020 when even Treasuries briefly became hard to sell, alarms central banks more than almost anything else. The plumbing that keeps this running is covered in Central banking.

Chapter 11

2022: the year the safe asset hurt

For about forty years, from the early 1980s to 2021, interest rates in rich countries mostly fell. Because falling rates push bond prices up, bondholders enjoyed steady gains on top of their coupons. A generation of investors came to think of bonds as dull and reliable. In 2022 that changed. Inflation surged, central banks raised rates quickly, and bond prices fell sharply. Broad US bond indexes had their worst year in their recorded history, losing around 13 per cent, and long-dated government bonds lost far more.

The damage spread in two famous cases. In September 2022 a sudden jump in UK gilt yields after a government budget hit pension funds using a strategy called liability-driven investment, which relied on borrowing against gilts. The Bank of England had to step in to buy bonds. In March 2023 the American lender Silicon Valley Bank failed, partly because it had put depositors' money into long-dated bonds that had lost value. When depositors withdrew, it had to sell at a loss. Neither borrower defaulted. The losses came entirely from the seesaw.

Why bonds still sit in portfolios

In most recessions, central banks cut rates and bond prices rise just as shares fall. That tendency to move in opposite directions makes bonds a useful counterweight to shares. In 2022, because inflation was the problem, both fell together. The counterweight works best against recessions and worst against inflation. See Diversification.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Predictable, but not risk-free

Bonds trade upside for certainty. Whether that is a good deal depends on the price you pay, the borrower you trust, and the inflation you live through.

Chapter 13

When the promise breaks

Defaults by large countries are rare but not unknown. Argentina has defaulted on its foreign debt many times, including a very large default in 2001. Greece's 2012 restructuring, in which private bondholders accepted large losses, was one of the biggest sovereign debt restructurings in history. Russia defaulted in 1998, and in 1918 the new Soviet government repudiated the Tsar's debts entirely.

More common, and easier to miss, is the slow default of inflation. After the Second World War, several heavily indebted governments, including the US and UK, kept interest rates below inflation for years. Bondholders were paid in full and lost a large part of their purchasing power. Economists call this financial repression. It is a reminder that a promise paid in full can still be a promise broken in real terms.

The deepest question hides in the first row. If governments find debt hard to bear, will they lean on inflation again, as after 1945? Nobody knows. But anyone holding long-dated bonds is, in effect, making a bet on the answer, which is why a bond's yield is the market's running verdict on a government's credibility.

The core idea

A bond is a tradable loan with fixed payments. Because the payments are fixed, the price moves opposite to interest rates, more so for long bonds (higher duration). The other two risks are default and inflation. Government bond yields are the measuring stick for every other investment.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Price falls by roughly duration × the rise in rates: duration 7, rates +1 point, price about −7 per cent.
  • BBB- is the lowest investment-grade rating. Below it is "junk".
  • Broad US bond indexes lost around 13 per cent in 2022, their worst recorded year.
The common mistake

Thinking government bonds cannot lose money. They rarely default, but their price can fall sharply, and inflation can erode them while they pay in full.

Test yourself

Rates rise from 3 to 5 per cent. Which loses more value, a two-year bond or a twenty-year bond, and why? If you can answer, you understand the seesaw and duration.
