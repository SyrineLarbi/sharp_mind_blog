Company valuation. Putting a price on a future nobody can see, and being honest about the guesswork.

What is a company worth? Not what the share price says, which is only what the last buyer paid. A company is worth the cash it will hand to its owners over its whole life, adjusted for how long they must wait and how uncertain that cash is. Every valuation method, from a quick price-to-earnings ratio to a fifty-tab spreadsheet, is a way of estimating that one thing. Understanding how they work lets you see what a price is quietly assuming, and when the assumptions have become absurd.

Question 1 · What is it?

Estimating worth, as opposed to reading price

Valuation is the attempt to work out what an asset is worth from its own characteristics, so that the result can be compared with the price on offer.

Chapter 01

Price is what you pay

“Price is what you pay; value is what you get.”

Warren Buffett · crediting his teacher Benjamin Graham, letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, 2008

Think about buying a small apartment to rent out. Before you agreed to any price, you would ask how much rent it brings in, what the costs are, how long the building will last, and what you could earn by putting the same money somewhere safer. You would not pay double just because the neighbour did. That reasoning, worked out carefully, is valuation.

Shares are no different in principle. A share is a slice of a business, and the business is worth the cash it will produce for its owners in the future. Value is an estimate built from the business; price is a fact set by the market. Investing is the act of comparing the two. When an estimated value is well above the price, the share looks cheap. When it is well below, the share looks expensive, or the market knows something you do not.

Chapter 02

Three families of method

There are hundreds of valuation techniques, but almost all belong to one of three families.

FamilyThe question it asksMain tools
IntrinsicWhat is the future cash worth today?Discounted cash flow (DCF), dividend discount models
RelativeWhat are similar companies selling for?Multiples: price-to-earnings, EV/EBITDA, price-to-book, price-to-sales
Asset-basedWhat would the pieces fetch?Book value, liquidation value, sum-of-the-parts

Professionals usually use more than one and compare the answers. When an intrinsic estimate and the multiples disagree sharply, the disagreement is itself a clue: either the comparison companies are mispriced, or the assumptions in the cash flow model are wrong.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Discounting the future, and borrowing the crowd's judgement

The intrinsic approach rests on one idea, the time value of money. The relative approach rests on another, that similar things should sell at similar prices.

Chapter 03

The time value of money

A hundred euros today is worth more than a hundred euros in a year. You could put today's hundred in a safe account and have more than a hundred next year. You also face the risk that the promised future money never arrives, and the certainty that inflation will nibble at it.

So future money must be discounted: shrunk to its equivalent value today. If you could earn 5% a year elsewhere, then 105 euros promised in a year is worth about 100 today, and 100 promised in a year is worth about 95. Money ten years away at the same rate is worth only about 61% of its face value. The further away the cash, and the higher the rate, the less it is worth today. Compounding, explained in Volume 38 (Interest, and the astonishing effect of compounding), works forward in time. Discounting is the same arithmetic run backwards.

Chapter 04

Discounted cash flow: the model behind all the others

A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation does three things. It forecasts the company's free cash flow (the cash left after running and maintaining the business, as described in Financial statements) for several years. It estimates a terminal value for all the years after the forecast, usually by assuming steady growth for ever. And it discounts everything back to today and adds it up.

The simplest version assumes that cash grows at a constant rate for ever. Then the value collapses into a neat formula, often named after the economist Myron Gordon.

The constant-growth shortcut
                  next year's cash flow
  value  =  ----------------------------------
             discount rate  -  growth rate

  Cash flow next year 10.3, growth 3%, discount rate 8%:
     value = 10.3 / (0.08 - 0.03) = 10.3 / 0.05 = 206

  Same company, discount rate 7%:   10.3 / 0.04 = about 258   (+25%)
  Same company, discount rate 9%:   10.3 / 0.06 = about 172   (-17%)

Look at what happened. Moving the discount rate by a single percentage point changed the value by a fifth or more. In real models the terminal value often makes up well over half of the total, so the answer depends heavily on assumptions about a distant future nobody can see. A DCF is less a calculation than a structured argument about growth, risk and time, and its precision is mostly illusion. Used honestly, it shows which assumptions matter most.

Chapter 05

Where the discount rate comes from

The discount rate is the return investors demand for waiting and for bearing risk. It is usually built from two pieces. The first is a risk-free rate, normally the yield on long-term government bonds of a safe country. The second is a risk premium, extra return for the chance that things go wrong. A stable utility gets a small premium; a young biotech firm gets a large one.

For a whole company, analysts often blend the cost of its debt and the cost of its equity into a single weighted average cost of capital (WACC). The details are technical, and estimates of the equity risk premium are genuinely disputed among academics. The intuition is not: the riskier and more distant the cash, the more heavily it is discounted.

This explains one of the most important links in finance. Because the risk-free rate is part of every discount rate, when interest rates rise, the value of every future cash flow falls, and companies whose cash lies far in the future fall the most. That link is explored in Interest rates and markets, and the general idea of a risk premium in Risk and return.

Chapter 06

Multiples: valuation by comparison

Most everyday valuation is done with multiples: a price divided by some measure of the business. Estate agents do this when they price a flat "per square metre" using recent sales nearby.

MultipleCalculationBest used for
Price-to-earnings (P/E)Share price / earnings per shareProfitable, stable companies
EV/EBITDAEnterprise value / earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisationComparing firms with different debts
Price-to-book (P/B)Market cap / accounting equityBanks and insurers, whose assets are mostly financial
Price-to-sales (P/S)Market cap / revenueYoung firms with little or no profit yet

A P/E of 20 means investors pay 20 for each 1 of yearly profit. Turn it upside down and you get the earnings yield: 1/20 = 5%. That figure can be compared directly with a bond yield, which is one reason share valuations tend to fall when bond yields rise.

A high multiple is not automatically "expensive". It usually means investors expect fast growth. A low one is not automatically "cheap"; it may mean they expect decline. Every multiple is a DCF with the assumptions folded up inside it. A P/E of 40 only makes sense if profits are expected to grow quickly for a long time.

Chapter 07

Comparing the whole market with its own past

Multiples can also be applied to an entire stock market. A widely watched version is the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE), popularised by the economist Robert Shiller. It divides prices by the average of the previous ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings, which smooths out booms and recessions.

Historically, when the US market's CAPE was very high, the following ten years' returns tended to be lower than average, and when it was low, they tended to be higher. In the mid-2020s the US CAPE was far above its long-run average, though below its record at the 2000 dot-com peak. CAPE is a fair guide to long-run expectations and a poor tool for timing. Markets have stayed "expensive" by this measure for many years, and people who sold on that signal alone often missed large gains. Critics also argue that changes in accounting and in the mix of companies make old and new readings hard to compare.

Chapter 08

Asset-based methods and the sum of the parts

Sometimes the best question is not "what will it earn?" but "what would its pieces fetch?" For a company in trouble, analysts estimate liquidation value: what the assets would raise if sold, minus the debts. For a property company, they compare the share price with the estimated value of the buildings it owns. For banks and insurers, whose assets are mostly loans and securities, book value is a natural anchor.

For a group made of very different businesses, analysts use sum-of-the-parts: value each division separately with the method that suits it, then add them up. When the total is well above the market cap, the gap is called a conglomerate discount, and it often leads to pressure to break the group up. Asset-based methods have one limitation: they work best where the value is in things you can sell, and worst where it lies in brands, software and people, which accounts barely record.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Valuations move fortunes, jobs and whole economies

Valuation decides takeover prices, how much new companies can raise, what pension funds pay for their assets, and how big a bubble can grow.

Chapter 09

Takeovers: paying for control

When one company buys another, the buyer almost always pays more than the market price. The extra is called a control premium, and it is often a substantial percentage of the pre-bid price. The buyer justifies it with synergies: savings and extra sales it expects from combining the two firms, such as closing duplicate offices or selling one company's products through the other's network.

Here valuation becomes a practical matter of billions. Many studies of mergers have found that a large share of acquisitions fail to create value for the buyer's shareholders, because the premium paid was larger than the synergies actually achieved. A frequently cited explanation is overconfidence: executives believe their own optimistic forecasts. Volume 35 describes the mental traps involved, including how the first number on the table anchors every later one (The first number sticks).

Chapter 10

Graham's margin of safety

Benjamin Graham, who taught at Columbia University and wrote Security Analysis (1934, with David Dodd) and The Intelligent Investor (1949), is often called the father of value investing. His central idea was the margin of safety. Because any estimate of value can be wrong, buy only when the price is well below your estimate. The gap is a cushion that absorbs your mistakes.

Graham also offered a famous image: the market as a moody business partner, "Mr. Market", who every day offers to buy your share or sell you his, at prices that swing with his emotions. You are free to ignore him, and to trade with him only when his offer is foolish. The point of valuation is not to predict the price tomorrow, but to know when the market's mood has made a price unreasonable. Buffett adapted these ideas, placing more weight on paying a fair price for an excellent business rather than a low price for a mediocre one.

Chapter 11

When the story outruns the numbers

In January 2019 the office-sharing company WeWork was valued at about 47 billion dollars in a private funding round. It was presented as a technology company, which justified a technology-style multiple. When it prepared to list its shares later that year, its published accounts showed large losses and long-term lease obligations, the profile of a property company. Investors balked, the share listing was withdrawn, and the valuation collapsed. WeWork eventually filed for bankruptcy protection in the US in November 2023.

The story is not unique. In the dot-com bubble, companies with tiny revenues were valued at billions on the strength of "eyeballs" and website visits. The common thread is that a valuation is only as good as the story behind its assumptions, and a compelling story can stop people checking the numbers. The professor Aswath Damodaran, a well-known teacher of valuation, argues that every good valuation joins a narrative with numbers, and that each must be used to test the other.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Every method is useful and every method can fool you

The choice is not between right and wrong methods, but between different kinds of error.

Chapter 13

Garbage in, confidence out

The greatest danger in valuation is not a wrong formula. It is false precision. A spreadsheet that gives a value of 47.83 per share looks authoritative, but each input behind it is a guess. Analysts can also work backwards: decide what answer they want, then adjust growth or discount rates until the model agrees. Bank analysts and sellers of companies face incentives that can push their numbers in one direction.

The useful defences are simple. Present a range, not a point. Run scenarios: a bad case, a central case and a good case. And reverse the question: instead of asking what the company is worth, ask what growth the current price already assumes, and whether that is believable. This "reverse DCF" turns valuation from prediction into a reality check.

Whatever the tools, the core will stay the same. A company is worth the cash it will produce, discounted for time and risk. Every method is a shortcut to that idea, and every price is a claim about it that you can check.

The core idea

A company is worth the future cash it will produce, discounted for time and risk. Intrinsic methods estimate that directly, relative methods borrow the market's view of similar firms, and asset-based methods ask what the pieces would fetch. All of them rest on assumptions.

The numbers worth knowing
  • In a simple growth model, a one-point change in the discount rate moved value by roughly a fifth or more.
  • A P/E of 20 equals an earnings yield of 5%, comparable with a bond yield.
  • WeWork's private valuation of about $47 billion (2019) ended in bankruptcy (2023).
The common mistake

Calling a share "cheap" because its P/E is low, or "expensive" because it is high. A multiple only makes sense next to the growth and risk it implies.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a rise in interest rates hurts a fast-growing company with distant profits more than a steady company that pays cash today? If so, you understand discounting and duration.

The words, in plain words
Valuation
Estimating what an asset is worth, to compare with its price.
Intrinsic value
The value worked out from the asset's own future cash, not from what others pay.
Discounting
Shrinking future money to its equivalent value today.
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation that forecasts future cash and discounts it back to today.
Terminal value
The value of all cash beyond the forecast years, often the largest part of a DCF.
Discount rate
The yearly return investors demand for waiting and for risk.
Risk premium
The extra return demanded above the risk-free rate for bearing risk.
WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: a blend of what a company pays its lenders and owners.
Multiple
A price divided by a business measure, such as earnings or sales.
P/E ratio
Share price divided by earnings per share.
Earnings yield
Earnings divided by price; the P/E turned upside down.
CAPE
Price divided by ten years of average inflation-adjusted earnings, used for whole markets.
Control premium
The extra above market price paid to take over a company.
Margin of safety
Graham's cushion: buying well below estimated value to allow for error.
