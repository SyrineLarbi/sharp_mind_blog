Interest rates and markets. One number sits underneath every price, and when it moves, everything moves.

Ask a professional investor which single figure they would want to know about next year, and many would not pick company profits or economic growth. They would pick the yield on long-term government bonds. That yield is the return you can get without much risk, so every other asset, from a corporate bond to a tech share to an office building, is priced against it. This file follows how a change in interest rates travels through markets: first to bonds, where the effect is mechanical and exact, then to shares and property, where it is powerful but messier.

Question 1 · What is it?

The price of money, seen from the market's side

For an economist, an interest rate is a cost of borrowing. For a market, it is a yardstick: the return every other investment has to beat.

Chapter 01

The seesaw: when yields rise, bond prices fall

Imagine you bought a government bond last year. It pays you 3% a year, fixed, for ten years. Today the government issues new ten-year bonds that pay 5%. Now try to sell your old one. Nobody will pay full price for a 3% bond when a 5% bond is on offer. To find a buyer, you must lower the price until the buyer's overall return on your old bond also works out at about 5%.

That is the most important mechanical fact in finance. A bond's price and its yield move in opposite directions, like the two ends of a seesaw. The coupon, the fixed payment printed on the bond, does not change. What changes is the price, and the price adjusts so that the bond's yield, the return a new buyer would earn, matches the going rate in the market.

This explains a headline that confuses many people: "bond prices fell as yields rose". It is one event described twice. It also explains why bonds, often called safe, can lose money. They are safe in the sense that a strong government will pay the promised amounts. They are not safe from changes in the price other investors will pay for those amounts.

Chapter 02

Which rates the markets watch

There are many interest rates, and markets watch them in layers.

The US ten-year Treasury yield, the yield on US government bonds due in ten years, is the most watched rate in the world, because the dollar is the main global currency and US government debt is the deepest and most traded bond market. When that one yield moves, prices adjust in almost every market on Earth.

Question 2 · How does it work?

From one bond to the whole market

The effect of rates on bonds can be calculated exactly. The same logic then spreads, less exactly, to shares, property and other assets.

Chapter 03

Bond arithmetic in one example

A bond's price is simply the value today of all the payments it promises, discounted at the current market yield. That is the same discounting described in Company valuation, applied to payments that are fixed in advance.

A 10-year bond, face value 1,000, coupon 30 a year (3%)
  market yield      price of the bond     what happened
  ------------      -----------------     -------------------------------
      1%              about 1,189         rates fell: old bond is precious
      3%                    1,000         yield = coupon: sells at face value
      5%              about   846         rates rose: old bond is discounted

  the payments never change: 30 a year, then 1,000 back at the end.
  only the price someone will pay for them changes.

The figure called yield to maturity is the single rate that makes all the future payments add up exactly to today's price. When people quote "the yield" of a bond, this is usually what they mean. It assumes you hold the bond until it is repaid and that the issuer pays everything on time. If you hold a high-quality bond to the end, price swings along the way do not change what you receive; they matter if you have to sell early. The bond itself is explained in Bonds.

Chapter 04

Duration: how sensitive a bond is to rates

Not all bonds react equally. A bond that is repaid in one year barely moves when rates change, because you will soon get your money back and can reinvest it at the new rate. A bond repaid in thirty years locks you into its coupon for a generation, so its price swings a lot.

Professionals measure this sensitivity with duration. The useful rule of thumb is that a bond's price changes by roughly its duration, in per cent, for each one-percentage-point change in yields, in the opposite direction.

Bond (3% coupon)Duration, roughlyPrice change if yields rise 1 point
2-yearabout 2about -2%
10-yearabout 8 to 9about -8%
30-yearnearly 20about -17%

Two things lengthen duration: a longer time to repayment, and a lower coupon, because more of the value sits in the distant final repayment. When coupons are very low, duration is high, so a small rise in yields can cause large losses. That is exactly the situation the world had built up by 2021, as Chapter 10 shows.

Chapter 05

The yield curve: rates across time

If you plot the yields of a government's bonds against their time to repayment, from three months to thirty years, you get the yield curve. Its shape tells you what the market expects.

Normally the curve slopes upward. Short rates follow the central bank's current policy rate. Long rates reflect the average short rate investors expect over the years ahead, plus a term premium for the risk of locking money up for a long time. When the central bank has pushed short rates up hard to fight inflation, and investors expect it will soon have to cut them because the economy is weakening, short yields can rise above long ones. The curve is then inverted.

Chapter 06

Real yields and the market's inflation forecast

An ordinary bond's yield is nominal: it ignores inflation. Many governments also issue inflation-linked bonds, whose payments rise with consumer prices. In the US they are called TIPS; in the UK, index-linked gilts. Their yield is a real yield, the return above inflation.

Comparing the two gives a market forecast. If an ordinary ten-year bond yields, say, 4.2% and the inflation-linked one 1.9%, the gap of about 2.3 points is the breakeven inflation rate: roughly the average inflation investors expect, plus some premiums. (These figures are illustrative.) Central banks watch breakevens closely to see whether the public still believes they will hit their inflation targets.

Real yields matter for valuation because they measure the true reward for waiting. When real yields are negative, as they were in many rich countries for much of the 2010s and in 2021, holding safe bonds guarantees a loss of purchasing power. That pushes investors towards riskier assets in search of a positive return.

Chapter 07

Credit spreads: the price of fear

A company is riskier than a strong government, so it must pay more to borrow. The difference between a company's bond yield and a government bond of the same length is its credit spread. Companies judged most likely to repay, rated "investment grade" by credit rating agencies, pay modest spreads. Riskier "high-yield" borrowers, sometimes called junk bonds, pay much more.

Spreads change with mood. In calm times they shrink as investors compete for extra yield. In a crisis they widen fast, because investors demand much more to lend to anyone but the safest borrowers. During the 2008 financial crisis and briefly in March 2020, spreads jumped sharply. A sudden widening of credit spreads is one of the clearest signs that markets are frightened about the economy, often before share prices show it fully.

Chapter 08

Shares: two channels pulling in different directions

Rates reach shares through two routes. The first is the discount rate. A share is worth its future cash, discounted, and a higher risk-free rate raises the discount rate, lowering the value today. The second is the economy. Higher rates slow spending and borrowing, which can cut company profits. Lower rates can help profits, unless they are low because the economy is in trouble.

The discount-rate effect falls hardest on companies whose profits lie far in the future, typically young, fast-growing technology firms. In bond language, they are "long-duration" assets. Mature companies that earn steady cash today are less sensitive. That is why growth shares tend to soar when rates fall and slump when rates rise, while so-called value shares, such as banks and energy firms, often hold up better.

A simple comparison captures the competition between shares and bonds. A share index with a price-to-earnings ratio of 20 has an earnings yield of 5%. When government bonds yield 1%, that 5% looks generous. When they yield 5%, investors can get the same return with far less risk, so share prices face pressure. The gap between the two is a rough guide to the extra reward investors receive for owning shares, the equity risk premium, explored in Risk and return.

Chapter 09

Property, gold and currencies

Property is valued much like a bond with rent instead of coupons. Investors talk about a building's capitalisation rate ("cap rate"): its yearly net rent divided by its price. A building earning 50,000 a year at a 5% cap rate is worth 1 million. If rising interest rates push investors to demand 6%, the same building is worth about 833,000, a fall of a sixth, with no change in the rent. This is a large part of what hit commercial property values after 2022. More is in Real estate investing.

Gold pays no interest at all. When real yields are high, holding gold means giving up a good safe return, so gold has often struggled. When real yields are low or negative, that cost disappears. This relationship has been strong over many periods, though it weakened in the early 2020s as central banks bought large amounts of gold. See Commodities.

Currencies respond too. Higher rates in one country attract foreign money seeking better returns, which tends to strengthen its currency, all else being equal. How that works, and how traders exploit rate differences, is the subject of Foreign exchange.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Rates decide which decade investors live through

Over long periods, the direction of interest rates has been one of the biggest forces behind asset prices, and the turning points have been brutal.

Chapter 10

2022: the year the seesaw slammed down

For about forty years, from the early 1980s to 2020, interest rates in rich countries fell more or less steadily. Falling yields meant rising bond prices, and lower discount rates lifted shares and property too. By 2020 and 2021, yields were near zero or below. At the peak, in 2020, well over 15 trillion dollars of bonds worldwide had negative yields: lenders were effectively paying governments and companies to hold their money.

Then inflation returned and central banks raised rates at the fastest pace in decades. Because coupons were so low, duration was high, and the losses were severe. The most widely used index of US investment-grade bonds fell about 13% in 2022, its worst year since the index began in the 1970s. Long-dated government bonds fell far more. Austria's 100-year bonds, issued when rates were near zero, lost well over half their market value from their peak. Shares fell too, especially technology shares.

The losses were not only on investors' screens. Banks that held long-dated bonds bought at low yields saw those assets lose value, which helped bring down Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, as the economics file describes (When rates bite governments and banks). Duration risk that had been invisible for a decade became real within months.

Chapter 11

The yield curve as a recession forecaster

In the 1980s the economist Campbell Harvey showed that the shape of the yield curve helped predict US economic growth. Since the late 1960s, an inverted US yield curve, with short rates above long ones, has come before every US recession, usually by somewhere between several months and two years. Few economic indicators have a better record, which is why inversions make headlines.

The signal is not perfect. The US curve inverted in 2022 and stayed inverted for a record length of time, ending in 2024. The widely forecast recession did not arrive on the usual schedule. Some economists argue the signal has weakened, because central banks' large bond purchases and other forces have distorted long-term yields. Others say the lag is simply longer this time. An inverted curve is best read as the market's warning that rates are too high for the economy to bear for long, not as a calendar.

Chapter 12

Why the zero-rate decade lifted almost everything

When safe assets yield nothing, money flows to everything else. Investors buy riskier bonds, then shares, then property, then start-ups and more speculative assets, each step searching for some return. Economists call this the search for yield. It helps explain why, in the late 2010s and 2020–21, shares, house prices, private company valuations and even cryptocurrencies all rose together, sometimes called "the everything rally".

The reverse is also true. When safe assets finally pay a real return, the bar every other investment must clear rises. Low rates do not just help prices; they change what investors are willing to believe about the future, because distant, uncertain profits cost almost nothing to wait for. Volume 38 describes how such optimism becomes self-feeding (Bubbles, and why they keep happening).

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

High rates and low rates both have victims

There is no rate that is good for every investor. Each level helps some and hurts others.

Market-set long rates also have strengths and weaknesses as a signal. On the plus side, they gather the views of millions of investors about inflation and growth, and they discipline governments that borrow recklessly. On the minus side, they can overreact, move on fear rather than facts, and be distorted by central banks buying bonds on a vast scale, a policy called quantitative easing. The market's interest rate is a powerful signal, but never a neutral fact.

Chapter 15

What an investor can take from this

Nobody forecasts interest rates reliably, including central banks. The practical response is not prediction but awareness. Know the duration of what you own, and match it to when you need the money: a bond fund with a duration of fifteen years is a poor home for a house deposit needed in two. Remember that very high share valuations often rest on low rates, so a portfolio full of long-duration growth shares and long bonds is making one big bet twice.

Above all, treat the risk-free rate as the yardstick for every decision. When safe bonds pay 4% or 5%, any investment that promises little more, with much more risk, needs a very good reason. When they pay nothing, be suspicious of how much optimism is being priced into everything else. Public debt, which sits behind those safe bonds, has its own file: Public debt.

The core idea

The yield on safe government bonds is the yardstick for every asset. Bond prices move opposite to yields, by an amount set by duration. The yield curve shows expectations across time, credit spreads show fear, and higher rates lower the value of shares and property by raising the discount rate.

The numbers worth knowing
  • A bond's price moves by roughly its duration in per cent for each 1-point move in yields.
  • The main US investment-grade bond index fell about 13% in 2022, its worst year on record.
  • At the 2020 peak, well over $15 trillion of bonds worldwide had negative yields.
The common mistake

Thinking bonds cannot lose money. A safe government will pay what it promised, but the price of that promise falls when rates rise, and long bonds can fall as much as shares.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a 30-year bond fell far more than a 2-year bond in 2022, and why the same rate rise hurt technology shares more than bank shares? If so, you understand duration across asset classes.
