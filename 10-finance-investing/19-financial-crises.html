Financial crises. The same story, told every generation, by people sure it could not happen to them.

Every few years, somewhere in the world, a financial system breaks. Banks fail, markets freeze, currencies collapse, and people who did nothing wrong lose their jobs and savings. The details change: tulips, railways, mortgages, crypto. The skeleton does not. Easy credit feeds a boom, borrowers and lenders convince themselves the old rules no longer apply, and then confidence turns, everyone wants cash at once, and there is not enough of it. Understanding that skeleton is the best protection there is, for an investor, a voter or a government.

Question 1 · What is it?

When the financial system stops doing its job

A financial crisis is a sudden breakdown in the system that moves money between savers and borrowers. Prices of assets crash, important institutions fail, and lending dries up across the economy.

Chapter 01

The queue outside the bank

In September 2007, television news in Britain showed something most people had only seen in old photographs: long queues of ordinary savers outside the branches of a bank, Northern Rock, waiting to take their money out. It was the first run on a British bank in well over a century. Northern Rock had lent heavily for mortgages, and it paid for much of that lending not with customers' deposits but by borrowing short-term from other financial firms. When those markets froze that summer, it could not refinance. Once savers heard it had asked the Bank of England for help, they rushed to withdraw.

The queue is the image of every financial crisis. A bank does not keep your money in a vault; it has lent most of it out. That is normal and useful. It only becomes a problem when everyone asks for their money at the same time. A bank that is perfectly sound in ordinary times can be destroyed in days by fear alone, and fear spreads because each person's best response to others running is to run too.

Chapter 02

Four kinds of crisis

Economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, in their 2009 history of eight centuries of financial folly, This Time Is Different, sorted crises into a few families. They often arrive together.

TypeWhat breaksFamous examples
Banking crisisBanks fail or need rescue because loans go bad or depositors runUS 1930–33; Sweden early 1990s; the 2007–09 global crisis
Asset crashA bubble in shares, property or another asset burstsWall Street 1929; Japan 1990; dot-com 2000
Currency crisisA currency collapses, often after a fixed rate breaksBritain 1992; Thailand and its neighbours 1997; Argentina 2002
Sovereign debt crisisA government cannot or will not pay its debtsMexico 1982; Russia 1998; Greece 2010–12

A fifth kind, high inflation or hyperinflation, where money itself loses its value, is often counted too; Volume 38 explains it in Inflation, properly understood. The worst episodes combine several: a property bubble bursts, banks that lent against it fail, the government rescues them and runs up debt, and investors start doubting the government too.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A fragile core, a boom that plants the bust, and chains of debt

Crises are not random lightning strikes. They grow from features built into finance, and they spread along the links between institutions.

Chapter 03

The fragile core: borrowing short, lending long

Banks take deposits that customers can withdraw at any time and lend them out for years, as mortgages and business loans. This is called maturity transformation, and it is what makes banks useful: savers get instant access, borrowers get long loans. But it means that a bank can never repay all its depositors at once. It is solvent (its assets are worth more than its debts) without being liquid (able to turn assets into cash quickly).

In 1983 the economists Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig showed in a simple model that this structure has two possible outcomes. If depositors expect others to stay calm, they stay calm and the bank is fine. If they expect others to run, running is the rational choice, and the bank fails. The same bank, with the same loans, can survive or die depending only on what people believe. For this work, and Ben Bernanke's work on the role of bank failures in the Great Depression, the three shared the 2022 Nobel prize in economics.

The same weakness exists outside banks. Money-market funds, investment firms that borrow overnight, and crypto lenders all do some version of borrowing short and lending long. Regulators call this shadow banking: bank-like activity without a bank's rules or safety net. In 2007–08 much of the run happened there, out of sight of ordinary savers.

Chapter 04

The boom that plants the bust

The American economist Hyman Minsky argued that stability itself breeds instability. In calm years, lenders relax their standards, borrowers take on more debt, and rising asset prices seem to justify both. He described three kinds of borrower. "Hedge" borrowers can repay interest and principal from their income. "Speculative" borrowers can pay the interest but must keep refinancing the principal. "Ponzi" borrowers can only pay at all if the asset they bought keeps rising in price. The longer a boom lasts, the more borrowers drift from the first group to the third.

The economic historian Charles Kindleberger, in his 1978 book Manias, Panics, and Crashes, showed this pattern recurring from the South Sea Bubble of 1720 onward. The turning point is now called a Minsky moment: the instant when it becomes clear that many borrowers cannot pay, and lenders all try to get out together. Nothing new has to happen that day. It is a change of belief about facts that were there all along.

Chapter 05

Contagion: how one failure becomes many

A single bank failure is a misfortune. A crisis happens when the failure spreads. It spreads in three main ways.

The first is direct exposure: banks lend to each other and trade with each other, so when one fails, its creditors take losses. The second is fire sales: a firm in trouble sells assets quickly to raise cash, which pushes their prices down, which reduces the value of the same assets held by everyone else, forcing more sales. The third is panic by resemblance: if one bank fails because of bad property loans, depositors and lenders run from every bank that looks similar, sound or not.

The collapse of Lehman Brothers on 15 September 2008 showed all three. Lehman was a large American investment bank with huge holdings of property-linked securities and debts to firms all over the world. When it filed for bankruptcy, a large money-market fund that held its debt "broke the buck", meaning its shares fell below their promised value of one dollar. Investors pulled money from other funds, which stopped lending to companies. Banks stopped lending to each other because nobody knew who held what. Within days the insurer AIG, which had sold insurance against the default of mortgage securities, needed a giant government rescue. The financial system is a web of promises, and a crisis is the moment people stop believing them.

Chapter 06

When currencies and governments break

In July 1997 Thailand, which had kept its currency, the baht, pegged to the US dollar, could no longer defend it and let it fall. Thai banks and companies had borrowed heavily in dollars, often short-term, to fund a property and construction boom. When the baht fell, their dollar debts became much larger in local money. Investors then pulled money from countries that looked similar: Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and South Korea. Indonesia's economy shrank by more than a tenth in 1998, and its long-serving president Suharto was forced out. Borrowing in a foreign currency turns a currency fall into a debt crisis. How pegs break is explained in Foreign exchange and Economics · Exchange rates.

In the euro area from 2010, the problem ran in a circle. Banks in countries such as Greece, Italy and Spain held large amounts of their own government's bonds. When investors doubted a government, its bonds fell, which weakened its banks. When banks needed rescue, the government had to borrow more, which made investors doubt it further. Economists called this the doom loop. Greece needed several international rescue packages and imposed a painful restructuring on its private creditors in 2012. The loop was broken only when the European Central Bank's president, Mario Draghi, made a promise in July 2012.

"Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough."

Mario Draghi · speech in London, 26 July 2012

Bond yields fell sharply in the following months. The bank built a programme to buy governments' bonds if needed, and in the event it was never used. A believable promise had done the work.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Crises leave scars for a decade

A financial crisis is not just a bad year for markets. It cuts lending to the whole economy, throws millions out of work, and can change politics for a generation.

Chapter 07

1929, and the cost of letting banks fail

In October 1929 the New York stock market crashed after years of speculation, much of it with borrowed money. The crash alone did not cause the Great Depression. What followed did. From 1930 to 1933, waves of bank runs swept the United States, and thousands of banks failed, taking their depositors' savings with them. Lending collapsed. Prices and wages fell, which made every debt harder to repay. By 1933 roughly a quarter of American workers were unemployed. The stock market, at its low in 1932, had lost close to 90 percent of its 1929 value.

In 1963 the economists Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued that the Federal Reserve had turned a severe downturn into a catastrophe by allowing the money supply to shrink by about a third as banks failed. Ben Bernanke later stressed how bank failures destroyed the knowledge and relationships that let credit reach small firms. The lesson drawn by a generation of policymakers was that the authorities must not stand back and let the banking system collapse. The United States created federal deposit insurance in 1933, so that ordinary savers would have no reason to run. That lesson, and Bernanke himself as chair of the Federal Reserve, were central to the response in 2008.

Chapter 08

2008, step by step

The global financial crisis grew from American housing. In the early 2000s, low interest rates and loose lending fed a house-price boom. Lenders gave subprime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit, often with low starting payments that would later jump. Banks bundled thousands of these loans into securities, a process called securitisation, sliced them into layers of supposed risk, and sold them around the world. Ratings agencies gave many of the layers their highest ratings.

When house prices began falling in 2006 and 2007, defaults rose, and nobody knew exactly which banks held the bad loans. Funding markets froze in 2007, Northern Rock ran in September, Bear Stearns was rescued through a takeover in March 2008, and Lehman failed in September. Governments then acted on a scale never seen before: guaranteeing bank debts, putting public money into banks, and cutting interest rates to near zero. The US Congress approved a 700-billion-dollar rescue fund in October 2008. Central banks began buying bonds in large quantities, known as quantitative easing. The world economy shrank in 2009 for the first time in decades. Small countries with oversized banks suffered most: Iceland's three main banks, whose combined size was many times the country's whole economy, all collapsed within a week.

Chapter 09

The long shadow

Recessions that follow financial crises tend to be deeper and longer than ordinary ones. Reinhart and Rogoff found that after major banking crises, unemployment often kept rising for years and house prices took years to bottom out. The reason is debt. After a crisis, households, firms and banks all try to pay down debt at the same time, so they spend and lend less, which slows recovery. Government debt also tends to rise steeply, because tax revenues fall and rescues cost money. See Economics · Recessions and Economics · Public debt.

The effects go beyond economics. A study of many countries over more than a century by economists Manuel Funke, Moritz Schularick and Christoph Trebesch found that votes for far-right parties tended to rise after financial crises. Many commentators link the anger after 2008, at bankers rescued while homeowners were not, to political upheavals in the decade that followed. That link is debated, but the scale of the resentment is not. A crisis is also a test of whether people believe the system is fair, and the answer can outlast the recession by years.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

What rescues fix, and what they break

Here the question is not whether crises are good, but which responses work and what they cost. Every rescue that stops a panic also teaches people to expect the next one.

Chapter 10

The toolkit, weighed

The oldest rule came from the British journalist Walter Bagehot, in his 1873 book Lombard Street. In a panic, he argued, the central bank should lend freely to sound institutions, against good collateral, at a high rate of interest. That rule, the idea of a lender of last resort, is still the core of crisis policy. The details are in Banking · Central banking.

Chapter 11

The bailout argument

The hardest trade-off is called moral hazard: when people are protected from the consequences of risk, they take more of it. If big banks know they will be rescued, they have reason to gamble, keeping the profits in good years and handing the losses to taxpayers in bad ones. Banks that are so large that their failure would wreck the economy are called "too big to fail".

The case for rescue

In a panic, sound and unsound institutions fall together. Letting the system collapse, as in the early 1930s, punishes millions of innocent workers and savers to teach a lesson to a few bankers. Rescues can be designed to wipe out shareholders, replace managers and charge high rates, as Bagehot advised. Several of the 2008 rescues were repaid with a profit to the US Treasury.

The case against

Each rescue teaches markets to expect the next, making the system riskier over time. Rescues mostly helped those who owned financial assets, while homeowners in trouble got far less help. And public money spent saving banks can turn a banking crisis into a government debt crisis, as happened in Ireland after 2008.

Since 2008 regulators have tried to make failure possible without catastrophe. Large banks must hold far more capital, the cushion of shareholders' money that absorbs losses. They must prepare "living wills" explaining how they could be wound down. Some of their debt is designed to convert into shares in a crisis, a process called bail-in, so that investors, not taxpayers, absorb losses first. Whether these tools would work in a true panic has been tested only partly.

Question 5 · What could happen in the future?

Faster runs, new corners of risk, the same human nature

The next crisis is certain to come and hard to place. The possibilities below are sorted by how confident anyone can honestly be.

Chapter 12

2023: the first bank run on a phone

In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank, a mid-sized Californian bank serving technology start-ups, announced a loss on bonds it had bought when interest rates were low and that had fallen in value as rates rose. Its depositors were mostly businesses with balances far above the insured limit, and many knew each other. Messages spread through group chats and social media. On a single day, customers asked to withdraw around 42 billion dollars, about a quarter of the bank's deposits, mostly through apps. It was closed by regulators the next morning. Days later, Switzerland arranged an emergency takeover of Credit Suisse, one of the world's biggest banks, by its rival UBS.

The mechanism was Diamond and Dybvig's, unchanged since 1983. What had changed was the speed. A run that once took weeks of queues now takes hours of taps. That puts pressure on how much cash banks must keep and how quickly central banks can lend.

Chapter 14

Why "this time is different" is always wrong, and sometimes right

Reinhart and Rogoff took their title from the phrase that appears in every boom: this time is different, because of new technology, better policy or smarter banks. Most of the time the phrase is wrong. Debt is still debt, and people still panic. But it is not always wrong. Deposit insurance really did end ordinary bank runs for decades in many countries. Central banks really have learned, since the 1930s, not to let the money supply collapse. Higher capital requirements have made big banks sturdier than they were in 2007.

The fair conclusion is that each generation fixes the last crisis and leaves room for the next. Risk moves to wherever the new rules do not reach. Crises cannot be abolished, but they can be made smaller, rarer and less damaging, and the countries that remember the last one usually handle the next one better. For an individual, the lessons are the same as Volume 38's: avoid borrowing that could force you to sell at the worst moment, be suspicious when everyone is sure, and keep enough cash that a panic is something you watch rather than join.

The core idea

Finance borrows short and lends long, so it is always exposed to a run. Long booms encourage more debt and weaker standards, until a change in belief, a Minsky moment, starts a rush for cash. Crises spread through exposures, fire sales and panic, and end with a rescue or a depression.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US unemployment reached about a quarter of workers in 1933.
  • Lehman Brothers failed on 15 September 2008.
  • SVB depositors tried to withdraw about 42 billion dollars in one day in 2023.
The common mistake

Looking for a single villain. Crises need lenders, borrowers, regulators and investors all believing the same story. Blaming one group makes it easier to miss the next version.

Test yourself

Can you explain how a bank can be solvent and still fail, and why deposit insurance helps? If you can, you understand the core of almost every banking crisis.
