Risk and return. The only free lunch is gone. Every extra bit of reward has a price.

Every investment offers a deal: some expected reward, in exchange for some uncertainty about getting it. The central idea of finance is that these two travel together. If an asset promises more, it must be because holding it is unpleasant in some way that others want to avoid. Once you see this, you can read any investment pitch differently. The question stops being "how much can I make?" and becomes "what am I being paid to put up with?"

Question 1 · What is it?

A trade between certainty and reward

In finance, return is what you expect to gain. Risk is how far the actual result might stray from that expectation, and how badly. The trade-off says you cannot raise one without accepting more of the other.

Chapter 01

Two envelopes

Imagine two envelopes. The first certainly contains 100. The second contains either 50 or 150, decided by a coin toss. On average, both are worth 100. Which would you pay more for?

Almost everyone prefers the first. The second has the same average but an uncertain outcome, and most people dislike uncertainty about money they need. So if someone wants to sell you the second envelope, they must lower its price, perhaps to 90. Now its expected gain, 100 on a cost of 90, is higher than the safe envelope's. That extra expected gain exists only because the envelope is risky. It is the payment for bearing the uncertainty.

That small story is the whole logic of the risk-return trade-off. Risky assets do not offer higher returns because the world is generous. They offer them because, if they did not, nobody would hold them, and their prices would fall until they did.

Chapter 02

"Risk" means several different things

The word causes endless confusion, because professionals and ordinary people use it differently.

MeaningThe question it answersExample
VolatilityHow much does the value swing up and down?Shares often move 20 per cent or more in a year, either way
DrawdownHow far can it fall from a peak before recovering?US shares fell by more than half in 2007–09
Permanent lossCould the money be gone for good?A company goes bankrupt; a fraud collapses
ShortfallWill I have enough when I need it?A pension pot too small at retirement
Inflation riskWill it keep its buying power?Cash in a bank during years of high inflation
Liquidity riskCan I sell quickly without a big discount?A house, or a share in a private company

Most financial theory measures the first, volatility, because it is easy to calculate. Most people care about the last four. A "safe" savings account has almost no volatility and plenty of inflation and shortfall risk. A broad share fund has high volatility and, over long periods, historically lower shortfall risk. Which is "riskier" depends on which meaning you choose, and on when you need the money.

Chapter 03

Expected return is not a promise

One more word needs care. The expected return of an investment is the average result you would get if you could repeat the same situation many times. It is not what you will get. Like the risky envelope, any one outcome may be far above or far below it.

This matters because the risk-return trade-off is a statement about expected returns, not guaranteed ones. Taking more risk raises what you can expect, never what you are sure to receive. If higher risk always produced higher returns, it would not be risk. Anyone who promises both high returns and low risk is either misunderstanding their product or describing a fraud, as Chapter 11 shows.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Measuring the wobble, and finding which part is paid

Finance measures risk with statistics, ranks assets on a ladder, and then makes a surprising claim: the market pays you only for the risk you cannot remove.

Chapter 04

Standard deviation: one number for the wobble

The standard measure of volatility is the standard deviation of returns. It describes how spread out the results are around the average. If an asset averages 8 per cent a year with a standard deviation of 20 per cent, then in roughly two years out of three its return lands between minus 12 and plus 28 per cent. In the remaining years it lands outside that range, sometimes far outside.

Two assets with similar averages, different spreads
   how often
      |            ___
      |           /   \          narrow: a high-quality bond fund
      |          /     \
      |     ____/_______\____    wide: a share fund
      |  __/   /         \   \__
      +---------------------------------- yearly return
       -30%   -10%   avg   +20%  +40%

   same centre, different width. the width is the standard deviation.

For broad US shares, the yearly standard deviation has historically been around 20 per cent. For short-term government bills it is a few per cent. Standard deviation is useful because it can be calculated for anything with a price history. Its weakness, which Chapter 13 explains, is that real markets produce extreme results far more often than the neat bell shape suggests.

Chapter 05

The ladder of assets

Long-run studies, such as those by Dimson, Marsh and Staunton covering many countries since 1900, show the trade-off clearly. Riskier assets have, on average, paid more.

AssetTypical volatilityLong-run real return, very roughly (world, since 1900)Main risk
Cash and short-term billsVery lowUnder 1% a year above inflationInflation
Government bondsLow to mediumAbout 2% a yearInterest rates, inflation
Corporate bondsMediumSomewhat above government bondsDefault, plus rates
Shares of large companiesHighAbout 5% a yearDeep, long falls
Small firms, emerging marketsHigherVaried widely by period and countryLarger falls, political risk

The gap between shares and safe bills, roughly four percentage points a year in these studies, is the equity risk premium. It looks small. But compounded over forty years, it is the difference between money growing a few times and money growing many times. Most of the wealth created by investing comes from collecting this one premium, patiently.

Chapter 06

The risk you are paid for, and the risk you are not

In the 1960s William Sharpe and others developed the capital asset pricing model, or CAPM, built on Harry Markowitz's earlier work on portfolios. Its key insight splits the risk of any share into two parts.

Specific risk

Risk that belongs to one company: a failed drug trial, a scandal, a lost contract. It can be almost removed by owning many companies, because one firm's bad news is balanced by another's good news. Also called diversifiable or idiosyncratic risk.

Market risk

Risk that hits nearly everything at once: a recession, a financial crisis, a jump in interest rates. Owning more companies does not help, because they all fall together. Also called systematic risk.

The CAPM's argument is this. Because specific risk can be removed cheaply by diversifying, the market will not pay you for carrying it. Only systematic risk, the kind nobody can diversify away, earns a premium. A share's exposure to systematic risk is measured by its beta. The market as a whole has a beta of 1. A share with a beta of 1.5 tends to rise 50 per cent more than the market in good times and fall 50 per cent more in bad times. A beta of 0.5 means it moves about half as much.

The practical lesson is powerful: holding one company's shares adds risk without adding expected reward. That is the reason the next file, Diversification, exists.

Chapter 07

Beyond beta: the factors

The CAPM is elegant, but its predictions fit the data imperfectly. In the early 1990s Eugene Fama and Kenneth French showed that two more characteristics helped explain returns: small companies had tended to beat large ones, and cheap "value" companies, priced low relative to their accounts, had tended to beat expensive "growth" companies. Later researchers added momentum, the tendency of recent winners to keep winning for a while.

These extra sources of return are called factors. Whether they are rewards for real hidden risks, or the result of investor behaviour, or partly patterns found by searching through data, is debated. The value premium in particular was weak for much of the period from the late 2000s to around 2020, which led some to question it and others to argue it was simply a long bad spell. There is also the puzzling low-volatility anomaly: calmer shares have historically done about as well as or better than wild ones, which is not what simple theory predicts.

Chapter 08

Return per unit of risk

Comparing returns without comparing risk is like comparing drivers by speed alone. In 1966 Sharpe proposed a simple adjustment, now called the Sharpe ratio: take an investment's return, subtract the risk-free rate, and divide by its standard deviation.

Two funds, adjusted for risk (risk-free rate 3%)
              return    volatility    (return - 3%) / volatility
   fund A      11 %        20 %          8 / 20  =  0.40
   fund B       8 %         8 %          5 /  8  =  0.63

   fund A earned more. fund B earned more per unit of risk taken.

Fund B might be the better investment, because you could in principle borrow a little to scale it up, or combine it with other holdings, and get A's return with less risk. A high return is only impressive if it did not require an equally high risk. The ratio has limits. It treats upside and downside swings as equally bad, and a short track record can make it look far better than it is.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Time, timing, and the deals that are too good

Understanding risk and return protects you in three ways: it tells you which money belongs where, it explains why the same loss hurts some people far more, and it lets you spot impossible promises.

Chapter 09

Time changes the picture, but not completely

Since 1926, the broad US stock market has fallen in roughly one year out of four. Over one year, shares are a gamble. Over twenty years, the picture changes: in the standard long-run data there has been no twenty-year period since the late 1920s in which US shares, with dividends reinvested, failed to beat inflation, though some came close. The good and bad years tend to average out.

This leads to the most important practical rule in personal investing. Money you need within a few years should not depend on the stock market. Money you will not touch for decades can usually afford to. The rule is about time horizon, not about courage.

But time is not a guarantee

The US is the most successful market in the long-run data, and studying only winners flatters the results. Twenty years after its 1989 peak, Japan's stock market was still far below it. Long horizons reduce risk a great deal. They do not remove it. This file is general education, not financial advice.

Chapter 10

Tolerance, capacity, and the order of returns

Advisers distinguish two things. Risk tolerance is how much uncertainty you can stand emotionally. Risk capacity is how much you can afford to lose without your plans failing. A young worker with a secure job and decades ahead has high capacity, even if they hate seeing losses. A retiree living on savings has low capacity, even if they enjoy the thrill.

The retiree also faces sequence-of-returns risk. Two people can earn the same average return over thirty years. If one suffers a crash in the first years of retirement, while selling investments to live on, they sell more units at low prices, and the money may run out. If the crash comes late, the damage is much smaller. The order of returns matters as much as their average once you start withdrawing. This is a central problem for pensions.

Emotion matters too. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's work on loss aversion, from 1979 onward, found that a loss feels roughly twice as painful as an equal gain feels good. That makes volatility harder to live with than the statistics suggest, and leads many people to sell after falls, locking in the loss. More in Behavioral economics.

Chapter 11

When the reward has no visible risk

For many years Bernard Madoff, a respected figure on Wall Street, reported returns to his clients of around 1 per cent a month, almost every month, through good markets and bad. The smoothness should have been the warning. Real investments with that level of return wobble. In December 2008 Madoff confessed that it was a fraud: a Ponzi scheme, paying old investors with new investors' money. Clients' statements showed around 65 billion dollars, most of which had never existed.

The opposite failure is real risk hidden by clever models. Long-Term Capital Management, a hedge fund whose partners included the Nobel-winning economists Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, earned steady profits in the mid-1990s on bets that looked low-risk in their models, magnified with heavy borrowing. In 1998, after Russia's default, markets moved in ways the models treated as nearly impossible. The fund lost most of its capital within months, and the New York Federal Reserve organised a rescue by major banks. In both cases, the risk was there all along. It was simply not visible in the numbers people were watching.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A powerful framework with blind spots

The standard tools for measuring risk are useful, widely used, and wrong in specific, predictable ways.

Chapter 12

The tools, weighed

Tool or ideaWhat it does wellWhere it fails
Standard deviationOne comparable number for any asset with a price history.Treats gains and losses alike. Understates rare, extreme falls.
Beta and the CAPMExplains why diversification is rewarded and concentration is not.Fits real returns only loosely. Betas change over time.
FactorsDescribe returns better than beta alone.Some may be data-mining. Premiums can vanish for a decade or more.
Sharpe ratioPuts returns of very different strategies on one scale.Easily flattered by short records or smoothed prices.
Historical averagesA century of evidence across many countries.Survivorship: the failed markets are under-represented. The future may pay less.

The deepest limitation is philosophical. Economists since Frank Knight in 1921 distinguish risk, where the odds can be measured, from uncertainty, where they cannot. Dice have risk. A pandemic, a war or a new technology bring uncertainty. Financial models handle the first well and the second badly.

Chapter 13

Fat tails

If daily market moves followed a bell curve, extreme days would be almost impossibly rare. On 19 October 1987, the Dow Jones fell about 22 per cent in a single day. Under bell-curve assumptions using the market's usual volatility, a move that size should essentially never happen in the history of the universe. Yet large crashes keep happening, every decade or so.

Market returns have fat tails: extreme results, especially losses, occur far more often than the bell curve predicts. Writers such as Nassim Nicholas Taleb have argued that this is not a detail but the main thing, because a few extreme events decide most long-run outcomes. The practical response is to plan for falls bigger than any model suggests: avoid heavy borrowing, keep a cash buffer, and never hold so much of one risk that a single bad event could finish you.

The broad lesson is unlikely to change. Any time you see a return, ask what risk produced it. Any time you see low risk, ask whether it has been measured or merely not yet observed. That habit is more durable than any model.

The core idea

Higher expected return requires accepting more risk, because otherwise nobody would hold the risky asset. Markets pay only for systematic risk, which cannot be diversified away. Volatility is the standard measure, but real risk also means permanent loss, shortfall and inflation, and real markets have fat tails.

The numbers worth knowing
  • World shares since 1900: about 5 per cent a year above inflation; bills under 1 per cent.
  • US shares have fallen in roughly one year in four since 1926.
  • Losses feel roughly twice as strong as equal gains (loss aversion).
The common mistake

Judging an investment by its return alone. A return means nothing until you know the risk taken to get it, and a very smooth high return is a warning sign.

Test yourself

Why does owning one company's shares add risk without adding expected return? If you can answer, you understand the split between specific and systematic risk.
