Commodities. The raw materials of everything, priced by a contract for things that do not exist yet.

A barrel of oil from one well is much like a barrel from another. A tonne of copper is a tonne of copper. That sameness lets the world trade raw materials on open markets, mostly through promises to deliver them months from now. Those markets set the price of the fuel in your car, the wheat in your bread and the metal in your phone. They also swing far more violently than the prices of finished goods, and when they lurch, governments fall, inflation jumps and whole countries get richer or poorer overnight.

Question 1 · What is it?

Goods so alike that only the price matters

A commodity is a basic good that is interchangeable with every other unit of the same grade. That one property explains how these markets are built.

Chapter 01

Why a commodity is not a brand

If you buy a pair of trainers, the maker matters. If you buy a tonne of wheat of a stated grade, it does not. Economists call this property fungibility: one unit can replace another without anyone caring. Fungibility is what makes a commodity a commodity, and it has a big consequence. Because the goods are the same, sellers cannot compete on anything except price. A copper mine in Chile and one in Zambia sell into the same market at the same price. The cheaper producer earns more, but neither sets the price.

Real goods are never perfectly identical, so markets define grades. Oil is priced against benchmarks: Brent, a blend from the North Sea, and West Texas Intermediate (WTI), delivered at a storage hub in Cushing, Oklahoma. Other oils trade at a discount or premium to these depending on how heavy or how sulphur-rich they are. Wheat, coffee and metals have similar standard specifications. A grade turns a messy physical good into something two strangers on different continents can trade without inspecting it.

The families behave differently. Crops can be replanted next season, so a bad harvest is usually followed by recovery. A new mine or oil field can take ten years or more from discovery to output, so shortages of metals and energy last longer. And gold is almost never used up. Nearly all the gold ever mined still exists, which makes its price behave more like a currency than like a raw material.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Contracts for the future, settled mostly in cash

Some commodities are bought and sold for immediate delivery. But the prices you hear quoted on the news are almost all futures prices, and most futures never end in a delivery at all.

Chapter 03

From Osaka rice to Chicago grain

A farmer plants in spring and harvests in autumn. A miller needs flour all year. Both would like to know today what the price will be months from now. The oldest solution is a promise: the farmer agrees now to sell a certain amount at a fixed price on a future date. That is a forward contract.

In eighteenth-century Japan, merchants at the Dojima rice exchange in Osaka traded standardised promises of future rice. In 1848 traders in Chicago, the hub where grain from the American Midwest met the railways, founded the Chicago Board of Trade. Over the following decades it turned informal forwards into futures: forward contracts made identical in size, grade and delivery date, so that they can be traded on an exchange by anyone. A futures contract for crude oil on the main American exchange is for 1,000 barrels of a set grade, delivered in a set month. Because every contract is the same, they can be bought and sold thousands of times before that month arrives.

Chapter 04

Margin, the clearing house, and why delivery is rare

Two strangers trading a promise worth hundreds of thousands of dollars need to trust each other. They do not have to, because a clearing house stands in the middle. It becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. If one side fails, the clearing house pays.

To protect itself, the clearing house asks each side to deposit margin, a safety deposit usually worth a small percentage of the contract's value. Every day it marks every position to the day's price. If the price moved against you, money is taken from your account; if it moved for you, money is added. If your account falls too low, you get a margin call and must pay in more or be closed out. Losses are paid every day, not at the end, which stops them from piling up unseen.

Most futures positions are closed before the delivery month by making the opposite trade. A coffee roaster that hedged with futures usually buys its actual beans from its normal supplier and closes the futures position at the same time; the gain or loss on the futures offsets the change in the bean price. Only a small share of contracts end with a physical delivery. Some contracts, such as many stock-index futures, cannot be delivered at all and settle only in cash.

Chapter 05

Contango and backwardation

At any moment there is not one oil price but a whole row of them, one for each delivery month. Plot them in a line and you get the futures curve. Its shape tells you what the market is worried about.

   |        *  *                            |        *  .
   |   *                                    |              *  .
   |*                                       |                    *  *
   +---------------------------- months     +---------------------------- months
     CONTANGO: later is dearer                BACKWARDATION: later is cheaper
     plenty now, storage costs money          scarce now, buyers pay to get it today

In contango, future prices are higher than today's. This is the normal shape when supplies are plentiful. Storing a commodity costs money, for tanks, warehouses and insurance, and ties up cash, so a barrel delivered in a year should cost more than one today. In backwardation, future prices are lower than today's. This means supplies are tight now and buyers will pay extra to get the commodity immediately rather than wait.

The shape matters a great deal to investors. A fund that holds oil futures must sell each contract before it expires and buy the next one, which is called rolling. In contango it keeps selling cheap and buying dear, so it loses a little each month even if the spot price does not move. An investor can be right that oil will rise and still lose money, because of the shape of the curve.

Chapter 06

Hedgers and speculators need each other

The people in the market fall into two broad groups. Hedgers have a real business exposed to a price. A wheat farmer fears a fall; a bakery fears a rise; an airline fears dearer jet fuel; a mining company fears cheaper copper. Each uses futures to lock in a price and remove a risk they do not want.

Speculators have no physical business. They are hedge funds, trading firms and individuals who buy or sell futures because they expect the price to move. Speculators are often blamed when prices spike, and sometimes their bets do exaggerate moves for a while. But they also do something useful. A farmer who wants to sell next year's crop today needs someone to buy it, and there may be no bakery wanting to buy at that exact moment. The speculator takes the other side and carries the risk the hedger wanted to get rid of, in exchange for the chance of profit. Without them, hedging would be harder and dearer.

How much speculation pushes prices away from what supply and demand justify is a long-running argument. After oil reached a record of about 147 dollars a barrel in July 2008 and then fell below 40 within six months, some politicians blamed index funds that had poured money into commodities. Many economists found that the evidence for a large effect was weak, and that the boom and bust fitted Chinese demand and the global recession well. The question remains contested.

Beside both groups sit the physical traders, large firms such as Vitol, Glencore and Trafigura, that buy, ship, store and blend real cargoes around the world. They earn money from moving goods between places and times when prices differ, and they are some of the largest private companies most people have never heard of.

Chapter 07

The day oil cost less than nothing

On 20 April 2020, the futures contract for WTI crude due for delivery in May settled at about minus 37 dollars a barrel. For a few hours, sellers paid buyers to take oil away. It had never happened before.

The cause was physical. Covid-19 lockdowns had cut world oil demand by a huge amount almost overnight. Oil kept flowing from wells, and the storage tanks at Cushing, Oklahoma, where WTI contracts are delivered, were filling up. Anyone still holding a May contract on its last trading days would have to take actual barrels and find somewhere to put them. Many holders were funds and traders with no tanks at all. They had to sell at any price, and there were almost no buyers who had storage to spare.

The episode taught two lessons. First, a futures contract is ultimately a claim on a real thing in a real place, and when that place is full, the financial price can do things that seem impossible. Second, retail investors in some oil funds, who had bought hoping for a recovery, discovered that they did not own "oil"; they owned rolling futures contracts, with all the costs described in Chapter 05.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Prices that move whole economies

Commodity prices feed into almost everything else. When they jump, they cause inflation and recessions. When a country depends on one of them, its fortunes rise and fall with the market.

Chapter 08

Shocks, from 1973 to 2022

In October 1973, Arab oil producers cut supplies and embargoed countries that supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Within months the price of oil roughly quadrupled. Western economies, built on cheap fuel, fell into a painful mix of high inflation and recession that came to be called stagflation. A second shock followed the Iranian revolution of 1979. Those two shocks changed car design, building insulation and energy policy for a generation.

In 2022 Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine produced a modern version. Brent crude briefly rose above 120 dollars a barrel. European natural gas prices, which depend heavily on pipeline supply, rose at their peak in summer 2022 to many times their usual level. Wheat and fertiliser prices also jumped, because Russia and Ukraine are both major exporters. Inflation across Europe reached levels not seen in decades, and governments spent heavily to shield households. A war between two countries became a cost-of-living crisis on several continents, because the goods involved were commodities priced on world markets.

Chapter 09

Countries that live on one export

Many countries earn most of their export income from one or two raw materials: oil in Nigeria, Angola or Venezuela, copper in Zambia and Chile, cocoa in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which together grow more than half the world's cocoa. For them a price swing is not a market story. It decides whether the government can pay salaries.

Economists talk of a resource curse: the observation that countries rich in a single resource often grow more slowly and govern worse than one would expect. The reasons suggested include volatile revenue that makes planning hard, a strong currency during booms that hurts other industries (sometimes called "Dutch disease", after the Netherlands' experience with natural gas in the 1960s and 1970s), and a government that relies on resource money rather than taxes and so answers less to its citizens. The curse is a tendency, not a law. Norway, Botswana and Chile show that strong institutions and savings funds can turn resource wealth into lasting prosperity. Norway's oil fund, built from petroleum revenue, has become one of the largest investment funds in the world.

Chapter 10

Corners and squeezes

Because commodity supplies are physical and limited, a rich enough player can try to corner a market: buy so much of the available supply, or so many futures, that anyone who promised to deliver must buy from them at whatever price they demand. Those caught on the wrong side suffer a squeeze.

The best-known attempt was by the Texan brothers Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt. In 1979 they and their partners bought enormous amounts of silver and silver futures. The price rose from about 6 dollars an ounce to a peak near 50 dollars in January 1980. Exchanges then raised margin requirements and restricted new buying, the price collapsed, and on 27 March 1980, later called Silver Thursday, the Hunts could not meet a margin call. The brothers were later found liable for market manipulation.

A modern squeeze came in March 2022 on the London Metal Exchange. Nickel prices more than doubled in a matter of hours, passing 100,000 dollars a tonne, as traders with large bets on a fall, including a big Chinese nickel producer, rushed to buy back. The exchange halted trading and took the extraordinary step of cancelling a day's trades. That decision protected the market from a cascade of defaults but angered traders who had been on the winning side, and it damaged trust in the exchange for years.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Essential for hedgers, awkward for investors

Commodity markets do their main job, sharing price risk, very well. As a place for ordinary savers to grow money, they are far more doubtful.

Chapter 11

Commodities in a portfolio: the case for and against

QuestionThe case forThe case against
InflationCommodity prices often rise when inflation does, as in the 1970s and 2021–22.The link is unreliable over short periods, and other assets can protect against inflation too.
DiversificationPrices sometimes move differently from shares and bonds, so they can steady a portfolio.In a broad crash, as in late 2008, commodities can fall alongside everything else.
Long-term returnThere have been long boom periods, such as the 2000s.A barrel of oil produces nothing. Unlike a company or a building, it pays no profits, rent or interest.
AccessFunds and exchange-traded products make buying easy.Most track futures, not the physical good, and rolling in contango can eat returns.
VolatilityBig swings create opportunities for skilled traders.Swings of 30 to 50 percent in a year are common, which most savers struggle to sit through.

The key point is in the third row. A share is a slice of a business that earns profits. A bond pays interest. A commodity just sits there, costing money to store. Over the long run its price tends to follow the cost of producing it, which technology usually pushes down. This is why most investment advisers treat commodities, if at all, as a small hedge rather than a core holding. See Diversification for how such a hedge fits into a mix.

Chapter 12

Gold, the strange exception

Gold breaks most of the rules above. Little of it is consumed by industry. Most sits in vaults, in jewellery or in central-bank reserves, so its price depends on what people believe about money rather than on mines and factories. It pays no interest, so it tends to do worse when interest rates are high and better when they are low or when people fear for the value of paper money.

From 2022 to 2024, central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes of gold a year, the highest pace in decades. Analysts link this partly to the freezing of Russia's foreign reserves in 2022, which reminded governments that reserves held in another country's banks can be blocked, while gold in your own vault cannot. The gold price passed 3,000 dollars an ounce for the first time in 2025. Gold has long stretches of excellent and terrible returns. Its defenders call it insurance against disorder. Its critics, including the investor Warren Buffett, have pointed out that it produces nothing. Both are describing the same property.

Chapter 14

From oil to copper: a change of centre

For a century the most important commodity in the world was oil. Its price shaped inflation, foreign policy and the wealth of nations. If the energy transition continues, that centre of gravity moves. Wind, sunlight and uranium replace some fuel, while the machines that capture energy need metal. A world that burns less oil will dig more copper, lithium and nickel.

That shift changes who holds power. Oil reserves are concentrated in the Middle East, Russia and the Americas. Processing of many critical minerals is concentrated in China. Copper is mined heavily in Chile, Peru and the Democratic Republic of Congo. The geography of commodities is the geography of influence, and the next decades may redraw it. The markets themselves, with their futures, grades and squeezes, will look much the same. What they price will change.

The core idea

Commodities are interchangeable raw materials sold mainly on price. Their markets run on futures, standardised promises guaranteed by a clearing house, which let hedgers pass risk to speculators. Supply is slow to change, so prices swing hard, and the swings spread into inflation and national budgets.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Oil roughly quadrupled in 1973–74.
  • WTI futures settled at about minus 37 dollars on 20 April 2020.
  • Central banks bought over 1,000 tonnes of gold a year in 2022–24.
The common mistake

Thinking a commodity fund holds the commodity. Most hold rolling futures, and in contango the roll can lose money even while the spot price holds steady.

Test yourself

Can you explain why oil went below zero in 2020, using the words storage, delivery and futures? If so, you understand that financial prices for commodities are tied to physical places.

The words, in plain words
Commodity
A basic good, such as oil or wheat, where one unit of a grade is as good as another.
Fungibility
The property of being interchangeable, unit for unit.
Benchmark
A standard grade, such as Brent or WTI crude, against which others are priced.
Futures contract
A standardised exchange-traded promise to buy or sell a set amount at a set price on a set date.
Clearing house
The body that stands between buyer and seller and guarantees the deal.
Margin
A deposit held against a futures position, topped up or drawn down every day.
Contango
A futures curve where later delivery costs more than now.
Backwardation
A futures curve where later delivery costs less, a sign of scarcity now.
Rolling
Selling a futures contract near expiry and buying the next one, to keep a position open.
Hedger
Someone with a real business exposure who uses futures to remove a price risk.
Speculator
Someone who takes on price risk in the hope of profit.
Squeeze
A rapid price rise that forces those who bet on a fall to buy back at a loss.
Resource curse
The tendency of countries dependent on one resource to grow and govern worse than expected.
