Foreign exchange. The biggest market on Earth has no building, no opening bell and no closing price.

Every time a German carmaker sells in America, a Japanese pension fund buys a British bond, or you pay for a hotel abroad with your card, one currency is swapped for another. Added together, those swaps make the foreign exchange market, usually called forex or FX. It trades around the clock, five days a week. Most of it is plumbing, not speculation: firms paying suppliers, investors protecting themselves, banks moving cash. But inside the plumbing sit some of finance's most famous bets and blow-ups.

Question 1 · What is it?

A network of dealers, swapping promises in pairs

The foreign exchange market is not a place. It is thousands of banks, funds and firms connected by screens and phone lines, agreeing prices for one currency in terms of another.

Chapter 01

A market that follows the sun

Most stock markets live in one city and keep office hours. The London Stock Exchange opens at eight and closes at half past four. The currency market has no such home. Its week begins on Monday morning in Wellington and Sydney, which is still Sunday evening in Europe. Trading then moves to Tokyo, Singapore and Hong Kong, then to London and Frankfurt, then to New York, and it ends on Friday evening New York time. At any moment of the working week, somewhere, a bank is quoting a price for dollars.

The scale is hard to picture. Every three years the Bank for International Settlements, a kind of central bank for central banks based in Basel, surveys dealers around the world. Its April 2022 survey found about 7.5 trillion US dollars changing hands each day. The April 2025 survey put the figure close to 10 trillion. For comparison, the whole world produces goods and services worth roughly 110 trillion dollars in a year. The currency market turns over that much in under two weeks.

Most of those trades pay for nothing physical. They are investors buying foreign shares and bonds, and above all short-term swaps that move cash into another currency for a few days and back. Trade in goods is why the market exists, but today it is a small part of the traffic.

The traffic is concentrated. London handles well over a third of all trading, ahead of New York, Singapore, Hong Kong and Tokyo. And the US dollar is on one side of almost nine out of ten trades. Swapping Mexican pesos for Thai baht often happens in two steps, through the dollar, because that is where the buyers and sellers are.

Chapter 02

What actually gets traded

"Buying a currency" can mean several different contracts. The differences matter, because each one solves a different problem.

InstrumentWhat it isWho uses it, and why
SpotExchange one currency for another at today's price, settled usually two working days laterAnyone who needs the money now: an importer paying an invoice, a fund buying foreign shares
Outright forwardAgree today on a price for an exchange on a fixed future dateFirms that know they will receive or pay foreign money later and want certainty
FX swapA spot trade and an opposite forward trade done together: swap now, swap back laterBanks and funds borrowing one currency against another for days or months. The largest category, roughly half of all turnover
Currency swapA longer deal where two sides exchange interest payments in different currencies for yearsCompanies that borrowed abroad, governments, development banks
OptionThe right, but not the duty, to exchange at a fixed price by a date, bought for a feeFirms that want protection against a bad move but want to keep the benefit of a good one
FuturesStandardised forward contracts traded on an exchange such as the Chicago Mercantile ExchangeTraders and funds who want a central clearing house to guarantee the deal

Almost all of this happens over the counter, which means directly between two parties rather than on a public exchange. There is no single official price at any moment. There is a crowd of dealers' prices that sit very close together, because anyone quoting far away from the crowd is either ignored or picked off instantly.

Chapter 03

Reading a currency quote

Currencies are always priced in pairs, and each has a three-letter code: USD for the US dollar, EUR for the euro, JPY for the Japanese yen, GBP for the pound sterling, CHF for the Swiss franc. A quote such as EUR/USD 1.0850 means one euro costs 1.0850 dollars. The first currency is the "base", the thing being priced. The second is the "quote" currency, the one you pay with.

A dealer never gives one price. They give two: a lower bid, at which they will buy the base currency from you, and a higher ask (or offer), at which they will sell it to you. The gap between them is the spread, and it is the dealer's reward for standing ready to trade. For the euro against the dollar between big banks, the spread is a tiny fraction of a cent. Professionals measure moves in pips, which for most pairs is the fourth decimal place: a move from 1.0850 to 1.0851 is one pip. For yen pairs, where prices are in the hundreds, a pip is the second decimal place.

A large bank trading hundreds of millions of euros pays almost no spread. A tourist at an airport kiosk may pay ten percent or more without seeing it. The market price is the same for everyone. The distance from it is what varies.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Layers of dealers, a formula for the future, and a risk in the gap

Prices are made by a small group of big banks and electronic firms, and passed down to everyone else. The forward price is not a forecast but arithmetic. And the moment of payment itself carries a danger that once brought a bank down in an afternoon.

Chapter 04

Who is in the market

Think of the market as a set of rings. At the centre is the interbank market: a few dozen large banks, plus a handful of non-bank electronic trading firms, that quote prices to each other and to big clients all day. A small number of them handle most of the volume. They trade with each other through electronic platforms where prices update in milliseconds.

Around the centre sit the big customers. Asset managers and pension funds buy foreign shares and bonds and must buy the currency first. Hedge funds bet on currency moves. Multinational companies convert sales revenue, pay foreign suppliers and protect themselves against swings. Central banks manage their reserves and sometimes intervene, buying or selling their own currency to push its price.

Further out are smaller firms and individuals, who trade through their bank, card networks, transfer companies and retail brokers. Each ring gets its price from the ring inside it, plus a margin. Over the last twenty years the centre has become mostly electronic: rooms of traders shouting into phones have given way to algorithms that make prices in milliseconds.

Chapter 05

The forward price is arithmetic, not a forecast

Many people assume a forward price is the bank's guess about where a currency will be. It is not. It comes out of interest rates, and would be the same if the bank had no opinion at all.

Here is why. Imagine euro interest rates are 2 percent a year and dollar rates are 4 percent. A bank that agrees to sell you dollars in a year could do this: borrow euros today, swap them for dollars at the spot rate, and leave the dollars on deposit for a year earning 4 percent, while paying 2 percent on the euro loan. In a year it has the dollars to hand you and knows exactly what they cost. The forward price is simply the spot price adjusted for the gap between the two interest rates. Economists call this covered interest parity.

Where the forward price comes from
  spot today: 1 EUR = 1.0850 USD
  euro rate 2%   dollar rate 4%   (one year)

  1 EUR  --- deposit at 2% --->  1.02 EUR in one year
  1.0850 USD --- deposit at 4% --->  1.1284 USD in one year

  so the fair one-year forward is 1.1284 / 1.02  =  about 1.106 USD per EUR

  the currency with the HIGHER interest rate trades
  CHEAPER in the forward market.  No forecast involved.

The result surprises people. The higher-interest currency, here the dollar, is cheaper in the forward market. If it were not, someone could make a risk-free profit by borrowing in one currency and lending in the other, and they would keep doing it until the gap closed. Since the 2008 crisis small, persistent gaps have appeared, because banks face stricter balance-sheet limits and cannot always take the free profit.

Chapter 06

The afternoon a German bank closed

A currency trade has two legs. If the two payments do not happen at the same moment, one side has paid and not yet been paid.

In June 1974 German regulators closed a mid-sized Cologne bank, Bankhaus Herstatt, which had lost heavily on currency bets. They shut it at the end of the German business day. Banks that had paid Herstatt Deutschmarks that morning were still waiting for the matching dollars, due in New York hours later. The dollars never came.

The danger is still called Herstatt risk, or settlement risk. It shaped the plumbing of the market for decades. Its main solution arrived in 2002 with CLS, a specialised bank owned by the largest dealers. CLS settles both legs of a trade at the same time, a method called payment versus payment: neither payment goes through unless both do. Many emerging-market currencies are still outside it, so the risk has not vanished.

Chapter 07

The carry trade: picking up coins in front of a steamroller

The forward arithmetic in Chapter 05 has a famous loophole. Theory says that if one currency pays more interest, it should be expected to fall by about that much, so there is no free lunch. In practice, for long stretches, high-interest currencies have not fallen as much as the gap predicts. Sometimes they have even risen.

Traders exploit this with the carry trade. You borrow in a currency with very low interest rates, the "funding" currency, and put the money in a currency with high interest rates. You earn the difference, called the carry, every day the position lasts. For most of the period from the late 1990s to the mid-2020s the classic funding currency was the Japanese yen, because Japan's rates sat near zero.

The catch is in the shape of the returns. The carry trade earns small, steady gains for months or years, and then loses a great deal in days. When markets get frightened, investors rush to close their positions all at once. They sell the high-interest currency and buy back the funding currency to repay their loans, and the funding currency jumps. Traders describe it as picking up coins in front of a steamroller: the coins are real, and so is the steamroller.

A recent example came in summer 2024. The Bank of Japan raised its interest rate at the end of July, weaker American job figures followed days later, and investors who had borrowed yen raced to unwind. The yen rose sharply and on 5 August Japan's main stock index fell by about 12 percent in a single day, its worst fall since 1987. It recovered much of the loss within days.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Every business that crosses a border has a currency problem

The market exists to let people trade and invest across borders without being ruined by moves they cannot control. When it works, nobody notices. When a price that seemed fixed suddenly breaks, people notice very quickly.

Chapter 08

Hedging: the exporter's problem

Picture a furniture maker in Italy that sells a large order to a store chain in the United States. The price is 10 million dollars, paid in six months. The company's costs, its workers, wood and rent, are all in euros. Today 10 million dollars is worth about 9.2 million euros, which gives a healthy profit. But if the dollar falls 10 percent in six months, the same payment will be worth about 8.3 million euros, and the profit might vanish.

The company did not choose to bet on the dollar. It just wanted to sell furniture. A hedge is any deal that cancels out that unwanted risk. The simplest is a forward: the firm agrees today to sell 10 million dollars to its bank in six months at a fixed rate. Whatever the dollar does, the firm knows how many euros it will get. It gives up the chance of a windfall if the dollar rises, in exchange for removing the chance of a loss.

Hedge with a forward

Lock in the rate now. Cheap and simple, often with no upfront fee. You are fully protected, but you also miss out if the currency moves in your favour. Good for firms whose margins are thin and predictable.

Hedge with an option

Pay a fee, called a premium, for the right to sell at a fixed rate. If the currency falls, you use the right. If it rises, you let the option expire and enjoy the better rate. Like insurance, the protection costs money even in years you do not need it.

Investors face the same choice. A Japanese pension fund buying American bonds is betting on the bonds and on the dollar, and many such funds hedge the currency part with swaps. Most currency trading is people trying to get rid of currency risk, not to take it.

Chapter 09

When a "fixed" price breaks

Some of the most violent days in markets come when a currency that everyone treated as stable suddenly moves. The moves are big precisely because people had stopped preparing for them.

On 16 September 1992, a day the British press named Black Wednesday, the United Kingdom was trying to keep the pound within a band against the Deutschmark as part of Europe's exchange rate mechanism. Speculators, most famously George Soros, bet that it could not. The Bank of England bought pounds and raised interest rates, failed, and left the mechanism that evening. Soros's fund was reported to have made around a billion dollars. Why pegs fail is covered in Economics · Exchange rates.

On 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank surprised everyone. For more than three years it had promised that one euro would never cost fewer than 1.20 Swiss francs, and it had printed francs to defend that floor. That morning it dropped the promise without warning. Within minutes the franc rose by roughly 20 to 30 percent against the euro before settling back somewhat. Several retail brokers took large losses when clients' accounts went below zero. The lesson for everyone in the market was the same: a price held up by a promise can disappear the moment the promise does.

Chapter 10

The fix, the scandal, and the price you pay at the till

With no exchange, the market needs agreed reference prices. The best known is a benchmark set in London at four in the afternoon, called "the fix", at which many funds value their holdings. Between about 2013 and 2015, regulators in Britain, the United States and Switzerland found that traders at several large banks had used chat rooms to coordinate trades and push the fix in their favour. The banks paid fines that together ran into billions of dollars, and the benchmark now measures prices over a longer window.

The fix matters to big funds. For ordinary people, the price that matters is the one at the till. When you pay with a card abroad, the terminal may offer to charge you in your home currency. This is called dynamic currency conversion, and it is usually more expensive, because the shop's provider sets the rate and adds a margin. Choosing to pay in the local currency lets your own card network do the conversion, almost always at a rate closer to the market's.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Deep, cheap and always open, for those near the centre

For a large institution, the currency market is one of the most efficient markets ever built. For someone far from the centre, it can be expensive, opaque and dangerous.

Chapter 11

What the market does well, and where it fails

FeatureThe strengthThe weakness
Enormous depthHuge trades in major pairs barely move the price. Costs between big players are tiny.Depth is uneven. Smaller currencies can become hard to trade at all in a panic.
Over the counter, no central exchangeFlexible: contracts can be tailored to any date or amount.Opaque. Prices are not public, which allowed the fix scandal and lets small clients be overcharged.
Open around the clockFirms anywhere can trade in their own working hours.Shocks can hit when local markets are shut and liquidity is thin, as in some sudden overnight crashes.
Hedging toolsExporters and investors can remove risks they never wanted.Hedging costs money, and badly designed hedges have ruined firms that thought they were protected.
Dollar at the centreOne common currency makes every pair easier to trade.The world depends on dollar funding. When dollars get scarce, as in 2008 and March 2020, stress spreads everywhere.
Leverage widely availableSmall amounts of capital can hedge or take large positions.Small moves can wipe out leveraged traders, especially retail clients.

In 2008 and March 2020, when banks everywhere needed dollars at once, the US Federal Reserve lent dollars to other central banks through swap lines. They worked, but they showed that the market's calm rests partly on one country acting as the world's emergency lender.

Chapter 12

Why most retail currency traders lose

Online brokers advertise currency trading to individuals as fast, exciting and open all hours. They offer high leverage, trading with borrowed money. With leverage of 100 to 1, a deposit of 1,000 dollars controls a position worth 100,000. A one percent move against you wipes out the whole deposit.

Regulators have pushed back. Since 2018 the European Union has capped retail leverage on major pairs at 30 to 1 and requires brokers to state what share of retail accounts lose money. The answer they print is typically a clear majority.

The reasons are structural. Short-term moves are close to random, the spread is paid on every trade, and leverage turns noise into account-ending losses. If the people with the best information and lowest costs make their living by serving traders rather than out-guessing the market, that should tell you something. The same warning appears in Volume 47's chapter on scams (The ones worth recognising by name), because fake "forex trading" platforms are a common fraud.

Chapter 14

Why the dollar's grip is so hard to loosen

The dollar's reign is declared over most years, and it keeps not ending, because of a network effect. A central currency is useful because everyone else uses it. A challenger needs deep markets for safe bonds, courts foreign investors trust, and money that moves freely in and out, all at once.

The euro's safe bond market is split among many governments. China controls how money crosses its borders. Gold pays no interest. None of this makes the dollar permanent, but change in the currency system is likely to be slow and to happen at the edges first: more trade invoiced in local currencies, more regional payment links, more central banks holding a broader mix. The bigger near-term change may be technical rather than political. If payment systems around the world become instant and linked, the old two-day gap between the legs of a trade, the gap that ruined Herstatt's counterparties, could finally close.

The core idea

The foreign exchange market is a global network of dealers, not a building. Most of its traffic is swaps, hedges and investment flows, not trade in goods. The forward price is set by the interest-rate gap, not by forecasts, and the biggest risks appear when a popular trade or a fixed price suddenly breaks.

The numbers worth knowing
  • About 7.5 trillion dollars a day in 2022, close to 10 trillion in 2025 (BIS surveys).
  • The dollar is on one side of almost 9 in 10 trades.
  • London handles more than a third of all trading.
The common mistake

Reading the forward rate as the market's prediction. It is arithmetic from interest rates. A currency that is cheaper in the forward market is not "expected to fall"; it simply pays more interest today.

Test yourself

Can you explain why an Italian exporter might happily give up the chance of a currency windfall, and why the carry trade looks safe right up until it isn't? If so, you understand hedging and tail risk together.

The words, in plain words
Spot
An exchange of currencies at today's price, usually settled two working days later.
Forward
An agreement today on the price of a currency exchange on a future date.
FX swap
Exchanging currencies now and agreeing to swap them back later; in effect, borrowing one currency against another.
Bid and ask
The price at which a dealer will buy, and the higher price at which it will sell.
Spread
The gap between bid and ask: the dealer's reward and the trader's cost.
Over the counter
Traded directly between two parties instead of on a public exchange.
Covered interest parity
The rule that the forward price equals the spot price adjusted for the interest-rate gap.
Hedge
A deal that cancels a risk you did not want to carry.
Carry trade
Borrowing in a low-interest currency to invest in a high-interest one.
Settlement risk
The danger of paying your side of a trade and not receiving the other; also called Herstatt risk.
Leverage
Trading with borrowed money, which multiplies both gains and losses.
Swap line
An agreement between central banks to lend each other their currencies in an emergency.
