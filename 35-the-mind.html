Part I

Two ways of thinking

Chapter 01

Fast and slow

You have two modes of thought, and almost every finding in this volume comes from the relationship between them.

The fast one runs constantly, without effort, and you cannot switch it off. It reads the word in front of you whether you want to or not, recognises a face instantly, knows that a sentence sounds hostile, and produces an answer to "what is two plus two" before you have decided to look for one. It is enormously capable and it is doing the overwhelming majority of your thinking.

The slow one is deliberate, sequential and effortful. It is what you use for seventeen times twenty-four, for filling in a form carefully, for holding two arguments in mind at once. It is expensive to run, it gets tired, and it is lazy by default.

The division of labour is sensible. Fast handles everything routine; slow is called in when fast flags something unusual. The trouble is the handover. Fast produces an answer with complete confidence and does not mark it as a guess, and slow — being lazy — usually accepts it without checking.

Almost every bias in the next chapters is an instance of that single pattern: a fast, plausible answer to an easier question, accepted without audit.

Chapter 02

Answering an easier question instead

When you are asked something hard, your mind will frequently answer a simpler question and present the result as if it had answered the hard one. You will not notice the swap.

Asked how happy are you with your life, people substantially answer what mood am I in right now — which is why the answer moves with the weather. Asked is this candidate competent, people partly answer do they look the part. Asked is this investment risky, people answer do I feel good about it.

The substitution is invisible from inside, and that is what makes it powerful rather than merely interesting. You do not experience yourself dodging the question; you experience having an opinion.

The practical defence is not vigilance, which does not work. It is to ask what question you actually answered, out loud, on decisions that matter — and to notice when your confidence arrived faster than your reasoning did.

Part II

The biases worth knowing

Chapter 03

What comes to mind easily feels common

You estimate how likely something is by how readily examples arrive. That is usually a decent rule, because common things genuinely are easier to recall — and it fails badly whenever something is memorable for reasons other than frequency.

Plane crashes are vivid, reported everywhere, and discussed for days; car journeys are not. So people overestimate the danger of flying and underestimate driving, and after a well-covered crash some of them drive instead — which on the numbers is the more dangerous choice.

This is the mechanism behind a great deal of misplaced fear. Anything dramatic, recent, or heavily covered will feel more probable than it is, and anything slow, statistical and undramatic will feel rarer. Your sense of risk is substantially a report on your media diet.

It also runs in the useful direction. If you cannot readily recall an example of something going wrong, you will feel safer than you should — which is precisely the normalisation of deviance from the disasters volume, seen from inside a single head.

Chapter 04

The first number sticks

Whatever figure you meet first becomes the reference point for everything after, even when you know it is arbitrary and even when it is obviously irrelevant.

In the classic demonstration, people are shown a number generated by a visibly random process and then asked an unrelated factual question. Those who saw a high number give higher answers. They report, accurately, that the random number had no influence.

Every price you see works this way. A recommended price makes the discounted one feel like a decision you have made rather than a number you were given. The first salary figure mentioned shapes the entire negotiation. The first estimate in a meeting anchors the discussion, which is why the most useful thing a chair can do is collect everyone's estimate privately before anyone speaks.

You cannot defend against this by trying not to be influenced; that has been tested repeatedly and does not work. What does help is generating your own number before you see theirs, and noticing when a conversation is being conducted entirely in a range somebody else chose.

Chapter 05

Looking for what you already believe

Given a belief, you will seek out information consistent with it, read ambiguous evidence as supporting it, and hold contrary evidence to a much higher standard — while experiencing all of that as simply being reasonable.

The asymmetry is the giveaway. When a study agrees with you, you accept it. When one disagrees, you find yourself examining the methodology. That examination is not wrong — but if you only ever do it in one direction, you are not assessing evidence, you are defending a position.

Worse, correcting someone's factual error sometimes strengthens their belief, because the encounter gets stored as an attack to be resisted rather than a fact to be absorbed. This is why arguments rarely change minds in the moment.

The only reliable defence anyone has found is structural rather than mental: write down in advance what would change your mind. If nothing would, you are not holding a belief about the world, and it is worth knowing that about yourself.

Chapter 06

Losses hurt more than gains please

Losing something feels roughly twice as bad as gaining the same thing feels good. That single asymmetry explains a surprising amount of behaviour that otherwise looks irrational.

It is why people hold a falling investment rather than sell and make the loss real. Why a free trial converts so well — once it is yours, giving it up is a loss. Why the identical offer framed as "save ten" and "avoid paying ten" performs differently. And why organisations will fight far harder to keep something than they ever fought to get it.

It combines with an equally strong effect: what you have already spent should be irrelevant to what you do next, and it never is. Money and time already gone are gone either way. The only question is whether continuing is worth it from here. Everybody knows this and almost nobody acts on it, which is why bad projects, bad films and bad relationships all get finished.

Chapter 07

How the question is asked changes the answer

The same facts, differently framed, produce different decisions — reliably, in trained professionals, on serious matters.

A treatment described as having a ninety per cent survival rate is chosen more often than one described as having a ten per cent mortality rate. These are the same statement. Doctors do this. Knowing about the effect does not remove it.

So whenever a choice is presented to you, the presentation is doing work. Try restating the options in the opposite frame before deciding — in gains if you were given losses, in absolute numbers if you were given percentages. If your preference flips, you have learned that you were responding to the wording rather than the substance, and you can then decide properly.

Part III

Memory, confidence and habit

Chapter 08

Memory is reconstruction, not playback

You do not store recordings. You store fragments and rebuild the event each time you recall it — and the rebuild uses whatever you know now, including things you learned afterwards.

Which means every recall is also a rewrite. A memory recalled often is not better preserved; it has been edited more times. Details get added from later conversations, from photographs, from what would make sense. And the resulting memory feels exactly as vivid and certain as an accurate one, because vividness is generated in the telling rather than stored with the event.

People can be led to remember events that did not happen, confidently and in detail, by nothing more than being asked about them repeatedly in a suggestive way.

This has consequences worth taking seriously. Eyewitness confidence is a poor guide to eyewitness accuracy, and misidentification is a leading cause of wrongful conviction. In ordinary life it means that a shared argument genuinely produces two different sincere accounts, and that "I remember it clearly" is not the trump card it feels like.

Chapter 09

Why bad drivers rate themselves highly

Competence at something and the ability to judge your competence at it draw on the same knowledge. So the less you know, the less equipped you are to notice how much is missing — and the confident feeling survives intact.

The other end is equally real and less discussed: genuine experts often underrate themselves, partly because they can see the extent of what they do not know, and partly because they assume that what they find easy is easy generally.

The practical reading is not "confident people are ignorant", which is too crude. It is that confidence is a poor signal of competence in either direction, and in any unfamiliar field the useful question is not how sure someone sounds but whether they can tell you where their knowledge stops — which is exactly why the closing volume made a point of that.

Chapter 10

How habits actually form

A habit is a loop with three parts: a cue, the behaviour, and a reward. Repeat it enough and the cue triggers the behaviour directly, without a decision being made.

That is why habits survive the intention to stop them. You are not deciding badly; you are not deciding at all. The trigger fires and the behaviour runs.

Which tells you where the leverage is. Changing the environment beats exercising willpower, because willpower is a limited deliberate resource and the environment is what supplies the cues. Removing the thing from the house does more than resolving not to eat it. Laying out the running shoes does more than intending to run.

Two further mechanisms are worth knowing. Replacing beats removing, because a cue with no available behaviour produces discomfort that eventually finds an outlet; give it a different behaviour instead. And attaching a new habit to an existing one works better than attaching it to a time, because an established habit is already a reliable cue.

The honest caveat about this whole volume

Knowing about a bias does not protect you from it. This has been tested and it is disappointing. People who can define anchoring are still anchored. What knowledge buys you is the ability to design around the problem — decide criteria before seeing options, write down what would change your mind, get your own estimate before hearing theirs, change the environment rather than trusting resolve. The defences that work are procedural, not mental.

Reference

What to carry forward

The engine

Fast thinking runs constantly and produces confident answers without marking them as guesses; slow thinking is lazy and usually accepts them. Most biases are one thing: a fast answer to an easier question, never audited.

The six worth knowing

What comes to mind easily feels common · the first number anchors everything · you hold disagreeing evidence to a higher standard · losses hurt about twice as much as gains please · money already spent still drives decisions · and how a choice is worded changes what people pick.

Memory and confidence

Memory is rebuilt each time, using what you know now — so recalling often means editing often, and vividness is no guide to accuracy. And confidence tracks competence poorly in both directions.

What actually helps

Procedures, not vigilance. Decide your criteria before you see the options. Write down what would change your mind. Produce your own number first. And for habits, change the environment rather than trusting willpower — the cue is doing the work.
