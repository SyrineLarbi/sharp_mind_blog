Chance. Probability without arithmetic — the ideas, not the formulas.

There is no mathematics in this volume. There is one thing you must do — count people instead of thinking in percentages — and it unlocks a set of ideas that reliably defeat intelligent people: why a positive test result often means nothing, why the second interview goes worse than the first for reasons nobody controls, and why a small hospital and a large one behave completely differently.

Part I

The one that matters most

Chapter 01

How common is it in the first place?

A condition affects one person in a thousand. There is a test for it. The test is right almost all the time — it catches essentially everyone who has the condition, and when someone does not have it, the test wrongly says they do about five times in a hundred.

Your test comes back positive. How worried should you be?

Most people, including a disturbing share of doctors when the question is put in percentages, say something around ninety-five per cent. The actual answer is about two per cent. Here is why, and notice that it takes no arithmetic beyond counting.

Imagine a thousand people like you. One of them has the condition, and the test finds them. Of the other nine hundred and ninety-nine, about fifty get a false positive. So fifty-one people are holding a positive result, and one of them is actually ill.

That is the whole trick. The condition is rare, so the rare true positives are swamped by the common false ones — and the test's accuracy, which felt like the important number, turns out to matter far less than how common the thing was before you tested.

Nothing in this library is more useful. It applies to medical screening, to security alerts, to fraud detection, to any rule that flags a small group out of a large one. Whenever someone tells you a test is ninety-nine per cent accurate, the question that matters is not the accuracy. It is: how many people have the thing, and how many are being tested?

The method, stated once

Never reason about this in percentages; percentages are what make it hard. Take a concrete number of people — a hundred, a thousand — and walk them through in groups. Done this way, people who fail the percentage version get it right routinely. The difficulty was never in the probability; it was in the presentation.

Chapter 02

The same idea, everywhere else

Once you have it, you see the failure constantly.

A description matches someone perfectly — quiet, tidy, fond of detail — and you conclude they are a librarian rather than a salesperson. But there are vastly more salespeople in the world, so even a poor match to a large group outnumbers a perfect match to a small one. The vividness of the fit overwhelms the size of the pool, and the fit is what your mind serves up instantly.

A screening programme catching a rare disease in a whole population will generate far more false alarms than true finds, whatever the leaflet says. Mass surveillance looking for a handful of dangerous people among millions will produce overwhelmingly innocent flags, however good the algorithm. This is not a criticism of the technology; it is arithmetic that no technology can escape.

And in the other direction: a symptom that is dramatic but has a common dull explanation usually has the common dull explanation. Rare things are rare. That sentence is the base rate, compressed.

Part II

What randomness looks like

Chapter 03

Random does not look random

Ask people to write down a sequence of imaginary coin tosses and you can spot the fake instantly: it alternates too much and has no long runs. Real randomness clusters. Streaks happen, patterns appear, and none of them mean anything.

Which sets up the two errors, which are opposites and are made by the same person on the same day.

After a run of one outcome, people expect the other to be "due". The coin has no memory and the roulette wheel does not know what it did. Conversely, after a run of successes people conclude someone is on a hot streak — and in most settings the streak is what chance produces anyway.

The general habit is more important than either example. Before explaining a pattern, ask what a pure coincidence would have looked like. If chance alone would produce something like this fairly often, you do not yet have a finding. A great deal of confident analysis — of markets, of crime figures, of one month's sales — is the detection of ordinary noise.

Chapter 04

Why extremes do not last

Anything measured with some element of luck in it will, on average, be less extreme the next time. Not because anything changed — because the extreme result was partly luck, and luck does not repeat to order.

The best month is followed by a worse one. The trainee who did brilliantly does merely well next time. The team at the bottom of the table improves. None of this needs a cause, and yet every one of them gets one, confidently supplied after the fact.

The consequences of missing this are real. A flight instructor notices that praising a good landing is followed by a worse one, and criticising a bad landing is followed by a better one, and concludes that criticism works and praise does not. Both observations are exactly what pure regression predicts, and the conclusion is wrong — but it is drawn from honest evidence, which is what makes it dangerous.

Any remedy applied at the worst moment will appear to work. New management after a disastrous year, a treatment started when symptoms peak, a policy introduced after a spike. Things were going to improve anyway, and the improvement will be credited to whatever happened to be there. This is also a large part of why ineffective remedies feel effective: people try them when they feel worst.

Chapter 05

Small numbers lie

Small samples produce extreme results more often than large ones. This is not a subtlety; it is the most under-appreciated fact in everyday statistics.

A large hospital and a small one both deliver babies, roughly half boys. Which is more likely to record a day where more than sixty per cent were boys? The small one, easily and repeatedly. Fewer births means more room for imbalance. The effect is obvious once stated and almost nobody predicts it.

So when a list of the best-performing schools, or the districts with the lowest disease rates, is dominated by small ones — that is not a discovery about small institutions. The list of the worst performers will be dominated by small ones too, for the same reason, and the two facts together give the game away.

Practically: ask how many before you ask how big. A striking percentage from a handful of cases is not evidence. A tripling of a rare event may be three cases instead of one. And a week of data is a week of noise wearing a chart.

Part III

Where chance hides

Chapter 06

The ones you cannot see

During the war, engineers examined returning aircraft to decide where to add armour, and the damage clustered on the wings and fuselage. The obvious conclusion was to reinforce where the bullet holes were. The correct conclusion was the opposite: reinforce where there were none, because planes hit there did not come back to be measured.

That inversion is the most valuable habit in this chapter. The data you have is the data that survived to reach you, and survival is rarely random.

So: successful founders who dropped out tell you nothing about dropping out until you count the ones who dropped out and failed. Old buildings are beautiful because the ugly ones were demolished. A strategy's track record looks good partly because the funds that used it and collapsed are no longer in the list. Remedies have satisfied customers because the dissatisfied ones stopped talking about it.

One question, asked reflexively, handles all of them: who is missing from this picture, and why?

Chapter 07

Coincidence, and the size of the world

You think of someone and they call. You dream of a disaster and one occurs. These feel like they demand an explanation, and the feeling is the thing to examine rather than the event.

With enough people and enough days, extremely unlikely things happen constantly — not despite being unlikely, but because there are so many opportunities. Something with a one-in-a-million chance per person per day happens to thousands of people every day in a large country. The remarkable thing would be if such things never happened.

Two further effects inflate the sense of the miraculous. You do not count the misses — the thousands of times you thought of someone and nothing happened leave no trace. And after the fact you specify the coincidence narrowly, when beforehand any of a huge number of near-matches would have felt equally remarkable.

The same mechanism produces false discoveries in serious work. Test enough relationships and some will look significant by chance alone, which is why a result found while looking for anything is far weaker than one found while testing a prediction made in advance. The time at which a hypothesis was formed changes how much its confirmation is worth, which is deeply counter-intuitive and entirely correct.

Chapter 08

Risk, as people actually feel it

The final piece is not about probability but about how probability is presented, which in practice matters more.

A treatment that halves your risk sounds dramatic. If the risk was two in ten thousand and is now one in ten thousand, the halving is true and nearly irrelevant to you. Relative change without the starting point is the most common honest-looking deception in public life, and it appears in medicine, in food scares, in crime reporting and in advertising. The question is always the same: from what, to what?

Beyond that, people fear the wrong things in a predictable pattern. Risks that are dramatic, involuntary, unfamiliar, and concentrated in time frighten far more than risks that are mundane, chosen, familiar and spread out — even when the second kind kills vastly more people. Which is why flying frightens and driving does not, and why a single catastrophe reshapes policy while a steady toll does not.

Knowing this does not make you fearless, and should not. It does let you separate this frightens me from this is likely to harm me, and to notice when someone is selecting which risks you are shown.

Reference

What to carry forward

The method

Count people, not percentages. Take a thousand imaginary people and walk them through in groups. Every hard probability question in ordinary life becomes easy this way, and the famous errors mostly disappear.

The base rate

How common something is beforehand matters more than how accurate the test is. Rare conditions plus large populations produce mostly false alarms, however good the instrument — and no technology escapes this.

Noise and extremes

Real randomness clusters, so streaks mean nothing. Extremes are followed by less extreme results with no cause required — which is why any remedy applied at the worst moment appears to work. And small samples produce extreme results far more often than large ones.

Two questions

Who is missing from this picture, and why? — the returning aircraft. And from what, to what? — because a halved risk means nothing without the number it started from.
