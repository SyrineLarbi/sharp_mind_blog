When something goes wrong. The small number of things worth knowing before you need them.

Most people will at some point be the nearest person to someone in trouble. In that moment you will not look anything up. This volume covers the handful of interventions that genuinely change outcomes, what to do in a few common emergencies, and — because being misled is its own kind of harm — how to read a risk figure in the news.

This is general knowledge, not training, and it cannot replace a proper first aid course — which takes an afternoon and is the single most useful afternoon most people never spend. Local emergency numbers and protocols vary; learn yours. Where anything here differs from what a professional tells you at the time, do what they tell you.

Part I

The ones that save lives

Chapter 01

The first thirty seconds of any emergency

The order matters and it is the same every time, which is why it is worth having.

Make sure you are safe first. This feels selfish and is not — a second casualty helps nobody and occupies the rescuers. Traffic, fire, electricity, gas, water. If the scene is not safe, do not enter it.

Check whether they respond. Speak loudly, tap their shoulders.

Get help coming. Call emergency services, or better, point at a specific person and tell them to. A crowd where nobody is named is a crowd where everybody assumes somebody else is doing it — which is a real and well-documented effect, and naming one person defeats it entirely.

Then check breathing. Tilt the head back gently, lift the chin, and look and listen for about ten seconds. Occasional gasping is not normal breathing and should be treated as absent.

Everything after this depends on what you found, and the next two chapters are the two answers that matter most.

Chapter 02

If they are not breathing

When the heart stops, the brain has only minutes. Chest compressions keep some blood moving until a defibrillator or paramedics arrive. Survival falls sharply with every minute of delay, which is why a bystander matters more than anyone else involved.

Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest. Around two compressions per second — the rhythm of a brisk song is the standard trick — and deep enough to be uncomfortable to do. Let the chest come fully back up between compressions, because that is when it refills.

Do not stop to check for improvement. Keep going until someone takes over, a defibrillator is attached, or professionals arrive.

Two things stop people acting, and both are worth dismantling. Rescue breaths are optional for untrained bystanders — compression-only is recommended precisely because hesitation about mouth contact was costing lives, and it works. And you may crack a rib. That is common, it heals, and the alternative is death; it is not a reason to push gently.

A public defibrillator will talk you through it out loud and will not shock a heart that does not need it. You cannot do harm with it by trying. Turn it on and follow the voice.

Chapter 03

If they are choking

The distinction that matters: can they cough?

If they can cough, speak or breathe at all, the airway is partly open. Encourage them to keep coughing — a cough generates far more force than anything you can do — and stay with them.

If they cannot make a sound, cannot breathe, and are often clutching their throat, the airway is blocked and they will lose consciousness within minutes.

Lean them well forward and give firm blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, checking after each. If that fails, stand behind them and give inward and upward thrusts below the ribcage, again checking between. Alternate the two.

If they become unresponsive, lower them and begin chest compressions as in the previous chapter.

For a baby, the same logic with far less force, supported face down along your forearm. And a person who has been treated with abdominal thrusts should be checked medically afterwards even if they seem fine.

Chapter 04

Bleeding, burns and breaks

Serious bleeding: press hard, directly on the wound, with whatever is to hand, and keep pressing. Pressure is the whole treatment. Do not lift the dressing to look — add more on top. Raising the limb helps a little. If it is catastrophic and pressure is not controlling it, a tourniquet above the wound is a legitimate last resort and modern advice is far more willing to use one than it used to be.

Burns: cool running water for a genuinely long time — twenty minutes, which is far longer than anyone naturally does, and the difference in outcome is large. Remove jewellery and loose clothing near the area before it swells, but leave anything stuck to the skin. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean non-fluffy cloth. No ice, no butter, no creams, and do not burst blisters. Ice causes further injury.

Suspected broken bone: do not straighten it and do not move the person unnecessarily. Support it where it lies, keep them warm, get help. Move someone only if staying is more dangerous than moving.

If they are unresponsive but breathing normally, roll them onto their side with the head tilted so the airway stays open and vomit can drain. That position alone saves a meaningful number of lives every year, and it is easy.

Chapter 05

Two things where minutes decide everything

Stroke. Look for a face drooping on one side, an arm that cannot be held up, speech that is slurred or confused. Any one of those means call emergency services immediately and note the time symptoms began — treatment options depend heavily on how long it has been. Do not wait to see if it passes. The brain tissue starved of blood is dying while you wait, and the treatments that work have a narrow window.

Heart attack. Central chest pain or pressure, possibly spreading to arm, jaw or back, with sweating, nausea or breathlessness. It is often not the dramatic collapse people expect — particularly in women, where presentation more often includes breathlessness, nausea and fatigue without crushing chest pain, which is part of why it is missed. Call, keep them still and calm, and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

The common failure in both is delay caused by embarrassment — not wanting to make a fuss or waste anyone's time. Ambulance services would very much rather attend and find nothing. That is not a platitude; it is their explicit position, and acting on it is the single most useful thing in this chapter.

Part II

Everyday hazards

Chapter 06

The invisible one

Carbon monoxide is produced by anything burning fuel incompletely — a faulty boiler, a blocked flue, a generator or barbecue used indoors. It has no smell, no colour and no taste.

It works by binding to the part of your blood that carries oxygen, and binding far more tightly than oxygen does. So your blood fills up with something useless and you suffocate with lungs full of air.

The symptoms are headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion — indistinguishable from flu, which is exactly why it kills people. The distinguishing clues are that it improves when you leave the building and returns when you come back, and that several people or pets in the same house feel unwell at once.

An alarm costs very little and is the only reliable detection there is. If one sounds, or you suspect it: open doors, get everyone out, and do not go back in. Never run an engine, generator or barbecue in a garage, tent or enclosed space, however cold it is — this specific mistake kills people every winter.

Chapter 07

Water, cold and heat

Drowning is silent. The thrashing and shouting of films does not happen — someone in trouble is usually vertical, low in the water, head tilted back, unable to call out because breathing takes priority. If someone looks like they are quietly doing nothing much, ask them.

If caught in a current pulling you out from shore, do not swim against it; swim parallel to the beach until you are out of it, then come in. And if you fall unexpectedly into cold water, the first reflex is an uncontrollable gasp — float on your back and do nothing for a minute until it passes, because most cold-water deaths happen in that first minute rather than from cold.

Hypothermia makes people confused and clumsy and sometimes makes them feel hot. Get them somewhere warm and dry, replace wet clothing, insulate from the ground, and warm them gradually. No alcohol — it feels warming and moves blood to the skin where heat is lost faster.

Heatstroke is the dangerous one, and the sign that distinguishes it from mere exhaustion is confusion or altered behaviour, often with hot dry skin and no sweating. That is a medical emergency. Cool aggressively — shade, water, wet cloths, fanning — while help comes.

Part III

Not being misled

Chapter 08

How to read a risk in the news

A headline says a habit raises your risk of a disease by fifty per cent. That sounds alarming and tells you almost nothing, because it is a relative figure.

If the risk was two in a thousand and is now three in a thousand, that is a fifty per cent increase and one extra case per thousand people. If it was forty per cent and is now sixty, the same phrase describes something far more serious.

So the question to ask every time is: fifty per cent of what? A relative risk with no baseline is not information. Reporting that omits the baseline is either careless or trying to impress you.

Three more habits are worth having. Absolute numbers over percentages — "one extra person in a thousand" is comprehensible in a way that "a fifty per cent rise" is not. Correlation is not causation, and people who do one thing usually differ in many other ways. And one study is not a finding; the ones that survive are those repeated by people who were not involved the first time.

The same applies to anything promising benefit. A supplement that improves a measurement in twenty people for six weeks has not been shown to help you live longer, and the gap between those two claims is where most health marketing lives.

Reference

The short list, for when you need it

The order, every time

Your safety · are they responsive · get help coming · check breathing. Point at one person and give them the job of calling — an unnamed crowd assumes somebody else is doing it. Gasping is not breathing.

Not breathing, or choking

Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, about two per second, and do not stop. Compression-only is fine and cracked ribs heal. A defibrillator talks you through it and will not shock a heart that does not need it. Choking: if they can cough, let them cough. If they cannot make a sound, back blows and abdominal thrusts, alternating.

The rest

Bleeding — press hard and keep pressing, add dressings on top rather than looking. Burns — twenty minutes of cool running water, no ice, no creams. Unresponsive but breathing — on their side. Stroke or heart attack — note the time and call immediately; they would rather attend and find nothing.

And the two that catch people out

Carbon monoxide has no smell and feels like flu — get an alarm, and never run an engine or barbecue in an enclosed space. Drowning is silent and vertical. And when a headline gives you a percentage, ask "per cent of what?"

Do this one thing

Book a first aid course. It is a few hours, it is the only part of this volume that will actually be in your hands under pressure, and almost nobody regrets it.
