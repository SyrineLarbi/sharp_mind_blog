How banks work. They do not lend out your savings. They create money, and live with the risk.

Most people picture a bank as a safe with a lending desk attached. Savers put money in, the bank lends it to borrowers, and it keeps the difference in interest. That picture gets two things wrong. First, when a bank makes a loan it does not hand over someone else's savings. It writes new money into the borrower's account. Second, a bank promises you your money back today while it has lent it out for twenty years. That mismatch is the bank's whole purpose, and it is also the reason banks can collapse in an afternoon.

Question 1 · What is it?

A business that borrows on demand and lends for years

A bank is a company whose debts are used as money. That single fact explains almost everything else about it, including why governments watch it so closely.

Chapter 01

Forty-two billion dollars in one day

On Thursday 9 March 2023, customers of Silicon Valley Bank in California asked to take out about 42 billion dollars in a single day. The bank had been one of the largest in the United States. Its customers were mostly technology start-ups and their investors, a small and tightly connected group. When word spread on group chats that the bank had lost money on its bond holdings, founders told each other to move their cash. They did it from their phones, in minutes. By the next morning regulators had closed the bank.

Nothing in that story involved a vault being emptied. No one queued at a counter. What failed was a promise. The bank had promised its depositors that they could have their money whenever they wanted. It had also put much of that money into long-term loans and bonds that could not be turned into cash quickly without a loss. As long as only a few customers wanted their money at once, both promises held. When many wanted it together, they could not. That tension is the heart of banking.

Chapter 02

The three jobs of a bank

Strip away the branches and the apps, and a bank does three things that are hard to do separately.

Keep money and move it

A bank account is a safe place to hold money and a way to pay other people. Your salary arrives in it, your rent leaves it, and your card draws on it. Banks are the members of the payment system, so most payments in an economy pass through them.

Lend

Banks judge who is likely to repay and lend to them: households buying homes, firms buying machines, governments covering deficits. Judging a borrower takes information and skill, and a bank builds both up over years of lending.

Transform

A bank turns short, safe, small promises (deposits you can take out today) into long, risky, large ones (a 25-year mortgage). Economists call this maturity transformation. It is the job no one sees, and the one that matters most.

The maturity of a debt is how long until it must be repaid. A deposit has a maturity of zero; a mortgage may run 25 years. No saver alone would lock up their cash for 25 years. Pooled through a bank, millions of savers effectively do.

Chapter 03

From goldsmiths' receipts to bank deposits

Lending money is ancient, but the word "bank" comes from the Italian banco, the bench where medieval money changers sat. Families such as the Medici of Florence built branch networks across Europe in the fifteenth century, moving money with letters of credit instead of carts of coins.

The step that created modern banking came later, in seventeenth-century London. Merchants left gold with goldsmiths for safekeeping and received a paper receipt. People soon found it easier to pay each other with the receipts than to fetch the gold. The goldsmiths then noticed something important. On any ordinary day, only a few receipt-holders came back for their gold. So they could lend out more receipts than they had gold in the vault, and earn interest on them. The receipts were now money, and most of that money was backed by loans rather than by metal.

That is still the structure today, without the gold. The Bank of England was founded in 1694, and over the next two centuries banking spread with industry. A modern bank deposit is a digital version of the goldsmith's receipt: a promise by the bank to pay you, which everyone accepts as money.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The balance sheet, the keystroke and the spread

To understand a bank, look at two lists: what it owns and what it owes. Almost every banking idea, from money creation to bank runs, is a change to one of those lists.

Chapter 04

A bank in one picture

Every company has a balance sheet. On one side are its assets, the things it owns or is owed. On the other are its liabilities, what it owes to others, plus its equity, the shareholders' own stake. The two sides must always add up to the same total. For a bank the picture looks like this.

A simplified bank balance sheet
        ASSETS (what it owns)          |   LIABILITIES + EQUITY (how it is funded)
  -------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------
  Reserves at the central bank   5     |   Customer deposits              75
  Cash in branches and ATMs       1     |   Borrowing from other banks     10
  Government bonds               14     |   Bonds the bank has issued       7
  Mortgages                      45     |
  Business and consumer loans    35     |   Equity (shareholders' money)    8
  -------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------
  Total                         100     |   Total                         100

  Your deposit is on the RIGHT: to the bank it is a debt.
  Your mortgage is on the LEFT: to the bank it is an asset.

Two things stand out. First, your deposit is the bank's debt to you, not a pile of your money in a drawer. Second, equity is thin. In this example, shareholders' money is only 8 per cent of the total. Banks are among the most highly borrowed businesses in the economy. That leverage, doing a lot with a small base of own money, magnifies profits in good years and losses in bad ones. If the bank's loans lose 8 per cent of their value, the shareholders are wiped out.

Chapter 05

Loans create deposits

Suppose a bank agrees to lend you 20,000 euros for a car. It does not go to the vault, and it does not take 20,000 euros out of another customer's account. It simply adds two entries to its balance sheet. On the asset side: a new loan of 20,000, which you owe it. On the liability side: a new deposit of 20,000 in your account, which it owes you. Both sides grow by the same amount, and the books still balance.

That deposit did not exist a moment earlier. You can now spend it, and the car dealer will accept it as money. The bank has created new money with a keystroke, matched by a new debt. When you repay the loan, the process runs backwards and the money disappears.

This sounds like a conspiracy theory, but it is the official description. In 2014 the Bank of England published an article called "Money creation in the modern economy" which explains exactly this, and adds that most money in a modern economy is bank deposits. Notes and coins are only a small share, a few per cent of the total in the UK. Most of the money you use was created by a commercial bank when someone took out a loan.

The textbook myth

Older textbooks describe a "money multiplier": the central bank supplies a fixed amount of reserves, banks must keep a fixed fraction, and they lend out the rest, over and over. Real banks do not work in that order. They lend first, when they find a borrower they trust at a profitable rate, and they find the reserves afterwards. Several countries, including the UK and Canada, have no reserve requirement at all, and the United States cut its requirement to zero in March 2020.

Chapter 06

So what stops a bank from lending forever?

If loans create money from nothing, why doesn't a bank lend without limit and get rich? Because several brakes operate at once.

Profit and risk. A loan is only worth making if the borrower is likely to repay. Lend to people who cannot pay and the losses come out of the bank's thin equity. Demand. Someone has to want to borrow at the rate the bank charges. Capital rules. Regulators require banks to hold a minimum amount of equity against their loans, weighted by how risky the loans are. The international standard is set by the Basel Committee in Switzerland and adopted, with local variations, by most countries. More lending needs more capital.

Liquidity. This one is subtle. When you spend your new car loan, the dealer probably banks somewhere else. Your bank must then transfer real reserves, the money banks hold in accounts at the central bank, to the dealer's bank. A bank that lends much faster than its rivals keeps losing reserves this way. It must then borrow them, often at a cost. Finally, the central bank's interest rate sets the cost of those reserves and so, indirectly, the price of every loan. That link is the subject of the next topic, Central banking.

Chapter 07

Borrowing short, lending long

Now look again at the balance sheet. Almost all of the right-hand side can leave quickly. Most deposits can be withdrawn today, and loans from other banks may last only overnight. Almost all of the left-hand side is locked up. A mortgage pays back slowly over decades, and a business loan cannot be recalled just because the bank would like the cash.

Banks live with this gap by relying on the law of large numbers. On a normal day, some customers withdraw and others deposit, and the flows roughly cancel. The bank keeps a buffer of cash, reserves and easy-to-sell government bonds to cover the difference. One rule, the liquidity coverage ratio, asks it to hold enough of these to survive thirty days of heavy withdrawals.

The 1946 film It's a Wonderful Life has the clearest explanation of this ever put on screen. During a panic, customers rush the small savings bank run by George Bailey and demand their money. He tells them it isn't in the building. It is in their neighbours' houses, lent out as mortgages. The money is safe, but it is not available today. That is maturity transformation, explained to a frightened crowd.

Chapter 08

How a bank earns its living

The core of a bank's income is the spread: the gap between the interest it earns on loans and the interest it pays on deposits and other borrowing. If it pays savers 2 per cent and charges borrowers 6 per cent, the 4-point difference has to cover staff, branches, technology, bad loans and a profit. This gap, measured across the whole balance sheet, is called the net interest margin. For many traditional banks it is the biggest single source of income.

The rest comes from fees: account charges, card fees, foreign-exchange margins, advice, and arranging deals for large companies. How the mix differs between a high-street bank and an investment bank is covered in Commercial banking.

The spread is why interest rates matter so much to bank profits. When rates rise, banks usually raise loan rates faster than deposit rates, so margins widen. When rates sit near zero for years, as in much of the 2010s, the spread is squeezed, because a bank cannot easily pay savers less than nothing.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Fragile by design, essential by function

A well-run bank can fail purely because people believe it will. That fact has shaped a century of law, and it explains why banking is the most regulated business most people deal with.

Chapter 09

Why runs happen, even to healthy banks

A bank run is when many depositors try to withdraw at once. The cruel logic is this. If you think other people are about to withdraw, you should withdraw too, because the bank pays customers in the order they arrive. The ones at the back of the line may get nothing. So a rumour can cause the very collapse it predicts. Economists call this a self-fulfilling crisis.

In 1983 the economists Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig wrote this down as a simple mathematical model. It showed that a bank doing exactly its useful job, turning short deposits into long loans, has two possible outcomes. In the good one, everyone trusts it and it works. In the bad one, everyone runs and it fails. The bank's assets are identical in both. Only belief differs. Diamond and Dybvig shared the 2022 Nobel prize in economics with Ben Bernanke, who studied how bank failures deepened the Great Depression.

In the early 1930s, thousands of American banks failed, and the money their loans had created disappeared with them. In September 2007 Britain saw queues outside branches of Northern Rock, widely described as the first run on a British bank in well over a century. SVB in 2023 showed the modern version: a run can now happen at the speed of a smartphone.

Chapter 10

The safety net

Because runs are self-fulfilling, the cure is to remove the reason to run. Over the twentieth century, governments built three layers of protection.

LayerWhat it doesExample
Deposit insuranceThe state guarantees ordinary deposits up to a limit, so small savers have no reason to run.The US created the FDIC in 1933. Its limit has been 250,000 dollars per depositor, per bank, since 2008. EU countries guarantee 100,000 euros.
Lender of last resortThe central bank lends cash to a solid bank that is short of it, against good collateral, so it need not sell assets at fire-sale prices.The rule was set out by Walter Bagehot in 1873. See Central banking.
Regulation and supervisionRules on capital and liquidity, plus inspectors who check the books, aim to stop banks taking on too much risk in the first place.The Basel rules, tightened after 2008 into "Basel III".

Deposit insurance is one of the most effective financial policies ever adopted. Classic retail bank runs became rare in rich countries after it spread. But notice what it does not cover: deposits above the limit. At SVB, the large majority of deposits were above the 250,000-dollar limit, held by companies keeping their payroll cash. Those uninsured depositors had every reason to run, and they did. In the end the US authorities chose to protect all of SVB's depositors anyway, to stop the panic spreading to other banks.

Chapter 11

Credit booms and the wider economy

Because bank lending creates money, banks do more than reflect the economy. They push it. In good times, banks lend more, and the new money raises spending and house prices. Higher house prices make borrowers look safer, so banks lend even more. In bad times the loop runs backwards: losses make banks cautious, lending shrinks, and spending falls further.

That loop was at the centre of the 2008 global financial crisis. When American house prices fell, losses on housing loans landed on thin bank equity, banks stopped trusting each other, and short-term funding dried up. The details belong in Finance · Financial crises. The lesson for this topic is simple: a healthy economy needs banks that lend, but banks that lend too freely in a boom make the bust worse.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A useful machine with a built-in weak point

The features that make banks valuable are the same features that make them dangerous. You cannot keep one set and drop the other.

Chapter 13

Too big to fail, and the problem of the safety net

The last row of that table is the hardest. When people know they are protected against a risk, they tend to take more of it. Economists call this moral hazard. Insured depositors stop checking whether their bank is careful. Large banks that expect a rescue can borrow more cheaply than small ones, because lenders assume the state will step in. This is called too big to fail. After 2008 it became a political scandal: bank profits had been private, but bank losses became public.

Regulators answered with extra capital for the largest banks and bail-in rules, under which a failing bank's bondholders take losses before any public money is used. Whether these fixes would hold in a real crisis is contested. The 2023 rescues of SVB and of Credit Suisse, taken over by UBS in a deal arranged by the Swiss authorities, showed that governments still step in when they fear contagion.

The core idea

A bank creates money when it lends, by writing a new deposit matched by a new loan. It funds long, illiquid loans with short deposits, which is maturity transformation. That makes it useful and fragile: a run can sink a sound bank, so states add deposit insurance, a lender of last resort and capital rules.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US deposit insurance covers 250,000 dollars per depositor per bank; the EU standard is 100,000 euros.
  • Notes and coins are only a few per cent of money in the UK. The rest is bank deposits.
  • SVB faced withdrawal requests of about 42 billion dollars in one day in March 2023.
The common mistake

Thinking a bank lends out savers' money from a vault. Lending comes first and creates the deposit; what limits it is profit, risk, capital rules and the cost of reserves.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a bank with good loans can still fail, and why deposit insurance stops that from happening? If you can, you understand maturity transformation and runs together.

The words, in plain words
Balance sheet
A list of what a company owns (assets) and how it is funded (liabilities and equity). The two sides always match.
Deposit
Money in your account. To you it is money; to the bank it is a debt it owes you.
Equity (capital)
The owners' own money in the bank, which absorbs losses first.
Leverage
Funding most of your assets with borrowed money, which magnifies gains and losses.
Reserves
Money that banks hold in their own accounts at the central bank, used to pay each other.
Maturity transformation
Funding long-term loans with short-term deposits.
Liquidity
How quickly something can be turned into cash without losing value.
Bank run
Many depositors withdrawing at once because they fear others will.
Deposit insurance
A state guarantee that ordinary deposits will be repaid up to a limit if a bank fails.
Net interest margin
The gap between what a bank earns on loans and pays on its funding.
Moral hazard
Taking more risk because someone else will bear the cost if it goes wrong.
Say this · five sentences you can use
  1. "Banks don't lend out your savings. When they make a loan, they create a new deposit, and that's most of the money in the economy."
  2. "A bank's real job is borrowing short and lending long. That's what makes it useful, and it's exactly what makes it fragile."
  3. "A bank run is self-fulfilling. If everyone believes the bank will fail, it fails, even if its loans are fine."
  4. "Deposit insurance works because it removes the reason to run. SVB failed because most of its deposits were above the limit."
  5. "Banks run on very little of their own money, often under a tenth. A small loss on the loans can wipe out the owners."
