Financial cybersecurity. The vault is now a network, and it has to stay open all day.

A bank used to be protected by thick doors and a guard. Today most of its money exists only as records in computers, and those computers must talk to millions of customers and thousands of other banks every second. Defending that system is not about keeping attackers out completely, which is impossible. It is about making sure that when they get in, they cannot move money, steal data or stop the system from working.

Question 1 · What is it?

Protecting money that exists as data

Financial cybersecurity is the work of keeping banks, payment systems and markets safe from digital attack and digital failure. It overlaps with fraud detection, but its target is the institution and its systems, not the single payment.

Chapter 01

"Because that's where the money is"

The American bank robber Willie Sutton is famous for answering the question "why do you rob banks?" with "because that's where the money is". He later wrote that he never said it. The line survives because it is true. Banks are the most obvious target for anyone who wants money, and now the robber does not need a gun or a getaway car. He needs a laptop and a way in.

The difference is scale. A physical robbery takes what is in one branch. A digital attack on the right system can, in principle, move any amount to anywhere in the world in minutes. That is why the financial sector is among the most attacked industries and also among the most heavily defended.

Chapter 02

Three things to protect

Security specialists describe their goals with three words, often called the CIA triad. (It has nothing to do with the intelligence agency.)

Confidentiality

Only the right people can see data. Your balance, your card number and your identity documents must not leak.

Integrity

Data cannot be changed without permission. A balance of 100 must not quietly become 100,000, and a payment to a supplier must not be redirected.

Availability

The system works when needed. If payments stop for a day, wages are not paid and shops cannot sell.

Most news stories are about confidentiality: millions of records stolen. For a bank, though, integrity is the deepest concern. Money is a ledger, a record of who owns what. If people stop trusting that the ledger is correct, the bank has nothing left to sell. Availability comes close behind, because modern economies cannot pause their payments.

The most striking change of the past decade is the role of states. US and UN investigators have repeatedly linked North Korean government hackers to thefts from banks and cryptocurrency firms, which they describe as a way of funding a heavily sanctioned regime. For the first time, a country runs bank robbery as a line of national income.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Layers, assuming that each one will fail

No single defence is enough. Banks stack many, so that an attacker who beats one still faces the next, and so that someone notices before the damage is done.

Chapter 04

Defence in depth

The core principle is called defence in depth, a term borrowed from military strategy. Think of a medieval castle: a moat, then a wall, then a gate, then an inner keep, with guards watching at every stage. Each layer slows the attacker and gives defenders a chance to react.

The layers of a bank's defence (simplified)
  PEOPLE         staff training, phishing tests, separation of duties
     |
  IDENTITY       strong login for staff and customers; least privilege
     |
  NETWORK        segments walled off from each other; traffic monitored
     |
  SYSTEMS        patching, hardened servers, approved software only
     |
  DATA           encryption, tokenisation, keys held in special hardware
     |
  MONITORING     a security operations centre watching for strange activity, 24/7
     |
  RECOVERY       offline backups, tested plans to restore service

A newer idea called zero trust pushes this further. Older networks were like a building with a strong front door: once you were inside, you could walk anywhere. Zero trust assumes the attacker may already be inside. Every user, device and request must prove itself each time, and each person gets only the access they need for their job. This is called least privilege. It limits how far an intruder can move after getting in.

Chapter 05

Guarding the money messages

Banks move money between each other by sending instructions over secure networks. The best known for international transfers is SWIFT, a cooperative that carries standardised payment messages between more than ten thousand institutions. SWIFT itself does not hold money. It carries the instructions that tell banks to move it.

That makes the computers that create these messages some of the most sensitive in any bank. Several protections surround them. The cryptographic keys that sign messages are stored in hardware security modules: tamper-resistant devices that perform signing inside themselves so the key never leaves. High-value payments need approval by two people, a rule known as four eyes. And the same person should not be able both to create and to approve a payment, which is called separation of duties.

These controls are old ideas from accounting, applied to computers. They exist because the most dangerous attack is not a stranger breaking in, but an attacker who has become, in the system's eyes, a trusted employee.

Chapter 06

Making stolen data worthless

If you cannot be sure data will never be stolen, the next best thing is to make it useless when it is. Card payments show how.

Firms that handle card numbers must follow an industry rulebook called PCI DSS (the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard), set by the major card networks. It requires encryption, access controls and regular testing. A more powerful technique is tokenisation: replacing the real card number with a substitute code that only works in one place. When you add a card to a phone wallet, the phone stores a token, not your card number. A criminal who steals the token from one shop cannot use it anywhere else. The best data to protect is data you no longer hold.

Chapter 07

Attacking yourself before others do

Defenders cannot know whether their layers work without testing them. Banks hire penetration testers to try to break specific systems, and red teams to act like a real attacker against the whole organisation, using any method, including phoning the help desk or sending phishing emails to staff.

Regulators now require this for important firms. In 2014 the Bank of England launched CBEST, a framework in which major UK financial firms are attacked by approved testers using real threat intelligence. The European Central Bank's TIBER-EU framework, from 2018, does the same in the euro area. The results are often humbling. The weakest point is usually not a clever technical flaw but a person: an employee who clicks a link, a help-desk worker who resets a password for a convincing caller.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Three kinds of failure, three famous cases

Each part of the CIA triad has failed in a way that changed how the industry defends itself.

Chapter 08

Integrity: the Bangladesh Bank heist

In February 2016, attackers who had been inside the computer systems of Bangladesh's central bank used its SWIFT access to send dozens of payment instructions to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, where Bangladesh kept part of its reserves. The requests added up to nearly one billion US dollars. They were timed for a weekend, when Bangladesh's offices were closed, followed by the Friday-to-Monday gap in New York.

Most of the requests were blocked or stopped. One was halted partly because of a spelling mistake in the name of the receiving organisation, which made a bank along the way ask questions. But about 81 million dollars reached accounts in the Philippines and disappeared, largely through casinos. The US later charged a North Korean programmer in connection with the attack.

SWIFT's network itself had not been broken. The attackers had compromised one member and sent messages that looked genuine. The system trusted the sender because the sender's keys were real. SWIFT responded with a security programme requiring all members to meet minimum controls, and the case is still taught as the model of an integrity attack.

Chapter 09

Confidentiality: when the data walks out

Two US breaches show the most common causes. In 2017, attackers broke into Equifax, the credit bureau, through a known flaw in web software that had not been patched, and took personal data on about 147 million people. In 2019, a former employee of a cloud provider exploited a badly configured firewall at Capital One and accessed data on around 100 million US customers.

Neither attack needed genius. One was a missed update. The other was a setting in the wrong position. Most serious breaches come from ordinary mistakes at scale, not from brilliant attackers. Large banks run thousands of systems, and the attacker needs to find only one that was overlooked. Volume 14's chapter on the cloud explains why moving to rented servers changes, but does not remove, this risk.

Chapter 10

Availability: when the system stops

In November 2023, a ransomware attack hit the US arm of ICBC, the world's largest bank by assets. It briefly disrupted some trades in the US government bond market, one of the most important markets on earth, and the firm reportedly had to settle some trades by other means while its systems were down.

Not every outage is an attack. On 19 July 2024, a faulty software update from the security firm CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers worldwide. Airlines, hospitals and banks were hit at once. No criminal was involved, but the effect looked like a large attack. The lesson was about concentration: when thousands of firms depend on the same few suppliers, one mistake becomes a shared disaster.

Regulators have responded by focusing on operational resilience: the ability to keep critical services running, or restore them quickly, whatever the cause. The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), which applies from January 2025, requires financial firms to test their resilience, report major incidents and manage the risks of their technology suppliers. It also brings critical suppliers, such as major cloud providers, under supervision. The question has shifted from "can we keep them out?" to "how fast can we recover?"

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The trade-offs every security team argues about

Security is never free. Each gain costs money, speed, convenience or independence.

Chapter 12

The legacy problem

Many large banks still run their core ledgers on systems designed decades ago, some written in programming languages, such as COBOL, that few young engineers learn. These systems are reliable in the sense that they rarely crash. They are also hard to change, hard to connect to modern security tools and wrapped in layer after layer of newer software.

Replacing them is expensive and risky. In 2018 the UK bank TSB moved its customers to a new platform, and many were locked out of their accounts or saw errors for weeks. The bank's chief executive left, and regulators later fined the bank. A failed upgrade can be as damaging as an attack, which is why many banks delay, and why the old systems remain one of the industry's quiet weaknesses. Newer digital banks, covered in Digital banking, began without this burden.

Chapter 14

The new front line: crypto

Traditional banks have spent decades hardening their systems. Cryptocurrency exchanges and platforms are younger, hold assets that can be moved irreversibly across borders in minutes, and have become a favourite target. In February 2025, the exchange Bybit lost crypto assets worth about 1.5 billion US dollars in a single attack, which the FBI attributed to North Korea. It was described as the largest crypto theft on record.

The weak point was again not the mathematics. Attackers manipulated the process and software staff used to approve a transfer, so that people signing what they thought was a routine transaction actually handed over control. Cryptography protects the keys; it does not protect the humans who use them. As more money moves onto new rails, described in Digital currencies and CBDCs, the old lessons of four eyes, least privilege and checking the instruction you sign become more important, not less.

The core idea

Financial cybersecurity protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of money that exists as data. Banks stack defences in depth, assume intruders may already be inside (zero trust), and increasingly focus on resilience: recovering fast, whatever the cause.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bangladesh Bank, 2016: nearly 1 billion dollars requested, about 81 million lost.
  • CrowdStrike outage, July 2024: about 8.5 million Windows machines crashed.
  • EU DORA rules apply from January 2025.
The common mistake

Picturing cyber attacks as genius hackers breaking clever encryption. Most damage comes from ordinary failures: an unpatched server, a misconfigured setting, a person tricked into approving something.
