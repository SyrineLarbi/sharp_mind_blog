Open banking. Your bank holds your financial history. Open banking says you may hand the key to someone else.

For most of history, the record of your money lived inside your bank, and only your bank could use it. If a rival wanted to offer you a better loan or a smarter savings account, it could not see what you earned or spent. Open banking changes that. With your permission, other companies can read your account data or start payments from your account through secure, standard connections. It sounds technical. In practice it is a deliberate attempt by governments to shift power from banks to customers.

Question 1 · What is it?

Two new powers, given to the customer

Open banking is a set of rules and technical standards that let licensed third parties, with your consent, read your bank account data or start payments from your account.

Chapter 01

The data your bank keeps to itself

Your current account is a remarkably rich record. It shows your income and when it arrives, your rent, your bills, your habits and whether you ever run short at the end of the month. That record is very valuable to a lender, who wants to know whether you can repay, and to anyone who wants to help you manage money.

Traditionally, only your own bank could use it. That gave existing banks a quiet advantage. They knew which customers were safe to lend to; rivals did not. Customers, meanwhile, rarely switched, because comparing offers was hard and moving accounts was a chore. Economists call this a switching cost, and in banking it was high. Open banking starts from a simple claim: the data describes your life, so you should be able to decide who uses it.

Chapter 02

Reading and paying

Most open banking laws grant two distinct powers. European law gave the firms that use them clumsy but useful names.

Account information (read)

A licensed firm, an account information service provider, can see your balances and transactions at one or more banks. Uses include budgeting apps that combine all your accounts, lenders checking income, and accounting software that pulls in a small firm's payments automatically.

Payment initiation (pay)

A licensed firm, a payment initiation service provider, can start a payment from your account, which you then approve in your bank's app. This lets you pay a shop or a bill straight from your account, without a card, often called "pay by bank".

Neither power lets the third party take money or look at data without you. Each use needs your consent, given for a stated purpose and usually for a limited time, and you can withdraw it.

Chapter 03

Before the rules: screen scraping

The idea is older than the law. From the early 2000s, budgeting and data firms offered to connect to your bank. They did it by asking for your online banking username and password, then logging in as you and copying the information off the screen. This is called screen scraping.

It worked, but it was unsafe and fragile. You were handing your password to a third party, often against your bank's terms. The third party could see and do everything you could. And when the bank changed its website, the connection broke. Open banking replaced the borrowed password with a proper door, built for the purpose, with its own lock and its own limited key. In the US, where rules came later, scraping lasted much longer and still exists alongside newer connections.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Standard doors, temporary keys, licensed visitors

The machinery has three parts: the technical connection, the consent process, and the licensing that decides who may connect at all.

Chapter 04

APIs: the waiter between two kitchens

The technical heart of open banking is the API, an application programming interface. An API is a defined way for one computer program to ask another for something. Think of a restaurant. You do not walk into the kitchen. You give your order to a waiter, who takes it to the kitchen in a set format and brings back what you asked for. You only get what is on the menu.

A bank's open banking API is a menu. A third party can ask "list the transactions on this account for the past 12 months" or "set up a payment of 40 euros to this payee". It cannot ask for anything else, and it cannot change the bank's systems. Standardising the menu is what makes open banking work at scale. In the UK the largest banks were required to build to one common standard, so an app could connect to all of them in the same way. Where every bank builds its own version, as in much of Europe, third parties must handle many slightly different menus.

Chapter 05

The consent journey

The step that matters most to security happens in your bank's own app or website, not in the third party's.

Connecting a budgeting app to your bank (simplified)
  1. In the app, you choose "connect my bank" and pick your bank.
  2. The app shows what it wants: e.g. "read balances and transactions for 90 days".
  3. You are sent to YOUR BANK's app or site.     <- the app never sees your password
  4. You log in to the bank as normal and confirm the request.
  5. The bank gives the app a TOKEN: a digital key that
        - works only for this account
        - allows only what you agreed
        - expires, and can be cancelled from your bank's app
  6. The app uses the token to call the bank's API and fetch the data.

This pattern, called redirect authentication, uses a widely adopted standard called OAuth, the same family of technology behind "sign in with" buttons on websites. The key point is that your password stays between you and your bank. The third party holds a token with limited powers, which you can revoke without changing your password.

Chapter 06

Who is allowed in

Not any company can connect. In the UK and EU, a firm that wants to read accounts or start payments must be authorised by a financial regulator, show that it can protect data, and hold insurance or capital. When it connects, it presents a digital certificate proving its identity, and the bank checks that the licence is valid.

Banks, in turn, must not block or degrade access for authorised firms. They must give them connections that work as well as their own apps, and they must report how reliable those connections are. That requirement mattered, because banks had little reason to make life easy for competitors. Open banking only works if someone forces the doors to stay open and well maintained.

Chapter 07

Pay by bank, and payments that repeat

The payment side runs on top of a country's instant bank-transfer system, such as Faster Payments in the UK or SEPA Instant in the euro area. When you pay by bank at checkout, the shop's provider sends a pre-filled payment request to your bank, you approve it, and the money arrives in seconds.

For a shop, this can be cheaper than accepting cards, because there are no card network or interchange fees, and there is less risk of card fraud because the customer approves inside their banking app. For the customer, it is often less familiar, and it lacks some protections cards offer, such as the right to ask your card issuer to reverse a payment when goods never arrive. That protection, called a chargeback, is one reason cards remain popular.

A newer UK development is the variable recurring payment: a standing permission for a firm to make repeated payments within limits you set, such as "up to 200 pounds a month". The first required form lets people automatically move money between their own accounts, for example sweeping spare cash into savings. Extending it to paying bills and subscriptions is under way.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Competition by design

Open banking was created mainly by competition authorities and regulators, not by the market. Its purpose is to make banking more competitive and to let new services be built on data that used to be locked away.

Chapter 08

How it began: two different starts

In the UK, the push came from a competition inquiry. In 2016 the Competition and Markets Authority concluded that the big banks did not compete hard enough for current-account customers, partly because customers rarely switched. It ordered the nine largest banks, known as the CMA9, to build common open APIs. They went live in January 2018. By 2024 the UK had more than ten million users of open banking services.

In the European Union, the second Payment Services Directive (PSD2), adopted in 2015 and applying from early 2018, required banks across the EU to give licensed third parties access to payment accounts. It also introduced stronger customer authentication for online payments. PSD2 set the legal principle but left technical standards to the market, which produced more variety and more friction.

The UK model mandated one standard and a central body to run it; the EU model mandated access and let the industry build the doors. Most observers think the UK approach produced faster take-up, though the EU has a larger market.

Most users do not know they are using open banking. They just notice that an app connected to their bank in a few taps, or that a mortgage application did not ask for three months of paper statements. Good infrastructure tends to become invisible.

Chapter 10

A challenge to the card networks

The payment side of open banking has a larger significance. Card networks such as Visa and Mastercard dominate payments in shops and online in much of the world. Merchants pay fees on every card transaction. Account-to-account payments, started through open banking, offer an alternative that bypasses the card networks entirely.

Several countries have gone much further with a different approach. India's UPI and Brazil's Pix, both built by public authorities, made instant bank-to-bank payments the everyday way to pay, as Volume 14 describes. They show that when a cheap, instant, well-designed account-to-account system exists, people switch to it in huge numbers. Open banking in Europe and the UK is a slower, more market-based attempt at a similar shift.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

More choice, more connections, more ways to go wrong

Opening data creates competition and convenience. It also creates new parties holding sensitive information and new questions about who is responsible.

Chapter 11

Weighing it up

Benefits

  • Competition: new firms can offer services without first winning your main account.
  • Safer than scraping: no shared passwords, limited and revocable access.
  • Better credit decisions: lending based on real cash flow, not only past borrowing.
  • Cheaper payments: account-to-account payments avoid card fees.
  • Less paperwork: income and identity checks in minutes.

Costs and risks

  • More copies of your data: every connected firm is another place it can leak or be misused.
  • Consent fatigue: people tap "agree" without reading what they shared.
  • Weaker protections: pay by bank lacks the chargeback rights of cards.
  • Scam risk: instant, irreversible transfers suit fraudsters as well as shops.
  • Unequal benefit: profitable uses may favour already well-served customers.

There is also a quieter worry about how the data might be used. A lender that can read every transaction can price more precisely. That helps the reliable customer who was previously misjudged. It could also be used to charge more to people whose spending reveals stress, such as regular gambling or payday loans. Data protection law limits this, but the line between "responsible lending" and "using your life against you" is debated.

Chapter 12

Who pays for the pipes?

Under UK and EU rules, banks must provide basic access to authorised third parties free of charge. Banks argue this is unfair: they bear the cost of building and running secure APIs, and fintech firms build profitable businesses on top. Fintech firms reply that the data belongs to the customer, and that charging for access would recreate the very barrier the rules were meant to remove.

The argument became loud in the US in 2025, when JPMorgan Chase, the largest US bank, said it would start charging data aggregators for access to customer data, and later reached agreements with some of them. The episode showed that without a binding rule, the bank holding the data has strong bargaining power. A sensible middle ground many people suggest is free access for the basics that the law requires, with paid "premium" services for extra features, an approach the UK has moved toward. Where the line should sit is still contested.

Chapter 14

Three national philosophies

Around the world, three approaches have emerged, and the choice between them reflects deeper views about the role of the state in markets.

Regulator-led

The UK, the EU and Australia write rules that force banks to open access to licensed firms. The state sets the principle; firms build and run the services.

Market-led

The US let private aggregators and bank agreements lead. A federal rule on data access was finalised in late 2024, then reopened for reconsideration in 2025, so its final shape remains uncertain as of 2026.

Public infrastructure

India built a state-backed framework of licensed "account aggregators" alongside UPI, and Brazil's central bank runs both Pix and an open finance programme. The state builds core rails itself.

Each has trade-offs. Market-led systems innovate quickly but leave customers with uneven protection. Regulator-led systems are safer but slower. Public-infrastructure systems can reach huge numbers of people fast, but they concentrate power in the state. Open banking is ultimately a political choice about who controls financial data, and the same question returns, in sharper form, with central bank digital currencies (Topic 15).

The core idea

Open banking lets licensed third parties, with your consent, read your account data or start payments from your account through standard APIs, using limited, revocable tokens instead of your password. It was created by regulators to increase competition, and it is widening into open finance.

The numbers worth knowing
  • UK open banking went live in January 2018, ordered for the nine largest banks.
  • The EU's PSD2 applied from 2018.
  • The UK passed ten million open banking users by 2024.
The common mistake

Thinking open banking means your data is public, or that apps can take money freely. Nothing happens without your consent, the app never sees your bank password, and you can withdraw access at any time.
