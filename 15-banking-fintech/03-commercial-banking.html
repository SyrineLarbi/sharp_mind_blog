Commercial banking. A shop that sells two things: somewhere safe for money, and money for later.

The previous topics looked at banks as machines that create money and as members of a payment system. This one looks at them as companies trying to make a profit. A commercial bank sells accounts, loans, cards and payment services to households and firms. Its choices about which customers to chase, what to charge and how much risk to take decide whether it thrives, merges or fails. They also decide who in the economy can borrow.

Question 1 · What is it?

The bank you actually deal with

"Commercial bank" means a bank that takes deposits and makes loans to the public and to businesses. It is the opposite of a bank that mainly trades and advises on deals.

Chapter 01

The free account that costs a fortune

In many countries, an ordinary current account costs the customer little or nothing. Yet running it is expensive. The bank pays for the card, the app, the fraud checks, the call centre, the branch, the regulators' reporting and the payment systems. So why give it away?

Because the account is not the product. It is the doorway. The money sitting in current accounts is the cheapest funding a bank can get, since it pays little or no interest on it. And the customer whose salary arrives there is the customer most likely to take the bank's mortgage, credit card and car loan, because the bank can see their income and spending. A current account is what shops call a loss leader: a product sold cheaply to bring in customers who will buy others. Most of commercial banking follows from that logic.

Chapter 02

Retail, commercial, investment

Banking divides roughly by customer and by activity.

Retail banking

Individuals and very small firms. Current and savings accounts, cards, mortgages, personal loans. High volume, small amounts, standard products, sold through branches and apps.

Commercial (corporate) banking

Businesses from the local bakery to large manufacturers. Business loans, overdrafts, trade finance, payments and cash management. Each relationship is larger and more tailored.

Investment banking

Large companies, governments and investors. Advice on mergers, issuing shares and bonds, and trading securities. Earns fees and trading profits rather than mostly interest.

"Commercial banking" in the broad sense, the subject of this file, covers the first two: taking deposits and lending. Many large groups do all three under one roof. They are called universal banks. JPMorgan Chase, the largest US bank, with assets of around 4 trillion dollars as of 2024, is one. So are BNP Paribas in France and HSBC in the UK. The largest banks in the world by assets are Chinese state-controlled commercial banks, led by ICBC.

Chapter 03

Why some countries keep them apart

After the 1929 crash and the bank failures that followed, US Senate hearings led by the lawyer Ferdinand Pecora exposed how some banks had sold risky securities to their own depositors. In 1933 the Glass-Steagall Act separated the two businesses: a bank could take deposits or deal in securities, but not both. The idea was that ordinary savings, now protected by deposit insurance, should not fund speculation.

The wall was worn down over decades and largely removed in 1999. After the 2008 crisis, the argument returned. The US added the Volcker rule, which limits banks with insured deposits from trading for their own profit. The UK chose ring-fencing: since 2019 its largest banks must keep everyday retail banking in a separate company inside the group, with its own capital and board, so that it can survive if the investment bank next door fails. The EU debated a similar split and did not adopt it. Whether separation makes banks safer, or just pushes risk elsewhere, remains contested.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Gather cheap money, lend it at a price that covers the risk

The business model has two sides, funding and lending, and a fee engine on top. Each is a set of decisions about price and risk.

Trade finance is the oldest item on the list and still one of the most important. A classic tool is the letter of credit. A bank promises a foreign seller that it will pay once the seller shows documents proving the goods were shipped. The seller trusts the bank rather than an unknown buyer on the other side of the world. Much of global trade still runs on such promises.

Chapter 05

The funding side: why deposits are gold

A bank can fund its loans with customer deposits, with money borrowed from other banks and investors, or with bonds. Deposits are usually the cheapest. Many customers leave money in a current account earning nothing because it is convenient. They are also usually the most stable. An ordinary household does not move its salary account every time another bank offers a slightly higher rate. Bankers call such money sticky.

How sticky it really is matters a great deal. When central bank rates rise, a bank chooses how much of the rise to pass on to savers. The share it passes on is called the deposit beta. A low beta means the bank keeps most of the gain, and its margin widens. That is why bank profits jumped in many countries in 2023. But if the bank pays too little, customers eventually move money to savings accounts at rivals, or to government bonds and money-market funds. A deposit is only cheap for as long as the customer does not notice.

Chapter 06

How a loan is priced

The interest rate on a loan is not a guess. A well-run bank builds it from parts.

Building a loan rate, step by step (illustrative numbers)
  Cost of funding the loan               3.0%   what the bank pays for the money
+ Expected loss                          1.0%   chance of default x share lost
+ Cost of the capital it must hold       1.2%   shareholders want a return on it
+ Operating cost                         0.8%   staff, systems, checks
+ Margin                                 0.5%   profit, and room for competition
  ------------------------------------------
= Rate offered to this borrower          6.5%

  A riskier borrower raises the expected loss AND the capital needed.
  That is why the rate climbs so steeply as credit quality falls.

Expected loss is the heart of it. It combines three estimates: the chance the borrower defaults, how much the bank would be owed at that moment, and how much it would lose after selling any security. A mortgage on a house has a low loss because the house can be sold. An unsecured card balance has a high one. This is why a card can charge many times the rate of a mortgage and still not be the more profitable product. The detail of how lenders judge borrowers is in Loans and Credit scores.

Chapter 07

Provisions: paying for bad loans before they happen

Some loans will never be repaid. A bank does not wait for them to fail before counting the cost. It sets aside a provision, an amount deducted from profit to cover losses it expects on its existing loans. Since 2018, international accounting rules (IFRS 9, used in Europe and much of the world) and, from 2020, a similar US standard require banks to provide for expected losses, not only for losses that have already appeared.

Provisions make bank profits swing with the economy. When a recession looks likely, banks raise provisions, profits fall, and capital is used up, even before any customer misses a payment. When the outlook brightens, provisions are released and profits rise. Investors watch the provision line more closely than almost anything else, because a bank's real profit in any year depends on judgments about losses that have not happened yet.

Chapter 08

Interest income and fee income

A commercial bank's income comes in two streams. Net interest income is the interest earned on loans and bonds minus the interest paid on deposits and borrowing. For most retail-focused banks it is the larger part, often well over half. Fee and commission income comes from cards, account packages, payments, foreign exchange, wealth management and corporate services.

Banks like fees because they do not need much capital and do not depend on interest rates. That is why, during the long period of near-zero rates in the 2010s, many European banks pushed into wealth management and payments. But fee businesses face more competition from specialists, such as payment firms and online brokers, that do one thing cheaply. That competition is the story of Fintech startups.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Who gets credit, and on what terms

Commercial banks decide which households can buy homes and which small firms can grow. How they make money shapes that decision.

Chapter 09

The small firm's only door

A large company can borrow by selling bonds to investors. A small one cannot. Investors will not study the accounts of a twelve-person engineering firm. So small and medium-sized enterprises, often shortened to SMEs, depend heavily on banks, especially in Europe, where bank loans are a much larger share of business funding than in the United States, where bond markets are deeper.

Lending to small firms is costly, because each loan is small and needs real knowledge of the business. A local manager who has known the owner for years can judge the risk. This is called relationship lending. As banks closed branches and moved decisions to central credit teams and scoring models, some of that knowledge was lost. Research on whether this has reduced lending to small firms is mixed. But when banks pull back in a downturn, small firms are the first and hardest hit, which is why governments often guarantee small-business loans in a crisis, as many did in 2020.

Chapter 10

Three numbers that tell you if a bank is any good

MeasureWhat it meansWhat to look for
Return on equity (ROE)Profit as a share of the shareholders' moneyAbove what investors demand for the risk, often estimated at around 8–12 per cent for large banks
Cost-to-income ratioOperating costs as a share of incomeLower is better; a bank spending 70 cents to earn a euro has little room for losses
Capital ratio (CET1)The highest-quality capital as a share of risk-weighted assetsComfortably above the regulatory minimum, which for large banks is often around 10 per cent or more once buffers are added

The tension between them is the daily life of a bank's management. More capital makes the bank safer but lowers return on equity, because profit is spread over more shareholders' money. Cutting costs raises returns but may weaken risk control or service. Through much of the 2010s, many European banks earned returns below what investors demanded, which is why their shares often traded below the book value of their assets.

Chapter 11

Not one model: banking around the world

The United States has an unusual number of banks, several thousand, many of them small community banks. Germany has a "three pillar" system: private banks, public savings banks (the Sparkassen) owned by towns and regions, and cooperative banks owned by their members. The savings and cooperative banks aim to serve their region rather than to maximise profit. China's banking system is dominated by large state-controlled banks that also serve as tools of government economic policy.

Islamic banking, important in the Gulf, Malaysia and elsewhere, follows rules that forbid charging interest. Instead of a loan with interest, a bank might buy a house and sell it to the customer at a higher price paid in instalments, or share in the profits of a business it finances. Critics argue some products resemble conventional loans in economic terms. Supporters answer that the sharing of risk and the link to real assets are genuine. Either way, it shows that the core functions of banking can be delivered through quite different contracts.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A bundle that works, and the temptations it creates

Bundling accounts, loans and fees under one roof gives banks real advantages. It also gives them reasons to sell people things they do not need.

Chapter 13

When cross-selling goes wrong

Two scandals show the dark side of the bundle. In the UK, banks spent years selling payment protection insurance (PPI) alongside loans and cards. It was meant to cover repayments if the borrower fell ill or lost their job. Many customers did not need it, could never have claimed on it, or did not know they had bought it. After regulators acted, British banks paid out tens of billions of pounds in compensation over roughly a decade, one of the largest consumer redress programmes anywhere.

In the United States in 2016, Wells Fargo was fined after it emerged that staff, under pressure from aggressive sales targets, had opened millions of accounts that customers had never asked for. The bank later paid far larger penalties, and in 2018 the Federal Reserve capped the growth of its balance sheet, a limit lifted only in 2025. Both cases had the same cause: incentives that rewarded selling more products rather than serving customers well. Regulators in many countries now judge banks partly on whether their products deliver fair value to customers.

Every one of these trends nibbles at the bundle described in Chapter 01. Deposits move to apps, loans move to funds, and services move to specialists. What stays with the bank is the expensive core: a licence, a balance sheet, a promise to repay deposits on demand, and a seat at the central bank. The next topic, Digital banking, looks at how that core is being rebuilt in software. The commercial bank of the future may be less a shop than a supplier to other shops.

The core idea

A commercial bank gathers cheap, sticky deposits, lends them at rates that cover funding, expected loss, capital and costs, and adds fee income on top. The current account is the doorway; the loans and fees are the business.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Glass-Steagall separated commercial and investment banking in 1933; most of the wall was removed in 1999.
  • UK ring-fencing of big banks' retail arms has applied since 2019.
  • JPMorgan Chase held assets of around 4 trillion dollars as of 2024.
The common mistake

Thinking the highest-rate product is the most profitable. A card's high rate mostly pays for its high losses; the cheap deposit behind every loan often earns the bank more.
