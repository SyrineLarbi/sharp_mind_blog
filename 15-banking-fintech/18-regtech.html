Regtech. The software that decides whether a bank is a gatekeeper or a getaway car.

Every large bank employs thousands of people whose job is not to lend or to trade, but to check. Who is this customer, really? Why did this account receive forty payments just under the reporting limit? Is this company on a sanctions list under a different spelling? After the 2008 crisis, and after a series of record fines, the amount of checking exploded. Regtech, short for regulatory technology, is the software built to do that checking faster and better. It is one of the least glamorous corners of finance. It is also one of the places where the fight against organised crime, terrorism and sanctions-busting is actually won or lost.

Question 1 · What is it?

Technology for following the rules, and for checking that others do

Regtech covers any software that helps a financial firm meet its legal duties. Its twin, suptech, is the same idea used by the supervisors who watch the firms.

Chapter 01

A branch in Tallinn

Danske Bank is Denmark's largest bank. In 2007 it bought a bank with a small branch in Estonia. Over the following eight years, around 200 billion euros flowed through that branch's accounts held by non-residents, many of them shell companies (companies with no real business, used to hide who owns money) linked to Russia and other former Soviet states. Much of it was later judged suspicious. Warnings from a whistleblower inside the bank in 2013 were not acted on properly. When the full story came out in 2018, the chief executive resigned. In 2022 the bank pleaded guilty in the United States and agreed to pay about two billion dollars.

The Estonian branch was a tiny part of Danske Bank. Its systems were separate from the head office's. Its customer checks were weak. Its staff were rewarded for bringing in business. The money did not hide from the bank. It passed through in plain sight, because nobody was looking at the right data in the right way.

Regtech exists because of stories like this. It is the attempt to make "looking at the right data" something a bank does automatically, every day, for every customer and every payment.

Chapter 02

What compliance actually involves

Compliance means obeying the laws and rules that apply to a business. For a bank, that list is long. The main duties, and the regtech that serves each, are below.

DutyWhat the law asksWhat the software does
Know your customer (KYC)Verify who each customer is before serving themChecks identity documents, matches faces to photos, searches databases
Anti-money laundering (AML)Spot and report suspicious money flowsMonitors transactions for unusual patterns and raises alerts
Sanctions screeningNever deal with listed people, firms or countriesCompares every customer and payment against official lists

Conduct and market abuseStop insider trading and market manipulationWatches traders' orders, emails and chats for warning signs
Operational resilienceKeep critical services running through outages and attacksMaps systems and suppliers, tests failures, records incidents

The word regtech became common around 2015, and Britain's Financial Conduct Authority was among the first regulators to use it publicly. The term suptech, supervisory technology, followed for the regulators' side: software that lets a central bank or a supervisor read thousands of reports automatically and spot which firms need a closer look.

Chapter 03

Why compliance became an industry

Banking rules are old. Rules aimed at money laundering are much younger. In 1989 the G7 group of rich countries created the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), an intergovernmental body that sets international standards against money laundering. After the attacks of September 2001, those standards were extended to the financing of terrorism. Countries that fall short can be placed on FATF's "grey list" for closer monitoring, which makes their banks' international business harder and more expensive.

Then came 2008. The financial crisis produced a wave of new rules on capital, liquidity, trading and consumer protection: the Basel III standards worldwide, the Dodd-Frank Act in the United States, and a long series of European rules. At the same time, enforcement became much tougher. In 2012 HSBC agreed to pay about 1.9 billion dollars in the United States over failures that let Mexican drug cartels move money through its accounts. In 2014 BNP Paribas agreed to pay about 8.9 billion dollars for breaking US sanctions.

Banks responded by hiring. Compliance departments of several thousand people became normal at the largest banks. Regtech is what happens when an activity grows so big and so repetitive that software becomes cheaper than people.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Identify, monitor, screen, report

A bank's compliance machinery runs as a chain. First it learns who the customer is. Then it watches what they do, checks them against lists, and reports what it finds.

Chapter 04

Knowing the customer, in four minutes

Opening an account used to require a branch visit. Today, in many countries, you photograph your passport and take a short video selfie. Behind that screen, several checks run in seconds. The software reads the document, checks its security features, and compares the photo with your face. A liveness check looks for signs that a real person is in front of the camera, not a printed photo, a mask or a deepfake video. Your name is then checked against databases of politically exposed persons (PEPs: senior public officials and their families, who carry a higher risk of corruption), sanctions lists and adverse media (negative news reports).

For companies, the hardest question is beneficial ownership: who ultimately owns and controls the company, behind any chain of other companies. A criminal rarely owns a bank account in their own name. They own a company that owns a company in another country that owns the account. Several countries now keep public or semi-public registers of beneficial owners, but their quality varies, and in 2022 the EU's top court restricted general public access to them on privacy grounds.

KYC is not a one-time event. Banks must refresh it, more often for higher-risk customers. This is called customer due diligence, and the stronger version for high-risk customers is enhanced due diligence.

Chapter 05

Watching the money: transaction monitoring

Money laundering is usually described in three stages. In placement, dirty money, often cash, enters the financial system. In layering, it is moved through many accounts, companies and countries to hide its origin. In integration, it comes back out looking clean, for example as the price of a property or the profit of a business.

The classic pattern is structuring, also called smurfing: splitting a large sum into many deposits just below the amount that triggers an automatic report. The software looks for that, and for hundreds of other patterns. When it raises an alert, an analyst investigates. If the analyst finds the activity suspicious, the bank files a suspicious activity report with the country's financial intelligence unit, a government agency that collects these reports and passes leads to police.

The weak point is the alerts. Traditional rule-based systems produce huge numbers of them, and industry estimates commonly put the share of false alarms at well over 90%. Analysts spend most of their day proving that innocent people are innocent. Much of modern regtech is an attempt to cut that noise with machine learning, which ranks alerts by how suspicious they look and links related accounts into networks.

Chapter 06

Sanctions screening: one name, many spellings

Governments publish lists of people, companies, ships and countries that banks must not deal with. The US Treasury, the EU, the UK and the United Nations all keep such lists. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, these lists grew faster than at any time before, and banks had to update their screening within hours of each new announcement.

Screening sounds simple: compare names. In practice it is hard. A name written in Arabic, Cyrillic or Chinese script can be transliterated into Latin letters in several ways. Mohammed can be Muhammad, Mohamed or Mohammad. Dates of birth are missing. Common names produce thousands of near-matches. So screening software uses fuzzy matching, which scores how similar two names are rather than demanding an exact match, plus other details such as nationality and date of birth.

Set the matching too strict and a sanctioned person slips through with a slightly different spelling. Set it too loose and thousands of innocent payments are stopped. Every compliance system is a setting on a dial between missing criminals and blocking ordinary customers.

Chapter 07

Reporting to supervisors, and rules a machine can read

Banks send their supervisors a constant stream of data: how much capital they hold, how much cash they could raise in a crisis, which large loans they have made, and, under European market rules such as MiFID II, details of millions of trades. Gathering this from dozens of internal systems, each with its own definitions, is a large part of a bank's compliance cost.

Regtech here is mainly about data plumbing: collecting, cleaning and checking the numbers before they go out. A more ambitious idea is machine-readable regulation. Today a rule is written in legal language, read by lawyers, translated by staff into a specification, and coded by engineers, with room for error at every step. If a regulator published the rule as precise code, or as a standard data definition, banks could plug it in directly. Regulators in the UK, the EU and elsewhere have run pilots, and the Bank for International Settlements created an Innovation Hub in 2019 that works on such tools.

The latest wave of rules targets technology itself. The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), which applies from January 2025, requires financial firms to manage risks from IT failures and from their technology suppliers, including cloud providers. Financial cybersecurity covers the threats behind it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Crime needs a bank account

Drug cartels, people traffickers, corrupt officials and sanctioned regimes all face the same problem: their money is useless until it looks legitimate. Banks are where it has to pass.

Chapter 08

The scale of the problem, and how little is caught

Nobody knows how much money is laundered each year, because the whole point is that it is hidden. A widely quoted, very rough estimate from the United Nations puts it at 2 to 5% of world economic output. Whatever the true figure, enforcement agencies agree that only a small fraction of criminal money is ever seized. The global system catches very little, at very high cost.

That is why the quality of banks' checks matters so much. A bank that files many low-quality reports to protect itself helps nobody. Financial intelligence units in several countries receive far more reports than they can read. A good report, which links accounts, explains the pattern and names the people involved, can start a police investigation. A pile of automatic ones mostly creates work.

Leaks have shown the gaps. The Panama Papers in 2016, a leak of documents from a Panamanian law firm, showed how easily shell companies could hide the owners of money. Investigations into what journalists called "laundromats" showed billions moving from Russia and Azerbaijan through European banks. Each scandal led to stricter rules, and each new rule created more demand for regtech.

Chapter 09

The cost of getting it wrong

For banks, failure is expensive and sometimes existential. In 2024 TD Bank, one of Canada's largest banks, pleaded guilty in the United States to failures in its anti-money-laundering programme and agreed to penalties of about three billion dollars. US regulators also capped the size of its US business, a punishment that limits growth for years. In 2023 Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, agreed to pay about 4.3 billion dollars in the US over money-laundering and sanctions violations, and its founder pleaded guilty and stepped down.

These cases share a pattern. The problem was rarely a lack of software. It was a culture that treated compliance as a brake on growth. Systems were underfunded, alerts were left unreviewed, warnings from staff were ignored. Regtech can make good compliance cheaper. It cannot make a bank want to comply.

Chapter 10

The people shut out: de-risking

There is a side effect that is less often discussed. When fines are huge and some customers are expensive to check, banks may simply close those customers' accounts. This is called de-risking. Money-transfer firms serving migrants, charities working in conflict zones, small banks in poor countries, and people from countries on FATF's grey list have all lost access to banking this way.

The effect can be serious. When large international banks cut ties with smaller banks in some regions, a process known as the decline of correspondent banking (one bank holding accounts for another bank abroad so that it can make international payments), whole countries found it harder and more expensive to receive money from abroad. FATF and the World Bank have both warned that de-risking pushes money into informal channels that are even harder to watch.

This connects directly to Financial inclusion. Good regtech should make it cheap enough to check a risky customer that the bank keeps them, instead of turning them away. That is one of its strongest arguments.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Cheaper checking, and the risk of checking badly at scale

Software can check more customers, more consistently, for less money. It can also spread one bad assumption across millions of decisions.

Chapter 12

Box-ticking versus judgement

Critics of the anti-money-laundering system make a hard argument. They say much of it measures effort rather than results. Banks are judged on whether they have the right policies, files and reports, not on how much crime they actually stop. That encourages defensive compliance: file a report whenever in doubt, refuse any customer who looks complicated, and document everything. It protects the bank and does little else.

Regtech can make this worse or better. It makes it worse if it simply automates box-ticking at higher speed. It makes it better if it frees analysts from false alarms to spend time on the cases that matter. The most useful tools are the ones that improve the quality of a human judgement, not the ones that replace it with a score.

There is also a risk of over-trust. A model trained on past suspicious cases learns what past criminals did, and the cases that were caught. Criminals adapt. A system that looks clean may only mean nobody has found the new method yet.

Chapter 14

New authorities, digital identity and the arms race

Institutions are changing too. The EU created a new Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA), based in Frankfurt, which began operating in 2025 and will directly supervise some of the riskiest financial firms in the coming years, replacing a patchwork in which each country checked its own banks. Europe is also building digital identity wallets that citizens could use to prove who they are to a bank, which could make KYC both faster and more reliable.

The criminals are changing as well. Generative AI makes fake documents and deepfake video selfies cheaper to produce, which attacks the identity checks of Chapter 04 directly. Crypto-assets create new paths for layering, though public blockchains also leave records that investigators can trace. Digital currencies explains why. AI regulation covers the EU rules that treat some financial AI, such as credit scoring, as high-risk.

The likely outcome is not victory but a continuing arms race. Regtech will not make money laundering impossible. At best, it makes it more expensive, and makes good compliance cheap enough that banks stop turning away honest customers they cannot afford to check.

The core idea

Regtech is software for compliance: knowing customers, monitoring transactions, screening against sanctions lists and reporting to supervisors. It exists because rules and fines grew after 2001 and 2008. Its central problem is the dial between missing criminals and blocking innocent customers, and its central limit is that software cannot fix a bank that does not want to comply.

The numbers worth knowing
  • FATF founded in 1989.
  • About 200 billion euros passed through Danske Bank's Estonian branch, 2007–2015.
  • False alarms in traditional monitoring: commonly estimated at well over 90%.
  • Laundering worldwide: a rough UN estimate of 2–5% of world output.
The common mistake

Thinking more alerts and more reports mean better protection. Quality beats quantity. A flood of defensive reports hides the few that matter.
