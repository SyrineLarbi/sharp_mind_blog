Central banking. The pipes that every payment runs through, and the hand on the valve.

The news covers central banks when they change interest rates. That is the visible tip. Underneath, a central bank runs the system that lets banks pay each other, holds the special money they use to do it, decides how much cash to lend them in a panic, and in many countries inspects their books. None of this makes headlines on a good day. On a bad day, it decides whether the whole financial system keeps working.

Question 1 · What is it?

The bank where banks keep their money

You have an account at a commercial bank. Your bank has an account at the central bank. That second layer is where the financial system actually settles its debts.

Chapter 01

A bank that closed at the wrong hour

On 26 June 1974, German regulators closed a small Cologne bank called Bankhaus Herstatt. It had lost heavily betting on currencies. The timing caused the real damage. Herstatt was shut in the afternoon, German time, after other banks had already paid it Deutsche Marks for that day's currency trades. It was due to pay them US dollars in return, later that day in New York. Those dollars were never sent. Its partners had handed over one half of each trade and never received the other.

The shock spread through the market for currency payments, and banks briefly stopped trusting each other to pay at all. The same year, the leading central banks set up the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, which still writes the world's main banking rules. The risk Herstatt exposed is now named after it. Herstatt risk is the danger that you pay your side of a deal and the other side fails before paying theirs. Much of central banking is about designing that danger out.

Chapter 02

Two layers of money

Money in a modern economy comes in two tiers. The money you use is mostly deposits at commercial banks, created when those banks lend (see How banks work). The money banks use with each other is central bank money, held in accounts at the central bank. Those balances are called reserves. Banknotes are the only kind of central bank money ordinary people can hold.

When you pay 500 euros to someone at a different bank, your bank lowers your deposit, their bank raises theirs, and to make the books balance, your bank sends 500 euros of reserves to their bank, across the central bank's own ledger. The deposits change at the edges; the reserves move in the middle. Because reserves are a claim on the central bank itself, a bank that receives them carries no risk that the payer's bank will fail later. The payment is final.

This is why the central bank is called the bank for banks. Its plumbing jobs are four: it holds banks' accounts and runs the system that moves reserves between them; it sets the price of reserves, which anchors every other interest rate; it lends reserves to banks that are short, including in a crisis; and in most countries it supervises the banks, or works closely with whoever does.

Chapter 04

Settlement: gross and instant, or netted later

There are two ways to settle many payments between banks. In net settlement, banks keep a running tally all day, cancel out what they owe each other, and move only the difference at the end. It saves a lot of money, because a bank that sends 10 billion and receives 9.8 billion needs only 200 million at settlement. But until settlement, every bank is exposed to every other one. If one fails during the day, the whole tally may have to be unwound.

In real-time gross settlement (RTGS), each payment is settled on its own, in full, the moment it is made, in central bank money. There is no tally and no exposure. The cost is that banks need far more reserves during the day. Large, urgent payments between banks, such as the money side of a big securities trade or a house purchase, usually go through RTGS. Everyday retail payments are usually netted first and settled in batches.

To make RTGS affordable, central banks lend reserves to banks within the day, free or nearly free, against collateral, to be repaid by evening. That lending is called intraday credit. For currency trades, the answer to Herstatt risk came in 2002, when banks launched CLS, a specialist system that pays both currencies in a trade at the same moment or not at all.

Chapter 05

Pricing reserves: how the policy rate is enforced

When a central bank "sets interest rates", it actually sets the price at which banks can borrow and lend reserves overnight. It has to make that price hold in a real market. The classic method is a corridor. The central bank offers two standing deals to any bank, every day. A bank with spare reserves can deposit them at the central bank at a low rate, which is the floor. A bank short of reserves can borrow from the central bank, against collateral, at a higher rate, which is the ceiling. No bank will lend to another below the floor or borrow above the ceiling, so the market rate stays between the two.

Since 2008 many central banks have used a simpler floor system. Their large bond purchases, known as quantitative easing, flooded banks with reserves. US bank reserves went from tens of billions of dollars before 2008 to several trillion afterwards. With so many reserves around, the market rate sinks to whatever the central bank pays on reserves. So the central bank sets policy simply by choosing that rate. The Fed began paying interest on reserves in October 2008 for exactly this reason. The rate paid on reserves became the main policy tool. The macro effects of those rates are covered in Interest rates.

Chapter 06

Lending in a crisis: the window nobody wants to use

The principle of the lender of last resort is old. In a panic, lend freely to solid banks against good collateral, at a penalty rate. Economics · Central banks tells that story. The practice is harder than the principle, for three reasons.

Collateral. A central bank lends only against assets it can take if the bank does not repay. The list of acceptable assets, and the discount applied to each one, called a haircut, decides how much a bank can actually borrow. A bank whose assets are mostly loans to small firms may find it can borrow far less than it needs.

Stigma. In the US, banks borrow emergency cash at the discount window. For decades, banks have avoided it even when they needed it, fearing that markets would find out and assume the worst. A safety valve that nobody dares to open does not work. Central banks have tried to fight stigma by making facilities routine, such as the Fed's standing repo facility, set up in 2021, which lends against government bonds every day.

Speed. A bank needs its collateral already registered and tested with the central bank before a crisis, not during one. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank was not set up to borrow quickly enough from the Fed while its deposits left in hours.

Chapter 07

Supervision: checking the books before the crisis

Rules on capital and liquidity only work if someone checks them. Supervisors read banks' reports, visit them, question their managers, and can order them to raise capital, stop paying dividends or change their leaders. A related job is macroprudential policy, which looks at the system as a whole rather than one bank: for example, limiting how much people can borrow compared with their income, if house prices look like a bubble.

One of the main tools since 2009 is the stress test. The supervisor writes a severe imaginary scenario, such as a deep recession, a crash in house prices and soaring unemployment. Each large bank must show what would happen to its capital. Banks that fall short must raise more or stop paying dividends. The Fed, the ECB and the Bank of England all run regular stress tests.

When a bank does fail, a resolution authority takes over. The aim is to keep the bank's payments and deposits running while the owners and bondholders take the losses. In the US the FDIC does this, often over a weekend, by selling the failed bank to a healthy one. Customers usually find on Monday morning that their bank has a new name and nothing else has changed.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

When the plumbing becomes the story

Three crises show what the plumbing is for. In each one, the decisive actions were not rate changes but changes to who could borrow what, against which assets.

Chapter 08

2008: when banks stopped lending to each other

In normal times, banks lend each other reserves overnight without a second thought. In 2007 and 2008, as losses on American housing loans spread, no bank knew which others were holding the bad assets. So they stopped lending to each other, or lent only at high rates and for very short periods. The market that moves reserves around the system jammed.

Central banks replaced it. They lent to banks for longer periods, accepted a wider range of collateral, and created new facilities for firms that were not banks at all, such as dealers in money markets. There was a second problem. Many European banks had borrowed in US dollars and could not get them. The Fed set up swap lines with other central banks: it lent dollars to the ECB, the Bank of England and others, which lent them on to their own banks. Since 2013 the swap lines between the Fed and five major central banks have been permanent. The Fed became, in effect, a lender of last resort to the world in dollars.

Chapter 09

2022 and 2023: the market maker of last resort

In September 2022, after a UK government budget alarmed investors, the price of long-term UK government bonds, called gilts, fell sharply. Many pension funds had used borrowed money in strategies that now forced them to sell gilts to raise cash, which pushed prices down further. The Bank of England stepped in and offered to buy long-dated gilts for a limited period to stop the spiral. It was not a rate decision. It was a temporary promise to stand behind a market.

In March 2023, after SVB failed, the Fed created a new facility that let banks borrow against government bonds valued at their face value, not their lower market price. That removed the reason banks had to sell bonds at a loss to meet withdrawals. The same weekend, US authorities protected all of SVB's depositors, including those above the insurance limit. Together these measures calmed the run on other mid-sized banks within weeks, although First Republic Bank still failed in May.

Both episodes show a shift. Central banks now act as a backstop for markets, not just for banks, because so much of the financial system now runs through markets. This is useful in a panic, and it raises a hard question explored in Question 4: if the central bank always steps in, who is left to be careful?

Chapter 10

Why one euro is always one euro

The quietest reason central banking matters is one most people never think about. A 100-euro deposit at one bank is worth exactly the same as a 100-euro deposit at another, and the same as a 100-euro note. Economists call this the singleness of money. It is not natural. In the United States before 1863, many private banks issued their own notes, and those notes often traded below face value, more so the further you were from the bank.

What keeps today's deposits equal is the plumbing in this file. Banks can always swap deposits into central bank money through the payment system. Deposit insurance makes small deposits safe. And supervisors check that banks can keep their promises. Remove those, and every bank's money would trade at its own price, as it once did. That is the deeper reason central banks care who gets access to their accounts, and why they watch new forms of private money, such as stablecoins, so closely.

A strong backstop, and the costs of leaning on it

The plumbing works well most of the time. The arguments are about how far the backstop should reach, and who pays when it is used.

Chapter 12

When a central bank loses money

Under the floor system, a central bank holds a big portfolio of bonds bought years ago at low interest rates, and pays today's interest rate on the reserves that were created to buy them. When rates rose quickly in 2022 and 2023, many central banks found themselves paying more on reserves than they earned on their bonds. The Fed began recording operating losses in late 2022 and booked them as a "deferred asset", to be paid off from future profits before it sends money to the US Treasury again. In the UK, the Treasury had promised to cover the Bank of England's losses on its bond purchases, so the losses fell on public finances directly.

A central bank cannot run out of its own currency, so these losses do not threaten it the way they would a company. But they are real money that taxpayers would otherwise have received. They also give politicians a new reason to criticise the central bank. Whether central banks should keep such large balance sheets in normal times is a live and contested debate. Some argue a smaller system, with banks borrowing reserves more often, would be cheaper and more disciplined. Others say the large-reserve system is simpler and safer, and that the losses are a small price.

Chapter 14

The oldest question, in a new form

Every change on that list returns to one question: who gets to hold central bank money, and on what terms? Today the answer is banks, plus the public through banknotes. As cash use falls in many countries, the public's direct link to central bank money is shrinking. A retail central bank digital currency would restore it. Critics fear it could also let depositors run from banks to the central bank in seconds.

The other side of the same question is private money. If stablecoins or tokenised deposits grow, central banks will have to decide whether to let them settle in central bank money, which would make them safer, or keep them out, which would keep them at arm's length. How that choice is made will shape banking more than any interest rate decision in the coming decade. The private side of the story is in Digital currencies.

The core idea

Banks hold reserves at the central bank and settle with each other in that money, which makes payments final. The central bank prices reserves to set interest rates, lends them in a crisis against collateral, and supervises banks so that crises are rarer.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Fedwire has been run by the Fed since 1918.
  • US bank reserves rose from tens of billions of dollars before 2008 to several trillion after QE.
  • Herstatt failed in 1974; the Basel Committee was founded the same year.
The common mistake

Thinking a central bank's job is only interest rates. The rate is just the price of reserves; running the system in which reserves move, and lending them in a panic, is the larger job.

The words, in plain words
Reserves
Money that banks hold in accounts at the central bank.
Central bank money
Reserves plus banknotes: money that is a claim on the central bank itself.
Settlement
The moment a payment is truly complete and cannot be undone.
RTGS
Real-time gross settlement: each payment settled in full, one by one, as it happens.
Net settlement
Adding up payments and settling only the difference at the end of a period.
Herstatt risk
The risk of paying your side of a trade and never receiving the other.
Corridor
A system where the central bank's deposit and lending rates form a floor and ceiling for market rates.
Floor system
A system with abundant reserves, where the rate paid on reserves sets the market rate.
Collateral
An asset pledged to a lender, which it keeps if the loan is not repaid.
Haircut
The discount a lender applies to collateral, so it lends less than the asset's value.
Discount window
The Fed's standing facility for lending to banks.
Swap line
An agreement between central banks to exchange currencies, used to supply dollars abroad.
Stress test
A check of how a bank's capital would hold up in an imaginary severe crisis.
