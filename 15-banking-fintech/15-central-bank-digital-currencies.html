Central Bank Digital Currencies. Digital cash from the state, and an argument about what money should be allowed to know.

The only money most people hold that comes straight from the state is cash. Everything else, the balance in your bank or payment app, is a private company's promise to pay. As cash fades from daily life, central banks are asking whether the public should have a digital form of state money too. That question has turned into one of the most heated debates in finance. Supporters see a safe public payment option for the digital age. Critics see a threat to banks, to privacy and to freedom.

Question 1 · What is it?

Central bank money, in digital form, possibly for everyone

A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, is a digital form of a country's official money, issued by its central bank and a direct claim on it, like a banknote.

Chapter 01

Public money and private money

To see why CBDCs matter, you need one distinction. Money comes in two kinds. Public money is issued by the central bank: banknotes, and the reserves that commercial banks hold in their accounts at the central bank. Private money is issued by commercial banks and payment firms: the deposits in your account and the balance in your payment app.

In daily life the two feel identical. A euro in your bank is worth a euro in cash. But they are different promises. If your bank fails, your deposit is protected only by deposit insurance and by the authorities' willingness to step in. A banknote is a claim on the central bank itself, which cannot run out of its own currency. Today, cash is the only way an ordinary person can hold public money. Businesses and banks have long used digital central bank money among themselves. Households never have.

A CBDC would change that. It would be public money, like cash, but digital, like a deposit.

Chapter 02

Retail and wholesale: two different projects

Retail CBDC

For the general public and shops. You would hold it in a wallet app or card and use it for everyday payments. This is the version that causes political debate, because it touches privacy, banks and daily life. The digital euro and China's e-CNY are retail projects.

Wholesale CBDC

For banks and large financial institutions only. It would settle large payments and trades in securities, often on new ledger technology. Banks already use central bank money digitally, so this is an upgrade of existing plumbing. It attracts much less controversy.

When people argue about CBDCs, they almost always mean the retail kind. Keeping the two apart avoids a lot of confusion: wholesale CBDC is mostly an engineering question; retail CBDC is a question about society.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A handful of design choices decide almost everything

A CBDC is not one technology. It is a bundle of choices: who runs the wallets, how payments are recorded, how much you may hold, and who can see what.

Chapter 04

The two-tier model

Almost no central bank wants to run millions of customer accounts. It has no branches, no call centres and no experience checking identities. So nearly every serious design uses a two-tier model, similar to how cash already works.

Chapter 05

Accounts, tokens and offline payments

There are two broad ways to record who owns what. In an account-based design, a ledger records balances, as a bank does, and each payment checks who you are. In a token-based design, the money is a digital object you hold, closer to a banknote, and the system checks that the token is genuine rather than checking you. Real projects usually mix the two.

Many designs also aim for offline payments: paying from one phone or card to another with no internet connection, using secure chips in the devices. The digital euro proposal includes an offline option with privacy similar to cash for small amounts. It matters for resilience, because a payment system that works during a network outage or a power cut is valuable. It is also technically hard, because devices must prevent double spending without checking a central ledger, the problem explained in Digital currencies.

A CBDC does not need a blockchain. Some projects test ledger technologies inspired by crypto, but a central bank is a trusted central party, so a conventional, very secure database can do the job. The interesting choices are about rules, not about technology.

Chapter 06

Holding limits, and why banks worry

Banks fund their lending partly with customer deposits. If people moved large amounts from bank deposits into a CBDC, banks would have less cheap funding and might lend less or charge more. Economists call this disintermediation: cutting the bank out of the middle.

The bigger fear is a crisis. Today, if people doubt a bank, moving money takes some effort. With a CBDC, a customer could move everything into risk-free central bank money with one tap. That could make bank runs faster and larger, the danger described in How banks work.

Most designs answer with holding limits: a cap on how much CBDC each person may hold, with any excess automatically swept into a linked bank account. The ECB has discussed a limit of a few thousand euros per person for the digital euro; the Bank of England has discussed a range of 10,000 to 20,000 pounds. Designs also usually pay no interest on CBDC, so it does not compete with savings accounts. The aim is a CBDC good enough for payments but deliberately poor as a place to store savings.

Chapter 07

Who can see your payments?

Cash is private: nobody records that you bought a coffee. Card and bank payments are not: your bank, the card network and the shop's provider all see them, and authorities can obtain records with legal permission. A CBDC could be designed anywhere along this range.

Western central banks say they would not see individual payments. In the two-tier model, intermediaries would know their customers, as banks do now, while the central bank would see only anonymised or aggregated data. Some designs promise near-cash privacy for small offline payments. In the EU, the draft law includes privacy rules, and anti-money-laundering checks would still apply to larger amounts.

Critics reply that promises can change. A system built so a government could see every payment is a standing temptation, especially under a future government with different values. This is the root of the political argument, and it cannot be settled by technology alone. Privacy in a CBDC is a legal guarantee, and it is only as strong as the law that protects it.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Three forces pushing central banks to act

Central banks did not wake up wanting to build apps. They are responding to cash disappearing, to private digital money spreading, and to slow, costly international payments.

Chapter 08

When cash fades

Sweden is the usual example. Over the past two decades, cash use there has fallen to a small share of payments, and many shops, cafés and even some bank branches stopped handling cash. The Riksbank, Sweden's central bank and one of the oldest in the world, began studying an "e-krona" in 2017.

The worry is not nostalgia. If cash disappears, every payment depends on private companies, and the public loses access to state money altogether. Central banks see three risks: people who struggle with digital banking, such as some older or poorer citizens, are left out; the payment system depends on a few private firms, often foreign card networks; and the "anchor" of public money weakens. Today, the fact that you can always swap a deposit for cash helps keep trust in bank money. Without cash, that link becomes invisible. Sweden has since also strengthened rules to keep cash available, showing that a CBDC is not the only response.

Chapter 09

Monetary sovereignty: Libra as the wake-up call

When Facebook announced its Libra stablecoin in 2019, central banks suddenly imagined billions of people using a currency run by a private company, outside their control. Libra failed, but the fright accelerated CBDC work, especially in Europe.

The concern is called monetary sovereignty: a country's ability to control its own money, set its own interest rates and supervise its own payments. If most Europeans paid with dollar stablecoins or through a few American tech platforms, European authorities would lose some of that control. The ECB has said openly that the digital euro is partly about strategic autonomy: reducing Europe's dependence on non-European payment providers, since Visa and Mastercard handle a large share of card payments in the euro area. Smaller countries face an even sharper version of this risk, as Topic 14 described with "digital dollarisation".

Chapter 10

Fixing cross-border payments

Sending money between countries is still slow and often expensive, because it passes through chains of correspondent banks, each taking time and a fee. Wholesale CBDCs offer a possible shortcut: if central banks connect their digital currencies on a shared platform, banks could settle across borders directly, in seconds.

The best-known experiment, Project mBridge, linked the central banks of China, Hong Kong, Thailand and the United Arab Emirates, later joined by Saudi Arabia, with the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), the central banks' own international bank. In 2024 the BIS withdrew from the project, saying it had reached a mature stage. Some observers linked the withdrawal to concerns that such systems could be used to avoid Western sanctions, a reading the BIS did not endorse. The BIS then began a separate project, Agorá, with several other central banks and private banks, exploring tokenised bank deposits alongside wholesale central bank money. Cross-border CBDC work has become part of geopolitics as well as plumbing.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A public good or a solution in search of a problem?

The arguments for and against are both serious. Which ones weigh more depends on the country, its payment system and how much its citizens trust the state.

A strong argument against comes from countries that already have excellent payment systems. In India and Brazil, public instant-payment systems (UPI and Pix) have transformed everyday payments without a CBDC. Critics ask what a retail CBDC adds where such systems exist. Supporters answer that those systems still move private bank money, and that a CBDC offers something different: public money that survives a bank failure.

Chapter 12

Lessons from the eNaira

Nigeria launched the eNaira in October 2021 with high hopes for financial inclusion. Take-up was very low; studies, including by the IMF, found that only a tiny share of Nigerians used it. In late 2022 and early 2023, the central bank redesigned the naira notes and limited cash withdrawals, which caused severe cash shortages. eNaira use rose briefly during that period, but mainly because people had no alternative, and the policy was widely criticised and partly reversed by the courts.

The episode offers two lessons that most observers accept. People do not adopt a new form of money just because it exists; it has to be more useful than what they already have, which in Nigeria included bank transfers and mobile money. And forcing adoption by restricting cash destroys trust, the one thing a currency cannot work without.

Chapter 14

Programmable money and the freedom debate

One phrase drives much of the public fear: programmable money. In principle, digital money could carry rules. It could expire if not spent, be restricted to certain goods, or be blocked for certain people. Critics, especially in the US, argue that a CBDC would give governments a tool for control that no democracy should build, and that the risk outweighs any benefit.

Central banks such as the ECB and the Bank of England reply that they have ruled out programmable money of this kind. They distinguish it from programmable payments, where you yourself set conditions, such as an automatic payment when a parcel arrives, which banks already offer today. They also point out that existing bank accounts can already be frozen by court order, and that a CBDC would sit under the same laws.

Both sides have a point. The technology does not force either outcome. What decides whether a CBDC protects or threatens freedom is the law around it, the independence of the central bank, and the strength of the courts. That is why the debate looks so different in different countries, and why the answer to "should we have one?" depends as much on politics as on economics. The wider direction of money and banks is picked up in The future of banking.

The core idea

A CBDC is public money in digital form, a direct claim on the central bank. The retail kind, for everyone, is distributed through banks in a two-tier model and usually comes with holding limits to protect banks. Central banks are motivated by fading cash, private digital money and slow cross-border payments. The debate turns on privacy, banks and trust in the state.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First nationwide retail CBDC: the Bahamas' Sand Dollar, October 2020.
  • Well over 100 countries are researching or testing CBDCs; only a handful have launched.
  • Holding limits discussed: a few thousand euros (ECB), 10,000–20,000 pounds (Bank of England).
The common mistake

Thinking a CBDC is a cryptocurrency, or that it must use a blockchain. It is ordinary central bank money, like a banknote, in a digital form, and its most important features are legal and design choices, not technology.
