Digital currencies. Money has been digital for decades. The new question is who is allowed to make it.

Almost all the money you own is already digital: numbers in a bank's computer. What changed in 2009 was not that money became electronic. It was that someone showed how to create a digital asset that could be passed from person to person with no bank in the middle, and that nobody could copy. That idea produced Bitcoin, thousands of imitators, and then stablecoins: private digital dollars now worth hundreds of billions. This file asks a narrow, important question about all of them. Do they actually work as money?

Question 1 · What is it?

Four kinds of digital money, with four different issuers

"Digital currency" covers very different things. The useful question is always: whose promise is this, and what stands behind it?

Chapter 01

Most money is already digital

In a modern economy, coins and banknotes are a small share of the money supply. Most money is bank deposits: entries in a commercial bank's ledger saying the bank owes you a certain amount. When you pay by card or transfer, banks change those entries. Volume 38 explains how banks create this money when they lend.

So what is new about digital currencies? Two things. First, who issues them: not a bank or a state, but a computer network or a private company. Second, how they move: like cash, they can pass directly from one holder to another without each payment going through a bank's books. Economists call money that belongs to whoever holds it a bearer instrument. A banknote is bearer money. A bank deposit is not. Cryptocurrencies tried to make bearer money that works online.

Chapter 03

A short history of private digital cash

The dream is older than Bitcoin. In 1989 the cryptographer David Chaum founded DigiCash, which offered private electronic cash using clever cryptography. Few banks adopted it, and the company went bankrupt in 1998. Other attempts, such as e-gold, a currency supposedly backed by gold, ran into fraud and money-laundering prosecutions. All of them had the same weak point: a central company that could fail, be shut down, or cheat.

On 31 October 2008, weeks after the collapse of Lehman Brothers, a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper describing "a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash". The Bitcoin network started in January 2009. Its novelty was removing the central company. The ledger would be kept by everyone, and no one could change it alone.

In 2010, a programmer in Florida paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas, often cited as the first real-world purchase. Over the next decade, bitcoin became much more valuable and much less used for buying pizza. In 2019 Facebook announced Libra, a global stablecoin for its billions of users. Governments reacted with alarm, and the project, renamed Diem, was abandoned in early 2022. That reaction showed how seriously states take the question of who issues money.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A shared ledger, a promise, and a key

Cryptocurrencies need a way to stop people spending the same coin twice. Stablecoins need a way to stay worth one dollar. Users need a way to hold either safely.

Chapter 04

Solving double spending without a bank

Digital files can be copied. If money is a file, what stops you sending the same coin to two people? This is the double-spending problem, and for digital cash it was the central obstacle. Banks solve it by keeping one authoritative ledger. If you spend money, they subtract it.

Bitcoin's answer was a ledger shared by thousands of computers, called a blockchain. Transactions are grouped into blocks, and each block is linked to the previous one with a cryptographic fingerprint, so changing an old entry would break every link after it. Computers called miners compete to add the next block by solving a costly puzzle, a system known as proof of work. Rewriting history would require out-computing most of the network, which is extremely expensive. The full technical story is in Blockchain.

The key point for money is this: the ledger replaces trust in a bank with trust in mathematics, economic incentives, and the honesty of a majority of the network's computing power. The bitcoin supply is set by the code, with a maximum of 21 million coins. No central bank can create more.

Chapter 05

How a stablecoin holds its value

A stablecoin promises that one token is always worth, say, one US dollar. There are three main ways to try to keep that promise, and they differ hugely in safety.

Backed by cash and bonds

For every token, the issuer holds a dollar's worth of reserves, such as bank deposits or short-term US government bonds. Large customers can redeem tokens for dollars. This is the model of USDC and Tether, and the one regulators now favour.

Backed by other crypto

Users lock up more crypto than the stablecoins they borrow, for example 150 dollars of ether for 100 stablecoins. If the collateral falls, it is sold automatically. It works until prices fall faster than the system can sell.

Algorithmic

No real reserves. A second token absorbs swings, and code creates or destroys tokens to push the price back to one dollar. It works only while people believe it will. See Chapter 10.

For reserve-backed coins, the mechanism that holds the peg is arbitrage: buying where something is cheap and selling where it is dear. If the token trades at 99 cents, a trader can buy it and redeem it with the issuer for a dollar, pushing the price back up. That only works if people believe the reserves are really there and can be reached quickly. A stablecoin is exactly as stable as its reserves and its redemption promise.

Chapter 06

Keys, wallets and who holds them

On a blockchain, you do not have an account with a name on it. You have a private key: a long secret number that proves you control certain coins. A wallet is software or a device that stores keys and signs transactions. Whoever has the key controls the money.

That creates a stark choice. With self-custody, you hold the key yourself. Nobody can freeze your coins, but if you lose the key or someone steals it, the money is gone, with no help desk and no refund. With custody by an exchange, a company holds the keys for you, as a bank would. It can reset your password, but you depend on it being honest and solvent. When the exchange FTX collapsed in November 2022, customers discovered their funds had been misused. The crypto saying "not your keys, not your coins" comes from such episodes.

Chapter 07

The cost of running the ledger

A shared ledger without a central owner is expensive to run. Bitcoin's network handles only a handful of transactions per second, far fewer than card networks. Fees rise when it is busy. Proof of work uses large amounts of electricity, because miners deliberately spend energy to make cheating costly.

Other systems made different choices. Ethereum, the second-largest network, switched in September 2022 to proof of stake, in which validators lock up coins as a deposit they lose if they cheat, instead of burning electricity. That cut its energy use by more than 99%. Faster "layer 2" networks process many payments off the main chain and settle them together later, much like the batch settlement of card payments described in Volume 14.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Testing digital currencies against the jobs of money

Money has three jobs: a means of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. Different digital currencies pass different tests.

Chapter 08

Is bitcoin money?

Means of exchangeUsed for some payments, especially across borders, but few shops accept it and fees varyWidely used for transfers and trading between crypto platforms
Unit of accountAlmost nothing is priced in bitcoin, because its value swings so muchPrices are in dollars anyway; the coin borrows the dollar's unit
Store of valueSupporters call it "digital gold"; its price has repeatedly fallen by more than halfStable while reserves hold; inflation still erodes it like a dollar

The best-known real-world test came in El Salvador. In September 2021 it became the first country to make bitcoin legal tender, which required businesses to accept it. Most people kept using dollars, which the country had used officially since 2001. In early 2025, as part of a loan agreement with the IMF, the law was changed so that accepting bitcoin became voluntary. Declaring something money does not make people use it as money.

Many economists therefore describe bitcoin as a speculative asset rather than a currency, which is why its investment side is covered in Cryptocurrencies. Supporters reply that young monies are always volatile, and that a fixed supply protects savers from governments that print too much. The disagreement is genuine and unresolved.

Chapter 09

Stablecoins: digital dollars for the world

Stablecoins have become the most used part of crypto. By 2025 their total value was more than 200 billion US dollars, and the great majority were linked to the US dollar. They are the main way traders move money between crypto platforms. But they have a second use that matters more for the world economy.

In countries with high inflation or currency controls, such as Argentina, Turkey or Nigeria, many people want to save in dollars but cannot easily open a dollar bank account. A dollar stablecoin on a phone offers something close to that. It is also used to send money across borders faster and more cheaply than some traditional services. For many users outside the US, a stablecoin is not "crypto" at all. It is a way to hold dollars.

That has a political side. Each stablecoin is backed largely by US government bonds, so their growth increases demand for US debt and extends the dollar's reach. Some US officials see that as a benefit. Central banks in other countries worry that it weakens their own currencies and their control over monetary policy, a concern sometimes called digital dollarisation.

Chapter 10

Runs, breaks and an old lesson

In May 2022, TerraUSD, a large algorithmic stablecoin, lost its dollar peg. As holders sold, the system created more of its sister token, Luna, whose price collapsed, which made the stablecoin less credible, which caused more selling. Within days both were nearly worthless, and tens of billions of dollars of value disappeared. It was a run, the same pattern that brings down banks, moving at internet speed.

Reserve-backed coins have wobbled too. In March 2023, Circle revealed that part of USDC's reserves were held at Silicon Valley Bank, which had just failed. USDC briefly fell to about 87 cents until it became clear the US authorities would protect all of that bank's deposits.

Historians point to a parallel. Between 1837 and 1863, many US banks issued their own banknotes, backed by whatever reserves they held. Notes from distant or weak banks traded below face value, and some banks failed, leaving holders with worthless paper. Private money has always been only as good as its issuer. The rules now being written for stablecoins, covered in Question 5, are largely an attempt not to relearn that lesson.

Chapter 12

Crime, and the myth of anonymity

Cryptocurrencies are used for crime: ransomware payments, darknet markets, scams and, as described in Financial cybersecurity, large state-backed thefts. That is real. But two common beliefs are wrong.

First, most public blockchains are not anonymous. They are pseudonymous: every transaction is visible forever, linked to an address rather than a name. Once an address is tied to a person, for example when they cash out at a regulated exchange that checks identity, investigators can trace the money backwards. Specialist firms and police have used this to recover stolen funds. Second, estimates by blockchain analysis firms usually put illicit activity at a small share of total crypto transactions, though measuring it is hard and the figures are disputed.

The honest summary is that crypto makes some crimes easier, especially ransomware and cross-border theft, and others easier to trace. Cash remains the most widely used tool for money laundering.

Chapter 14

The deeper question: who issues money?

For about a century and a half, most countries have settled on a two-layer system. The central bank issues the base money, banknotes and reserves, and commercial banks issue deposits under close supervision, as explained in Central banking. Digital currencies challenge that arrangement from two directions.

Cryptocurrencies propose money with no issuer at all. Stablecoins propose money issued by technology and payment companies, possibly with global reach and billions of users. Both raise the same concern for governments: if people hold their money outside the banking system, the central bank loses some of its grip on interest rates, financial stability and the payments that tax and law enforcement depend on.

States have answered in two ways. One is to bring private digital money inside the rules, as the new stablecoin laws do. The other is to offer a state-issued digital currency of their own. That second answer is the subject of the next topic, Central Bank Digital Currencies.

The core idea

Most money is already digital. What is new is who issues it and how it moves. Cryptocurrencies solve double spending with a shared ledger and float freely in price; stablecoins try to hold a fixed value through reserves. Each is only as trustworthy as the thing behind it: a network's incentives, or an issuer's reserves.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Bitcoin's white paper: 31 October 2008; maximum supply 21 million.
  • Stablecoins passed 200 billion dollars in total value by 2025, mostly dollar-linked.
  • Ethereum's switch to proof of stake in 2022 cut its energy use by more than 99%.
The common mistake

Treating "crypto" as one thing. A bitcoin and a dollar stablecoin share technology but behave in opposite ways: one is designed to float, the other to stay fixed.
