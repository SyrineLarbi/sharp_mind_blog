Credit cards. Free money for the disciplined, and some of the most expensive debt there is for everyone else.

A credit card is two products in one small rectangle. One is a payment tool: fast, accepted almost everywhere, with fraud protection and often rewards. The other is a loan that you can take out at any moment, without asking, at a high interest rate. People who repay in full every month get the first product almost free. People who do not are paying for it, and for other people's rewards too. Understanding which of the two you are using is the whole skill.

Question 1 · What is it?

A payment card with a credit line attached

A credit card lets you pay now with the bank's money and repay later. How much later, and at what price, is up to you, within limits the bank sets.

Chapter 01

Sixty thousand cards nobody asked for

In September 1958 Bank of America mailed about sixty thousand credit cards to residents of Fresno, California. Nobody had applied for them. The cards, called BankAmericard, simply arrived, each with a spending limit, and could be used in local shops that had agreed to accept them. The bank wanted to reach enough cardholders and enough shops at the same time to make the system worth joining for both. Within about a year it had sent out some two million cards across California.

The launch was messy. Many cardholders did not repay, fraud was common, and the bank lost a great deal of money at first. But the idea worked, and BankAmericard eventually became Visa. Mass mailing of unsolicited cards was banned in the United States in 1970. The Fresno drop solved the problem every payment network faces: shops will not accept a card nobody carries, and people will not carry a card no shop accepts. Economists call this a two-sided market.

There were earlier cards. Diners Club, founded in 1950 in New York, issued a card for paying at restaurants. But it had to be paid off in full each month. BankAmericard's important novelty was that the balance could be carried over, at interest. That is what made it a credit card.

How much people use each one varies a lot by country. Americans, Canadians and Britons use credit cards widely. Germans have long preferred debit cards and cash. In many developing countries, debit cards and mobile payments are far more common than credit cards. The plastic looks identical; the money behind it is not.

Chapter 03

Revolving credit

A normal loan, such as a car loan, is a fixed sum repaid on a fixed schedule. A credit card is revolving credit. The bank sets a credit limit, the most you can owe at once. You can borrow any amount up to that limit, repay some or all of it, and borrow again, without asking anyone. There is no end date.

That flexibility is what makes the card useful and what makes it dangerous. A household that hits a surprise car repair can pay for it instantly and spread the cost over a few months. A household that is spending a little more than it earns every month can do the same, quietly, for years. The card never forces a decision. It only sends a statement.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The cycle, the interest and the minimum

Almost everything about a credit card's cost is decided by one choice each month: pay the full balance, or pay less.

The free period between buying something and the payment date is called the grace period, or interest-free period. Depending on when in the cycle you buy, it gives you from about three weeks up to about eight weeks of free credit. But in most countries the grace period only applies if you paid the previous statement in full. Once you carry a balance, new purchases usually start costing interest straight away. Many people do not realise this.

Two other things are rarely free. A cash advance, taking cash from a cash machine on a credit card, usually costs a fee plus interest from the first day, often at a higher rate. And spending in a foreign currency may add a foreign transaction fee of a few per cent, although many cards now waive it.

Chapter 05

How the interest is worked out

Card interest is quoted as a yearly rate, the APR (annual percentage rate). It is high compared with other loans. In the United States, average card rates were above 20 per cent in 2023 and 2024. In the UK they have typically been in the low twenties too. Because the loan is unsecured, meaning there is no house or car the bank can take if you do not pay, the rate has to cover a much higher rate of losses than a mortgage does.

In practice, interest is usually charged on your average daily balance: the bank works out what you owed on each day of the month, takes the average, and applies one-twelfth or one-365th of the APR, depending on the method. The interest is added to the balance, so next month you pay interest on it too. That is compounding, and it is why card debt grows faster than people expect.

Chapter 06

The minimum-payment trap

Every statement shows a minimum payment, often a small percentage of the balance plus that month's interest, or a fixed floor amount. Paying it keeps the account in good standing. It does not pay the debt off in any reasonable time. Here is what happens to a 5,000-dollar balance at a 22 per cent APR, with no new spending (illustrative, interest compounded monthly).

Monthly paymentTime to clear the debtTotal interest paid
Minimum only (1% of balance plus interest, at least 25 dollars)About 19 yearsAbout 8,100 dollars
Fixed 100 dollarsAbout 11 yearsAbout 8,700 dollars
Fixed 150 dollarsAbout 4 years and 4 monthsAbout 2,800 dollars
Fixed 200 dollarsAbout 2 years and 10 monthsAbout 1,750 dollars

Look at the gap between 100 and 150 dollars. A modest increase in the payment cuts the time by more than half and the interest by two-thirds. At 100 dollars a month, almost all of the early payments go on interest, because 22 per cent of 5,000 is about 92 dollars a month. Small payments mostly rent the debt; they barely shrink it. In 2009 the US Credit CARD Act began requiring statements to show how long repaying at the minimum would take, for exactly this reason.

The one rule

If you carry a card balance, paying it down is usually the best return available to you, as Volume 38 explains. A balance transfer to a card with a 0 per cent introductory rate can help, but only if you repay before the offer ends, and there is usually a fee. This is general education, not personal financial advice.

Chapter 07

Who pays for the rewards

Card issuers earn from three main sources: interest from people who carry balances, interchange from shops on every purchase, and fees such as annual fees, late fees and cash-advance fees. Their customers fall into two groups. Bankers call people who pay in full every month transactors, and people who carry a balance revolvers. Transactors generate interchange and little else. Revolvers generate interest, which in the US is the largest source of card profit.

Rewards, such as cashback, points and airline miles, are paid mostly from interchange, which shops pay and pass on in their prices. A 2010 study by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston estimated that, as a result, households that pay by cash or debit subsidise card users, and lower-income households subsidise higher-income ones, on average. A transactor with a premium card is being paid partly by people who never use one. The exact size of this effect is debated, and card issuers dispute the framing.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Credit history, protection and a mountain of debt

For many people a credit card is their first loan, their strongest legal protection when buying, and their most expensive debt, all at once.

Chapter 08

A key to the rest of borrowing

In countries with credit scores, such as the US and the UK, lenders judge you partly on how you have handled credit before. A person with no history is hard to judge, so they may be refused a mortgage or offered a worse rate. A credit card used for small amounts and repaid in full is one of the simplest ways to build a record. Two habits matter most: paying on time, and not using a large share of your limit. The share of the limit you use is called credit utilisation. How scoring works is covered in Credit scores.

This has a strange consequence. In those countries, people who avoid debt entirely can find borrowing harder later. It is one reason newcomers to a country are often advised to get a basic card early. In other countries, such as Germany or France, credit decisions rely more on income, bank records and national registers, and a credit card matters less.

Chapter 09

Why paying by credit card is often safer

Credit cards carry legal and contractual protections that cash and many other payment methods do not. Under card network rules, customers can ask their bank for a chargeback if goods never arrive, are not as described, or the charge is fraudulent. Some countries add legal rights on top. In the UK, Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes the card issuer jointly responsible with the seller for purchases between 100 and 30,000 pounds paid at least partly on a credit card. If the seller goes bust, you can claim from the card company.

Fraud protection is strong too. In the US, federal law limits a cardholder's liability for unauthorised credit card charges to 50 dollars, and the main networks advertise zero liability policies. A thief who spends on your credit card is spending the bank's money while the dispute is sorted out. With a debit card, the money has left your account first. For large purchases from unfamiliar sellers, a credit card is usually the safest way to pay, as long as you clear the balance.

Chapter 10

Card debt, at the scale of a country

American credit card balances passed 1 trillion dollars in 2023, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and have stayed above that since. Some of that is just spending that will be repaid at the end of the month. But a large share is revolving debt at rates above 20 per cent. In the UK, the regulator introduced rules in 2018 on persistent debt: if a customer has paid more in interest and fees than in repayment over 18 months, the issuer must contact them and, after three years, help them repay faster.

Card debt is also a warning signal for the economy. When households start borrowing more on cards, or missing payments, it often means their incomes are being squeezed. Banks raise their provisions for losses, which tightens credit for everyone. Rising card delinquencies are one of the early indicators economists watch for consumer stress. A loan becomes delinquent when payments are overdue.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

The best payment tool, attached to the worst loan

The same card can be excellent or ruinous. What decides it is how it is used.

Chapter 11

Weighing it up

Advantages

  • Free short-term credit for up to about eight weeks, if paid in full.
  • Strong protection: chargebacks, Section 75 in the UK, low fraud liability.
  • Accepted almost everywhere, including for hotel and car-hire deposits.
  • Builds a credit history where scores are used.
  • Rewards and cashback, especially generous in the US.
  • An emergency buffer when cash runs short.

Disadvantages

  • High interest, often above 20 per cent, compounded.
  • Minimum payments that stretch debt over decades.
  • Spending feels painless; studies suggest people spend more with cards than with cash.
  • Fees: late fees, cash-advance fees, annual fees.
  • Hidden cross-subsidy: rewards are partly paid by cash and debit users.
  • Profits rely on customers who struggle, which creates bad incentives.
Chapter 12

How regulators push back

The last point in that list is the core worry. A product that earns most from customers who carry expensive debt has a reason to keep them in debt. Regulators have responded in different ways.

The US Credit CARD Act of 2009 banned many surprise rate increases, required notice before changes, and made issuers apply payments above the minimum to the highest-rate balance first. The UK's persistent-debt rules, and its rules on unsolicited limit increases, try to stop customers drifting deeper into debt. The EU capped interchange, which made rewards much less generous in Europe. Some countries cap interest rates on consumer credit; others prefer disclosure and leave the price to the market. Whether rate caps protect borrowers, or just push them towards worse lenders, is one of the oldest arguments in consumer finance, and the evidence is mixed.

Chapter 14

Will the credit card outlive its rivals?

The credit card has survived many predicted deaths: debit cards, PayPal, mobile wallets, instant bank payments. It survives because it bundles things that are hard to replace together: universal acceptance, consumer protection, a credit line and rewards. Where the card is weakest, in countries with cheap instant bank payments like Brazil and India, it has often become a credit product inside an app rather than a payment method in a wallet.

The likely future is a split. The payment side of the card is being absorbed into phones and bank rails; the credit side is being rebuilt as instalment loans and credit lines inside apps. The underlying questions do not change: how much does it cost, who pays, and does the product make it too easy to borrow more than you can repay? Those are the questions of the next topic, Loans.

The core idea

A credit card is a payment tool plus revolving credit. Pay the full statement and you get weeks of free credit and strong protection. Pay less and you pay a high, compounding rate, which, with interchange, funds the rewards that transactors enjoy.

The numbers worth knowing
  • US card rates averaged above 20% in 2023–24; US card balances passed 1 trillion dollars in 2023.
  • 5,000 dollars at 22% repaid at the minimum takes roughly 19 years.
  • UK Section 75 covers credit card purchases of £100–£30,000.
The common mistake

Treating the minimum payment as the bill. The minimum keeps the account open; the statement balance is the bill.

Test yourself

Can you explain why carrying even a small balance can make your new purchases start costing interest at once? If so, you understand the grace period.

The words, in plain words
Revolving credit
Credit you can borrow, repay and borrow again up to a limit, with no end date.
Credit limit
The most you are allowed to owe on the card at one time.
Statement balance
What you owed at the end of the billing cycle.
Grace period
The interest-free time before payment is due, usually only if you paid in full last time.
APR
Annual percentage rate: the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest.
Minimum payment
The smallest amount you must pay to keep the account in good standing.
Unsecured
A loan with no asset the lender can take if you do not pay.
Cash advance
Taking cash on a credit card, usually with a fee and immediate interest.
Transactor
A cardholder who pays in full every month.
Revolver
A cardholder who carries a balance from month to month.
Chargeback
Reversing a card payment through your bank after a dispute.
Credit utilisation
How much of your credit limit you are using.
Delinquent
