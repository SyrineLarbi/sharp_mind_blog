Fintech startups. A better screen on top of very old pipes.

A fintech startup looks like a technology company. It has an app, a hoodie-wearing founder and a growth chart. Underneath, it is almost always doing something banks have done for centuries: holding money, moving it, lending it or insuring it. The startup's real innovation is usually the layer you touch: the sign-up that takes four minutes instead of four days, the notification that arrives the second you pay. That is a real achievement. It also explains why so many fintechs struggle to make a profit, and why the dangerous moments in their history have nearly always happened in the pipes they did not build.

Question 1 · What is it?

Young companies that sell financial services through software

"Fintech" is short for financial technology. In practice the word means new firms that compete with banks, insurers and brokers by building a better customer experience first and getting the licences and the plumbing second.

Chapter 01

Seven lines of code

In 2010 two Irish brothers, Patrick and John Collison, started a company to solve a boring problem. If you built a website in the late 2000s and wanted to accept card payments, you had to open a special "merchant account" with a bank, fill in long forms, wait weeks, and then connect to payment systems written decades earlier. The brothers' company, Stripe, promised that a developer could start taking payments by pasting a few lines of code into a site. Early marketing talked about "seven lines of code".

Stripe did not invent a new way to move money. The payment still travelled through Visa or Mastercard and still landed in a bank account. What Stripe changed was the experience of the person using the system. It took a slow, paper-based process and turned it into something a programmer could do on a Sunday afternoon. Within about a decade Stripe had become one of the most valuable private companies in the world.

That story is the whole of fintech in miniature. The startup wins by removing friction from a service that already exists, and the hard financial machinery underneath stays mostly the same. Keep that idea in mind. It explains both the successes and the failures in the rest of this file.

Chapter 02

The map of fintech

Fintech is not one industry. It is a set of attacks on different parts of finance. The main families are below, with well-known examples.

A neobank (from the Greek neo, new) is a bank that exists only as an app. Some hold a full banking licence. Many, especially in their early years, do not. They hold an e-money licence, which lets them store and move money but not lend it out, or they work through a partner bank that holds the licence for them. That difference sounds technical. It matters enormously when something goes wrong, as Chapter 12 shows.

Chapter 03

A short history: three waves

The first wave (late 1990s to 2000s) moved existing services online. PayPal, founded in 1998, let people pay each other by email address and became the payment method of eBay, which bought it in 2002. Online brokers let people trade shares without phoning anyone.

The second wave (around 2008 to 2020) was the big one. Three things arrived together. The smartphone put a computer in every pocket. The 2008 financial crisis damaged trust in banks and made them cautious and slow to change. And cheap cloud computing meant a team of ten could run software that once needed a data centre. Out of that came the neobanks (Nubank in Brazil in 2013, Monzo and Revolut in the UK in 2015), buy-now-pay-later firms, and commission-free trading apps. Money from investors poured in, peaking in 2021 when interest rates were near zero.

The third wave (2022 onward) is the sobering-up. When interest rates rose, investors began to ask for profits instead of growth. Funding for fintech fell sharply, valuations were cut, and the firms that survived focused on earning money. By 2025 several had listed on stock exchanges, including Chime and Klarna in the United States, while others had shrunk, been sold or shut down.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rented rails, borrowed licences, and a race for customers

A fintech has to solve three problems at once: how to touch the financial system without being a bank, how to earn money from customers who pay no fees, and how to grow fast enough before the money from investors runs out.

Chapter 04

The stack: who does what behind the app

When you open a neobank app and pay for coffee, several companies are involved. The startup owns the app and the relationship with you. Below it, there is usually a chain of partners.

What sits under a typical young fintech

             |

        card issuing processor   (prints the card, approves payments)

        partner bank             (holds the licence and, often, your money)
             |

        card network + central bank payment systems

  The fintech owns the top layer. Everything below is rented.

This arrangement is called banking-as-a-service (BaaS). A licensed bank, often a small one, rents out its licence and its access to payment systems to fintechs through software connections called APIs (application programming interfaces: standard ways for one program to ask another to do something). The bank earns fees and cheap deposits. The fintech gets to launch in months instead of years.

In some markets a middle layer sits between the two: a technology company that connects many fintechs to one or more banks. Every extra layer is one more place where records about whose money is whose can drift apart. That is not a theoretical worry, as Chapter 12 explains.

Chapter 05

How a "free" app earns money

Most neobanks charge their basic customers nothing. So where does the revenue come from? There are five main sources, and most fintechs rely on two or three of them.

Interchange

Each time you pay by card, the shop pays a small fee and most of it goes to the card issuer. Volume 14 explains why that fee is capped in the EU (at 0.2% for consumer debit cards) and not in the US. That cap is one reason US neobanks lived more comfortably on card fees than European ones.

Interest

Customer deposits can be placed at the central bank or in safe bonds, or lent out if the firm has a full licence. When interest rates rose in 2022 and 2023, many fintechs suddenly became profitable for this reason alone.

Subscriptions

Paid tiers with travel insurance, better exchange rates or metal cards. A minority pays, but each paying customer is worth many free ones.

Lending and add-ons

Overdrafts, loans, buy-now-pay-later, foreign exchange spreads, share trading and, in some firms, crypto. These carry higher margins and higher risk.

The fifth source is selling to businesses rather than people, as Stripe and Adyen do. Business customers pay a percentage of every transaction, which is why payments firms that serve merchants have generally been more profitable than consumer apps. A shop that processes a million euros a month is a customer worth fighting for. A student with forty euros in their account is not, until there are ten million of them.

Chapter 06

The growth machine and its arithmetic

Startups are funded by venture capital: investors who buy a share of a young company, expecting most of their bets to fail and a few to return the whole fund many times over. That model rewards speed. A fintech raises money, spends it on finding customers, shows a rising user count, and raises more money at a higher valuation.

Two numbers decide whether the machine works. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what it costs, in advertising and sign-up bonuses, to win one new customer. Lifetime value (LTV) is the profit that customer brings over all the years they stay. If LTV is comfortably bigger than CAC, growth creates value. If not, every new customer makes the company poorer, and growth only hides the problem until the investors stop paying for it.

Fintech has an extra twist. Many customers open an account, get the sign-up bonus and never use it again. A neobank with ten million "customers" may have far fewer who receive their salary into it. That is why analysts look closely at primary accounts (the account where someone's wages arrive) rather than at downloads. The salary account is where the deposits, card spending and loan demand actually live.

Chapter 07

Licences, sandboxes and the cost of being trusted

Finance is one of the most regulated industries in the world, for good reason: if a bank fails, people lose money they needed. So a startup must decide early how close to the regulator it wants to stand. Broadly there are three options. It can partner with a licensed bank (fast, but dependent). It can get a limited licence, such as e-money or payments (medium). Or it can apply for a full banking licence (slow and expensive, but it allows lending from deposits and brings deposit insurance).

Regulators have tried to make the first steps easier. In 2016 the UK's Financial Conduct Authority launched what is usually described as the first regulatory sandbox, a programme in which young firms can test products with real customers under close supervision and lighter rules for a limited time. Many countries copied the idea. Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia and several Gulf states run sandboxes, and the EU encourages them.

Getting a full licence is a long march. Revolut, founded in 2015, operated for years in Britain without a UK banking licence. It received one in 2024, with restrictions during an initial "mobilisation" period, in which a new bank can hold only limited deposits while it builds up its systems. The licence is the moat. Software can be copied in months. A regulator's trust takes years.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Banks changed because startups made them look slow

Most fintechs will never be as large as a big bank. Their importance lies in what they forced the whole industry to do, and in the people they reached that banks had ignored.

Chapter 08

The experience bar moved for everyone

Twenty years ago, opening a bank account in many countries meant a branch visit, a utility bill, a passport, and a wait of days or weeks for a card in the post. Changing an address could take a letter. Sending money abroad took days and arrived with fees nobody could explain.

Fintechs reset expectations. Opening an account by photographing your passport and filming your face. Instant notifications for every payment. Freezing a lost card with one tap. Spending categories that tell you where your money went. Fair, visible exchange rates. Wise (formerly TransferWise, founded in London in 2011) built a business on showing customers the real exchange rate and a separate fee, which made the hidden markups of traditional transfers obvious.

Today almost every large bank offers these features too. Some built them, some bought startups, some licensed their technology. Customers who never used a fintech still benefited, because their own bank had to catch up.

Chapter 09

Reaching people banks did not want

The most striking fintech story may be Brazil. In 2013 Brazil's banking market was dominated by a handful of large banks with high fees, and tens of millions of adults had no credit card. Nubank, founded that year by David Vélez with two co-founders, offered a no-fee credit card controlled from an app. Brazilians queued, virtually, to join. By 2025 Nubank reported around 100 million customers across Brazil, Mexico and Colombia, which made it one of the largest digital banks in the world.

Similar stories exist elsewhere. Mobile money in Kenya, which Financial inclusion covers in depth, gave tens of millions of people their first way to store money safely. Payment apps in India and China brought small shopkeepers into digital payments. A cheap, phone-based service can serve customers whom a branch-based bank would lose money on.

There is a caution here. Reaching new customers with credit is not the same as helping them. Easy loans offered through an app can also trap people in debt, a point Chapter 11 returns to.

Chapter 10

Competition, and the pressure on bank profits

Banks earn a large part of their income from customers who do not shop around: people who leave savings in an account paying almost nothing, or who pay high fees for currency exchange because switching seems like too much work. Economists call this customer inertia. Fintechs attack it directly. When moving money takes one tap and a rival pays more interest, inertia weakens.

This matters beyond banking. In 2023, a run on Silicon Valley Bank in California saw depositors try to withdraw about 42 billion dollars in a single day, organised partly through phones and group chats. That bank was not a fintech, and its problems were about its own investments, not apps. But the speed of the run showed what fintech-style convenience means for the whole system: money that can leave in seconds will leave in seconds. Regulators are still adjusting their rules for that speed. How banks work explains why a run is so dangerous.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Speed is the gift, and speed is the risk

Moving fast with money produces wonderful products and occasional disasters. Both come from the same features.

Chapter 11

What fintech gives, and what it costs

FeatureWhat it givesWhat it costs
Software-first designFast sign-up, clear apps, real-time information, low running costs.Accounts frozen by automated systems, with little human help when the algorithm is wrong.
Venture fundingMoney to build products banks would not risk.Pressure to grow before the business is sound, and sudden cuts when funding dries up.
Rented infrastructureLaunch in months without a full licence.Customers may not know who actually holds their money, or whether it is protected.

Gamified investingCheap access to markets for small savers.Design that encourages frequent trading, which on average loses money for the trader.

The investing row deserves a story. In January 2021, small investors coordinated online to buy shares of GameStop, a struggling video-game shop, driving its price up many times over. Robinhood, the commission-free trading app many of them used, suddenly restricted purchases. The reason was not a conspiracy but plumbing: the clearing house that settles trades demanded much larger cash deposits from Robinhood to cover the risk, and the firm had to raise billions of dollars within days. The app had made trading feel free and instant, but the settlement underneath was neither.

Chapter 12

When the pipes break: three collapses

Wirecard (2020). Wirecard was a German payments company that had joined the DAX, Germany's index of its largest listed companies. Journalists at the Financial Times had reported for years that its accounts did not add up. In June 2020 Wirecard admitted that 1.9 billion euros which its accounts said were held in Asian bank accounts probably did not exist. It collapsed within days. The scandal also damaged Germany's financial regulator, which had filed criminal complaints against the journalists and banned short-selling of the shares rather than looking hard at the company.

Ant Group (2020). Ant, the Chinese company behind the Alipay payment app, was days away from what would have been the largest share sale in history when Chinese regulators halted it in November 2020. The authorities then forced Ant to restructure as a financial holding company, under bank-like rules. The message was clear: a payments app that also lends at enormous scale will be regulated like a bank.

Synapse (2024). Synapse was a US middleware company that connected fintech apps to partner banks. When it went bankrupt in April 2024, the records of which customer owned which money turned out not to match the money actually held at the banks. Tens of thousands of customers of apps built on it found their savings frozen for months, and a shortfall of tens of millions of dollars emerged. Many had believed their money was insured like a bank deposit. Deposit insurance protects you if the bank fails, not if the software company in between loses track of your money.

Chapter 14

The real competition: big tech and public rails

The biggest threat to fintech startups may not be banks. It may be two other forces. The first is large technology companies. Apple, Google and others already sit between millions of people and their cards, through phone wallets. In China, Alipay and WeChat Pay showed how far an app with a billion users can go. Regulators everywhere are now deciding how much finance they will let these platforms do.

The second is public payment systems. Brazil's Pix and India's UPI, both built or backed by public authorities, offer instant transfers for free or nearly free. Where they exist, a startup cannot easily charge for simple payments. The future value shifts to what sits on top: credit, savings, advice and service. Projects such as central bank digital currencies and open banking push in the same direction.

So the sensible bet is that fintech stops being a separate category. All banks become software companies with licences, and the surviving startups become banks that are good at software. The interesting question is no longer "fintech or bank?" but who owns the customer and who carries the risk. The future of banking takes that question further.

The core idea

A fintech startup sells old financial services through new software. It usually owns the app and the customer and rents the licence and the rails from banks and networks. It earns from interchange, interest, subscriptions, lending and merchant fees, and it lives or dies on whether a customer's lifetime value beats the cost of winning them.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Fintech venture funding peaked in 2021 and fell sharply when rates rose.
  • EU interchange on consumer debit cards is capped at 0.2%.
  • Wirecard's missing money: about 1.9 billion euros (2020).
  • Nubank: about 100 million customers by 2025.
The common mistake

Assuming money in a fintech app is protected exactly like money in a bank. Ask who holds the licence and where the money actually sits. The answer varies by firm and by country.

Test yourself

Can you explain, without notes, why a neobank with ten million customers can still lose money, and why rising interest rates made many of them profitable? If you can, you understand the business model.
