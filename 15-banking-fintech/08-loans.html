Loans. Buying time with money, and paying for the time.

A loan is the simplest financial contract there is: someone gives you money now, and you promise to give back more later. Almost every large thing a person or a company does depends on that promise. Homes, cars, degrees, factories and governments are all paid for partly with borrowed money. Yet most borrowers never look closely at how a loan is built: how the monthly payment is calculated, why the early payments are mostly interest, what the lender was really judging, and what happens when the promise cannot be kept.

Question 1 · What is it?

A promise to repay, with a price on the waiting

Every loan has the same three parts: an amount, a price and a schedule. The many kinds of loan are just different ways of setting those three, plus what happens if the borrower does not pay.

Chapter 01

Older than coins

Lending is older than money as we know it. Clay tablets from Mesopotamia record loans of barley and silver, with interest, thousands of years ago. The Code of Hammurabi, a Babylonian law code from around 1750 BC, set maximum interest rates on loans of grain and silver. From the start, societies saw that loans were useful and that they could be abused, and tried to regulate both sides.

Many religious traditions went further. Medieval Christian teaching condemned usury, a word that first meant charging any interest and later came to mean charging too much. Islamic law forbids interest, which is why Islamic banks structure finance as sales, leases or partnerships instead. Over centuries, Europe's ban weakened as trade grew, and lenders found ways to call interest something else. The tension between the usefulness of credit and the danger of debt is as old as writing. It runs through every modern rule on lending.

Chapter 02

The main kinds of loan

ChoiceOption AOption B
Is there security?Secured: backed by an asset the lender can take if you do not pay, such as a house or car. Lower rate.Unsecured: backed only by your promise. Higher rate. Personal loans, cards, most student loans.

How is it repaid?Amortising: each payment covers interest and part of the debt, so the debt reaches zero.Interest-only or bullet: only interest along the way; the whole amount at the end.

Any real loan is a combination. A typical car loan is secured, term, fixed and amortising. A company's bond is usually unsecured, term, fixed and bullet. A business overdraft is revolving and variable. Once you can place a loan on these four choices, you understand most of its risks, both to you and to the lender.

Chapter 03

The price: interest rate, APR and total cost

The principal is the amount you borrow. The interest rate is the yearly price of borrowing it. But loans also carry fees: arrangement fees, insurance sold alongside, early-repayment charges. To make loans comparable, many countries require lenders to show a single figure that includes most fees, spread over the life of the loan. In the US, UK and EU this is the APR, the annual percentage rate.

The APR is the right number to compare offers. But the number that matters to your budget is the total amount repayable. A longer loan has a lower monthly payment and a higher total cost, because you pay interest for more years. Lenders know that borrowers look at the monthly payment, so they often advertise it. Always ask what the loan costs in total, not just per month.

Question 2 · How does it work?

The schedule, the decision and the fine print

Behind every loan there is a calculation of the payments and a judgment about the borrower. Both are less mysterious than they look.

Chapter 04

Where your monthly payment goes

Take a loan of 10,000 euros at 8 per cent a year, repaid over three years in equal monthly payments. The monthly payment works out at about 313 euros. Each month, interest is charged on whatever is still owed, and the rest of the payment reduces the debt.

The payment never changes, but its make-up does. At first, a fifth of it is interest. By the end, almost all of it repays the debt. This pattern is called amortisation, and the table is an amortisation schedule. Over the three years you pay about 1,280 euros of interest. Stretch the same loan to five years and the payment falls to about 203 euros, but total interest rises to about 2,170.

The pattern matters most for long loans. On a 25-year mortgage, the early years are dominated by interest, so after five years the debt has fallen far less than people expect. Paying a little extra early in a loan saves far more interest than paying extra late, because it removes principal that would have been charged interest for the most years.

Chapter 05

How a lender decides: the five Cs

Lending is a forecast: will this borrower repay? Bankers have long summarised what they look at as the five Cs of credit.

The CThe questionWhat the lender checks
CharacterDoes this borrower repay?Credit history, past defaults, how long at the same job or address
CapacityCan they afford the payments?Income, other debts, the share of income going to debt payments
CapitalDo they have their own money at stake?Savings, deposit, the owners' investment in a business
CollateralWhat can be recovered if they do not pay?The value of the house, car or business assets
ConditionsWhat is the loan for, and what is the wider situation?The purpose, the industry, the state of the economy

For consumer loans, most of this is now automated. Scoring models turn credit history into a number, and affordability rules set limits on how much of a borrower's income can go on debt. A key measure is the debt-to-income ratio: total monthly debt payments divided by monthly income. For business loans, especially large ones, human judgment still matters a great deal, because each business is different.

Chapter 06

The fine print that protects the lender

A loan contract does more than set a rate. Business loans usually contain covenants: promises the borrower makes for the life of the loan, such as keeping its debt below a set multiple of its earnings, or not selling key assets. If a covenant is broken, the lender can demand early repayment or renegotiate the terms, often charging more. Covenants let a lender act early, before the borrower is actually unable to pay.

Consumer loans have their own fine print: early-repayment charges that compensate the lender for lost interest, rules on what happens if you miss a payment, and sometimes a guarantor, another person who promises to pay if you cannot. Very large loans to companies are often syndicated: one bank arranges the loan and shares it among many lenders, so that no single one carries the whole risk.

Chapter 07

When payments stop

A borrower who misses a payment is in arrears. Lenders usually contact them, charge late fees and report the missed payment to credit bureaus. After a set period, often around three months for consumer loans, the loan is treated as in default. For a secured loan, the lender can then take and sell the collateral: it can repossess a car, or start proceedings to sell a house, which is called foreclosure in the US. For an unsecured loan, it may sell the debt to a collection agency, often for a fraction of its face value.

When a borrower cannot pay their debts at all, the law offers a final route: bankruptcy or insolvency. The rules vary widely. The United States has comparatively forgiving personal bankruptcy, which can wipe out many debts and give people a fresh start, though student loans are very hard to discharge. Many European countries traditionally made debts harder to escape, though several have softened their rules in recent decades. For companies, procedures such as Chapter 11 in the US let a firm keep operating while it restructures its debts. How a country treats people who cannot repay shapes how much its lenders are willing to lend, and to whom.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Opportunity, inequality and the business cycle

Credit decides who can invest in their future before they have saved for it. That makes it a source of opportunity, and a source of danger when it is too easy or too hard to get.

Chapter 08

Borrowing against your future self

Economists explain borrowing with a simple idea. Most people earn little when young, more in middle age, and little again in retirement. If they could only spend what they earned each year, they would be poor when they most need to spend, on a home, on children, on education. A loan lets someone move money from their richer future self to their poorer present self. Economists call this consumption smoothing.

For businesses, the logic is investment. A firm that can borrow to buy a machine today can earn from it for years. A loan is valuable when what it pays for earns, or saves, more than the interest. A loan for a degree that raises lifetime earnings, or a machine that pays for itself, passes the test. A loan for a holiday usually does not, although it may still be a reasonable choice. The test is easy to state and easy to ignore.

Chapter 09

Who gets credit, and at what price

Credit is not given out evenly. People with stable jobs, assets and long credit histories get the cheapest loans. People with irregular incomes, few assets or no history pay more, or are refused. The most expensive end of the market is high-cost short-term credit, such as payday loans: small loans repaid on the next payday, which in the past could carry annual rates in the hundreds or thousands of per cent.

Countries have responded in different ways. In 2015 the UK capped payday lending at 0.8 per cent a day in interest and fees, with total charges never more than the amount borrowed. Many payday lenders left the market afterwards. Some US states ban payday loans; others allow them. In poorer countries, microcredit, small loans to people without collateral, became famous through the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, whose founder Muhammad Yunus shared the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize with the bank. Later rigorous studies found its effects on poverty more modest than early claims suggested. More on this in Financial inclusion.

Chapter 10

Debt and the business cycle

Lending rises and falls with confidence. In good times, lenders relax their standards and borrowers feel sure their incomes will grow. In bad times, both pull back at once. The American economist Hyman Minsky argued that long periods of stability encourage ever riskier borrowing, until the debts can only be repaid if asset prices keep rising. When they stop rising, the borrowing collapses. After 2008 this became known as a "Minsky moment".

Household debt can also hold back an economy for years after a crisis, as families cut spending to pay down what they owe. Student debt has become a large part of that picture in the US, at about 1.6 trillion dollars in total as of 2025. Debt pulls spending forward from the future. Eventually the future arrives. The wider story of crises is in Financial crises.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

A tool that magnifies whatever it is used for

Borrowing makes good decisions better and bad decisions worse. The same is true for the economy as a whole.

Chapter 12

Responsible lending, and who decides what that means

After 2008, many countries tightened the rules on how lenders judge borrowers. Lenders must now check that a loan is affordable, not just that the borrower can be made to pay. In the EU, consumer credit and mortgage directives require creditworthiness assessments. In the UK, mortgage lenders must test whether borrowers could still pay if rates rose. In the US, rules after 2008 required lenders to make a reasonable judgment of a borrower's ability to repay a mortgage.

The difficulty is that the same rule can protect one person and exclude another. A strict affordability test stops some people from borrowing more than they can bear. It also stops some who would have repaid. There is no lending standard that refuses all bad loans and approves all good ones, so every rule chooses which mistake to make more often. That choice is a matter of values as well as evidence, and it is argued about in every country.

Chapter 14

The same promise, a different lender

The loan contract has hardly changed in four thousand years: an amount, a price, a schedule and a consequence for not paying. What changes is who lends, what they know about you, and how fast they decide. Data and software make credit faster and, for some people, fairer. They also make it easier to borrow without thinking.

For a borrower, the old questions remain the best defence. What will this cost in total? What happens if my income falls? Does what I am buying earn or save more than the interest? Technology can change how a loan is sold. It cannot change what a loan is. The largest loan most people ever take is the subject of the next topic, Mortgages.

The core idea

A loan is an amount, a price and a schedule. It can be secured or unsecured, term or revolving, fixed or variable, amortising or bullet. Lenders judge character, capacity, capital, collateral and conditions. Borrowing pays off when what it buys earns or saves more than the interest.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 10,000 euros at 8% over 3 years costs about 313 a month and 1,280 in interest; over 5 years, about 203 a month and 2,170 in interest.
  • The UK payday cap since 2015: 0.8% a day, total charges no more than the amount borrowed.
  • US student debt: about 1.6 trillion dollars as of 2025.
The common mistake

Choosing a loan by its monthly payment. A lower payment usually means a longer loan and a higher total cost. Compare APR and total repayable.
