Financial inclusion. Being poor is expensive, and being outside the system is more expensive still.

If you have a bank account, a card and a phone that can send money, it is hard to imagine life without them. For a large part of humanity, that life is normal. Savings are kept as cash under the mattress, as livestock or as jewellery. Money sent home from a city job travels in a bus driver's pocket. A sick child means borrowing from a moneylender at a rate that would be illegal in a rich country. Financial inclusion is the effort to give everyone access to useful, affordable financial services: a safe place to keep money, a way to pay and be paid, credit, and insurance. In the last fifteen years it has been one of development's genuine success stories. It has also produced some painful lessons about what access alone cannot do.

Question 1 · What is it?

Access to useful, affordable financial services, for everyone

The goal is not that everyone opens a bank account. It is that everyone can save, pay, borrow and insure themselves safely, at a fair price, in a way that fits their life.

Chapter 01

Money in a matatu

Before 2007, if a man working in Nairobi wanted to send money to his family in a village in western Kenya, he had few good choices. He could travel home himself, losing days of wages. He could give cash to a friend, or to the driver of a matatu, one of Kenya's private minibuses, and hope it arrived. Theft was common. Banks had few rural branches, and most poor Kenyans had no account anyway.

In 2007 Safaricom, Kenya's largest mobile network, launched a service called M-Pesa (pesa means money in Swahili). It let people store money as credit on their phone's SIM card and send it to anyone else by text message. To turn cash into phone money and back, they visited an agent: usually a small shop that also sold airtime. No bank branch was needed. The phone could be the simplest model, without internet.

Within a few years most Kenyan adults used it. A payment network built on corner shops and basic phones did what decades of bank expansion had not. M-Pesa became the world's best-known example of financial inclusion, and it changed how governments, banks and aid agencies think about the subject.

Chapter 02

Who is excluded, and how we know

The best source is the World Bank's Global Findex database, a large survey of adults in well over a hundred countries, run roughly every three years since 2011. In 2011 about half of the world's adults had an account at a bank or a mobile-money provider. By 2021 that had risen to about three-quarters, leaving roughly 1.4 billion adults unbanked. The 2025 edition reported further progress.

The unbanked are not spread evenly. They are concentrated in South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, parts of the Middle East and North Africa, and South-East Asia. They are disproportionately women, poorer, less educated, rural, young or out of work. When Findex asks why people have no account, the most common answers are: too little money to make it worthwhile, accounts are too expensive, a family member already has one, banks are too far away, they lack the required documents, they distrust banks, and, for some, religious reasons.

Exclusion is not only a poor-country problem. In the United States, recent surveys by the FDIC, the federal deposit insurer, find that around 4 to 5% of households have no bank account, many of them relying on cheque-cashing shops and payday lenders that charge high fees. In the EU, a 2014 directive gave every legal resident the right to a basic payment account.

Chapter 03

Four services, not one

It helps to break inclusion into its parts, because each solves a different problem and each has its own risks.

Payments

Paying and being paid, sending money home, receiving wages and government transfers. This is usually the entry point, and the one where technology has done the most.

Savings

A safe place to keep money that is not cash, not a goat and not a relative who might ask for it. Savings let families absorb shocks without selling assets.

Credit

Borrowing to invest, or to smooth a bad month. Useful when fair, very harmful when costly and easy to take on.

Insurance

Protection against illness, crop failure or death. The least developed of the four, and possibly the most important. See Insurtech.

Researchers now distinguish access (you could open an account), use (you actually use it) and quality (it helps you rather than harms you). For years policy counted accounts opened. The better measure is whether people's lives become more secure.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Cut the cost of serving a poor customer

Banks did not ignore poor people out of malice. A branch, a clerk and a paper file cost the same whether an account holds a thousand euros or ten. Every successful inclusion model found a way to make small accounts cheap to run.

Chapter 04

Mobile money and the agent network

Mobile money works because it replaces the expensive parts of a bank with cheap ones. The branch becomes a shop. The clerk becomes the shopkeeper, paid a small commission on each deposit and withdrawal. The paper file becomes a phone number. The money itself is usually held by the mobile operator in trust accounts at regular banks, so it is backed by real deposits.

The key ingredient is the agent network. Without enough shops willing to turn cash into phone money and back, the system fails. Agents must hold both kinds of float, which ties up their capital, and they need enough customers to make the commission worthwhile. Mobile money succeeded where agents were dense and failed where they were thin.

Regulation mattered as much as technology. Kenya's central bank allowed M-Pesa to launch before writing detailed rules, watching it closely instead. In countries where only banks could offer such services, or where agents needed bank-level licences, mobile money grew much more slowly. By the mid-2020s, according to the industry body GSMA, mobile-money accounts worldwide numbered well over a billion, with Sub-Saharan Africa the largest region.

Chapter 05

India's approach: identity, accounts and a public payment rail

India took a different route, led by the state. It built three layers. The first was Aadhaar, a biometric identity number based on fingerprints and iris scans, launched in 2010 and now held by well over a billion people. It solved the document problem: many poor Indians had no birth certificate or proof of address, but everyone has fingerprints.

The second was a mass account-opening drive. Under the Jan Dhan scheme, launched in 2014, banks opened basic no-minimum-balance accounts for hundreds of millions of people within a few years. Many of those accounts were empty at first. They began to fill when the government paid subsidies and welfare directly into them, which also cut the leakage that occurred when cash passed through intermediaries.

The third was the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched in 2016 by a body set up by India's central bank and banks. It lets anyone send money instantly from one bank account to another using a phone number or a QR code, for free to the user. Street vendors display QR codes on cardboard. By the mid-2020s UPI was handling well over ten billion transactions a month. India showed that public digital infrastructure, open to every bank and app, can include people at a speed no single company could.

Chapter 06

Microfinance: the big idea and the evidence

Before mobile money, the star of inclusion was microfinance: very small loans to poor people, usually women, to start or grow tiny businesses. Its best-known pioneer is Muhammad Yunus, a Bangladeshi economist who founded the Grameen Bank. Grameen lent to groups of women whose members were jointly responsible for repayment, which replaced collateral (property pledged against a loan) with social pressure. Repayment rates were high. Yunus and the Grameen Bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.

Then economists tested the claims carefully. In 2015 a leading journal published six randomised controlled trials (studies that offer a service to randomly chosen groups and compare them with similar groups that did not get it) from six countries on four continents. The findings were consistent. Microcredit gave people more choices, helped some existing businesses grow, and let households manage their money more flexibly. But it did not, on average, lift people out of poverty, raise incomes much, or transform women's lives.

That is not failure. It is a correction. Credit is a tool, not a cure for poverty. Researchers also found that poor people valued safe savings and the ability to handle emergencies at least as much as loans, which shifted attention toward savings, payments and insurance.

Chapter 07

Credit scoring without a credit history

To lend, a lender must judge whether a borrower will repay. In rich countries, credit bureaus hold years of records. Credit scores explains how. Most poor people have no record at all. They are "credit invisible".

Alternative data tries to fill the gap. A mobile-money history shows how regularly money comes in. Airtime top-ups show spending patterns. Utility bills paid on time suggest reliability. In Kenya, M-Shwari, launched in 2012 by Safaricom and a bank, offered small loans and savings through the phone, priced by such data. Similar digital lenders spread across Africa, Asia and Latin America.

The approach works in the sense that millions received small loans quickly. It raises two questions. First, privacy: lenders may read contacts, messages and location from a borrower's phone. Second, quality: a loan approved in seconds and repaid in a month can carry a very high annual interest rate. Chapter 11 returns to both.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Shocks, savings and the power to choose

Poor households face more shocks than rich ones and have less to absorb them with. Good financial tools do not make people rich. They make bad luck less destructive.

Chapter 08

What the evidence shows: M-Pesa and poverty

The strongest evidence comes from Kenya. Economists Tavneet Suri and William Jack followed thousands of households for several years and compared those who lived near M-Pesa agents with those who lived further away, as the network spread. Their study, published in the journal Science in 2016, estimated that access to mobile money lifted around 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty. The effects were larger for households headed by women, partly because some women moved from farming into business or retail work.

The mechanism matters more than the figure. When a family member falls ill or a harvest fails, relatives elsewhere can send money within minutes. Households with M-Pesa coped with shocks without cutting food spending as much. The biggest benefit of payments is not convenience. It is that support from a network of family and friends arrives in time.

Remittances make the same point at global scale. Migrants send home several hundred billion dollars a year to low- and middle-income countries, more than all foreign aid combined. Yet sending 200 dollars across a border still costs, on average, around 6% according to the World Bank, double the 3% target that the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals set for 2030. Every point cut from that fee is money that stays with families.

Chapter 09

Women's money, and who controls it

Women are less likely than men to have an account in many developing countries. That matters because money that arrives in a woman's own account is more likely to stay under her control. Cash given to a household can be taken by whoever is strongest. Money in a phone wallet with a PIN is harder to take.

Several studies support this. In an experiment in India, paying wages from a government work programme directly into women's own accounts, along with simple training, increased how much women worked and shifted attitudes about women working. In the Philippines, a study by Nava Ashraf, Dean Karlan and Wesley Yin found that a savings account whose money was locked until a goal was reached, a commitment savings product, increased savings, and was associated with more decision-making power for women at home.

The lesson for design is concrete. Accounts that need a husband's documents, branches far away, or phones that only men own all keep women out. Inclusion that ignores who holds the phone can deepen the gap it claims to close.

Chapter 10

Governments, transfers and the pandemic test

Financial inclusion also matters to states. A government that can pay citizens directly into accounts can deliver pensions, welfare and emergency aid faster, more cheaply and with less theft. The COVID-19 pandemic made this vivid. Countries with digital payment systems and widespread accounts, such as Brazil, India and Togo, sent emergency cash to millions of people within weeks. Countries without them struggled to reach informal workers at all.

Brazil's experience is especially clear. Its emergency aid programme in 2020 opened digital savings accounts at a state bank for tens of millions of people. Later that year the central bank launched Pix, an instant, free payment system for individuals, which spread faster than almost any payment system in history. Central bank digital currencies discusses whether state money itself should go digital.

Inclusion also broadens the tax base and brings economic activity into the open. That is a benefit for states and a cost for some citizens, a tension discussed in the next part.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Real gains, and the danger of including people into debt

Access to finance can protect people or exploit them. The difference lies in price, design and regulation, not in the technology itself.

Chapter 12

When inclusion goes wrong

The clearest warning came from the Indian state of Andhra Pradesh in 2010. Microfinance had grown very fast there, with several lenders, some newly listed on the stock market, competing for the same borrowers. Many households had loans from several lenders at once and borrowed from one to repay another. Reports linked aggressive collection to suicides among borrowers. The state government passed emergency rules restricting microlenders, repayment collapsed, and the industry in the state nearly disappeared. India later tightened national rules on how much one borrower could owe.

Digital credit repeated some of the pattern a decade later. Instant phone loans were easy to take and easy to default on. In Kenya and elsewhere, large numbers of borrowers were listed with credit bureaus for small unpaid amounts, which could block them from future borrowing. Some lending apps in Asia and Africa used borrowers' phone contacts to shame them into paying. Regulators responded with licensing, interest caps or bans on such tactics.

Access to debt is not the same as access to opportunity. The best inclusion policies now put protection first: clear prices, limits on total debt, fair collection, and an easy way to complain. Consumer protection covers the legal side.

Chapter 14

The last billion, and the new exclusions

The people still outside the system are the hardest to reach: the poorest, the most remote, those without phones, refugees without documents, people who distrust institutions for good reasons. Each new percentage point costs more than the last. Cheap smartphones, better identity systems for refugees and simple voice-based services for people who cannot read are among the ideas being tried.

At the same time, new forms of exclusion are appearing in rich countries. As shops and transport stop accepting cash, older people, people with disabilities and people on the margins can be shut out of daily life. Sweden, one of the most cashless countries in the world, has debated whether banks must keep providing cash services. Several countries and cities have passed rules requiring shops to accept cash. AI-based credit and fraud models can also exclude people silently, with no one able to explain why an account was refused.

So the honest future of inclusion is not a finish line. Every new system creates new insiders and new outsiders. The test of a good financial system is not how advanced its technology is, but how it treats the person at the edge of it.

The core idea

Financial inclusion means everyone can pay, save, borrow and insure safely and affordably. It advanced fast when someone found a way to make small accounts cheap to serve: mobile money and agents in Kenya, digital ID and a public payment rail in India, Pix in Brazil. The evidence shows payments and savings protect people from shocks, while credit helps less than once hoped and can harm.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Adults with an account: about half in 2011, about three-quarters in 2021 (Global Findex).
  • Unbanked adults in 2021: roughly 1.4 billion.
  • M-Pesa launched in 2007; estimated to have lifted about 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty.
  • Average cost of sending 200 dollars abroad: about 6%; UN target 3% by 2030.
The common mistake

Counting accounts and loans as success. An empty account helps nobody, and an expensive loan can make things worse. What counts is whether people become more able to handle bad luck.

Test yourself

Can you explain why mobile money spread in Kenya through corner shops, while banks had failed to reach the same people with branches? If you can, you understand the economics of inclusion.
