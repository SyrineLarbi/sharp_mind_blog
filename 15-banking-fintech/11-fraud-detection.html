Fraud detection. Finding one bad payment among millions, in less time than it takes to blink.

Every time you tap a card, a computer you will never see asks a question: is this really you? It has a fraction of a second to answer. If it says no too often, honest customers get angry and leave. If it says yes too easily, criminals empty accounts. Fraud detection is the craft of getting that answer right billions of times a day against opponents who study the system and change their methods the moment it catches them.

Question 1 · What is it?

A permanent contest between two learning systems

Fraud detection is the set of checks, models and people that try to stop payments, accounts and loans that are not what they claim to be.

Chapter 01

An opponent that adapts

Most prediction problems are passive. A weather model does not have to worry that the clouds are reading its code. Fraud is different. The thing you are trying to predict is a person who wants you to fail and changes behaviour as soon as you succeed. When banks block one trick, criminals move to the next, often within weeks. Security people call this an adversarial problem.

That single fact shapes everything else. A fraud system can never be "finished". It has to be watched, retrained and adjusted all the time. And it must be judged not only by how much fraud it stops, but by how much honest business it blocks along the way.

A related problem is money laundering: hiding where criminal money came from by passing it through accounts until it looks clean. Banks are legally required to watch for it, and the same tools are used for both jobs. The rules behind that duty are covered in Regtech.

Chapter 03

Unauthorised versus authorised: why the difference matters

The most important split in the table is between payments you did not make and payments you were tricked into making. In the first case, a stranger used your card or account. Banks in most rich countries must usually refund you, unless you were grossly careless. In the second case, you pressed the button yourself. The bank's systems saw the right customer, on the right phone, with the right password. Every security check passed.

That is why authorised fraud has grown as card security improved. It goes around the technology by working on the person. It is also why it is so hard to detect: the bank must notice that a real customer, doing a real transfer, is acting under someone else's control.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Rules, models and people, all racing the clock

A modern fraud system stacks several layers. Each catches what the others miss, and each has to finish its work in milliseconds.

Chapter 04

The first layer: rules

The oldest tool is a written rule. "Decline any card used in two countries within one hour." "Flag any first payment over 5,000 to a new payee." Rules are easy to understand, easy to explain to a regulator, and quick to add when a new attack appears.

Their weakness is that they are rigid. A rule that catches a lot of fraud usually also catches a lot of honest customers, such as the traveller who lands in a new country. Criminals also learn the thresholds. If transfers over 5,000 are checked, they send 4,900. Rules are good at stopping yesterday's attack and poor at noticing tomorrow's. Most banks still keep them, as a fast safety net on top of the models.

Chapter 05

The second layer: models that learn what normal looks like

Since the 1990s, card issuers have used machine learning: programs that learn patterns from examples instead of following written rules. A model is shown millions of past transactions, each labelled "genuine" or "fraud" once the outcome was known. It learns which combinations of details tend to go with fraud. Then it gives each new transaction a risk score.

The raw material is called features: measurable facts about a transaction and its context. Some typical ones:

About the payment

Amount, currency, the type of merchant, time of day, whether the card was physically present.

About your habits

How this compares with your usual spending: how far from home, how much larger than normal, how many payments in the last hour (called velocity).

About the device

Which phone or browser, whether it has been seen before, its location, whether it hides its location.

About behaviour

How you type, swipe and hold the phone. This is behavioural biometrics: a fraudster using your password still moves differently from you.

A second family of models does not need labels at all. Anomaly detection learns what normal activity looks like and flags anything unusual, which helps against attacks nobody has seen before. The catch is that unusual is not the same as criminal. Your first holiday abroad is an anomaly too.

Chapter 06

Inside the split second

For a card payment, the whole check has to fit inside the authorisation message described in Volume 14, which takes a second or two end to end. The fraud part gets only a slice of that.

The middle band is where much of the craft lies. Instead of a plain yes or no, the system can step up: ask you to approve the payment in your banking app or enter a one-time code. In Europe, rules on strong customer authentication, phased in around 2019 to 2021, require two independent factors for many online payments, which pushed this approach everywhere. The factors and their strengths are covered in Volume 47.

Chapter 07

Following the money through networks

Stolen money has to land somewhere. Criminals use money mules: accounts belonging to people, often young and often recruited with offers of "easy money", who receive funds and pass them on. Looked at one by one, each mule account may look harmless. Looked at as a network, the pattern is obvious: many accounts receiving from victims and sending to the same few destinations.

That is why fraud teams increasingly use graph analysis. A graph here is a map of connections: accounts, phones, addresses and devices as points, and payments or shared details as lines between them. Ten new accounts opened from the same device, or sharing one address, stand out on a graph even when each looks fine alone. Fraud is often invisible in a single record and obvious in the connections.

Banks also share data. In the UK, for example, members of the fraud-prevention service Cifas share records of confirmed fraud. A criminal rejected at one bank can be recognised at the next.

Chapter 08

The rare-event problem

Fraud is rare. Only a tiny fraction of card payments are fraudulent, far below one in a hundred. This creates a trap that catches even clever people.

Imagine a model that is right 99% of the time on genuine payments, meaning it wrongly flags 1% of them. That sounds excellent. Now imagine one payment in a thousand is fraud. Out of a million payments, there are 1,000 frauds and 999,000 genuine ones. Even if the model catches every fraud, it also flags about 9,990 honest payments. For every real fraud it stops, it annoys about ten innocent customers. Statisticians call this the base-rate effect: when the thing you are hunting is rare, false alarms dominate.

So no fraud team asks only "how much fraud did we catch?" It also asks "how many good customers did we decline?" Those two numbers move in opposite directions as the threshold moves, exactly like the false-accept and false-reject dial in biometrics. There is no setting that removes both errors. The bank chooses where to sit, and that choice is partly a business decision about how much friction its customers will tolerate.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Trust in payments, and who pays when it breaks

Fraud is not a side issue. In some countries it is the most common crime people experience, and the rules about who carries the loss shape how hard banks try.

Chapter 09

Fraud moves; it rarely disappears

The UK's switch to chip-and-PIN cards in the mid-2000s is a clean example. Before it, criminals copied the magnetic stripe on cards and made counterfeits. The chip is very hard to copy, and counterfeit card fraud in shops fell sharply. But fraud as a whole did not vanish. It moved to where the chip did not help: online shopping, where no card is present; overseas cash machines that still read stripes; and later, scams that trick people into paying themselves. The United States, which moved to chip cards later, around 2015, saw the same shift.

This pattern, called displacement, is the most reliable rule in fraud. Strengthen one door and pressure moves to the weakest remaining one. Today that weakest door is usually a human being, which is why authorised scams have grown into one of the largest kinds of loss.

Fraud is also enormous in scale. In England and Wales, the official crime survey has for several years found fraud to be the single most common type of crime adults experience, roughly four in ten of all incidents measured. Much of it is never reported to police.

Chapter 10

Who carries the loss

Whoever bears the cost of fraud has the strongest reason to stop it. Payment systems therefore use liability rules to push that cost where it can best be controlled.

For card fraud, card networks set rules on whether the issuing bank or the merchant pays. When the US moved to chip cards in 2015, the networks shifted liability for counterfeit fraud to whichever party had not upgraded. That gave shops a strong reason to install new terminals. Online, merchants who skip extra authentication often carry the loss themselves.

Authorised fraud was long the exception: because the customer pressed the button, they often lost the money. That changed in the UK. From October 2024, payment firms must reimburse most victims of authorised push payment fraud on the Faster Payments system, up to a cap of 85,000 pounds, with the cost split between the sending and the receiving firm. Making the receiving bank pay half gave it a direct reason to find the mule accounts. Other countries have debated similar rules, and the right balance between protecting victims and encouraging care is contested.

Chapter 11

The hidden cost: saying no to honest people

A wrongly declined payment is called a false decline. It looks harmless on a fraud report, because nothing was stolen. It is not harmless. The customer is embarrassed at a till, abandons an online basket, or switches to another card and never comes back. Industry studies have long suggested that the sales lost to false declines can be larger than the fraud itself, though the estimates vary widely.

The same tension appears in branches and call centres. When a bank stops a customer's transfer and asks questions, it may be saving them from a scam, or treating an adult as unable to manage their own money. Both happen. Good fraud work is as much about friction design as about mathematics: knowing when to interrupt, how to explain, and how to make the honest path quick.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Powerful, invisible, and never quite fair to everyone

Automated fraud detection makes instant payments possible. It also makes decisions about people that they cannot see or easily challenge.

Chapter 12

Weighing the approaches

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
Written rulesFast, simple, easy to explain and to change in an emergency.Rigid. Many false alarms. Criminals learn the limits.

Human analystsJudgement, context, the ability to phone the customer.Slow and costly. Can only review a small share of alerts.

Beyond the techniques, there is a wider concern. Models trained on past cases can treat some groups unfairly, for example by flagging people whose spending patterns differ from the majority, such as migrants sending money home. Being wrongly flagged can mean a frozen account or, in some countries, a closed one, sometimes without a clear reason given. Speed and secrecy help catch criminals, and they make mistakes harder for innocent people to fix.

Chapter 14

When seeing is no longer believing

In early 2024, police in Hong Kong described a case in which an employee of the engineering firm Arup transferred about 25 million US dollars after a video call with people he believed were senior colleagues. All of them were reportedly deepfakes: computer-generated video and voice. The case became a reference point for every fraud team, because the old advice, "check on a video call", had been defeated.

The lesson for detection is that appearance stops being evidence. A face, a voice or a scanned passport can all be faked. What remains harder to fake is context: is this the usual device, the usual pattern, the usual process, confirmed through a second channel the attacker does not control? Volume 47 gives the personal version of this advice (When you cannot trust your eyes or ears). For banks, it means the future of fraud detection is less about spotting the fake and more about designing processes where a fake alone is not enough.

The core idea

Fraud detection is an adversarial problem: the opponent adapts. Banks layer rules, machine-learning risk scores, network analysis and human review, all within a fraction of a second. Because fraud is rare, every system trades caught fraud against false declines, and fraud moves to the weakest remaining door, increasingly the customer.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Fraud is far below 1 in 100 card payments, so false alarms easily outnumber real catches.
  • UK victims of authorised push payment fraud can be reimbursed up to 85,000 pounds (from October 2024).
  • In England and Wales, fraud is roughly four in ten crimes in the official survey.
The common mistake

Judging a fraud system by how much fraud it catches. A system that blocks everything catches all the fraud and ruins the business. The real measure is fraud stopped against honest customers inconvenienced.
