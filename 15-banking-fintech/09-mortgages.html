Mortgages. The largest promise most people ever sign, and the one that ties their home to the whole economy.

Most people never buy a house outright. They pay a small part with their own money, and a bank lends the rest against the house itself, to be repaid over twenty or thirty years. That looks like a private deal. It is not. Mortgages carry central bank decisions into ordinary kitchens, and when they are written badly they can bring down the world's banks.

Question 1 · What is it?

A loan with a house standing behind it

A mortgage is a loan secured on property. The security is the whole point: it is why the loan can be so large, so long and so cheap compared with almost any other credit.

Chapter 01

The "dead pledge"

The word comes from old French: mort means dead and gage means pledge. The pledge "died" in one of two ways. Either the debt was paid and the pledge ended, or it was not paid and the land was lost for good.

In modern terms, a mortgage is a loan where the borrower gives the lender a legal claim on a property until the loan is repaid. That claim is called a lien in the US and a charge in the UK. The borrower lives in the house and owns it in every ordinary sense. But if payments stop, the lender can take and sell it. That process is called foreclosure in the US and repossession in the UK.

A card lender can only chase the borrower. A mortgage lender has something solid to fall back on. Because the lender expects to recover most of its money even in the worst case, it will lend far more, for far longer, at a far lower interest rate.

Most home loans are repayment mortgages: each payment covers that month's interest plus a slice of the principal, so the debt reaches zero at the end. With an interest-only loan you pay just the interest and owe the full amount at the end: cheaper each month, riskier at the finish.

Chapter 03

One idea, very different national designs

Every country has mortgages, but the basics differ more than people expect.

United States: the 30-year fixed

The standard American mortgage fixes the rate for thirty years, usually with free early repayment. Few countries offer this. It exists largely because government-sponsored companies, Fannie Mae (1938) and Freddie Mac (1970), buy or guarantee most such loans and pass the rate risk on to investors.

United Kingdom: short fixes

Most British borrowers fix their rate for two to five years, then take a new deal ("remortgage") to avoid the lender's higher standard rate. So UK households feel rate changes within a few years, not decades.

Germany and France: long fixes, cautious lending

Fixes of ten to twenty years are common, deposits tend to be larger, and lenders look hard at income. Germany has one of the lowest home-ownership rates in Western Europe; renting for life is normal there.

Denmark: the bond-matched mortgage

Danish lenders fund each mortgage by selling a matching bond to investors. A borrower can repay early by buying back that bond at its market price. When rates rise, bond prices fall, so the borrower can clear the debt for less than they borrowed. This system has run for about two centuries.

There is also Islamic home finance, which avoids charging interest. In one common structure, the bank buys the house and sells it to the customer at a higher price paid in instalments (murabaha). In another, the bank and the customer co-own the house. The customer pays rent on the bank's share and gradually buys it out (diminishing musharaka). The payments can look similar; the legal relationship is different.

Question 2 · How does it work?

A fixed payment with a shifting inside, and a lender with a lot to check

Behind a single monthly figure sit three mechanisms: the amortisation schedule, the choice of who carries the risk of rates changing, and the funding chain that brings the money to the lender in the first place.

Chapter 04

Amortisation: why the first ten years feel like treading water

Take a simple example. You borrow 200,000 euros over 25 years at a fixed 5% a year. The standard formula gives a monthly payment of about 1,169 euros. That payment stays the same for all 300 months. What changes is what it pays for.

In month one, the interest on 200,000 euros at 5% a year is about 833 euros. So only about 336 euros of your first payment reduces the debt. As the debt shrinks, the interest shrinks with it, and a larger slice goes to the principal. By the last year almost the whole payment is principal.

Two lessons follow. First, after ten years of payments you still owe about three quarters of the loan. Second, the total interest is about three quarters of the amount borrowed. Stretch the same loan to 40 years and the payment falls to about 964 euros, but total interest rises to about 263,000 euros, more than the loan itself. This is compound interest working against you, the same force described in Volume 38.

It also shows why paying a little extra early is so powerful: every euro of principal removed in year two stops charging interest for the next 23 years.

Chapter 05

Fixed or variable: who carries the rate risk?

Rates move over the life of a long loan, and someone has to absorb the moves. Fixed versus variable is really a choice about who carries that risk.

With a variable (or adjustable) rate, the rate follows a benchmark, such as the central bank's policy rate, plus a fixed margin. When rates rise, your payment rises. The borrower carries the risk. With a fixed rate, the payment stays the same for the fixed period whatever happens. The lender carries the risk, or passes it on to investors, and charges for it. That is why fixed rates are often a little higher than variable rates on the day you sign.

The size of the risk is easy to underestimate. Using the same 200,000-euro, 25-year loan: at 2% the payment is about 848 euros a month; at 6% it is about 1,289 euros. A move from 2% to 6%, which happened to many borrowers in 2022 and 2023, raises the bill by about half. For a family already spending a third of its income on housing, that is the difference between comfortable and desperate.

The hidden option

A fixed rate with free early repayment, as in the US, gives the borrower a one-way bet. If rates rise, they keep their cheap loan. If rates fall, they refinance. Investors know this and demand a higher rate to compensate. Where early repayment carries a penalty, the option is priced out.

Chapter 06

How a lender decides to say yes

The process of checking a borrower is called underwriting. It asks three separate questions, and a good application has to pass all three.

Can you afford it? The lender checks income, existing debts and regular spending. Many use a debt-to-income ratio: total monthly debt payments divided by monthly income. Others cap the loan at a multiple of income; since 2014 the Bank of England has limited how many new mortgages can be written at four and a half times income or more. Many lenders also run a stress test: could you still pay if the rate were a few points higher?

Will you pay? Your history with debt is the best predictor of your future behaviour. Lenders check credit files and scores, the subject of the next topic. A recent default or missed payment can mean a refusal or a much higher rate.

What if you don't? The house must be worth enough to cover the loan if it has to be sold. The lender sends a valuer or appraiser to check the price, and it lends a share of the lower of the price and the valuation. This is where the deposit matters. A 20% deposit means prices can fall by a fifth before the lender is exposed. A 5% deposit leaves almost no cushion, which is why low-deposit loans cost more and, in the US, often require mortgage insurance paid by the borrower to protect the lender.

Chapter 07

Where the money comes from

As Volume 38 explains, a loan creates a deposit. But that money leaves the bank when you pay the seller, so the bank must fund the loan over its life. Lenders use three main sources.

Deposits

Ordinary savers' money. Cheap, but it can leave at short notice, while the mortgage lasts decades. This mismatch is the classic banking risk explained in How banks work.

Covered bonds

Bonds sold to investors and backed by a pool of mortgages that stays on the bank's books. If the bank fails, bondholders have a claim on the pool. Common in Europe, especially Germany and Denmark.

Securitisation

The lender sells the mortgages into a separate company, which issues bonds paid from the borrowers' payments. These are mortgage-backed securities. Dominant in the US, where Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guarantee much of the market.

Securitisation let lenders recycle money quickly and spread risk. It also created a new problem. When a lender sells the loan, it no longer suffers if the borrower defaults. Economists call this the "originate to distribute" model, and it is at the centre of the next chapter.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A household contract with the power to move economies

Mortgages decide who can own a home, carry interest rates into daily life, and, when enough of them go wrong at once, start financial crises.

Chapter 08

The biggest number in the family budget

For most homeowners, the house is their largest asset and the mortgage their largest debt. In most rich countries, mortgages are the largest part of household debt, well ahead of cars and cards.

That has effects far beyond the family. For many, a paid-off house is the bulk of their retirement wealth. And because the house is used as security, rising house prices let owners borrow and spend more, and falling prices do the reverse. Economists call this the wealth effect of housing, and it is one reason house-price crashes hurt the wider economy so badly.

Mortgages also shape who gets ahead. In the US, "redlining", marking mainly Black neighbourhoods as too risky to lend in, was widespread from the 1930s until the Fair Housing Act of 1968 and later laws banned it. Historians trace part of today's racial wealth gap to who could borrow in those decades.

Chapter 09

2008: when the mortgage machine broke

In the early 2000s, US house prices rose for years, and lending standards loosened with them. Lenders wrote subprime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit, often with low "teaser" rates that reset much higher after two or three years. Some loans required little or no proof of income. The logic held as long as prices kept rising: a borrower who could not pay could sell or refinance.

Many of these loans were securitised, repackaged and sold on, often with top credit ratings, to banks around the world. When prices began to fall in 2006 and 2007, defaults rose. Nobody was sure who held the bad loans, so banks stopped trusting each other. Investment bank Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, and the same month the US government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

The lesson regulators drew was not "mortgages are dangerous". It was that the danger lies in loans made without asking whether the borrower can repay, funded by people who never see the borrower. Many countries then made affordability checks a legal duty. The full story of the crisis is in Financial crises.

Chapter 10

The pipe that carries interest rates home

When a central bank raises rates, mortgages are one of the main ways the decision reaches ordinary people. How fast depends on the national design from Chapter 03.

In countries with short fixes or variable rates, such as the UK, Spain or Australia, a rate rise shows up in household budgets within months or a couple of years. In the US, where most borrowers hold 30-year fixed loans, existing owners barely notice. Instead, the pain lands on new buyers. When US mortgage rates rose from around 3% in 2021 to around 7% in 2023, many owners with cheap loans simply stopped moving, because selling meant giving up their low rate. This "lock-in" effect reduced the number of homes for sale and kept prices high even as borrowing got more expensive.

After the UK government's September 2022 "mini-budget" unsettled bond markets, lenders withdrew a large share of their mortgage offers within days to reprice. The episode showed that mortgage rates follow bond markets, not just the central bank. The details of how central bank rates spread through the economy are in Monetary policy.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Leverage cuts both ways

A mortgage lets you control an asset many times larger than your savings. That magnifies every gain and every loss.

Chapter 12

Buying versus renting, honestly

"Rent is dead money" is half true. Owning has dead money too: interest, property taxes, insurance, repairs and the cost of buying and selling. The honest comparison is between the unrecoverable costs of owning and the rent on a similar home, not between rent and the whole mortgage payment.

Which side wins depends on how long you stay, on rates, on how fast prices and rents rise, and on what the deposit would have earned invested. Over long periods, owning has often worked well, thanks to leverage and forced saving. Over short periods, or where prices fell, renting often won. Many Irish buyers near the 2007 peak spent years in negative equity.

Owning gives security and control; renting gives flexibility. Neither is a mistake by itself. The mistake is a loan you could only afford if nothing went wrong.

Chapter 14

The question underneath: who gets to buy?

In many large cities, house prices have risen much faster than incomes for decades. First-time buyers are older and rely more on help from parents. The main explanations differ sharply.

One side argues that the core problem is supply: not enough homes are built where people want to live, often because of planning rules, so cheaper credit just bids prices higher. A second side stresses demand and finance: years of low interest rates, tax advantages for owners and investors, and buyer subsidies pushed prices up. A third stresses public and social housing, arguing that markets alone will not house lower-income people. Most economists see some truth in all three, and the weight given to each is genuinely contested.

Looser lending helps today's buyer and can raise tomorrow's prices; tighter lending protects borrowers but keeps some people out. That is why central banks now use loan-to-value and loan-to-income limits as policy tools. The mortgage, a private contract, has become one of the main levers of public policy on housing.

The core idea

A mortgage is a loan secured on property. The security lets lenders offer large, long, cheap loans. Payments follow an amortisation schedule that is mostly interest at first. The choice of fixed or variable decides who carries the risk of rates changing, and how lenders fund loans decides how risk spreads through the financial system.

The numbers worth knowing
  • 200,000 at 5% over 25 years costs about 1,169 a month and about 150,000 in total interest.
  • After 10 years of that loan, about three quarters is still owed.
  • On the same loan, a move from 2% to 6% raises the payment by about half.
The common mistake

Asking only "can I afford the monthly payment today?" The right question is whether you could still afford it if rates rose by a few points or your income fell, and how big a price fall your deposit could absorb.
