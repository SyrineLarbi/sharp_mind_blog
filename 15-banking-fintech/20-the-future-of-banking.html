The future of banking. The jobs will stay. Who does them, and who carries the risk, is up for grabs.

Predictions about the death of banks are almost as old as the internet. In the 1990s people said online banking would end them. In the 2010s it was fintech, then cryptocurrency. Banks are still here, and the largest are bigger than ever. Yet banking in 2026 really is changing: money moves instantly between phones, customers can share their banking data with rival apps, software writes credit memos, private funds lend money that banks once lent, and dollar-based digital tokens have their own law in the United States. The useful question is not whether banks survive. It is which of their jobs move elsewhere, and what happens to safety when they do.

Question 1 · What is it?

The future of a bundle of jobs

To think clearly about the future of banking, separate what banks do from what banks are. The jobs are ancient. The institution that does them has changed shape many times.

Chapter 01

The weekend a bank vanished from its customers' screens

In April 2018 TSB, a British bank with several million customers, moved their accounts from the computer systems of its former owner to new ones built for its new Spanish parent. The move happened over a weekend. On Monday, a large number of customers could not log in. Some saw other people's accounts. Payments failed. Problems lasted for weeks, the chief executive resigned, and the bank and its managers were later fined by regulators.

The story is a reminder of something that predictions about banking often forget. A bank is, underneath, a set of very old computer systems that must never stop. Many large banks still run core accounting on mainframes and on code written decades ago in languages such as COBOL. Replacing them is like changing the engine of a plane in flight. That is one reason banks change more slowly than their critics expect, and one reason the newcomers, with clean systems, have an advantage.

Chapter 02

What banks actually do

Strip away the branches and apps and a bank does four things.

JobWhat it meansWho else now competes for it
Keeping money safeHolding deposits that can be withdrawn at any timeMoney-market funds, e-money firms, stablecoin issuers
Moving moneyRunning accounts that make and receive paymentsCard networks, payment apps, public instant rails, big-tech wallets
Lending and creating moneyTurning short-term deposits into long-term loansPrivate credit funds, fintech lenders, buy-now-pay-later firms
Managing risk and trustChecking customers, absorbing losses, obeying regulatorsRegtech vendors, insurers, specialised platforms

The third job is what makes banks different. When a bank lends, it creates a new deposit in the borrower's account, which is new money. And it funds long loans, such as thirty-year mortgages, with deposits that customers can take out tomorrow. This is called maturity transformation. It is useful, and it is the reason banks can suffer runs. How banks work explains both. Because banks create money and can fail suddenly, they get a public safety net, and in exchange they accept strict regulation. That bargain shapes everything in the rest of this file.

Chapter 03

Unbundling: "banking is necessary, banks are not"

A line widely attributed to Bill Gates in the 1990s says that banking is necessary but banks are not. It captures the central idea behind most predictions: unbundling. A bank sells a bundle of services together. Specialists could pick them off one by one: a payments firm for transfers, a fund for savings, a platform for loans, a broker for investments.

That has partly happened. Payments have been unbundled furthest, by card networks long ago and by apps more recently. Investing moved largely to funds and brokers. Lending to companies is shifting toward private funds. But unbundling has a natural opposite, rebundling. Successful specialists keep adding products until they look like banks again. Many neobanks began with a single card and now offer savings, loans, shares and insurance, and the strongest have applied for full banking licences. Fintech startups describes that journey.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Six forces acting on the same institution

The future of banking is being shaped by several pressures at once. Each of them attacks a different job, and they interact.

Chapter 04

Payments become instant and public

For most of history, moving money between banks took days. Instant payment systems change that. Brazil's Pix (2020) and India's UPI (2016) are the famous examples. Europe adopted rules in 2024 requiring banks in the euro area to offer instant euro transfers at no extra cost compared with ordinary transfers. The US Federal Reserve launched its own instant service, FedNow, in 2023.

Instant, cheap, public payment rails do two things to banks. They take away fees that once came from slow or international payments. And they make deposits more mobile. When money can leave in a second, a bank's customers are only as loyal as its last interest rate. The value of a bank shifts from running the pipes to what it offers on top: credit, advice, service and safety.

Chapter 05

Data becomes portable

For decades your bank was the only company that could see your full transaction history, which gave it a large advantage in selling you loans and other products. Open banking rules changed that. In the EU, the second Payment Services Directive (PSD2), applied from 2018, and the UK's open banking programme from the same year require banks to let customers share their account data, through secure connections, with other licensed firms they choose. Open banking covers the details.

The next step is open finance: extending the same idea to savings, pensions, insurance and investments. The EU has proposed such rules, and the US consumer-finance regulator issued an open-banking rule in 2024 that then became the subject of legal and political dispute. The principle, wherever it lands, is that the customer, not the bank, controls the customer's financial data.

Chapter 06

AI enters the back office, then the front

Banks have used statistical models for decades, for credit scoring and fraud detection. What changed after 2022 is the arrival of large language models, which can read and write ordinary text. Banks first applied them inside: helping programmers, summarising documents, drafting reports for compliance analysts, answering staff questions about internal policies. Those uses are relatively safe, because a human checks the output.

The harder step is letting AI deal directly with customers and money. A chatbot that explains a fee is one thing. An AI that gives investment advice, decides a loan or moves money on a customer's behalf is another, because it carries legal responsibility. The EU's AI Act treats AI used for credit scoring of individuals as high-risk, with strict requirements. AI regulation explains the rules. The likely pattern is that AI changes banks' costs long before it changes their customers' experience.

Chapter 07

Money as code: stablecoins and tokenised deposits

A stablecoin is a digital token that runs on a blockchain and is designed to keep a fixed value, usually one US dollar, backed by reserves such as cash and short-term government bonds. Stablecoins grew from a tool for crypto traders into a large market. In July 2025 the United States passed its first federal stablecoin law, the GENIUS Act, setting rules for reserves and for who may issue them. The EU's MiCA regulation already governed them in Europe. Digital currencies explains how they work.

Why does this matter to banks? If people and companies hold dollars as stablecoins instead of bank deposits, banks lose deposits, which are their cheapest source of funding for loans. Banks are responding with tokenised deposits: ordinary bank deposits represented as tokens that can move on blockchain-style systems, while remaining regulated deposits. Central banks are exploring the same idea for wholesale payments between banks, and some are preparing retail digital currencies, as Central bank digital currencies describes.

Chapter 08

Lending moves outside the banks

After 2008, regulators required banks to hold much more capital against risky loans. Lending to mid-sized and risky companies became expensive for banks. Into that gap came private credit: investment funds, often run by large asset managers, that lend directly to companies using money from pension funds, insurers and wealthy investors. By the mid-2020s private credit had grown into a market of well over a trillion dollars.

The appeal is clear. A fund that lends with investors' money, locked up for years, does not face a bank run. If a loan fails, investors take the loss, not depositors or taxpayers. The concern is also clear. These funds are less transparent, their loans are rarely traded so their value is hard to check, and they often borrow from banks themselves. Risk does not disappear when it leaves the banks. It moves to places supervisors can see less clearly. Economists call this non-bank finance, or more critically, shadow banking.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Stability, money creation and who pays when it fails

The shape of banking decides how safe your savings are, how easily businesses can borrow, and how often taxpayers end up rescuing someone.

Chapter 09

The run at the speed of a phone

In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank in California collapsed after depositors, many of them tech start-ups linked through the same investors and group chats, tried to withdraw about 42 billion dollars in a single day. Within days another US bank, Signature Bank, was closed, and in Switzerland the authorities arranged the takeover of Credit Suisse by UBS. Old rules assumed that a run would take days or weeks. This one took hours.

The episode changed how regulators think. Liquidity rules, which require banks to hold enough cash and easily sold assets to survive a run, were written on assumptions about how fast deposits leave. Those assumptions now look too slow. Central banks are asking banks to be ready to borrow from them at any moment, using collateral already placed in advance. The same technology that makes banking convenient makes bank runs faster. Every future design of banking has to cope with that.

Chapter 10

Who creates the money, and who guarantees it

Most of the money people use is not cash printed by the central bank. It is bank deposits, created by commercial banks as they lend. That is why the line between regulated banks and everything else matters so much. Deposits are protected, up to a limit, by deposit insurance, for example 100,000 euros per depositor per bank in the EU and 250,000 dollars in the US. Banks can borrow from the central bank in an emergency. In return they are heavily supervised.

If more money moves into stablecoins, money-market funds or e-money apps, more of people's savings sits outside that protection. If more lending moves to private funds, the central bank has less direct influence on credit. Some economists welcome this and argue for narrow banking, an old proposal in which deposits would be backed fully by central bank reserves or government bonds and lending would be done only by investment funds. Others warn that it would reduce lending and simply move runs elsewhere. The debate is genuinely unresolved.

Chapter 11

Five scenarios supervisors have mapped

In 2018 the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, the body that sets global bank standards, published a paper on the effects of fintech. It sketched five possible futures for banks. They remain a useful map.

ScenarioWhat happensWho owns the customer
Better bankIncumbent banks modernise and keep their positionBanks
New bankDigital challengers with licences replace many incumbentsNew banks
Distributed bankBanks and fintechs share the work, each doing what it does bestShared
Relegated bankBanks become back-office utilities; platforms own the relationshipPlatforms and big tech
Disintermediated bankCustomers deal directly with lenders, payment systems and markets; banks fadeNobody, or the customer

Real life looks like a mix that varies by country. In much of Europe and the US, the "better bank" and "distributed bank" scenarios dominate. In China, the platforms of Alipay and WeChat Pay pushed toward the "relegated bank" until regulators pulled them back. In Brazil and India, public payment systems moved part of the system toward disintermediation for payments, while banks kept lending. The deciding factor is often not technology but regulation.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Better service, thinner margins, and new places for risk to hide

Almost every change ahead makes banking cheaper and more convenient. Many of them also make the system harder to keep stable.

Chapter 12

What the changes give, and what they risk

Instant public paymentsFree, fast transfers; less power for card networks.Faster bank runs; instant scams that are hard to reverse.

One pattern runs through the table. The benefits arrive quickly and spread to everyone. The risks are rare and concentrated, and they show up in a crisis. That is why banking regulators move slowly and why innovators find them frustrating. Both are responding to the same asymmetry.

Chapter 14

The risks nobody advertises

Three slower risks may shape banking more than any app. The first is concentration on a few technology suppliers. Many banks now run on the same handful of cloud providers. Europe's DORA rules, applied from 2025, allow supervisors to oversee critical technology suppliers directly, because an outage at one of them could hit hundreds of banks at once. Regtech explains the rules.

The second is cryptography. Banking's security relies on encryption that a large enough quantum computer could break. Nobody knows when, or whether, such a machine will exist, but data stolen today could be decrypted later. Banks and standards bodies have begun moving to new "post-quantum" algorithms. Volume 14 explains why (Encryption, and what quantum computers would break).

The third is climate. Banks hold mortgages on homes in flood zones and loans to industries that may lose value as economies decarbonise. Supervisors in Europe and elsewhere now run climate stress tests. Insurtech shows how this is already hitting insurance.

The deepest prediction is the simplest. Whatever technology wins, the bargain will return. Any institution that holds people's money and promises it back on demand will eventually need a safety net, and anything with a safety net will eventually be regulated like a bank. The names on the app may change. The logic underneath has survived every revolution so far.

The core idea

Banks bundle four jobs: keeping money safe, moving it, lending and creating it, and managing risk. Instant payments, open data, AI, stablecoins and private credit each pull one job away. But because banks create money and can suffer runs, they live under a bargain: a public safety net in exchange for strict regulation, and anyone who takes over their jobs eventually meets the same bargain.

The numbers worth knowing
  • SVB depositors tried to withdraw about 42 billion dollars in one day (March 2023).
  • Deposit insurance: 100,000 euros (EU), 250,000 dollars (US).
  • Basel Committee's five scenarios for banks: published 2018.
  • Private credit: well over a trillion dollars by the mid-2020s.
The common mistake

Predicting the end of banks because a new technology does one of their jobs better. Banks have lost individual jobs many times. The hard part, holding money safely and lending it, keeps coming back to regulated institutions.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a stablecoin that is fully backed by government bonds might still suffer a run, and why banks worry about stablecoins even when they are safe? If you can, you understand the link between deposits, lending and stability.
