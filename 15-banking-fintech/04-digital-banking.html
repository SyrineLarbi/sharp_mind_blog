Digital banking. The branch moved into your pocket, and the hard part moved underground.

Thirty years ago, opening a bank account meant an appointment, a stack of paper and a wait of a week or more for a card. Today, in many countries, it takes ten minutes, a photo of your passport and a selfie. That change looks like a better app. Underneath, it is a fight over the oldest and most fragile software in the economy, over who holds a banking licence, and over how a bank can make money when its customers never walk through its door.

Question 1 · What is it?

Banking delivered through screens instead of counters

"Digital banking" covers two different things: old banks offering their services online, and new banks built from the start to exist only online.

Chapter 01

A weekend that went wrong

In April 2018 the British bank TSB moved its customers' accounts from the old computer system it had used under its former owner to a new one run by its Spanish parent, Sabadell. The switch was planned for a weekend. When it was turned back on, many customers could not log in. Some saw other people's accounts. Payments failed, and the problems lasted for weeks. Around two million customers were affected. The chief executive resigned, the bank paid hundreds of millions of pounds in costs and compensation, and in 2022 regulators fined it about 49 million pounds.

The TSB story is a good place to start because it shows what digital banking is really about. The app is the easy part. The hard part is the system behind it that keeps the record of who owns what. Every bank must change that system eventually, and doing it while millions of people keep using their accounts is like replacing an aircraft's engines in flight.

Chapter 02

Three kinds of digital bank

TypeWhat it isExamples
Incumbent bank, digitisedAn established bank with branches that now offers most services through an app and website.Most large banks; many now serve the majority of customers digitally

The word neobank simply means "new bank". Some neobanks are fully licensed banks with deposit insurance. Others hold a narrower licence, or rent the licence of a traditional bank in the background. Customers often cannot tell the difference from the app, and the difference matters, as Chapter 06 explains.

Chapter 03

From cash machines to phones

Banking began moving away from the counter long before the internet. In 1967 Barclays opened one of the first cash machines, in Enfield, north London. In 1989 the UK's First Direct launched as a bank with no branches, run entirely by telephone, open 24 hours. In the late 1990s banks began offering internet banking, and new online-only savings banks such as ING Direct competed by paying higher rates, since they had no branches to fund.

The smartphone changed the shape of the problem. A phone has a camera that can photograph a passport, a fingerprint reader to confirm identity, and a screen that can show a payment the second it happens. The first generation of neobanks, founded around 2013 to 2015, were built around those features. The 2008 crisis helped too. Trust in big banks was low, and regulators in some countries, especially the UK, wanted more competition and made it easier to get a new licence.

Question 2 · How does it work?

An app on top, a ledger underneath, and a licence holding it together

A digital bank is a stack of systems. Each layer has its own technology and its own rules.

The most important layer is the core banking system. It is the ledger that records each customer's balance and calculates interest and fees. In many old banks it runs on mainframe computers with code written decades ago, often in COBOL, a programming language from 1959. It is reliable, but it was built for a world of overnight batches, where transactions were collected during the day and processed at night. That is why, for years, a payment could show as "pending" until the next morning.

Neobanks built their cores from scratch, or bought modern ones, designed for real-time processing and to run on cloud servers. That is what lets them send a notification within a second of a card payment, split a bill instantly, or let you freeze your card from the app. The visible features are mostly consequences of a modern ledger.

Chapter 05

Opening an account in ten minutes

Every bank must know who its customers are. These rules are called KYC, short for "know your customer", and they exist to fight money laundering, fraud and the financing of terrorism. Traditionally, KYC meant showing documents in a branch. Digital banks do it remotely. The app asks you to photograph an identity document. Software checks the document's security features, reads its data, and compares the photo with a short selfie video to make sure a live person is holding it. Other checks run against databases of addresses, sanctions lists and known fraud.

This is genuinely hard to get right. Make it too strict and genuine customers give up halfway. Make it too loose and criminals open accounts to receive stolen money. Accounts like these are called mule accounts, because they carry money for someone else. Fast onboarding was the neobanks' biggest selling point, and weak onboarding has been one of their biggest regulatory problems.

Chapter 06

The licence question

Not every company with a banking app is a bank. The differences matter most if something goes wrong.

ModelWhat the firm may doWhat happens to your money
Full banking licenceTake deposits, lend, create money by lendingCovered by deposit insurance up to the national limit
E-money or payment licence (common in Europe)Hold customers' money for payments, but not lend itNot insured, but must be kept separate ("safeguarded") in a bank or in safe assets
Partner bank (common in the US)The app firm runs the customer side; a licensed bank holds the depositsInsured at the partner bank, provided the records of who owns what are accurate

The last condition is not a formality. In 2024 the US firm Synapse, which connected fintech apps to partner banks, went bankrupt. Its records of which customer owned which money did not match the banks' records, and many customers were left unable to reach their funds for months. Deposit insurance protects you against a bank failing, not against a middleman losing track of your money.

Chapter 07

How a bank without branches makes money

Neobanks saved on branches, but they started with a weak revenue model. Their early income came mostly from interchange, the fee a shop pays on each card payment, part of which goes to the card issuer. That is small per transaction, and in Europe it is capped by law. So the neobanks added other income over time.

Fees and subscriptions

Paid plans with extras such as travel insurance or better exchange rates; fees on currency exchange, trading or premium cards. Revolut built a large part of its business this way.

Lending and deposits

Personal loans, overdrafts and credit cards, plus interest earned on customer deposits. When interest rates rose in 2022, deposits suddenly became very profitable, and several neobanks reached their first annual profits soon after.

The underlying lesson is that a neobank eventually has to become a bank. The cheapest way to win customers is a free, beautiful app. The way to make money from them is the old one: hold their salary, lend to them, and sell them other products. Nubank in Brazil is the clearest success, with more than 100 million customers across Brazil, Mexico and Colombia as of 2025, built largely on credit cards issued to people the big banks had ignored.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Cheaper service, more competition, and banking for people who had none

Digital banking changes what banking costs, who can offer it, and who can get it.

Chapter 08

The cost of serving one customer

A branch has rent, staff, security and cash handling. An app has servers and software engineers, whose cost barely rises as the number of customers grows. The cost of serving one more customer digitally is a small fraction of serving them in a branch. That changes which customers are worth having. A traditional bank in many countries could not profitably serve someone who kept a small balance and made small payments. A digital bank can.

The savings also explain why incumbent banks have closed branches so fast. They face a trap: the cheaper digital channel competes with their own expensive network, but customers who still need branches, often older people and small businesses handling cash, are the ones left behind. Several countries have responded with rules or schemes to protect access to cash and to shared banking hubs.

Chapter 09

Leapfrogging: where digital banking matters most

The biggest effects have been in middle-income countries, where many people had no bank account at all. In Brazil, banking was long dominated by a few large banks with high fees. Nubank offered a free credit card and account through an app and grew into one of Latin America's largest financial firms. In China, digital banks owned by technology groups, such as WeBank, backed by Tencent, lend small amounts through apps millions of people already use every day.

In parts of Africa, the leap went further. Mobile money services such as M-Pesa in Kenya, launched in 2007, let people store and send money from a basic phone, without a bank account at all. These are not banks, but they did the job of a bank account for many people who had none. The full story is in Financial inclusion. Where branches were never built, the phone did not replace the bank. It became the first bank.

Chapter 10

The incumbents fight back

For a while it looked as if neobanks would sweep away the old banks. That has not happened. Large banks had three advantages: huge numbers of existing customers who rarely switch, cheap deposits, and the experience of managing credit risk through several recessions. Most large banks have rebuilt their apps, and in many countries their apps now score as well with customers as the challengers'.

The result is less a revolution than a raising of the standard. Instant notifications, card freezing, easy transfers and spending categories were neobank novelties around 2016. Within a few years they were expected everywhere. Many customers now use a neobank as a second account, for travel or budgeting, while keeping their salary at an old bank. Turning that second account into the main one is the neobanks' central challenge.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Faster and cheaper, with new ways to fail

Digital banking removed old frictions. Some of those frictions were protecting people.

Chapter 12

When growth outruns control

The fastest-growing neobanks have repeatedly been criticised by regulators for weak controls against financial crime. In 2024 the UK regulator fined Starling Bank about 29 million pounds, saying its checks had not kept pace with its growth and that it had opened accounts for very high-risk customers in breach of restrictions. Other neobanks in Europe have faced limits on how many new customers they could take on until their controls improved.

Scams are the other weak point. In authorised push payment fraud, a criminal persuades the victim to send money themselves, for example by posing as the bank's fraud team. Because the customer authorised the payment, banks long refused to refund it. Instant payments made such scams faster. In October 2024 the UK made reimbursement mandatory in most cases, up to 85,000 pounds per claim, with the cost shared between the sending and receiving banks. Speed is a feature for customers and for criminals alike. More on this in Fraud detection.

Chapter 14

The bank you do not see

The longer trend may be that banking stops being something you go to at all. With embedded finance, a shop, a ride-hailing app or an accounting tool offers a payment account or a loan inside its own service, using a bank's licence in the background. Through open banking, customers can let other firms see their accounts and start payments from them, with their permission.

If that happens at scale, the question "which bank am I with?" becomes less important to customers and more important to regulators. Someone still has to hold the licence, the capital and the responsibility. Digital banking began as a better front end; its end point may be a banking system that is mostly back end. That possibility runs through The future of banking.

The core idea

Digital banking moved the front end of banking to phones, but its real substance is a modern real-time ledger, remote identity checks and the right licence. Neobanks won customers with design and low cost; to make money they have had to become ordinary banks that lend.

The numbers worth knowing
  • First Direct, a branchless telephone bank, launched in 1989.
  • TSB's 2018 migration failure affected around two million customers.
  • Nubank passed 100 million customers in Latin America by 2025.
The common mistake

Assuming every banking app is a bank. Check the licence: e-money firms and fintechs using partner banks protect your money in different ways, and not always by deposit insurance.

The words, in plain words
Neobank
A bank, or bank-like firm, built from the start to operate through an app, with no branches.
Incumbent
An established bank, usually with a branch network.
Core banking system
The software ledger that records accounts and balances and calculates interest.
Batch processing
Collecting transactions and processing them together, often overnight.
Real-time processing
Handling each transaction the moment it happens.
API
A defined way for one piece of software to request data or actions from another.
KYC
"Know your customer": checks banks must run to confirm who a customer is.
Mule account
An account used to receive and pass on stolen or laundered money.
E-money institution
A firm licensed to hold money for payments but not to lend it.
Safeguarding
Keeping customers' money separate from the firm's own, so it can be returned if the firm fails.
Interchange
The fee a shop's side pays to the card issuer on each card payment.
Authorised push payment fraud
A scam in which the victim is tricked into sending money themselves.
Embedded finance
Financial products offered inside a non-financial company's app or service.
