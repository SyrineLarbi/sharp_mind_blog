Credit scores. A reputation squeezed into three digits, and read by strangers who will never meet you.

A century ago, a bank manager lent to people he knew. Today a lender in another city decides in seconds, using a number built from your past behaviour with debt. That number shapes whether you get a mortgage, what interest you pay, and in some countries whether you can rent a flat. It is one of the most powerful pieces of data about an ordinary person, and most people do not know how it is made.

Question 1 · What is it?

A prediction, not a judgement of character

A credit score is a statistical estimate of how likely you are to fall seriously behind on a debt. It is built from a credit report, which is a different thing.

Chapter 01

From the shopkeeper's memory to a formula

Lending has always needed one piece of information: will this person pay me back? In a village, the answer came from reputation. The shopkeeper who let you "put it on the slate" knew your family and your job. As cities grew and people moved, that local knowledge stopped working. In the 19th century, merchants in Britain and the US began sharing information about customers who did not pay. These shared lists grew into credit bureaus: companies that collect records of how people handle debt and sell them to lenders.

For a long time, lenders read those files and decided by judgement. That was slow, inconsistent and open to prejudice. In 1956 an engineer, Bill Fair, and a mathematician, Earl Isaac, founded a company to replace judgement with statistics. Their firm, now FICO, sold scoring systems to lenders, and in 1989 launched a general-purpose score that became the US standard. The idea was simple: look at what people with files like yours did in the past, and use it to predict what you will do next.

Chapter 02

The report and the score are two different things

People mix these up constantly, and the difference explains most of the confusion.

The credit report (the facts)

A file held by a credit bureau. It lists your loans, cards and mortgages; their limits and balances; whether each payment was on time; any defaults, court judgments or bankruptcies; and which lenders have looked at your file. In the US the three national bureaus are Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. The UK has the same three. Each may hold slightly different data about you.

The credit score (the prediction)

A number calculated from a report by a formula. There is no single score. A FICO score uses one formula; VantageScore, created by the three US bureaus in 2006, uses another; lenders also build their own. Your "score" can differ by dozens of points depending on which formula and which bureau's file were used.

The most common US scores run from 300 to 850. UK bureaus each use their own scale, so a number from one cannot be compared with another. What stays the same is the logic: higher means lower predicted risk.

Scoring around the world

Countries organise credit information in very different ways.

The difference between positive and negative systems matters. A negative-only file can tell a lender who has failed, but not who has succeeded. A young person with ten years of perfect payments looks the same as one who has never borrowed.

Not the same thing

China's "social credit system" is often described as a credit score for citizens. In practice it is a patchwork of government blacklists, records about companies, and local pilot schemes, separate from the commercial credit scores some Chinese tech firms run. Reporting on it has often been exaggerated, and it is a different subject from consumer credit scoring.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Patterns from millions of past borrowers, applied to you

A score is built by looking backwards at people whose outcome is already known, and then applying what was learned to people whose outcome is not.

Chapter 04

What goes in, and how much it counts

FICO publishes rough weights for the five groups of information its standard scores use. Other models differ in detail, but the same themes appear in almost all of them.

CategoryRough weightWhat it looks at
Payment historyAbout 35%Late payments, how late, how recent, and defaults
Amounts owedAbout 30%Especially utilisation: card balances as a share of card limits
Length of historyAbout 15%The age of your oldest and average accounts
New creditAbout 10%Recently opened accounts and recent applications
Credit mixAbout 10%Whether you have handled different kinds, such as cards and instalment loans

Just as important is what is not in it. In the US, the law forbids lenders from using race, religion, sex, marital status or national origin in credit decisions (the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974). Standard scores also do not use income, savings or job title. Income matters for whether you can afford a loan, but the lender checks that separately. The score measures how you have handled credit, not how much money you have.

Chapter 05

How a formula turns history into points

To build a score, a modeller takes a huge sample of credit files from, say, two years ago. For each one, they already know what happened next: did the person fall seriously behind? FICO's standard definition of "bad" is being 90 days or more late on a payment within the following two years. The modeller then asks which features of the old files best separated the people who went bad from those who did not.

The classic tool is a scorecard. Each feature is split into bands, and each band gets points. "No late payments ever" earns many points. "One payment 30 days late, three years ago" earns fewer. "Card balances above 90% of limits" earns very few. The points are added up. Behind the scenes, the statistics are usually a method called logistic regression, which turns a weighted sum of features into a probability. The total is then rescaled to a familiar range.

What a score means, in principle
  file features          scorecard points          score       predicted outcome
  ----------------       ----------------          -----       -----------------
  never late        -->        +high

  higher score  =  more "good" borrowers for every "bad" one

Two things follow. A score is about groups, not individuals. It says how people like you behaved, not what you personally will do. And it has to be rebuilt from time to time, because behaviour, products and the economy change. A model trained on a boom can be too optimistic in a recession.

Chapter 06

What the lender does with the number

A lender rarely uses the score alone. It sets a cut-off: below a certain score, decline or send to a human; above it, approve if affordability checks pass. Many lenders also use risk-based pricing: the better the score, the lower the interest rate offered. On a large mortgage, the gap between the best and a middling price band can add up to tens of thousands over the life of the loan.

The score is also used after you borrow. Card issuers watch your file and may raise or cut your limit. And a refusal is not secret. US and UK lenders generally have to tell you if a credit file contributed to a decline, and in the US they must give the main reasons. That is called an adverse action notice.

Chapter 07

Why scores sometimes behave strangely

Because the formula reacts to patterns, some sensible actions can lower a score for a while.

Closing an old card

It shrinks your total limit, so your utilisation rises, and it can shorten your average account age. The prudent act looks riskier to the formula.

Never borrowing at all

No history means nothing to score. A person who has always paid cash can be "unscorable", even if they are very reliable.

Hard versus soft searches

When you apply for credit, the lender makes a "hard" search, which is visible and can cost a few points. Checking your own score is a "soft" search and does not count. Scores usually treat several mortgage or car-loan searches in a short window as one, so shopping around is not punished.

Paying in full but late in the cycle

Some cards report your balance on the statement date, before you pay. You may pay in full every month and still show high utilisation.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A gatekeeper for far more than loans

Scores made credit cheaper and faster for millions. They also became a gate that some people cannot get through, for reasons unrelated to whether they would repay.

Chapter 08

Cheaper, faster, more consistent credit

Before scoring, a loan could take days and depend on the mood of one manager. A score lets a lender decide in seconds and decide the same way for everyone with the same file. That made it possible to lend to people the lender had never met, in another region, through a website. It also made it possible to package loans and sell them to investors, because investors could see the score distribution of a pool. Consistency is not the same as fairness, but it is a precondition for checking fairness: you cannot audit a gut feeling.

The reach of the score has spread well beyond lending. In the US, landlords commonly check credit, and most states allow insurers to use credit-based "insurance scores" in pricing car and home cover. Employers may see a version of the credit report, with your consent, for some jobs, although several states restrict this. In Germany, a poor SCHUFA entry can make it hard to rent a flat or get a phone contract. A number built to predict loan defaults ends up shaping where people live.

Chapter 09

The credit invisible

A score needs history. People without it are stuck in a loop: you cannot get credit without a score, and you cannot get a score without credit. In 2015 the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau estimated that about 26 million American adults had no credit file at all, and millions more had files too thin to score. They were disproportionately young, poor, Black or Hispanic, and recent immigrants.

Immigrants feel this sharply. A person with twenty years of perfect payments in one country usually arrives in another with no score, because bureaus do not share files across borders. Many pay higher rates or larger deposits for years. In much of the developing world the problem is larger still: most adults have never had a formal loan, so there is no file to start from. Topic 19, Financial inclusion, follows this further.

Chapter 10

Errors, breaches and disputes

A score is only as good as the file behind it. A 2012 study by the US Federal Trade Commission found that about one in five consumers had an error in at least one of their three reports, and a smaller share had errors serious enough to affect the price they were offered. Errors come from mixed-up files of people with similar names, debts reported twice, and fraud.

The data is also a target. In 2017, criminals broke into Equifax and stole personal details of about 147 million people, mostly Americans. The breach did not change anyone's score, but it gave fraudsters the information needed to open accounts in other people's names, which then damage those people's files. Topic 12, Financial cybersecurity, covers how such breaches happen.

What you can do

In the US and UK you have a legal right to see your reports and to dispute errors, and US residents can get free reports from each bureau. You can also freeze your file, which blocks new credit being opened in your name until you lift the freeze. It is free in the US and one of the most effective defences against identity fraud.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Better than a gut feeling, worse than its reputation

The case for scoring is strong when you compare it with what came before. The case against is strong when you compare it with what people assume it is.

Chapter 12

The fairness debate

The hardest question about credit scores is whether they are fair. Here the positions genuinely differ.

Supporters argue that standard scores do not use race or sex, and that studies by regulators have found they predict default about equally well for different groups. If a score predicts accurately, they say, it is doing its job, and the fix for unequal outcomes lies elsewhere.

Critics reply that the inputs are not neutral. If one group was historically shut out of cheap credit, pushed into worse loans, or hit harder by medical debt or job loss, their files will look worse, and the score will faithfully pass that history on. Economists call this disparate impact: a rule that is neutral on its face but produces unequal results. US law can treat this as discrimination unless the rule has a legitimate business justification.

Machine learning sharpens the question. In 2019, a well-known tech entrepreneur complained that the Apple Card had offered him a far higher limit than his wife. The New York regulator investigated and in 2021 found no unlawful discrimination, but the case showed how hard it is to explain automated decisions. A model can avoid using sex and still find data that acts as a stand-in for it. The general issue of bias in AI systems is covered in Volume 49.

Chapter 14

Who gets to question the number?

The direction in Europe is toward more rights for the person being scored. The EU's data protection law, the GDPR, gives people rights around decisions made purely by automated means. In December 2023, the EU's top court ruled in a case involving SCHUFA that producing a score can itself count as such a decision when lenders rely on it heavily. That means bureaus can face stronger duties to justify and explain their scores. The EU's AI Act also lists credit scoring of individuals as a "high-risk" use, with extra requirements (see AI regulation).

The US relies more on its older credit-reporting and anti-discrimination laws, enforced case by case, and policy has shifted with changes of government. The common thread is that a score which decides people's lives is increasingly expected to explain itself. For a formula built to be fast and automatic, that is the biggest change since 1989.

The core idea

A credit report is the record of how you have handled debt; a credit score is a statistical prediction, built from that record, of how likely you are to fall seriously behind. Payment history and utilisation matter most. Scores made lending faster and more consistent, but they exclude people with no history and can carry old inequalities forward.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Common US scores run from 300 to 850.
  • Payment history is about 35% of a FICO score; amounts owed about 30%.
  • The 2017 Equifax breach exposed data on about 147 million people.
The common mistake

Thinking there is one score, and that it measures wealth or character. There are many scores, and they measure only your past behaviour with credit. A rich person who never borrows can score worse than a modest earner with a long, tidy record.

Can you explain why closing an old, unused credit card might lower your score, and why that does not mean you became riskier?

The words, in plain words
Credit bureau
A company that collects records of how people handle debt and sells them to lenders.
Credit report (file)
Your record at a bureau: accounts, balances, payments, defaults and searches.
Credit score
A number calculated from a report, predicting the chance of serious late payment.
FICO
The US company whose scores are the most widely used, and the name of its score.
Utilisation
Card balances as a share of card limits. Lower is better.
Thin file
A report with too little history to score reliably.
Scorecard
A table that gives points to bands of each feature and adds them up.
Logistic regression
A standard statistical method that turns weighted features into a probability.
Cut-off
The score below which a lender declines or refers an application.
Risk-based pricing
Charging higher rates to borrowers with lower scores.
Hard search
A lender's check after you apply, visible to other lenders.
Credit freeze
A block on your file that stops new credit being opened in your name.
Disparate impact
A neutral-looking rule that produces unequal outcomes between groups.
