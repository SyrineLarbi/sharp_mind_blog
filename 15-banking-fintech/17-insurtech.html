Insurtech. Software meets the oldest promise in finance: we will pay if it goes wrong.

Insurance is a strange product. You pay for it hoping never to use it, and the company that sells it does not know its own costs until years later. Insurtech is the name for the startups, and the older insurers copying them, that use apps, sensors and data to sell, price and pay out insurance differently. Some of it is cosmetic: a nicer way to buy the same policy. Some of it goes to the heart of the business, because better data changes who pays what. That raises a question no app can answer by itself. If insurance is about sharing risk, how much should each person's price reflect their own risk?

Question 1 · What is it?

Technology applied to an industry built on pooled risk

To understand insurtech you first need to understand what an insurer actually does. Then the word means simply: any use of modern technology to do those jobs differently.

Chapter 01

A coffee house and a pool of strangers

In the 1680s, a man called Edward Lloyd ran a coffee house near the River Thames in London. Ship owners, captains and merchants met there to trade news about voyages. Wealthy individuals began to agree, in writing, to cover part of the loss if a particular ship sank, in exchange for a fee. They wrote their names under the description of the risk, which is where the word underwriter comes from. That market grew into Lloyd's of London, still one of the centres of world insurance today.

The idea underneath is older than Lloyd's and very simple. One ship sinking would ruin its owner. But if a hundred owners each pay a small amount into a pool, the pool can pay for the one ship that sinks. Nobody knows whose ship it will be, but everyone can know roughly how many ships sink in a year. Insurance turns an unpredictable disaster for one person into a predictable cost for many.

Mathematicians call the reason this works the law of large numbers: the more independent cases you combine, the closer the actual result comes to the average. One house may or may not burn down. Out of a million houses, the number that burn is steady from year to year.

Chapter 02

The four jobs of an insurer

Every insurer, old or new, does four things. Insurtech firms usually attack one or two of them.

JobWhat it meansWhat technology changes
DistributionFinding customers and selling policiesBuying in minutes on a phone, or inside another purchase
UnderwritingDeciding whom to cover and at what priceMore data per customer: driving behaviour, satellite images, sensor readings
ClaimsChecking and paying when something goes wrongPhoto assessment, automatic fraud checks, payment in minutes
Carrying the riskHolding enough money to pay, and passing some risk onBetter catastrophe models; new ways to share risk with investors

There is an important split between firms that only sell, called brokers or managing agents, and firms that actually carry the risk, called carriers. A comparison website is insurtech in distribution. It never pays a claim. A full insurtech carrier must hold capital approved by regulators, and that is a much harder business.

Chapter 03

Where insurtech came from

Insurance was one of the last parts of finance to move online. Policies were sold by agents who earned commission, priced on a few broad facts such as age and postcode, and paid after forms, phone calls and sometimes a visit from a loss adjuster, the person who inspects damage. Comparison websites, which spread in Britain and elsewhere in the 2000s, were the first big shake-up. They made car and home insurance a price war.

The startup wave arrived in the 2010s, on the same tide as other fintech: smartphones, cloud computing and cheap venture money. In the US, Lemonade (launched in 2016) sold renters' and home insurance through a chatbot. Root sold car insurance priced on how people drove. Many others built software for existing insurers rather than competing with them. Investment peaked in 2021, as it did across fintech, and then fell sharply. Fintech startups explains that cycle.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Price the risk, keep the float, pay the claim

An insurer's economics are unusual. It collects money first and finds out its costs later. Every insurtech idea is an attempt to improve one step of that cycle.

Chapter 04

The economics: premiums, float and the combined ratio

The money customers pay is called the premium. Out of every 100 euros of premium, an insurer must pay claims, pay its staff and its sales costs, and keep a margin. The industry measures this with one number, the combined ratio: claims plus expenses, divided by premiums. Below 100% means the insurance itself makes a profit. Above 100% means it loses money on underwriting.

Many insurers survive with a combined ratio a little above 100% anyway. The reason is the float. Premiums arrive today, but claims are paid months or years later. In between, the insurer invests the money. Warren Buffett built much of Berkshire Hathaway on this: insurance float gave him cheap money to invest for decades. An insurer is partly an investment fund that is paid to hold other people's money.

For a new insurtech, this is hard. It has few customers, so its results swing wildly with a few large claims. It spends heavily on advertising, so expenses are high. And when it is small it often buys a great deal of reinsurance, insurance for insurers, which gives away much of the profit. Several of the best-known insurtechs ran combined ratios well above 100% for years.

Chapter 05

Pricing with data: the black box in the car

Traditional car insurance prices drivers by groups: age, postcode, car model, years without a claim. These are proxies. A careful 19-year-old pays for the average 19-year-old, who crashes far more often than the average 45-year-old.

Telematics means measuring behaviour directly. A small device in the car, or an app on the phone, records how hard someone brakes, how fast they take corners, what time of night they drive and how many kilometres they cover. In Britain, "black box" policies became a standard way for young drivers to afford cover. In the US, large insurers have offered such programmes for years. This is called usage-based insurance: pay-as-you-drive (by distance) or pay-how-you-drive (by behaviour).

The same logic spreads elsewhere. Home insurers use water-leak sensors and satellite images of roofs. Some health and life insurers offer discounts for sharing step counts from fitness trackers. The promise is a price that reflects you, not your category. Chapter 11 looks at the price of that promise.

Chapter 06

Claims in minutes: photos, models and fraud checks

For a customer, the claim is the moment of truth. It is when the promise is tested. It is also, for insurers, the most expensive part of the operation, because people must check each claim and some claims are dishonest. Estimates of insurance fraud vary a lot, but every major market treats it as a large and permanent cost.

Insurtech attacks claims in three ways. Image assessment: the customer photographs a damaged car or a flooded kitchen, and a computer-vision model, trained on many past claims, estimates the repair cost. Firms such as the British company Tractable built businesses on this. Automatic triage: simple, low-value claims that pass the checks are paid at once, and humans look only at the complicated or suspicious ones. Fraud models: software looks for patterns, such as the same phone number on several unrelated claims, or a policy bought days before an expensive "accident".

Lemonade became famous for advertising a claim paid in a few seconds by its software. Fast payment of honest claims is a real gain. But speed and suspicion pull in opposite directions. Every rule that catches more fraud also delays or wrongly rejects some honest customers.

Chapter 07

Insurance that pays on a trigger

Ordinary insurance pays for the actual loss, after someone has assessed it. That takes time, and after a hurricane or a drought, time is what people do not have. Parametric insurance works differently. It pays a fixed amount when a measurable event crosses a threshold agreed in advance: wind speed above a certain level at a certain place, rainfall below a certain level over a season, an earthquake of a certain magnitude within a certain distance.

No inspection is needed, so payment can come within days. A well-known example is the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility, set up in 2007, which insures Caribbean governments and pays quickly after hurricanes and earthquakes so they can fund emergency response. Similar schemes cover farmers against drought in parts of Africa and Asia, often sold through mobile phones.

The weakness is called basis risk: the gap between the trigger and the real loss. A farmer's field may be ruined by a local dry spell while the official weather station, twenty kilometres away, records enough rain. No payout. Or the trigger may pay when there was little damage. Good parametric design is mostly about choosing triggers that match losses closely.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

Who is covered, who is priced out, and who pays for disasters

Insurance decides whether a flood, an illness or an accident is a setback or a catastrophe. Changing how it is sold and priced changes who can live with risk.

Chapter 08

Embedded insurance: cover at the moment you need it

Most people never think about insurance until they need it, which is too late. One answer is to offer it inside another purchase: travel cover when you book the flight, breakage cover when you buy the phone, damage cover when you rent the car or the holiday flat. This is embedded insurance, and software connections between shops and insurers have made it far easier.

It can close real gaps. A gig worker can get accident cover for exactly the hours they work on a delivery app. A small business can get cover through the accounting software it already uses. Mobile-phone networks in Africa and South Asia have sold small life and health policies, called microinsurance, to millions of people who had never held a policy before, often paid for a few cents at a time. Financial inclusion explains why that matters for families without savings.

Embedded insurance also has a history of mis-selling. In Britain, payment protection insurance (PPI), added to loans and credit cards, became the country's largest consumer-finance scandal, with banks paying back tens of billions of pounds. Insurance sold as an add-on is easy to sell and easy to sell badly.

Chapter 09

The fairness question: what is a fair price?

Here is the deepest issue in insurtech. Pooling works because people with different risks share a pool. If an insurer could predict each person's risk perfectly, there would be no pool left. Each person would simply pay for their own future losses, and the people with the worst luck, often through no fault of their own, would pay the most or be refused.

Societies have drawn the line in different places. In 2011 the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled, in a case known as Test-Achats, that from the end of 2012 insurers in the EU could no longer charge men and women different prices. Young women had been paying less for car insurance because, as a group, they crash less. The court decided that sex was not an acceptable basis for price, even if it predicted risk. In the United States, a 2008 law (the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act) bars health insurers and employers from using genetic test results, though it does not cover life insurance.

Data-heavy pricing makes this question sharper. A model may not use a forbidden factor directly, yet still use information closely linked to it, such as where someone lives or which shops they use. Economists call these proxies. The more an insurer knows, the more society has to decide which knowledge it is allowed to use.

Chapter 10

Climate change and the protection gap

The biggest force reshaping insurance is not technology. It is weather. Losses from floods, wildfires and storms have risen, driven by more building in exposed places, higher property values and, increasingly, a changing climate. In 2023 State Farm, the largest home insurer in California, stopped accepting new home insurance applications in the state, citing wildfire risk and building costs. Several insurers left or shrank in Florida. Homeowners in such places face rising prices or no private cover at all.

The difference between total economic losses from disasters and the part that is insured is called the protection gap. In rich countries a large share of disaster losses is insured. In many poorer countries almost none is. When insurance retreats, the cost does not vanish. It moves to households, banks holding mortgages, and governments.

This is where better data is most useful and most uncomfortable. Detailed flood and fire models let insurers price risk house by house. That is accurate, and it tells people the truth about where they live. It can also make some homes uninsurable, and therefore hard to sell or mortgage. Several governments run or back insurance schemes for exactly these risks. The Environment & Climate topics give the scientific background.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Sharper prices, faster claims, and a thinner pool

Most gains from insurtech are real. Most of the costs come from the same feature: knowing more about each customer.

Chapter 12

Why many insurtechs struggled

The insurtech stock-market listings of 2020 and 2021 were greeted with excitement. Several of the companies then lost most of their market value over the following years. Lemonade bought Metromile, a pay-per-mile car insurer, in 2022 after Metromile's shares had collapsed. The reasons are instructive.

Insurance punishes fast growth. A firm that grows quickly by offering low prices attracts the customers that other insurers have priced higher, often for good reason. This is adverse selection: the customers who choose you are the ones you have priced too cheaply. The losses arrive a year or two later, when the claims come in. By then the growth has been celebrated and the money has been spent.

Data is not the same as experience. Established insurers have decades of claims history. A clever model trained on a few years of data can miss rare, expensive events. And trust is fragile. In 2021 Lemonade faced a public backlash after a social-media post suggested its software analysed customers' claim videos for signs of fraud. The company deleted the post and said it did not reject claims automatically on that basis. The episode showed how quickly people react when they feel judged by a machine.

Chapter 14

Self-driving cars and the question of who is liable

Car insurance is the largest consumer insurance market in many countries, and it rests on one assumption: a human is driving. If cars drive themselves, much of the risk shifts from the driver to the maker of the car and its software. Insurance might then look more like product liability, bought by manufacturers, than personal cover bought by drivers. Britain's 2018 Automated and Electric Vehicles Act already set out how insurance should work when an automated vehicle causes a crash. Autonomous vehicles describes how far the technology really is from that point.

A second long-run question is about AI itself. As companies use AI systems to make decisions, new kinds of insurance appear: cover for errors by software, for cyber-attacks and for data leaks. Cyber insurance is already a fast-growing line, and insurers have struggled to price it, because one attack can hit thousands of customers at once. Financial cybersecurity covers the attacks.

The sensible view is that insurtech will not abolish the pool, but it will keep pushing on its edges. Each society will keep deciding which differences between people insurers may price, and which risks are too large, or too unfair, for private insurance to carry alone.

The core idea

Insurance pools risk, so that a disaster for one becomes a small, predictable cost for many. Insurtech applies software and data to the four jobs of an insurer: selling, pricing, paying claims and carrying risk. The more precisely it prices each person, the more it challenges the pooling that makes insurance work.

The numbers worth knowing
  • Combined ratio below 100% means the insurance itself is profitable.
  • Lloyd's began in a 1680s London coffee house.
  • EU ban on sex-based insurance prices: ruled 2011, in force from the end of 2012.
The common mistake

Thinking "fairer" pricing means the same thing as "more accurate" pricing. Perfectly accurate prices would leave the unluckiest people uninsurable. Fairness is a social choice, not a statistical result.

Test yourself

Can you explain why a fast-growing insurer with cheap prices can look healthy for two years and then report large losses? If you can, you understand float, adverse selection and the delay between premiums and claims.
