Payment processors. The toll booths on the road between your card and the shop.

When you pay a shop by card, at least four companies take part, and you have heard of perhaps two of them. Your bank and the card network get the attention. The firms on the shop's side, which accept the payment, check it, route it and pay the shop afterwards, are mostly invisible. Yet they decide how easy it is to start a business online, which payments are blocked as fraud, and how much of every sale the shop gets to keep. Some of them have become among the most valuable financial companies in the world.

Question 1 · What is it?

The companies that let a shop say yes to a card

A shop cannot talk to Visa or to your bank directly. It needs a firm that connects it to the system, takes responsibility for it, and pays it. That firm, in its various forms, is the payment processor.

Chapter 01

A few lines of code

Around 2010, accepting card payments on a website was painful. A new business had to apply for a merchant account with a bank, fill in long forms, wait weeks for approval, and then connect to a separate gateway with its own contract and software. Two Irish brothers, Patrick and John Collison, thought this was absurd for software developers who could build a whole website in a weekend. Their company, Stripe, let a developer start taking payments by pasting a few lines of code into a site. Stripe signed up the merchant almost instantly and handled the bank relationship behind the scenes.

That simplification turned into one of the largest financial technology companies in the world. It did not invent a new way to move money. Cards still went through the same networks and banks. What it changed was who could get access, and how fast. The same idea, applied to small shops through a little card reader that plugged into a phone, built Square, now called Block, in the United States.

In everyday speech, "payment processor" covers all the firms on the merchant's side. The distinctions matter because responsibility follows them. The acquirer is the one the card network holds liable if a merchant turns out to be fraudulent or goes bust.

Chapter 03

Four parties or three

Visa and Mastercard run what is called a four-party model: cardholder, issuer, acquirer and merchant, with the network in the middle connecting thousands of banks. The networks do not lend money or issue cards themselves. Visa began as a Bank of America card programme in 1958 and became a separate network owned by member banks. Both Visa and Mastercard became listed companies in the 2000s.

American Express traditionally runs a three-party model. It issues the card, signs up the merchant and runs the network itself, so it sees both sides of every payment. That gives it rich data and control over pricing, but it has to build both sides alone. China's UnionPay and the national debit schemes of some countries follow other variants. The model matters because it decides who sets the fees and who can compete.

Question 2 · How does it work?

Message now, money later, fee in between

A card payment is a sequence of messages followed by a sequence of money movements. The processor sits at both stages, and takes its fee from the gap.

Chapter 04

The life of a payment, from the shop's side

What happens to a 100-euro online sale
  SECOND 0    Customer enters card at checkout. The gateway encrypts it.
  SECOND 0-2  AUTHORISATION. Processor -> network -> issuer: "is this good?"
              Fraud checks run on BOTH sides. Issuer says yes and holds 100.
  LATER       CAPTURE. The shop confirms the sale, often when the goods ship.
  NIGHT       CLEARING. Captured sales are sent to the network in batches.
              Each side's bank learns exactly what it owes or is owed.
  DAY 1-2     SETTLEMENT. The issuer's bank pays the network's settlement
              bank, which pays the acquirer, in central bank money.
  DAY 1-3     FUNDING. The acquirer pays the shop: 100 minus all the fees.
  UP TO ~120  CHARGEBACK WINDOW. The customer can dispute; the money can be
  DAYS        pulled back from the shop.

The split between authorisation and capture is why a hotel can hold an amount on your card at check-in and charge a different total when you leave. The delay before funding matters for the shop's cash flow, and some processors charge extra for faster payout. The long chargeback window, set by the network rules and varying by reason, means a sale is not really final for the merchant for weeks.

Chapter 05

Where the fee goes

The shop pays a total fee, called the merchant service charge or merchant discount rate. It has three parts.

Interchange

Paid by the acquirer to the card issuer. Usually the largest part. Set by the networks, varying by card type and country. In the EU it has been capped since 2015 at 0.2 per cent for consumer debit and 0.3 per cent for consumer credit cards.

Scheme fees

Paid to Visa or Mastercard for using the network. Smaller per transaction, but there are many separate fees, and they have risen over time, which merchants complain about.

Acquirer margin

What the processor keeps for its service, risk and technology. Large merchants negotiate it hard; small ones often pay a simple flat rate that bundles everything.

In the United States, interchange on credit cards is not capped and often runs at around 2 per cent or more, especially on premium rewards cards. Debit is different: the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act included the Durbin amendment, which capped debit interchange for large banks at about 21 cents plus a small percentage per transaction. A flat-rate processor might charge a small shop a single figure of around 1.5 to 3 per cent plus a fixed fee per payment, depending on the country. The shop rarely sees how that is split. Every card payment carries a fee, and in the end it is built into prices that cash payers pay too.

Chapter 06

The platform model: one account, millions of merchants

Before platforms like Stripe and Square, each merchant needed its own account with an acquirer, which meant checks on the business, its owners and its finances. A payment facilitator holds one master account and signs up merchants beneath it in minutes. It runs its own lighter checks and watches merchants' behaviour after they join, rather than vetting them thoroughly before.

This shifted risk in an interesting way. Fast onboarding means some fraudsters get in. So platforms invest heavily in monitoring: sudden spikes in sales, many refunds, or orders from unusual countries can trigger a freeze. They also hold back part of a merchant's money for a while, a rolling reserve, to cover possible chargebacks. Merchants often find that the account that opened in five minutes can also be frozen in five minutes. Easy entry and sudden exit are two sides of the same model.

The other big change is embedded payments. Software firms that serve gyms, restaurants or online shops now build payment processing into their own product, using a platform in the background, and take a share of the fee. For many small businesses, the first time they think about a processor is when they choose their booking or till software.

Chapter 07

Keeping card numbers out of the wrong hands

Card details are valuable to criminals, so the networks require everyone who touches them to follow a security standard called PCI DSS, first published in 2004. Meeting it is expensive. Modern processors take the burden away from the shop: card details go straight from the customer's browser to the processor, and the shop only ever sees a token, a substitute number that is useless anywhere else.

For online payments, the main fraud tool in Europe is strong customer authentication, required under the EU's second Payment Services Directive (PSD2) and fully enforced from around 2021. The customer must confirm many online payments with two of three things: something they know, something they have (their phone), or something they are (a fingerprint). The technical system that delivers this, 3-D Secure, sends the customer to their bank's app to confirm. Fraud fell, but some genuine sales were lost when customers gave up at that step. Processors now use risk scoring to decide which payments can safely skip it, under exemptions the rules allow.

Question 3 · Why does it matter?

A cut of almost everything, and a gate on who can sell

Payment processing touches most sales in a modern economy. Small changes in its cost or its rules move large sums.

Chapter 08

The sale you lose without knowing

For a merchant, the most expensive failure is not the fee. It is the genuine customer whose payment is declined. Issuers decline payments that look risky, and a cautious issuer will refuse many good ones. For a large online merchant, a small rise in the share of payments approved, the authorisation rate, can be worth more than a big cut in fees.

This is where processors compete. Adyen, founded in the Netherlands in 2006, built one platform covering many countries and payment methods, and became a major processor for global brands. Its pitch, like that of rivals, is better data: sending payments to issuers in the format they prefer, retrying smartly when a payment fails, and sharing fraud signals. A processor is judged as much by the payments it gets approved as by the price it charges.

Chapter 09

Fees, rules and the long fight over interchange

Because card fees are built into prices, everyone pays them, including people who pay cash. And because rewards cards carry higher interchange, part of the cost of one customer's airline miles is paid by everyone else at the till. Economists and regulators have debated this for decades. Card issuers answer that cards bring benefits, such as security, credit and convenience, that are worth paying for.

Different places made different choices. The EU capped consumer interchange in 2015. Australia regulated it earlier. The United States capped only large banks' debit interchange, and merchants have fought the networks in court over credit card fees for many years, with large settlements and continuing disputes. The contrast is visible to travellers: generous American card rewards and modest European ones come from the same system under different rules.

Chapter 10

Wirecard: when a processor invented its money

Wirecard, a German payment processor, grew into one of Germany's most valuable companies and joined the DAX, the index of the country's largest firms, in 2018. Journalists, notably at the Financial Times, reported serious problems with its accounts. German authorities responded at first by investigating the journalists and short-sellers, and briefly banned bets against the company's shares. In June 2020 Wirecard admitted that 1.9 billion euros supposedly held in bank accounts in Asia probably did not exist. It collapsed within days.

The case showed how a processor's business could hide fraud. Much of its claimed revenue came from "third-party acquirers" in distant countries, partners that were hard for auditors to check. The failure was not of payment technology but of oversight: auditors, the German financial regulator and the company's board all missed or dismissed the warnings. Germany later reformed its financial supervisor. Fraud in payments is covered in Fraud detection.

Question 4 · Advantages and disadvantages

Easy access, at a price, with someone else holding the switch

Modern processors made selling easier than ever. They also concentrated power in a few hands.

Chapter 12

Gatekeepers of commerce

Because almost all online selling needs a processor, processors and networks have become gatekeepers. They can refuse whole industries they consider risky, such as some forms of gambling, adult content or firearms, even where those businesses are legal. In 2010 Visa, Mastercard and PayPal stopped processing donations to WikiLeaks. Supporters of such decisions say firms should not be forced to handle money for activities they find harmful or legally risky. Critics say private companies have too much power over legal speech and trade.

There is also outage risk. When a major processor or network has a technical failure, shops across a continent can be unable to take cards for hours. These events are rare, but they show how much depends on a small number of systems. Concentration makes payments cheap and reliable on most days, and fragile on the day something breaks. That is one reason central banks have backed public alternatives.

Chapter 14

Will the toll booths survive?

The card system is old but extremely good at what it does: it works almost everywhere, protects buyers, and settles reliably. Its weakness is cost. Wherever a cheap public alternative has appeared with strong backing, as in Brazil and India, it has taken a large share of payments very quickly.

Processors are adapting by becoming neutral switches. A modern platform offers cards, bank payments, digital wallets, buy-now-pay-later and local methods through one checkout, and earns from the service around the payment rather than the card fee alone. The future processor may care less about which rail a payment uses, and more about being the one that routes it. The cards themselves are the subject of the next topic, Credit cards.

The core idea

Payment processors connect merchants to the card system. The acquirer takes responsibility for the shop, the network connects it to the issuer, and the shop pays a fee made of interchange, scheme fees and the acquirer's margin. Platforms like Stripe made access instant by carrying the risk themselves.

The numbers worth knowing
  • EU interchange caps since 2015: 0.2% for consumer debit, 0.3% for consumer credit.
  • US large-bank debit interchange is capped at about 21 cents plus a small percentage (Durbin amendment, 2010).
  • Wirecard's missing money: 1.9 billion euros, admitted in June 2020.
The common mistake

Thinking Visa takes the card fee. Most of it is interchange, which goes to the card issuer, your own bank. The network's share is smaller.

Can you explain why a shop's sale is not truly final for weeks after the customer pays? If so, you understand capture, settlement and chargebacks.

The words, in plain words
Issuer
The bank that issues the customer's card.
Acquirer
The bank or firm that accepts card payments for a merchant and is responsible for it.
Card network
The company, such as Visa or Mastercard, that sets the rules and connects issuers and acquirers.
Gateway
Software that links a checkout to the processor and handles card data securely.
Payment facilitator
A firm that signs up many merchants under one master account.
Authorisation
The issuer's real-time approval of a payment, which places a hold on the funds.
Capture
The merchant confirming a sale, so the authorised amount is charged.
Merchant service charge
The total fee a shop pays to accept a card payment.
Interchange
The part of the fee paid to the card issuer.
Chargeback
A payment reversed after a customer disputes it with their bank.
Rolling reserve
Part of a merchant's takings held back for a time to cover possible chargebacks.
PCI DSS
The card industry's security standard for anyone handling card data.
Strong customer authentication
The EU rule requiring two forms of proof for many online payments.
