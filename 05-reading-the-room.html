Sharp Mind 05 · people

Reading the room. The same good answer lands three different ways on three different people.

You can diagnose the problem, ask the right question, structure the answer and deliver it without a single hedge — and still lose, because you gave the detail to someone who wanted the headline, or pushed on a decision to someone who was worried about something you had not noticed. This handbook is the missing input: who is in front of you, what they need, and how to adjust without becoming a different person for each of them.

What "emotional intelligence" means here

Not empathy as a feeling. The workable definition is narrower and more useful: reading someone's state accurately, and adjusting what you do because of it. Two separate skills, and most people who are described as bad at this are actually fine at the reading and simply do not change anything as a result.

Which is good news, because the adjusting half is entirely mechanical.

What this does not cover

A great deal of this territory is already taught next door and is not repeated here. master-yourself/phase-1, file 02 has Goleman's four domains and emotional regulation. phase-9, file 01 has active listening, the levels of listening, nonviolent communication, and empathy versus sympathy. phase-9, file 02 has labelling, mirroring and tactical empathy. phase-9, file 03 has Gottman's repair work and attachment.

What follows is only the part none of those cover: the organisational version — managing up, stakeholders, disagreement that does not cost you, and the politics you cannot opt out of by being nice.

Part I · Reading

What is actually going on

Three sections on diagnosis. The first is the one to memorise — it explains more workplace friction than any other single idea in this library.

Reading 01

The three unspoken questions

Everyone you deal with is running three background questions about you, continuously, and almost never out loud. Most friction that looks like a disagreement about the work is one of these three being quietly answered "no".

THE THREE QUESTIONS, RUNNING UNDERNEATH EVERY INTERACTION ① AM I SAFE WITH YOU? Will you use this against me? Will you react badly if I tell you something you don't want to hear? Am I going to regret being honest? ② DO YOU SEE ME? Do you know what I actually do? Did you notice the hard part? Am I a person here or a function? ③ ARE YOU COMPETENT? If I back you, do I look good or stupid? Can you actually deliver the thing you're describing? ⇒ ① AND ② ARE THE WARMTH AXIS. ③ IS THE COMPETENCE AXIS. (file 04 §01) ⭐ THE DIAGNOSTIC: When someone is being difficult and the stated reason does not quite add up, ask yourself WHICH OF THE THREE IS UNANSWERED. It is almost always one of them, and it is almost never ③.
QuestionWhat "no" looks like from outsideWhat answers it
Am I safe? Careful, formal answers. Bad news arrives late or via someone else. They agree in the meeting and not afterwards. Nobody tells you things. Predictability more than kindness. React the same way to bad news as to good — visibly. Thank people for early warnings, out loud, even when the news is terrible. Never punish the messenger, not even with a sigh.
Do you see me? Withdrawal. Doing the minimum. Small resentments about credit. A good person going quiet in meetings they used to drive. Specific attention. Name what they did, precisely — "the reconciliation script, which is why this took two days and not two weeks". Vague praise does not answer this question; specificity is the entire signal.
Are you competent? Being checked. Questions about detail you have already covered. Someone else being copied on your work. One exact number, a clear position, and the answer before the reasoning — file 01, 03 and 04 are all about this one.
Why the safety question matters most if anyone reports to you

Whether people tell you things early is not a personality outcome, it is a consequence of how you reacted the last three times. One visible flash of irritation at bad news buys you months of finding out late. And finding out late is the single most expensive thing that can happen to someone running anything.

The corollary is a rule worth adopting permanently: the first sentence out of your mouth when someone brings you a problem is "thanks for flagging it early" — before any question, before any reaction. Say it even when you are annoyed. Especially then.

Reading 02

State versus content

People say things for two reasons at once: because of the content, and because of the state they are in. Reading only the content is how you end up winning an argument about a deadline with someone whose actual problem was that they expect to be blamed.

The signals worth watching

None of these mean anything on their own. What matters is the change — this person against this person an hour ago.

Tempo

Answers get shorter, or much longer. Both are signals. Short usually means guarded; long usually means justifying.

Formality

Someone who normally writes "sure, will do" writes "Noted, I will action this accordingly." That shift is almost never about the task.

The pre-emptive defence

They explain why something is not their fault before anyone has suggested it is. Reliable tell that they expect blame — usually because they got it somewhere before.

Going quiet

Someone who usually drives a meeting says nothing in it. The most under-read signal in working life, and worth a two-line message afterwards every single time.

Volunteering the surprising detail

They mention something irrelevant-seeming and slightly personal. Often a test of question ① — are you safe. Answering it well pays for months.

Copying someone in

A thread that was between two people suddenly has a manager on it. That is a state signal, not an administrative one, and it is usually about safety.

Check, do not conclude
⚠ THE FAILURE MODE IS MIND-READING, AND IT IS WORSE THAN
  MISSING THE SIGNAL ENTIRELY.

  ✗ "You're clearly annoyed about this."
     → now they have to defend against your reading of them
       before anything real can be discussed. And if you
       were wrong, you have created the friction you thought
       you detected.

  ✓ "You seem less keen on this than last week — am I
     reading that right?"
     → tentative, checkable, and easy to say no to. Which
       is exactly what makes it easy to say yes to.

⭐ THE GRAMMAR THAT MAKES IT WORK IS THE HEDGE.
  "It seems like", "I might be misreading this", "am I
  right that" — these are the ONE PLACE hedging is
  correct (file 04 §04 says cut hedges; this is the
  documented exception). An assertion about someone's
  inner state invites correction. A tentative one invites
  elaboration.

⇒ THIS IS LABELLING, AND IT IS TAUGHT PROPERLY AS PART OF
  TACTICAL EMPATHY IN master-yourself/phase-9 FILE 02.
  What is added here is only the workplace framing and
  the rule below.

⭐ THE RULE: THE CONTENT IS OFTEN NOT THE ISSUE.
  When an objection does not quite add up — the stated
  reason is too small for the resistance — the real
  objection is somewhere else. Ask:
    "Is there something about this that worries you that
     we haven't talked about?"
  Said warmly, this unlocks more than any argument.
Reading 03

Register — the same content, fitted to the person

People differ, consistently and predictably, in how they want information delivered. Mismatching this is the most common way good content gets judged as bad communication — and it is entirely fixable, because the preferences are stable and you only have to learn them once per person.

DimensionThe tellThe adjustment
Headline vs detail Headline people interrupt your context to ask "so what's the ask?". Detail people ask three clarifying questions before engaging with your conclusion. Lead with the answer for both (file 03), then offer the depth rather than delivering it: "I can go a level down on any of that."
Written vs verbal Some people answer a five-line message in ten seconds and are hopeless in a meeting. Others never read anything and decide everything in conversation. Use their channel for the actual decision, then confirm in the other one. "As discussed — here's the summary" covers both.
Fast vs considered Some decide in the room. Others need to think and will say yes to anything in the moment and change their mind by Thursday. For considered people, send it the day before. Never spring a decision on them; you will get a soft yes that does not hold.
Direct vs contextual Direct people find preamble evasive. Contextual people find directness abrupt and slightly aggressive. Same content, one sentence of framing added or removed. This is not dishonesty — it is the same point, addressed to someone.
You are allowed to just ask

"Do you want the summary or the whole thing?" · "Is it easier if I send this first or talk it through?"

Asked once, remembered permanently, and it reads as considerate rather than uncertain. Almost nobody does this, and it removes an entire category of avoidable friction. Write the answer down in your notes on that person — you will not remember it in four months.

Part II · Direction

Up, across, and against

Three relationships that need different handling: the person you report to, the people whose agreement you need, and anyone you have to disagree with.

Reading 04

Managing up

The largest untaught skill in professional life. Nobody explains it, most people learn it by getting it wrong for several years, and it accounts for a great deal of the variance in whose career moves.

Start from their side of the desk

Four things about your manager that change everything
① THEY ARE OPTIMISING ACROSS THINGS YOU CANNOT SEE
   Your project is one of six. A decision that looks
   irrational from inside your project is often correct
   across all six. When something makes no sense, the
   missing information is usually at their altitude —
   and "what am I not seeing?" is a better opening than
   an argument.

② THEY HAVE A BOSS TOO
   Almost everything they ask you for is something they
   have to defend upward. Which leads to the single
   highest-leverage habit in this section — see below.

③ THEY ARE TIME-POOR IN A SPECIFIC WAY
   Not short of hours. Short of CONTEXT-SWITCHES. The
   cost of your update is not its length, it is the
   reload it requires. Which is why the answer goes first.

④ THEY HATE SURPRISES MORE THAN THEY HATE BAD NEWS
   ⭐ THIS IS THE WHOLE RELATIONSHIP.
   Bad news early is a gift — it gives them room to act.
   The same news late is a betrayal, because you took
   their options away while they thought things were fine.
   A manager who is surprised in front of THEIR boss will
   remember it for years.

   ⇒ THE RULE: NO SURPRISES. Tell them while it is still
     small, even when you think you can fix it. Especially
     when you think you can fix it.

The highest-leverage habit: make it forwardable

Write so your manager can forward it without editing

Your manager has to repeat your update to someone else — their boss, another team, a steering group. If your update is a wall of context, they have to rewrite it, and rewriting it is work you handed them.

So write every significant update as though it will be forwarded verbatim: answer first, two supports, the number, the ask, the date. No inside references, no "as discussed", no jokes that need context. Do this consistently and something specific happens — you become the person whose updates get quoted upward with your framing intact, which means your framing is what senior people hear.

This is file 03 applied with one extra constraint, and it is worth more than any other single habit in this handbook.

The three upward formats
THE STATUS UPDATE — weekly, five lines maximum
  "On track for the 22nd.
   Changed this week: vendor confirmed the auth fix.
   Watching: the migration rehearsal on Thursday — if
     that slips we lose the date, and I'll know by 5pm.
   Need from you: nothing this week."
  ⭐ "NEED FROM YOU: NOTHING" IS A REAL LINE AND YOU
    SHOULD USE IT. It tells them they can stop reading,
    which is exactly what builds the trust that makes
    them read carefully when you DO need something.

THE ESCALATION — when you need them to act
  ✗ bringing a problem
    "The vendor won't commit to a date, what should we do?"
    → you have handed them your work
  ✓ bringing a recommendation
    "The vendor won't commit. I see two options: hold the
     date and drop the reconciliation step, or move two
     weeks. I'd move two weeks. I need a decision by
     Thursday to hold the tender slot."
    → position · options · recommendation · deadline
    ⇒ ALWAYS ARRIVE WITH A RECOMMENDATION, EVEN A WEAK
      ONE. "I don't have a strong view but I lean X" is
      infinitely better than no view. It gives them
      something to react to, which is much cheaper for
      them than generating something.

THE ASK — when you need something for yourself
  ✗ "I've got a lot on at the moment."
  ✓ "I can do the migration or the analysis this month,
     not both. Which do you want first?"
  ⇒ SPECIFIC, WITH OPTIONS. A vague complaint about
    workload asks them to solve it. A trade-off asks them
    to choose, which is their actual job.
What they need and will never say
  • To not have to ask. Being chased for an update is a small tax that accumulates into a real impression. Send it before they ask, every time, even when there is nothing to report.
  • To know you have it handled. Not that it is going perfectly — that you know where it stands and you will tell them if it changes. Those are different, and the second one is what actually buys you autonomy.
  • To be able to defend you. When someone questions your work in a room you are not in, your manager needs a sentence and a number. Give them that sentence in advance and they will use it.
Reading 05

Stakeholder mapping

Before anything that needs other people's agreement, spend five minutes on this. It is the cheapest insurance available and almost nobody does it.

File 02, §07 gave you four roles — decides, consulted, does, informed. Two more matter for anything contested:

The six roles, and the five-minute map
DECIDES      can say yes and make it stick
CONSULTED    must be asked first; can block informally
DOES         performs the work; can block by not doing it
INFORMED     needs to know afterwards; no say
BLOCKS       ⭐ can stop it without owning it — Legal,
             Security, Finance, the one senior sceptic.
             The most commonly missed role, and the one
             that kills projects late.
INFLUENCES   the person the decider actually phones
             before deciding. Often not senior. Often
             not on any org chart.

THE MAP — five minutes, on paper, before you propose
  ┌──────────────┬──────────┬───────────────────────────┐
  │ NAME         │ ROLE     │ WHAT THEY CARE ABOUT      │
  ├──────────────┼──────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ Karim        │ decides  │ hitting the Q3 number     │
  │ Legal        │ blocks   │ not being surprised       │
  │ Ops lead     │ does     │ not absorbing more work   │
  │ Rim          │ influ.   │ whether it's been tested  │
  └──────────────┴──────────┴───────────────────────────┘

  ⇒ THE THIRD COLUMN IS THE WHOLE EXERCISE. "What are they
    measured on?" is the question that predicts behaviour
    better than anything about personality.

⭐ THE PRE-BRIEF — the move this map exists to enable
  NEVER LET A SENIOR PERSON, OR ANYONE WHO CAN BLOCK,
  HEAR YOUR PROPOSAL FOR THE FIRST TIME IN THE ROOM.

  Five minutes beforehand: "I'm proposing X on Thursday —
  wanted to check it with you first. What am I missing?"

  WHY IT WORKS, AND WHY IT IS NOT MANIPULATION:
    · they can object privately, where it costs them
      nothing to be wrong
    · you find the fatal objection BEFORE it is public
    · "what am I missing?" is a genuine question, and
      they often have the answer
    · nobody has to defend a position in front of an
      audience, which is when positions harden

  ⇒ People who skip this and then lose the room call it
    politics. It is the opposite: it is doing your
    homework about the humans, exactly as you would about
    the numbers.
Reading 06

Disagreeing without damage

Most people have two settings: agree, or disagree in a way that costs something. There is a third, and it is mechanical.

The four-move sequence
① RESTATE THEIR VIEW — better than they said it
   "So the case for shipping now is that the window
    closes in November and a partial release still
    gets us the learning. That's a strong argument."
   ⇒ THIS IS THE STEELMAN (master-yourself/phase-0 file 03).
     Do it genuinely. If you cannot state their position
     in a form they would endorse, you do not understand
     it well enough to disagree with it yet.
   ⇒ And it does something structural: they now know they
     were heard, so they can consider your point instead
     of repeating theirs.

② AGREE WITH WHAT IS TRUE
   "The window is real and I don't think we should
    ignore it."
   ⇒ almost never nothing to agree with. Finding it costs
     you nothing and lowers the temperature immediately.

③ STATE THE DIFFERENCE — plainly, once
   "Where I come out differently is the partial release.
    I think shipping without reconciliation gives us a
    number Finance can't use, so we'd get the window
    and not the learning."
   ⇒ no hedging (file 04 §04), no softening it into
     mush. The warmth came from ① and ②; the clarity
     comes here. Both, not one.

④ HAND IT BACK AS A QUESTION
   "What would have to be true for the partial release
    to still be useful to Finance?"
   ⇒ file 02 §03. Converts the disagreement into a
     checkable list instead of a contest.

⭐ THE UNIVERSAL OPENER WHEN YOU DO NOT YET UNDERSTAND
  "Help me understand how you got there."
  Genuinely curious, impossible to take offence at, and
  it frequently dissolves the disagreement — because
  roughly half the time you were disagreeing about
  different things.
  ⚠ tone is everything. Said flatly it is curiosity.
    Said with an edge it is an accusation, and everyone
    hears the difference.

Public or private?

Disagree in public about substance — a number, a risk, a fact. Disagree in private about judgement or competence. The test: could they change their mind here without losing face? If not, take it offline. Public disagreement that forces someone to defend themselves hardens the position you were trying to move.

Disagree and commit

When the decision goes against you: say so once, clearly, then back it fully. "I'd have gone the other way, but it's decided and I'll make it work." Re-litigating a settled decision costs more standing than losing it ever did — and being visibly good at losing a decision is a strong credibility signal.

Pick your count

You have a limited budget of disagreement per quarter with any given person. Spend it on things that matter and let the rest go. Someone who objects to everything is not rigorous, they are noise, and their objection to the thing that actually matters gets discounted with the rest.

Leave the exit open

Never construct the disagreement so that the other person has to admit they were foolish. Give them a door: "this only became obvious once we had October". People accept conclusions far more readily when accepting does not require a confession.

Part III · Politics and repair

The part nobody teaches on purpose

Two sections on the things that are usually left implicit, and the drill set.

Reading 07

Political intelligence, without being political

"Office politics" is usually said as a complaint, and the complaint smuggles in a false idea: that there exists a version of collective work where people have identical incentives and decisions are made purely on merit. There is not, and there never has been.

Politics is just the fact that decisions involve people with different incentives. Refusing to see that does not make you principled. It makes you someone whose good proposals keep mysteriously failing.

Where the line actually is
AWARENESS — legitimate, and most people are bad at it

  ✓ knowing what each person is measured on
  ✓ pre-briefing people before a decision (§05)
  ✓ framing the same true proposal in the terms that
    matter to this audience — cost for Finance, risk
    for Legal, workload for Ops
  ✓ knowing who talks to whom, and who the decider
    actually listens to
  ✓ timing — not raising something in the week of the
    quarter close
  ✓ building relationships before you need them

MANIPULATION — not legitimate, and it is not a grey area

  ✗ taking credit for other people's work
  ✗ withholding information someone needs to do their job
  ✗ telling two people different versions of the same thing
  ✗ making someone look bad to look better by comparison
  ✗ agreeing in the room and undermining it outside

⭐ THE TEST THAT SEPARATES THEM:
  WOULD THIS STILL WORK IF EVERYONE COULD SEE IT?

  Pre-briefing survives that test completely — you can
  say out loud "I checked this with Legal first" and it
  reads as competence. Undermining someone in a corridor
  does not survive it for a second. The test is reliable
  and it takes two seconds.

⚠ AND THE ONE RULE ABOVE ALL OTHERS:
  NEVER MAKE SOMEONE LOOK BAD IN PUBLIC. NOT EVEN WHEN
  YOU ARE RIGHT. ESPECIALLY WHEN YOU ARE RIGHT.
  It is the most expensive thing you can do in an
  organisation, the debt never clears, and the person
  who does it is remembered for it long after the point
  they were right about is forgotten.
The cheapest political asset there is

Be useful to people before you need anything. The five-minute favour — an introduction, a document you already had, an answer to a question that took you thirty seconds and would have taken them an hour — is the highest-return investment available in any organisation.

It is also not a tactic, which is why it works. People can tell instantly whether help is being banked for later. Help because it is cheap for you and valuable to them; the returns arrive anyway, and they arrive from directions you did not predict.

Reading 08

Repair after friction

Something goes wrong between you and someone else — a sharp exchange, a missed handover, a meeting where one of you was undermined. What happens next matters more than what happened.

The repair, in four parts
⭐ THE 24-HOUR RULE
  Address it within a day. Not because feelings fade, but
  because EXPLANATIONS SET. Given a day or two, people
  build a story about why you did it, and the story is
  always less generous than the truth. After that you are
  arguing with a narrative rather than an event.

THE SHAPE — in person or on a call, never over text

  ① NAME IT PLAINLY
     "That got sharp yesterday and I don't want to leave
      it there."
     ⚠ not "I feel like maybe things were a bit off?" —
       vagueness makes them do the naming, which is the
       work you came to do.

  ② OWN YOUR PART, SPECIFICALLY
     "I cut you off twice and I was short about the
      timeline."
     ⚠ SPECIFIC, NOT GLOBAL. "Sorry if I upset you" is
       not an apology, it is a conditional about their
       reaction. Name the actual behaviour.
     ⚠ AND ONCE — file 04 §06. Repeated apology turns a
       repair into a scene, and asks them to comfort you.

  ③ ASK, THEN STOP TALKING
     "How did it land for you?"
     ⇒ then be quiet. This is the hard part and the part
       that does the work. Do not fill the silence, do not
       explain yourself further, do not pre-answer it.

  ④ SAY WHAT CHANGES
     "I'll bring the timeline concern to you before the
      meeting next time, not in it."
     ⇒ one concrete thing. Not a promise to be better.

⭐ WHEN THEY WERE THE ONE IN THE WRONG
  Give them the exit ramp. "I think we were both working
  off different versions of the date" is often literally
  true and always more useful than establishing fault.
  ⇒ you are buying a working relationship, not a verdict.
    Deciding which one you actually want is the whole
    decision, and people who choose the verdict usually
    regret it within a month.

⚠ WHAT NOT TO REPAIR
  Not everything needs a conversation. A single sharp
  exchange between people who work well together is
  usually just a Tuesday. Reserve this for when something
  has actually shifted — when the tempo, formality or
  openness signals from §02 have changed and stayed
  changed.
The relationship version

Repair in close personal relationships is a deeper subject with real research behind it — Gottman's work on repair attempts, the Four Horsemen, and the 5:1 ratio is taught in master-yourself/phase-9, file 03. What is above is the workplace version: shorter, more behavioural, and deliberately less about feelings, because a colleague has not signed up to process feelings with you and the repair works better when it respects that.

Reading 09

The drills

1 · The three questions · daily ⭐

Once a day, when an interaction is friction-y, ask yourself which of the three unspoken questions is unanswered. Do not act on it yet — just name it. After a few weeks the naming starts happening live, which is the point.

2 · "Thanks for flagging it early" · every time

Make it the first sentence whenever anyone brings you a problem. Before questions, before reaction. This is one sentence and it changes what people tell you within about a month.

3 · The register note · per person

Keep two lines per person you work with regularly: headline or detail, written or verbal, fast or considered. Add to it when you learn something. You will not remember otherwise, and the file is worth more each year.

4 · The forwardable update · weekly

Write one update this week as though your manager will forward it verbatim to their boss. Answer first, two supports, one number, the ask, the date. No inside references.

5 · The pre-brief · before anything contested

Five minutes with the one person most likely to object, the day before. "What am I missing?" If you do one thing from this handbook, do this — it has the highest hit rate of anything here.

6 · The steelman · weekly

Before disagreeing with anyone that matters, write their position in a form they would endorse. If you cannot, you are not ready. This takes four minutes and prevents most bad disagreements.

The minimum viable version

Drill 2 and drill 5. "Thanks for flagging it early" changes what information reaches you; the pre-brief changes what happens to your proposals. Both are one-line habits with disproportionate returns, and neither requires you to be a different person.

Five handbooks in, you can diagnose a situation, open it, structure your answer, deliver it credibly and fit it to the person in front of you. There is one thing left that no amount of technique supplies: something worth saying in the first place. File 06 treats that as what it actually is — a supply problem, not a personality problem — and shows you how to stock the shelf, including a worked route into the 247 chapters sitting in culture/ and into the technology briefings in files 11 to 16.

On sourcing. The three-questions framing is this library's own compression of well-established ideas — psychological safety, recognition, and the competence axis from file 04 — rather than a citation of a single study; treat it as a diagnostic that earns its place by being useful, not as a research finding. The state-reading signals, the managing-up formats, the stakeholder roles and the repair protocol are craft: widely used, field-tested, and to be judged by whether they work for you. Where a technique here has a researched cousin next door — labelling, steelmanning, Gottman's repair work — this file says so and points at it rather than restating it with borrowed authority.
